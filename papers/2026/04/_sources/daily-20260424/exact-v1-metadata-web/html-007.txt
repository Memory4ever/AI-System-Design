L33: # Hill’s level surfaces in the circular restricted three-body problem solved
L34: 
L35: Jean-Marc Huré Affiliation: Université de Bordeaux, CNRS, LAB, UMR 5804, F-33600 Pessac, France
L36: 
L37: August 24, 2026
L38: ###### Abstract
L39: 
L40: We report the closed-form expression for Hill’s surfaces in the circular restricted three-body problem. The solution $\phi(r,\theta)$, derived in the primary-centric spherical coordinate system, is deduced from a cubic equation delivering at most two roots on each side of a separatrix. The famous patterns (tadpole, horseshoe and peanut shapes, Roche lobes and Hill’s quasi-spheres) are exactly produced.
L41: ###### Keywords:
L42: 
L43: Gravitation – Methods: analytical – Celestial mechanics – (Stars:) binaries: general

L46: ## I Context and challenges
L47: In the circular restricted three-body problem (hereafter CRTBP), a particle moves in the gravity field of two point-like massive stars orbiting each other at constant rate and separation. This problem occupies a central place in celestial mechanics and dynamical astronomy for more than two centuries.
L48: It is fundamental for the physics of binary stars (e.g., Roche lobe overflow, lightcurves), for the dynamics of particles in star-planet systems and protoplanetary discs, and for space guidance and Solar System exploration. It is widely reviewed in textbooks [cite21†1 , cite22†2 , cite23†3 , cite24†4 , cite25†5 , e.g.], and has several interesting varieties [cite26†6 , cite27†7 , cite28†8 , cite29†9 , cite30†10 , cite31†11 , cite32†12 , cite33†13 , cite34†14 , e.g.].
L49: A remarkable property of the CRTBP is that the test particle is allowed to visit not all but certain regions of space, depending on the Jacobi constant $C=-2W({\mathbf{r}})-{\mathbf{v}}^{2}$, which is set by initial conditions in velocity ${\mathbf{v}}$ and position ${\mathbf{r}}$. The knowledge of equipotential surfaces $W({\mathbf{r}})={\rm const.}$ (also known as Hill’s surfaces or even “zero-velocity surfaces”) that precisely separate permitted and forbidden regions is therefore of great interest.
L50: In the plane of the binary, these frontiers draw famous patterns: tadpole-shaped curves around equilateral Lagrange points, degenerating in horseshoe shapes as the potential decreases, then in a central peanut figure and an outer oval. Then comes the Roche lobes and Hill’s quasi-spheres [cite35†15 , cite36†16 ]. The literature apparently does not evoke the eventuality that $W({\mathbf{r}})$ is reversible, unless some approximations [cite37†17 , cite38†18 ].
L51: Usually, an equipotential $W_{0}$ is found from three different numerical techniques, which are easily accessible with modern programming language and softwares: i) by successive approximations based on the fixed-point interaction, for instance $z=f(z;x,y,W_{0})$, ii) by integration of a total derivative formed by differentiating $W$, e.g.
L52: $dx/dy|_{w_{0}}=-\partial_{y}W/\partial_{x}W$ in a plane $z={\rm const.}$, or iii) by detecting the zeros of the function $W(x,y,z)-W_{0}$ inside a precomputed grid from a root-finding method. In this article, we derive the solution ${\mathbf{r}}(W_{0})$ by analytical means, therefore avoiding any systematic and complicated numerical procedure, and the generation of tables [cite39†19 , cite40†20 , e.g.].
L53: The solution is exact, generally degenerate (roots of a cubic equation), and involves no series but standard functions.
L54: ## II Level surfaces from a cubic
L55: In the CRTBP, the coordinate system is usually rotating with the two bodies. We follow this convention: the $x$-axis is chosen as the binary axis, and $z$ is perpendicular to the binary’s plane of motion, as depicted in Fig. cite41†1 . With the standard barycentric frame, the distance between this particle and each star depends explicitly on the mass ratio, which is not optimal here.
L56: Instead, we use the primary-centric coordinate system, where one of the stars (the primary, here) stands at the origin [cite42†21 , e.g.]. If all lengths are normalized to the binary separation $a$, then the potential $w$ that dictates the displacement of the particle at $\bm{r}(x,y,z)$, in units of $GM_{1}/a$, is
L57:  | $$w(x,y,z)=-\frac{1}{r}-\frac{q}{\Delta}-\frac{1+q}{2}\left[\left(x-\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{2}+y^{2}\right],$$  |  | (1)
L58: 
L59: where $q=M_{2}/M_{1}\in\,]0,1]$ is the mass ratio, and $\Delta$ is the distance from the secondary (with mass $M_{2}$), with
L60: 
L61:  | $$\Delta^{2}=(x-1)^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}.$$  |  | (2)
L62: Figure 2: Values of the parameter $p<-1$ of the depressed cubic (left), computed form (cite43†4 ) by scanning the range $r\in[0,2]$, for $q=0.3$, $\sin\theta=1$ and $w_{0}=-2.4$ (case III; see Sect. cite12†III ). Positive roots of the cubic versus the radius (middle left). For $1.28\lesssim r\lesssim 1.36$ (vertical lines), branches A_{0} and B_{1} are fed simultaneously. Cosine of the azimuth $\phi$ deduced from (cite44†5 ) (middle right).
L63: Equipotential (oval and interior peanut shapes) in the plane of the binary (right), limited to the half-plane $y\geq 0$.
L64: By converting (cite45†1 ) and (cite46†2 ) in spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$ still attached to the primary-centric frame (see Fig. cite41†1 ), we notice that the azimuth $\phi$ can be eliminated in the first equation, leading to a depressed cubic equation in the variable $\Delta$, namely
L65: 
L66:  | $$\Delta^{3}+3p\Delta+2s=0,$$  |  | (3)
L67: 
L68: where $s=1$, and
L69: 
L70:  |  | $\displaystyle 3p=\frac{2}{q}w+\frac{2}{qr}-\frac{1}{1+q}+r^{2}\left(\frac{1+q}{q}\sin^{2}\theta-1\right).$  |  | (4)
L71: This simple manipulation offers a direct way to construct a given equipotential $w(x,y,z)=w_{0}$ because the cubic equations are solvable by analytical means. There are four steps : i) selection of a pair of coordinates $(r,\theta)$, ii) determination of the parameter $p\equiv p(q,w_{0};r,\theta)$ from (cite43†4 ), iii) calculation of the roots of the depressed cubic (if any; see below), and iv) calculation of the coordinate $\phi$ through its cosine from (cite46†2 ), that is
L72:  | $$\cos\phi=\frac{1+r^{2}-\Delta^{2}}{2r\sin\theta}\in[-1,1].$$  |  | (5)
L73: 
L74: A level curve is therefore found by varying either the radius $r\in]0,\infty[$ or the colatitude angle $\theta\in]0,\pi[$, or both, then leading to surface levels^{1}^{1} 1 Note that he function $w$ being symmetric with respect to the $xz$- and $xy$-planes, the analysis can be limited to $y\geq 0$ and $z\geq 0$..
L75: ### II.1 Real, positive roots
L76: 
L77: The nature of the three roots of (cite47†3 ) depends on the sign of the discriminant $\delta=-108(1+p^{3})$. If $p>-1$ there are two complex roots and a real root, but it can be shown that $\Delta<0$ in this case, which is not acceptable. The three real roots obtained for $p\leq-1$ are
L78: 
L79:  | $$\Delta_{k}=2\sqrt{-p}\cos\left[\frac{1}{3}\arccos\left(\frac{s}{p\sqrt{-p}}\right)-\frac{2k\pi}{3}\right],$$  |  | (6)
L80: with $k\in\{0,1,2\}$, but only $\Delta_{0}$ and $\Delta_{1}$ are positive and relevant. Then, two cosine $\cos\phi_{k}$ follow from (cite44†5 ), which is enough to get the points on the equipotential.
L81: ### II.2 The main separatrix and the second cubic
L82: The case $p=-1$ corresponds to a vanishing discriminant $\delta$ for the cubic, and we have $\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{1}=1$. We see from (cite43†4 ) and (cite44†5 ) that it corresponds to a certain surface, a separatrix, denoted ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$, which separates the space into two regions [cite48†22 ]. From (cite44†5 ), we get $x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi=r^{2}/2$ (then, $y$ and $z$ easily follow), meaning that this separatrix is the sphere with unit radius and the secondary as the center.
L83: It can be shown that the relevant root is $\Delta_{0}$ (and $\Delta_{1}$) when ${\mathbf{r}}$ is outside (inside, respectively) ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$. Setting $p=-1$ in (cite43†4 ) leads to another depressed cubic
L84:  | $$r^{3}+3p^{\prime}r+2s^{\prime}=0$$  |  | (7)
L85: 
L86: where
L87: 
L88:  | $\displaystyle 3p^{\prime}$  | $\displaystyle=s^{\prime}\left[2w+q\left(\frac{3q+2}{q+1}\right)\right],$  |  | (8a)
L89:  | $\displaystyle s^{\prime}=$  | $\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+q)\sin^{2}\theta-q}.$  |  | (8b)
L90: As a consequence, the roots of this new cubic equation (if any) give the positions where $\Delta_{0}$ and $\Delta_{1}$ meet on ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$. Again, the real, positive roots of (cite49†7 ), denoted $r_{k}$, depend on the sign of the discriminant $-108({s^{\prime}}^{2}+{p^{\prime}}^{3})$. As for the first cubic, and for the same reasons, only the roots obtained for $k\in\{0,1\}$ are relevant^{2}^{2} 2 Formally, the expression for the three roots $r_{k}$ is simmilar as (cite50†6 )..
L91: When ${p^{\prime}}^{3}=-{s^{\prime}}^{2}$, (cite49†7 ) has a double root, i.e. $r_{0}=r_{1}=1$. This happens for a special value of $w_{0}$ that is deduced from (cite51†8a ) and (cite52†8b ) (it depends on $\theta$). As for the first cubic, there is a closed curve belonging to ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$, denoted ${\cal C}$ (another separatrix), where $r_{0}$ and $r_{1}$ merge. That curve is in fact the circle with radius $\sqrt{3}/2$ and centre at Cartesian coordinates $(\frac{1}{2},0,0)$.
L92: It meets the plane of the binary at the triangular Lagrange point L_{4} because $r=\Delta=1$, and we have
L93:  | $$w_{L_{4}}=-\frac{3q^{2}+5q+1}{2(1+q)}.$$  |  | (9)
L94: 
L95: We set $r_{-}\equiv r_{1}$ and $r_{+}=r_{0}\geq r_{-}$ from now on.

L98: ### II.3 Equatorial plane of the binary
L99: 
L100: The orbital plane of the stars has always been of immense interest and the shape of equipotentials is relatively familiar [cite54†23 , e.g.]; see the Introduction. This is the reason why we propose to illustrate the method by setting $\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}$. An equipotential at $z=0$ can be composed of at most three components depending on $w_{0}$ (by order of appearance as $w_{0}$ decreases):
L101: 
L102:   * •
L103: component A, for tadpole and horseshoe shapes, obtained for the highest permitted values of $w_{0}$,
L104: 
L105:   * •
L106: 
L107: component B, for the interior peanut shape, for “intermediate” values of $w_{0}$
L108: 
L109:   * •
L110: 
L111: component C, for the Roche lobes and Hill’s quasi-spheres, drawn as $w_{0}\rightarrow-\infty$.
L112: It follows from above that any component of the equipotential $w(x,y,0)=w_{0}$ that crosses the separatrix ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$ once is composed of two branches, because of the two roots for the cubic (this holds for $z>0$). For component A, one branch is computed with $\Delta_{0}$ (it is denoted A_{0}), and the other, denoted A_{1}, is obtained from $\Delta_{1}$, and so on for components B and C.
L113: Figure cite53†2 shows a first example obtained with a mass ratio $q=0.3$ and for $w_{0}=-2.4$, by basically scanning the range $r\in[0,2]$ with regular spacing. In this interval, all values of $r$ do not lead to $p\leq-1$ and subsequently to positive roots (any complex or negative roots is rejected). Panel (a) shows that $p\leq-1$ only for $0.518\lesssim r\lesssim 1.723$, (again, see Sect.
L114: cite9†II.2 ), and there is a minimum^{3}^{3} 3 This minimum $r_{\rm min}$ of $p$ is easily found by differentiation of (cite43†4 ), which leads to $r_{\rm min}^{3}\left[(1+q)\sin^{2}\theta-q\right]=1$. at $r=1$. The physical roots $\Delta_{0}$ and $\Delta_{1}$ of the cubic equation are displayed in panel (b), and $\cos\phi$ is plotted in panel (c).
L115: Panel (d) shows the components A and B of the equipotential in the $xy$-plane, deduced from the standard spherical to Cartesian conversion formula combined with (cite44†5 ). Figure cite55†3 displays a set of six equipotentials computed in a similar fashion, again by a raw scan of the interval $r\in[0,2]$. We have selected values of $w_{0}$ corresponding to the four classical patterns, from tadpole shape to Hill’s quasi-spheres.
L116: ### II.4 Remarks
L117: This method yields exact solutions and works very well. Three slight issues must be quoted. First, the radial sampling plays a crucial role, especially when $-w$ becomes large. As expected, the equipotentials become more and more circular as the energy decreases (in the vicinity of each star, and far enough from the system), and $\cos\phi$ becomes less and less sensitive to $r$; see Fig. cite53†2 c. This can be improved by a densification of points at the edges of branches (see below).
L118: A second difficulty is caused by a double degeneracy: a single value of the parameter^{cite56†3 } $p\in[p(r_{\rm min}),-1]$ is obtained by two different values of $r$, and there are at most two roots at a given radius. It means that each branch is not revealed in a continuous fashion, but piecewise. This is visible in Fig.
L119: cite53†2 b: as $r$ decreases, the roots $\Delta_{0}$ and $\Delta_{1}$ feed both branches A_{0} and A_{1} of component A, then, there is a short range of radii for which both components A and B are constructed simultaneously (namely, $1.28\lesssim r\lesssim 1.36$), then branches B_{0} and B_{1} are revealed in parallel. This can be circumvented by making an inventory of the intersections of an equipotential with the binary axis, as explained in Sect. cite12†III .
L120: Third, unless a happy coincidence, there is no value of $r$ radius in the user-selected interval that exactly leads to $|\cos\phi|=1$ in (cite44†5 ). It means that the intersections of the equipotential with the binary axis (if needed) are not expected to be output from the cubic. This point can be fixed (see Sect. cite12†III ).
L121: ## III Computing branches individually. The equatorial plane as a fully solvable case
L122: ### III.1 Preliminaries
L123: We solve the cubic for a given mass ratio $q$ and level $w_{0}$ by decreasing or increasing the radius $r$ in a given interval, say $[r_{a},r_{b}]$. As quoted already, the equipotential is revealed piece-by-piece and its components and branches simultaneously, because $r(p)$ is not a bijection and there are two roots for the cubic. If one wishes to isolate a branch in the form of a continuous curve, then we must determine in advance which intervals in radius are concerned.
L124: In this purpose, the following quantities are required:
L125:   * i)
L126: 
L127: the position of the four Lagrange points ${\mathbf{r}}_{L_{l}}$, with $l\in[1,4]$, each one belonging to a specific equipotential, $w_{L_{l}}$, which is computed from (cite45†1 ).
L128: 
L129:   * ii)
L130: 
L131: the intersections of the equipotential with the main separatrix ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$, i.e. the two real roots of the second cubic, $r_{-}$ and $r_{+}\geq r_{-}$ of (cite49†7 ); see Sect. cite9†II.2 . We find $s^{\prime}=1$ from (cite52†8b ), and $w_{0}$ is given by (cite57†9 ).
L132: 
L133:   * iii)
L134: the intersections of the equipotential with the axis of the binary. We can establish that the equation that localizes these points is an ensemble of three general quartic equations (see Appendix cite18†A ). At most, there are six roots, denoted $x_{k}$ with $k\in\{1,6\}$, and sorted in increasing order $x_{1}\leq x_{2}<x_{3}\leq x_{4}<x_{5}\leq x_{6}$; see Fig. cite53†2 where $x_{1}$, $x_{2}$, $x_{5}$ and $x_{6}$ are reported.
L135: 
L136:   * iv)
L137: the value of the equipotential, $w_{\rm\Delta}$, that crosses the binary axis at $r=2$ and $\Delta=1$ (and tangent to the main separatrix ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$). Actually, for $w\leq w_{\rm\Delta}$, component A does not cross the separatrix anymore, and there is a single branch, namely A_{1}. That value is directly deduced from (cite43†4 ) with $p=-1$:
L138: 
L139:  | $$w_{\rm\Delta}=\frac{q^{2}+q-1}{2(1+q)}-2(1+q)\sin^{2}\theta,$$  |  | (10)
L140: which becomes $w_{\rm\Delta}=-\frac{3q^{2}+7q+5}{2(1+q)}$ for $\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}$.
L141: ### III.2 Procedure (decision tree)
L142: 
L143: From these preliminary quantities, the procedure to construct a given component or branch is as follows (there is no solution for $w_{0}>w_{L_{4}}$) :
L144: 
L145:   * •
L146: case I (tadpole shape): $w_{L_{3}}<w_{0}\leq w_{L_{4}}$. The equipotential has a single component A: branch A_{0} (outside the main separatrix ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$) is obtained with $\Delta_{0}$ for $r\in[r_{-},r_{+}]$ (see Sect. cite7†II ), while branch A_{1} is obtained with $\Delta_{1}$ in the same interval in radius. Note that $r_{-}=r_{+}=1$ at L_{4}.
L147: 
L148:   * •
L149: case II (horseshoe shape): $w_{L_{2}}<w_{0}\leq w_{L_{3}}$. Again, there is single component. Branch A_{1} is still revealed for $r\in[r_{-},r_{+}]$, but branch A_{0} has two parts: the interior part is obtained for $r\in[-x_{1},r_{+}]$ and the exterior part is found for $r\in[-x_{2},r_{-}]$.
L150: 
L151:   * •
L152: case III (interior peanut shape): $w_{L_{1}}<w_{0}\leq w_{L_{2}}$. The equipotential has two components, A and B. Component A has two branches, A_{0} and A_{1}, computed for $r\in[-x_{1},r_{+}]$ and $r\in[r_{+},x_{6}]$, respectively. Component B has also two branches, B_{0} and B_{1}, computed for $r\in[-x_{2},r_{-}]$ and $r\in[r_{-},x_{5}]$, respectively. Branch B_{0} corresponds to the interior part of branch A_{0} of case II.
L153: 
L154:   * •
L155: case IV (Hill’s quasi-spheres): $w_{\rm\Delta}<w_{0}\leq w_{L_{1}}$. The equipotential has now three components, A, B and C. Component A is determined as in case III. Components B surrounds the primary and is made of two branches, B_{0} and B_{1}, computed for $r\in[-x_{2},r_{-}]$ and $r\in[r_{-},x_{3}]$, respectively. Component C has single branch, C_{1}. It surrounds the secondary and is obtained for $r\in[x_{4},x_{5}]$.
L156: Note that the Roche lobes appear for $w_{0}=w_{L_{1}}$ when components B and C get in contact.
L157:   * •
L158: 
L159: case V (Hill’s quasi-spheres; continued): $w_{0}\leq w_{\rm\Delta}$. The three components are obtained as for case IV, except for component A, which is now fully outside the main separatrix. There is therefore a unique branch A_{0}, which is computed for $r\in[-x_{1},x_{6}]$.
L160: This five cases are easily implemented in any program. For each interval in radius here-above, the sampling in radius remains totally free (it can be uniform or not). We observe that the density of points obtained by a uniform spacing is generally low close to the edges of branches (i.e. vicinity of the main separatrix and vicinity of the binary axis). This can be corrected by an adaptive sampling, for instance from a scaled Chebyshev grid.
L161: Figure 4: Components B and C of the equipotential $w_{0}=w_{L_{1}}$ in three dimensions (the Roche lobes) computed from the two roots $\Delta_{0}$ (purple) and $\Delta_{1}$ (green) of the cubic (cite47†3 ) for a mass ratio $q=0.3$, the main separatrix ${\cal S}_{\Delta}$ (plain red lines) and the second separatrix ${\cal C}$ (dashed red line). The intersections at $r_{-}$ and $r_{+}$, obtained from the second cubic equation (cite49†7 ), are also plotted (red dots).
L162: ## IV Conclusion
L163: We have shown that the equipotentials $W({\mathbf{r}})={\rm const.}$ (or, level surface of the Jacobi constant) in the CRTPB can be determined exactly by solving a cubic equation. This is possible by selecting one star (the primary) as the center of coordinates, leaving a single radical in the expression of the potential in the corotating frame, namely the separation from the secondary.
L164: Along the axis of the binary system, the equipotentials can be exactly located from quartics (this is also the case of the $z$-axis). The function $W({\mathbf{r}})$ is therefore fully reversible at any point ${\mathbf{r}}$ of the equatorial plane of the binary (see Appendix cite19†B for the off-plane case). The formula for the three components of ${\mathbf{r}}(W_{0})$ are simple and easy to implement in codes.
L165: Numerical solvers (see the Introduction) can therefore be avoided to construct equipotential maps, which already represents a huge gain in terms of computing time. As usual in Physics, having a formula is always a considerable advantage over numerical approaches, in particular when it is exact. This opens a few interesting perspectives, to begin with the dynamics of the test particle in the CRTBP itself.
L166: The expression ${\mathbf{r}}(W_{0})$ can be used for perturbative calculus, in the value of $W_{0}$, in the position ${\mathbf{r}}$ or in the mass ratio $q$. It is also interesting in the context of binary evolution, as long as the point-mass approximation for the two massive bodies is justified. The geometry of peanut shapes, Roches lobes and Hill’s quasi-spheres rule mass transfers between the two components and subsequently impact the orbital elements [cite58†24 , cite59†25 , cite25†5 , cite60†26 , cite61†27 ].
L167: In particular, in semi-detached and contact binaries, Roche lobe overflow is a complex problem which requires an accurate description of the equipotential in the vicinity of the inner Lagrange point L_{1} where matter transits [cite62†28 , cite63†29 ]. The knowledge of a closed-form for ${\mathbf{r}}(W_{0})$ enables to get the exact shape of the Roche lobes (see Fig. cite64†4 as an example obtained with the formula with $q=0.3$) and around, and this should help in improving the realism of models.
L168: Actually, the potential is usually Taylor-expanded at point L_{1} [cite62†28 , cite65†30 ], which has limited validity, in contrast with the relationship ${\mathbf{r}}(W_{0})$. The geometry of equipotentials is also a key ingredient for simulating light-curves of close binaries [cite66†31 , cite67†32 , cite68†33 ], from semi-detached ot overcontact binaries.
L169: The results presented here are particularly well suited to such a task, as the local normal to any equipotential surface can be determined exactly by differential calculus from $\phi(r,\theta)$. This should considerably simplify the production of synthetic diagrams, that could serve as true references.
