L27: # irreducible representations of conjugacy quandles
L28: 
L29: Mohamad Maassarani
L30: ###### Abstract.
L31: For $G$ a finite group, we show that the irreducible quandle representations of the conjugacy quandle $Conj(G)$ are the products of what we call characters of $Conj(G)$ by irreducible linear group representations of $G$. The unitary quandle representations can be distinguished. We apply this to the study of the envelopping group of $Conj(G)$ showing that it injects into $G\times\mathbb{Z}^{c_{G}}$, where $c_{G}$ is the number of conjugacy classes of $G$. The injection is an isomorphism for $G$ perfect.
L32: ###### Key words and phrases:
L33: 
L34: Groups, Quandles, Enveloping groups, representations, symmetric $2$-cocycles
L35: ## Introduction and main results
L36: A quandle is a set equiped with binary operation satisfying some axioms. To a group $G$ one can associate a quandle $Conj(G)$ called the conjugacy quandle of $G$. The underlining set of $Conj(G)$ is $G$. A quandle representation of $Conj(G)$ is a quandle morphism $\rho:Conj(G)\to Conj(GL(V))$ ($\rho(hgh^{-1})=\rho(h)\rho(g)\rho(h)^{-1}$) for some vector space $V$.
L37: A quandle representation $Conj(G)\to Conj(GL(V))$ is irreducible if the image of the morphism stabilizes non subspace of $V$ other than $V$ and $0$. In [cite14†MMa26 ], we proved than an irreducible quandle representation over $\mathbb{C}$ of a finite quandle is finite dimensional.
L38: In [cite15†MMb26 ], we prove, under some assumptions on a finite group $G$, that the irreducible quandle representations of $Conj(G)$ over $\mathbb{C}$ are the products of what we call quandle characters of $Conj(G)$ and irreducible linear representations of the group $G$. Here we prove that this result is valid for any finite group. We apply these results to study the envelopping groups of conjugacy quandles.
L39: Showing essentially that the envelopping group of the conjugacy quandle of $G$ (finite) injects into $G\times\mathbb{Z}^{c_{G}}$, where $c_{G}$ is the number of conjugacy classes of $G$ and that the injection is an isomorphism for $G$ perfect.
L40: The notes are divided into $4$ sections. In the first section, we recall the basic definitions and properties of quandles, conjugacy quandles, quandle representations, envelopping groups and projective representations. In section $2$, we prove that a symmetric $2$-cocycle of a finite group with values in $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is a coboundary. This is used to prove the results in the other sections. In the third section, we provide proofs of the results concerning irreducible quandle representations.
L41: The third section is devoted to the study of envelopping groups of conjugacy quandles.
L42: ## 1. Reminders on quandles and projective representations of groups
L43: 
L44: ### 1.1. Quandles
L45: ###### Definition 1.1.
L46: 
L47: A quandle is a set $Q$ equipped with a binary operation $\triangleright$ such that :
L48: 
L49:   * -
L50: 
L51: $x\triangleright x=x$ for $x\in Q$.
L52: 
L53:   * -
L54: 
L55: For all $x,y\in Q$ there exist a unique $z\in Q$, such that $x\triangleright z=y$.
L56: 
L57:   * -
L58: 
L59: $x\triangleright(y\triangleright z)=(x\triangleright y)\triangleright(x\triangleright z)$, for $x,y,z\in Q$.
L60: One can construct a quandle from a group $G$ using the binary operation defined by $x\triangleright y=xyx^{-1}$ for $x,y\in G$. The defined quandle is called the conjugacy quandle and is usually denoted by $Conj(G)$.
L61: ###### Definition 1.2.
L62: 
L63: A quandle morphism is a map between two quandles $(Q_{1},\triangleright_{1})$ and $(Q_{2},\triangleright_{2})$ such that $f(z\triangleright_{1}w)=f(z)\triangleright_{2}f(w)$, for $z,w\in Q$.
L64: The composition of two quandle morphism is a quandle morphism. A group morhism $f:G\to H$ is quandle morphism if we regard both sets as the conjugacy quandles. For $Q$ a quandle the left translation $L_{x}$ by $x\in Q$ given by $L_{x}(y)=x\triangleright y$ for $y\in Q$ is a bijective quandle morphism
L65: ###### Definition 1.3.
L66: 
L67: The inner automorphism group $Inn(Q)$ of a quandle $Q$ is the subgroup of bijections of $Q$ generated by the left translations.
L68: 
L69: ###### Proposition 1.4.
L70: 
L71: The map $Q\to Inn(Q),x\mapsto L_{x}$ is a quandle morphism if $Inn(Q)$ is regarded as the conjugacy quandle of the group $Inn(Q)$.
L72: 
L73: For $G$ a group, the group $Inn(Conj(G))$ is equal to $Inn(G)$.
L74: ###### Definition 1.5.
L75: 
L76: [cite16†ME18 ]Let $Q$ be a quandle
L77: 
L78:   * 1)
L79: 
L80: A linear representation of $Q$ is the data of a vector space $V$ and a quandle morphism $\rho:Q\to Conj(\mathrm{GL}(V))$, i.e. :
L81: 
L82:  | $$\rho(x\triangleright y)=\rho(x)\rho(y)\rho(x)^{-1},$$  |
L83: 
L84: for all $x,y\in Q$.
L85: 
L86:   * 2)
L87: 
L88: A subrepresentation of $\rho$ is a vector subspace $W\subset V$, such that $\rho(x)(W)\subset W$ for all $x\in Q$.
L89: 
L90:   * 3)
L91: An irreducible representation is a representation $V$ that has no subrepresentations other than $0$ and $V$.
L92: 
L93: A linear group representation $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ is a quandle representation of $Conj(G)$.
L94: ###### Definition 1.6.
L95: 
L96: [cite15†MMb26 ] A character of a quandle $Q$ is a quandle morphism $\chi:Q\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, i.e. :
L97: 
L98:  | $$\chi(x\triangleright y)=\chi(y),$$  |
L99: 
L100: for $x,y\in Q$.
L101: 
L102: ###### Definition 1.7.
L103: 
L104: The enveloping group $G(Q)$ of a quandle $Q$ is the group :
L105: 
L106:  | $$G(Q)=\langle x\in Q|xyx^{-1}=x\triangleright y,\text{ for }x,y\in Q\rangle.$$  |
L107: 
L108: The enveloping group is also known as : the associated group, adjoint group or structure group. The group $G(Q)$ is infinite.

L110:

L115: ### 1.2. Projective representations
L116: 
L117: In this subsection, the groups are finite and representations are over $\mathbb{C}$.
L118: ###### Definition 1.9.
L119: 
L120: A projective representation of a group $G$ is a morphism $G\to PGL(V)$.
L121: 
L122: A projective representation $G\to PGL(V)$ is irreducible if $P(V)$ admit no proper projective subspace stable by $G$.
L123: 
L124: Let $\rho:G\to PGL(V)$ be a projective representation and $\tilde{\rho}$ be a lift of $\rho$ to $GL(V)$ such that $\tilde{\rho}(1)=Id$. For $g,h\in G$, we have:
L125: 
L126:  | $$\tilde{\rho}(gh)=\alpha(g,h)\tilde{\rho}(g)\tilde{\rho}(h),$$  |
L127: for some $\alpha(g,h)\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$. The mapping $\alpha:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times},(g,h)\mapsto\alpha(g,h)$ is a $2$-cocycle with values in $\mathbb{C^{\times}}$. If $\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}$ is another lift of $\rho$ to $GL(V)$ such that $\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}(1)=Id$, then the cocycles $\alpha$ and the one associated to $\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}$ will differ by a boundry. This shows that $\rho$ definies a cohomology class in $H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})$ .
L128: The group $H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})$ is called the Schur multiplier of $G$. The Schur multiplier of a finite group is finite.
L129: ###### Proposition 1.10.
L130: 
L131: A projcetive representation $\rho:G\to PGL(V)$ lifts to a linear representation $G\to GL(V)$ if and only if its class in $H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})$ is trivial.
L132: 
L133: A proof of the following can be found in [cite17†Ka87 ] (p. 40-42) :
L134: 
L135: ###### Proposition 1.11.
L136: 
L137: To each class $\alpha$ of the Schur multiplier $H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})$ there is at least one irreducible projective representation of $G$ associated to $\alpha$.
L138: ## 2. Symmetric $2$-cocyles with values in $\mathbb{C}^{*}$
L139: 
L140: In this section $G$ is a finite group. Recall that a $2$-cocyle of $G$ with values in $\mathbb{C}^{*}$ is a map $\alpha:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ satisfying the cocyle condition :
L141: 
L142:  | $$\alpha(gh,k)\alpha(g,h)=\alpha(g,hk)\alpha(h,k),$$  |
L143: 
L144: for $g,h,k\in G$. Such $2$-cocyle defines a cohomology class in $H^{2}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})$. A normalised $2$-cocycle $\alpha:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is a $2$-cocycle such that :
L145:  | $$\alpha(1,1)=1.$$  |
L146: 
L147: It follows from the cocycle condition that for a normalised cocycle :
L148: 
L149:  | $$\alpha(g,1)=\alpha(1,g)=1,$$  |
L150: 
L151: for $g\in G$.
L152: ###### Definition 2.1.
L153: 
L154: A symmetric $2$-coycle $\alpha:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is a $2$-cocyle such that :
L155: 
L156:  | $$\alpha(g,h)=\alpha(h,g),$$  |
L157: 
L158: for $h,g\in G$.
L159: 
L160: Let $\beta:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ be a normalised symmetric 2-cocycle : We define $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ as the set $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G$ equipped with the product :
L161: 
L162:  | $$(x,g)(y,h)=(xy\beta(g,h),gh).$$  |
L163: 
L164: We denote by $\pi$ the natural projection $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G\to G$.
L165: ###### Proposition 2.2.
L166: 
L167: $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ is a group with unit $(1,1)$.
L168: 
L169: $\pi$ is a group morphism.
L170: 
L171: We have a central extension $1\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G\overset{\pi}{\to}G\to 1$.
L172: ###### Proof.
L173: 
L174: We prove $1)$. The cocycle condition insures that the product is associative. The fact that $(1,1)$ is a unit follows from the fact that $\beta(g,1)=\beta(1,g)=1$ since $\beta$ is normalised. The inverse of $(x,g)$ is $(x^{-1}\alpha(g,g)^{-1},g^{-1})$. $2)$ can be readly checked. $3)$ follows from the fact that $\beta(g,1)=\beta(1,g)=1$. ∎
L175: 
L176: ###### Lemma 2.3.
L177: 
L178: The intersection of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1$ with the derived group of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ is tirvial.
L179: ###### Proof.
L180: 
L181: To prove the lemma we will prove that the commutator of any two elements of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ lies in $1\times G$. We will use the notation $[-,-]$ for the commutator. Since any element of the form $(a,1)$ lies in the center and $(a,1)(1,g)=(a,g)$, we have that :
L182: 
L183:  | $$[(a,g),(b,h)]=[(1,g),(1,h)].$$  |
L184: 
L185: We will compute the left hand side :
L186:  | $$\begin{split}[(1,g),(1,h)]&=(1,g)(1,h)(1,g)^{-1}(1,h)^{-1}\\
L187: &=(\beta(g,h),gh)(\beta(g,g^{-1})^{-1},g^{-1})(\beta(h,h^{-1}),h^{-1})\\
L188: &=(\beta(g,h),gh)(\beta(g,g^{-1})^{-1}\beta(h,h^{-1})\beta(g^{-1},h^{-1}),g^{-1}h^{-1})\\
L189: &=(\beta(g,h)\beta(g,g^{-1})^{-1}\beta(h,h^{-1})^{-1}\beta(g^{-1},h^{-1})\beta(gh,g^{-1}h^{-1}),[g,h]).\end{split}$$  |  | (1)
L190: 
L191: We will show that the coordinate along $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ in the last line :
L192:  | $$A=\beta(g,h)\beta(g,g^{-1})^{-1}\beta(h,h^{-1})^{-1}\beta(g^{-1},h^{-1})\beta(gh,g^{-1}h^{-1}),$$  |
L193: 
L194: is equal to $1$. For that we will use the cocycle condition :
L195: 
L196:  | $$\beta(xy,z)\beta(x,y)=\beta(x,yz)\beta(y,z),$$  |
L197: 
L198: and the symmetry of the cocycle. Using $x=g,y=h,z=(gh)^{-1}$:
L199: 
L200:  | $$\beta(gh,(gh)^{-1})\beta(g,h)=\beta(g,h(gh)^{-1})\beta(h,(gh)^{-1})$$  |
L201:  | $$\beta(gh,g^{-1}h^{-1})=\beta(g,g^{-1})\beta(h,h^{-1}g^{-1})\beta(g,h)^{-1}$$  |
L202: 
L203: Substituting this in $A$, gives :
L204:  | $$A=\beta(h,h^{-1})^{-1}\beta(g^{-1},h^{-1})\beta(h,h^{-1}g^{-1}),$$  |
L205: 
L206: By symmetry, $\beta(h,h^{-1}g^{-1})=\beta(h^{-1}g^{-1},h)$. Using $x=h^{-1},y=g^{-1},z=h$:
L207: 
L208:  | $$\beta(h^{-1}g^{-1},h)\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1})=\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1}h)\beta(g^{-1},h)$$  |
L209:  | $$\begin{split}\beta(h,h^{-1}g^{-1})&=\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1}h)\beta(g^{-1},h)\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1})^{-1}\\
L210: &=\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1}h)\beta(g^{-1},h)\beta(g^{-1},h^{-1})^{-1}.\end{split}$$  |  | (2)
L211: 
L212: Substituting into the last expression of $A$, we get :
L213:  | $$A=\beta(h,h^{-1})^{-1}\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1}h)\beta(g^{-1},h),$$  |
L214: 
L215: Using $x=g^{-1},y=h,z=h^{-1}$ in the identity:
L216: 
L217:  | $$\beta(g^{-1}h,h^{-1})\beta(g^{-1},h)=\beta(g^{-1},1)\beta(h,h^{-1})$$  |
L218: 
L219: Since $\beta(g^{-1},1)=1$ and $\beta(g^{-1}h,h^{-1})=\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1}h)$ by symmetry:
L220: 
L221:  | $$\beta(h^{-1},g^{-1}h)\beta(g^{-1},h)\beta(h,h^{-1})^{-1}=1$$  |
L222: 
L223: This proves that $A=1$ and therefore :
L224: 
L225:  | $$[(a,g),(b,h)]=[(1,g),[1,h)]=(1,[g,h]).$$  |
L226: This proves that the intersection of the derived group of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ with $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1$ is trivial. ∎
L227: ###### Proposition 2.4.
L228: 
L229: The central extension $1\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G\overset{\pi}{\to}G\to 1$ splits, i.e. we have a group morphism $\varphi:G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ such that $\pi\circ\varphi=id$.
L230: ###### Proof.
L231: 
L232: Denote by $f:\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G\to(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G)^{ab}$ the abelianisation morphism. By the previous lemma $f$ restricted to $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1$ is injective. Since $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is divisible, the abelianisation of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ splits as :
L233: 
L234:  | $$(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G)^{ab}=f(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1)\oplus A,$$  |
L235: for some abelian group $A$. Denote by $H$ the subgroup of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ equal to $f^{-1}(A)$. One can check that $(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1)\cap H=1$ and $(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1)H=\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$. This implies that the restriction of $\pi$ to $H$ is a group isomorphism. The proposition follows. ∎
L236: ###### Theorem 2.5.
L237: 
L238: A symmetric $2$-cocycle $\alpha:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is a coboundary.
L239: ###### Proof.
L240: 
L241: Let $\alpha$ be such a cocycle and define $\lambda:G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ by :
L242: 
L243:  | $$\lambda(1)=\alpha(1,1)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda(g)=1,$$  |
L244: 
L245: for $g\in G$ with $g\neq 1$. The cocycle $\beta:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ given by :
L246: 
L247:  | $$\beta(g,h)=\lambda(g)\lambda(h)\lambda(gh)^{-1}\alpha(g,h),$$  |
L248: is a normalised symmetric cocycle cohomologus to $\alpha$. To prove the theorem we only need to prove that $\beta$ is a coboundary. By the previous proposition the extension $1\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times 1\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G\overset{\pi}{\to}G\to 1$ splits. Let $\varphi:G\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times_{\beta}G$ be a homomorphism such that $\pi\circ\varphi=id$. For $g,h\in G$ we have that :
L249: 
L250:  | $$\varphi(g)=(a_{g},g),$$  |
L251: for some $a_{g}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$. Using this notations, we have :
L252: 
L253:  | $$\varphi(gh)=(a_{gh},g)=(a_{g},g)(a_{h},h)=(a_{g}a_{h}\beta(g,h),gh).$$  |
L254: 
L255: This proves that :
L256: 
L257:  | $$\beta(g,h)=a_{g}^{-1}a_{h}^{-1}a_{gh}.$$  |
L258: 
L259: . Hence, $\beta$ is a coboundary. We have proved the theorem. ∎
