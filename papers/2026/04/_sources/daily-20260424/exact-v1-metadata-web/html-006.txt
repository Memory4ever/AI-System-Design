L51: # A simple classification of AI incident trajectories
L52: Isaak Mengesha Affiliation: Arcadia Impact AI Governance Taskforce Affiliation: Computational Science Lab, University of Amsterdam, Noord Holland, Netherlands Correspondence to: isaak.mengesha@arcadiaimpact.org    Branwen Owen Affiliation: Arcadia Impact AI Governance Taskforce    Charlie Collins Affiliation: Arcadia Impact AI Governance Taskforce    Tina Wong Affiliation: Arcadia Impact AI Governance Taskforce    Simon Mylius Affiliation: MIT FutureTech, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA 02139, USA    Peter Slattery Affiliation: MIT FutureTech, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA 02139, USA    Sean McGregor Affiliation: Responsible AI Collaborative, Los Angeles, CA, USA
L53: ###### Abstract
L54: Public AI incident counts conflate reporting propensity, deployment growth, and actual harm rates, muddying policy conclusions. Emerging legislative frameworks adopt narrow reporting requirements unlikely to generate the feedback loops that have driven safety improvements in other high-reliability industries. Meanwhile, news-sourced public databases remain the primary record of AI harm and require methodology to interpret.
L55: We propose a framework that separates exposure from harm-rate trends and classifies results into trajectory categories for governance decisions. Borrowing from epidemiology, it pairs structured monitoring questions with estimation procedures that scale to the evidence available. Applied to worked case studies, the framework yields interpretable classifications, clarifies what can and cannot be claimed from available data, and identifies the binding constraint on more robust classifications going forward.
L56: ###### Keywords:
L57: 
L58: AI governance, incident monitoring, incident classification
L59: ## 1 Introduction
L60: Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are prone to unforeseen failures, security breaches, adverse events and malicious use (cite39†Jeanmaire and Boger, 2025 ). Public databases now catalogue thousands of incidents (cite40†OECD, 2024a ; cite41†McGregor, 2021 ), providing a public record of harms involving AI systems (cite42†OECD, 2024b ). Translating these databases into actionable insight, however, requires bridging the gap between what is recorded and what is actually happening (cite43†Paeth and McGregor, 2025 ).
L61: Populated primarily from news reports, public incident databases systematically over-represent acute, dramatic, and Anglophone harms while under-capturing the chronic, diffuse and systemic harms (cite44†Richards et al., 2025 ; cite45†Nixon, 2011 ; cite46†Teo, 2025 ) (see Appendix A).
L62: More fundamentally, simple “raw” incident counts conflate at least three distinct factors: the propensity for incidents to be observed and reported; the scale of system deployment and use (“exposure”); and the frequency of harm per unit exposure (”hazard”) (cite47†Paeth et al., 2025 ; cite44†Richards et al., 2025 ). They also conceal the nature and severity of harm in each incident.
L63:  | Problem  | Framework response
L64: P1  | Complexity and lack of standardisation confounds analytical consistency  | Structured monitoring question (Sort; §cite9†2 )
L65: P2  | More “harmful from AI systems” indistinguishable from “more harmful” AI systems  | Separate exposure and harm estimates (§cite10†2.1 )
L66: P3  | Sparse or missing data treated as absence of harm  | Evidence tiers; proxy measures; confidence statements; principled abstention (§cite13†2.2 )
L67: P4  | Absolute values sensitive to reporting bias and uncertainty  | Trend estimates robust to stable biases and uncertainties (§cite20†4.3 )
L68: P5  | Point estimates imply undue precision  | Order-of-magnitude estimates resolve to simple directional classification (§cite13†2.2 )

L70: Disaggregating these factors and accounting for, or acknowledging, the inherent biases and limitations, can help decision makers understand and distinguish genuine risk escalation from increased media attention (cite43†Paeth and McGregor, 2025 ). The practical utility extends across sectors: in structured interviews with AI risk and safety practitioners we found a consistent demand for clarity on which harms are increasing in frequency or severity, and which are relevant to specific operational contexts.
L71: Table 1 summarises five problems with raw incident counts and the corresponding responses proposed in this paper.
L72: Figure 1: The interpretive pipeline for AI incidents. At top: rising counts of monthly AI incidents and hazards (defined here as “potential dangers’) reported in the OECD AI Incidents Monitor conflate trends in media attention, deployment growth and the frequency of harm per use of AI systems (cite42†OECD, 2024b ).
L73: Below: our framework takes recorded incidents as inputs to structured monitoring questions, then estimates harm and exposure trends separately to classify each monitoring question into a trajectory category.
L74: Incident reporting has driven safety improvements in other high-reliability, safety-critical industries (aviation, nuclear power) and population-scale technologies (pharmacovigilance, healthcare). AI stands to benefit similarly (cite48†Perrow, 2011 ) but lacks the necessary institutional infrastructure and interpretive framework (cite49†Wei and Heim, 2025 ).
L75: Existing mandatory incident reporting frameworks for AI remain narrow in scope and fragmented across jurisdictions. The EU AI Act (Articles 73 and 55) requires providers and deployers of high-risk AI systems, and providers (but not deployers) of systemic-risk general purpose AI models, to report serious incidents to national authorities and the EU AI Office respectively.
L76: However, reports flow into different parts of the EU system with no mandate to consolidate or publicly share them, and the definition of “serious incident” excludes many harms that any common-sense reading would consider serious. California’s SB-53 and New York’s RAISE Act (from 2027) are narrower still, applying only to a handful of frontier AI developers, and capturing only catastrophic-scale harms, security breaches, and loss-of-control events, with no public disclosure requirement.
L77: No other jurisdiction currently mandates AI-specific incident reporting or encourages voluntary reporting, leaving the vast majority of global AI deployments outside any reporting framework.
L78: Despite the OECD developing standardised incident definitions (cite40†OECD, 2024a ; cite49†Wei and Heim, 2025 ), there is currently no agreement on how AI incident data should be analysed or compared across time, and no validated methodologies for post-deployment AI monitoring (cite50†Rao et al., 2026 ; cite51†Whittlestone and Clark, 2021 ). This presents an opportunity to establish shared approaches before national regimes fragment and solidify.
L79: Despite their limitations, news-sourced public databases are likely to remain the primary records of AI harm for the foreseeable future. Methods to extract reliable signal from them are urgently needed (cite43†Paeth and McGregor, 2025 )—both for immediate governance application and to lay the foundations for, and encourage the adoption of, AI incident monitoring more broadly.
L80: In aviation safety reporting, every incident is investigated, causal factors are identified, and the findings are fed back into the system to inform design choices and modifications (cite41†McGregor, 2021 ). The conditions for such analysis (a known fleet size, mandatory and incentivised voluntary reporting, and identifiable operators) do not exist for AI.
L81: We often do not know how many people use a given AI system, how many automated decisions are made, or at what scale generative output is produced (cite52†Stein et al., 2024 ; cite53†Tanjaya and Pratt, 2025 ). Epidemiological surveillance was built for this kind of uncertainty. Its operational methods can accommodate incomplete reporting, unknown denominators, and media-driven distortions of apparent incidence (cite54†McCarty et al., 1993 ).
L82: Public health principles such as standardised case definitions, the separation of exposure from hazard, and tiered approaches to estimation that accept coarse data (cite55†Dolin et al., 2025 ) can be applied meaningfully to AI incident monitoring.
L83: Applying a public health lens, we propose a methodology for monitoring AI harms from incident data with the goal of informing governance prioritisation. Section 2 introduces the framework: a structured approach to defining monitoring questions, separate estimation procedures for harm and exposure trends, and a four-way trajectory classification. Section 3 applies the framework to two monitoring questions on autonomous vehicles and AI chatbot safety.
L84: Section 4 discusses the classification’s explanatory value, its limitations, and implications for reporting standards.
L85: ## 2 Framework
L86: The problems in Table cite56†1 arise when question definition, estimation, and interpretation are not treated as distinct steps. Our procedure takes each in turn: first defining a precise monitoring question (MQ); secondly estimating trends in exposure and harm separately, then calibrating confidence to available evidence (§cite10†2.1 ); and lastly classifying the resulting pair of trends into one of four trajectory categories (§cite13†2.2 ).
L87: Each step is robust across incident types and evidence constraints, and transparent about uncertainty.
L88: Figure 2: The SORT framework. Structured questions provide a solution to the problem of ill defined incident types more broadly. It decomposes a monitoring question into four explicit components.
L89: ##### Why ask a monitoring question?
L90: 
L91: A precisely defined MQ helps ensure comparability, reproducibility, and internal consistency in the estimation and classification steps.
L92: ##### Structuring the monitoring question.
L93: We developed a structured framework that splits AI harm monitoring questions into four components: Subject (who or what is at risk), Opportunity (those actually exposed to the harm mechanism), Risk event (the countable harm), and Timeframe (the observation period), producing questions of the form “Among [S] that [O], how many [R] per [T]?” (See figure cite57†2 for a worked example.) SORT is analogous to the PICO framework (Patient/Problem, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome) in evidence-based medicine (cite58†Richardson et al., 1995 ), and is similarly flexible: S and O can refer to people, systems, content, conversations or deployments, allowing the same underlying harm to generate multiple valid monitoring questions depending on who needs the answer, while forcing analytical choices to be explicit rather than buried in unstated assumptions.
L94: T can be set to accommodate different harm dynamics and data availability. An interactive SORT tool is available to guide users in developing a precise MQ ^{1}^{1} 1 Available as a cite59†Claude artifact†claude.ai (see GitHub)..
L95: ### 2.1 Estimation procedures
L96: 
L97: Answering a monitoring question requires estimating two variables: the total harm associated with [R], and the exposure defined by [S] and [O]. We propose a four-tier approach (Table cite60†2 ), shared by both harm and exposure estimation, ordered by decreasing evidential strength.
L98: Table 2: Tiered estimation approach, ordered by decreasing evidential strength.
L99: Tier  | Method  | Sensitive to  | Confidence
L100: 1  | Copy from reporting  | Authoritative Source  | High
L101: 2  | Derive from proxies  | Proxy construction  | Medium
L102: 3  | Elicit from experts  | Panel selection  | Low
L103: 4  | Abstain  | —  | —
L104: At Tier 1, the estimate can be read directly from an existing reporting system, e.g. NHTSA crash filings, pharmacovigilance registries, or platform transparency reports. At Tier 2, no single source provides the data, but bounds can be constructed from available data. The two quantities differ in how these bounds are obtained. For harm, recorded incidents provide an automatic lower bound, and proxy measures (e.g. the harm from a broader class that contains [S]) typically supply the upper bound.
L105: Exposure has no analogous floor: both bounds must be constructed from proxies, which makes higher Tier outcomes more common. At Tier 3, no quantitative data supports even a decomposition, and domain experts are asked to bound the plausible range. Tier 4 is principled abstention, which should itself be considered a finding and the honest acknowledgment that current evidence cannot support even an order-of-magnitude estimate.
L106: If the plausible range spans more than two orders of magnitude, the quantity cannot be reasonably estimated and is assigned to Tier 4.
L107: Comparing upper and lower bounds across consecutive time periods yields four possible outcomes: both increase, both decrease, they diverge, or some lack sufficient data. When directions agree, the trend claim matches the direction (increasing or decreasing). When directions diverge, expert elicitation is needed. No trend claim can be made where the data are insufficient or where the required expert assessment cannot be found or fails to converge.
L108: Figure 3: Estimation procedure. When incident reports and proxy measures agree in direction, a trend claim follows directly. When they diverge, the confidence interval (CI) either grows (increasing uncertainty) or shrinks (converging bounds but ambiguous direction), both requiring expert elicitation for a trend determination. When data is insufficient for either source, the procedure abstains.
L109: ##### Estimating harm.
L110: In the absence of high quality datasets, incident reporting becomes the common default in estimating lower bounds for total harm associated with [R] in Tier 2. Much of the database search task can be automated. We wrote a LLM-powered script that takes as inputs an MQ and a database of incident reports and filters for matching and partially matching incidents (see Appendix cite33†D.1 ). A partial match is an incident that matches some but not all SORT components.
L111: For example, an MQ specifying S as “teenagers” and R as “LLM-assisted completed suicides” would yield few full matches but many partial matches: adult cases that share the harm mechanism but fall short on S, and cases of AI-assisted suicide attempts or planning that fall short on R. A high ratio of partial to full matches suggests that the MQ might be overspecified and can be adjusted accordingly.
L112: Where relevant data exist, proxy measures can help bound the harm range. One approach is to use the known harm from a broader class that contains [S]. In Figure cite57†2 , for instance, harm from all motor vehicles places an upper bound on harm from autonomous vehicles, and pre-AV baseline statistics can tighten that bound further. The analyst aims for the lowest upper bound that the evidence can defensibly support.
L113: ##### Estimating Exposure
L114: We define exposure as the opportunity for a harm to occur. It is needed to distinguish a technology that is becoming more dangerous from one that is simply becoming more widely used. For most AI harm types, deployment or use data does not exist: we rarely know how many people interacted with a particular system, how many decisions were automated, or how many conversations took place.
L115: Tier 1 sources are therefore uncommon, and exposure estimation typically relies on Tier 2 constructions that combine multiple partial sources.
L116: For example, census age data can be combined with survey-based scam exposure rates to estimate the population vulnerable to AI voice-clone fraud, or published daily active user figures with survey data on reasons for use to estimate the number of AI chatbot users seeking emotional support. For an exposure estimate, we provide a best-guess point estimate with a plausible range, and an order-of-magnitude estimate.
L117: The plausible range represents the upper and lower limits outside of which the value is unlikely to fall.
L118: ### 2.2 Classification
L119: 
L120: To classify a MQ, we first derive the harm-per-exposure trend $\hat{H}$ by comparing the rates of change of the harm $H$ and exposure $E$ trends from our estimation procedures. If $H$ grows faster than $E$, $\hat{H}$ is rising, and vice versa. At order-of-magnitude levels of accuracy, this requires only the determination of which trend is dominant, not precise division. MQs for which $E$ or $H$ cannot be determined are unclassifiable.
L121: The classification framework is a 2$\times$2 grid taking raw exposure trend $E$ and harm-per-exposure trend $\hat{H}$ as inputs (see Figure cite61†4 ), and mapping their combinations to four categories with distinct governance implications as follows:
L122: 
L123: Escalating. Both the population at risk and the harm per unit exposure are growing. This demands urgent response: expanded monitoring, active investigation into causal drivers, and possibly regulatory intervention.
L124: Mitigating. More people are exposed, but harm per unit exposure is decreasing, suggesting existing safeguards are working. Continued monitoring is warranted: a failure of current controls could shift the trajectory to escalating.
L125: 
L126: Concentrating. Fewer people are exposed, but those who are face worse outcomes. This calls for targeted protective measures and investigation into why harm is intensifying.
L127: Receding. Neither dimension is worsening. Additional intervention may not be required. Where specific measures preceded this trajectory, maintaining or extending them to related domains may be worthwhile.
L128: Figure 4: Trajectory classification. Each monitoring question is placed in one of four categories based on the directional trends of harm-rate and exposure. Monitoring questions for which either trend could not be determined during estimation (§cite10†2.1 ) are labelled unclassifiable and do not enter the grid. The fill intensity encodes governance urgency.
L129: The framework prioritises transparency over precision. Directional trends are the most defensible claim the estimation procedure can support under realistic data constraints. A 2$\times$2 grid is the simplest non-trivial classification that captures them, highlighting, rather than masking, our epistemic limitations.
L130: ## 3 Application and Results
L131: ### 3.1 Case Study: Conversational AI systems and self-harm.
L132: 
L133: Monitoring question: [S] Among people living in the United States [O] who use conversational AI systems for emotional support, [R] how many receive responses that encourage, or fail to discourage, suicidal ideation or self-harm [T] per calendar year?
L134: Harm estimation, H: Considering the two most recent, full, consecutive calendar years, LLM filtering of the AIID found two full matches in 2024 and 17 in 2025 (cite41†McGregor, 2021 ). Having two matches in 2024 falls below the threshold for a reliable signal; we therefore supplement with an alternative AI incident database.
L135: LLM analysis of the OECD AIM dataset after filtering for US-based incidents involving conversational AI systems resulting in physical or psychological injury yields 8 full matches in 2024, with a harm count range between 9 and 17. 2025 results reflect 55 full matches, with a harm count ranging from 100k to 1,000k — an explosive increase.
L136: As a proxy measure, an OpenAI publication from October 2025 has stated that “around 0.15% of users active in a given week have conversations that include explicit indicators of potential suicidal planning or intent”, amounting to over 1.2 million people.
L137: Trend: Increasing [$H\;\uparrow$ ]. OECD results increase over consecutive time periods.
L138: Confidence: Low–Medium. The limited matches in the AIID dataset and the lack of proxy measures likely reflect limited awareness and detection methods in 2024. At the same time, the OECD data support the trend of increasing frequency and severity. In response, companies have begun strengthening safeguards, including OpenAI’s efforts to improve model behaviour in sensitive conversations in late 2025.
L139: Given these shifts in measurement and mitigation, either expert elicitation or close monitoring of 2026 data is necessary before drawing high-confidence conclusions about trend, severity, and the effectiveness of these new interventions.
L140: Exposure estimation, $E$: In the absence of direct survey data on emotional support use, we used Pew Research data on ChatGPT use “to learn new things” and “for entertainment” by age group (2024–2025) as a suitable proxy (cite62†Sidoti and McClain, 2025 ). We extracted both data points and applied them to the US population in the corresponding age groups.
L141: For the point estimate, we used the mid-point between the two data points; for the lower bound we used the lower estimate (“for entertainment”), and for the upper bound we used “to learn new things”. Additionally, for the point estimate we assumed that ChatGPT holds 80% of the market share of LLM personal use (cite63†FATJOE, 2025 ). For the upper and lower bounds, we assumed a market share of 90% and 70%, respectively.
L142: In 2024, we estimate that 64 million people in the US used conversational AI for emotional support (54–73 million, plausible range). In 2025, we estimate 88 million (75–99 million, plausible range). Order-of-magnitude estimate: $10^{8}$.
L143: Trend: Increasing [$E\uparrow$]. Our estimates suggest an approximately 40% increase from 2024 to 2025.
L144: 
L145: Confidence: Medium. Tier 2 (derived from reasonable sources).
L146: ### 3.2 Case Study: Autonomous vehicles and injury/damage.
L147: 
L148: Monitoring question: [S] Among autonomous vehicles (SAE Levels 3 through 5) [O] on US public roads, [R] how many incidents involving injury or property damage occur per million vehicle-miles [T] per calendar year?
L149: Harm estimation, H: To start, LLM filtering of the AIID found 4 full matches in 2024 (two Minor, one Moderate, one Catastrophic), with a harm count lower bound of 15 and upper bound of 305. 2025 AIID data result in 4 full matches (one Minor, three Moderate), with a harm count lower and upper bound of 37.
L150: For a more comprehensive dataset, we also leverage the NHTSA, which requires manufacturers and operators to report certain collisions involving vehicles equipped with automated driving, or SAE Level 2 advanced driver assistance, systems. The number of Automated Driving System (ADS) incidents increased from 526 in 2024 to 975 in 2025, $\sim$85.4% increase.
L151: Trend: Increasing in number of incidents [$H\;\uparrow$ ]. AIID counts remain flat and the observed rise in NHTSA incidents is primarily driven by property-damage-only cases rather than injury-related events.
L152: 
L153: Confidence: High. Mandatory reporting requirements ensure that the NHTSA provides a comprehensive dataset for analysis.
L154: Exposure estimation, $E$: From June 2024 to May 2025, the Autonomous Vehicle Industry Association (AVIA) estimated that AVs drove 145 million miles on US public roads, compared to 75 million miles in 2023–2024, suggesting a doubling in exposure in one year (cite64†Autonomous Vehicle Industry Association, 2025 ).
L155: Other sources suggest this trend continued rapidly throughout 2025, with Waymo delivering approximately 250,000 paid rides per week in April 2025 and 450,000 by December 2025, an 80% increase over 8 months (cite65†CNBC, 2025 ). For our point estimate, we used the AVIA estimate and assumed the monthly growth rate suggested by Waymo’s 2025 reporting. For the lower bound we used the AVIA’s May 2024 and May 2025 estimates for the whole of 2024 and 2025, respectively.
L156: For the upper bound, we increased the point estimate by 10%, similar to the difference between the lower bound and the point estimate. In 2024, we estimate that AVs drove 78 million miles in the US (75–86 million miles, plausible range). In 2025, we estimate 156 million miles (145–171 million miles, plausible range). Order-of-magnitude estimate: $10^{8}$.
L157: Trend: Increasing [$E\uparrow$]. Exposure is estimated to have approximately doubled between 2024 and 2025.
L158: 
L159: Confidence: Medium. Tier 2 (derived from reasonable sources).
L160: ## 4 Discussion
L161: 
L162: Our examples demonstrate the nature of incident reporting and the clarifying power of the classification process, with implications for the AI governance ecosystem.
L163: ### 4.1 What classification reveals
L164: 
L165: Trends in raw incident counts can be misleading, and the exposure trend is needed to provide context. For example, despite their comparable harm trends, Example 1 (conversational AI systems and self-harm) and Example 2 (autonomous vehicles and injury/damage) are classified escalating and mitigating, due to their differing exposure trends (P2).
L166: Classification reveals the data constraints on both harm and exposure, including which is more limiting. The tiered estimation approach makes explicit the evidential strength of each estimate. Unclassifiable is a valid result (P3), revealing data gaps for a given S and O (P3).
L167: In Example 1: a combination of precise but sparse AIID database filtering and richer, lower precision (category adjacent) OECD database filtering permits a (Tier 1–2; Direct data/Category-based) lower bound harm estimate, whereas two, “category adjacent” indirect (survey) proxies provide the basis for a (Tier 2; ) exposure estimate (in the absence of system-provider disclosures of conversational AI use).
L168: In Example 2: NHTSA data are sufficiently relevant and precise for a (Tier 1; Direct data) harm estimate, whereas three partially relevant and overlapping “within category” sources combine for a (Tier 2) exposure estimate.
L169: Classification based on estimated trends, rather than absolute values, is more robust to some sources of uncertainty, including systematic reporting biases in incident databases (P4). Provided reporting propensity is approximately stable over time, the relationship between absolute harm and its lower and upper bounds likely also remains stable across estimation periods, such that uncertainty in order-of-magnitude absolute levels cancels out in the trend, even for wide confidence intervals.
L170: The effect weakens as incident counts drop and small-sample noise grows, and provides no benefit for non-stationary biases (e.g. viral media incidents) or harms that are invisible to and systematically excluded from the database.)
L171: Where confidence in both trends is sufficient, the relative movement of harm and exposure can also inform the choice of intervention: exposure-dominant trajectories point toward deployment, access, or eligibility measures, while harm-per-exposure-dominant trajectories point toward the harm mechanism itself.
L172: 
L173: In Example 2, exposure is rising faster than harm-per-exposure is falling, suggesting the absolute harm will continue to rise if current trends continue, despite a mitigating classification.
L174: The coarseness of the 2$\times$2 classification balances interpretive value with data requirements, asking only which trend is dominant (P5).
L175: 
L176: Practitioner interviews confirmed that clarity on what can and cannot be claimed is a gap our framework can fill.
L177: ### 4.2 Toward routine trajectory classification
L178: Exploring variations of a given MQ indicates that different specifications can affect the classification. Where partial matches substantially outnumber full matches, the MQ may be overspecified. Broadening its specification (by relaxing S or O) can increase the number of full matches, increasing statistical confidence at the cost of some precision.
L179: (P1) Alternatively (as in Example 1) adjacent datasets can, where available, serve as proxy measures to strengthen confidence in a trend estimate based on sparse data without changing the MQ. (P1, P3)
L180: The modular structure (SORT, estimation, classification) encourages reproducibility, transparency and partial recycling of estimation efforts. The interactive SORT tool and methodological guidance lower barriers to use. A public repository of answered MQs (with sources and assumptions) could provide a common analytical resource. Sharing intermediate outputs (attempted S/O specifications; resulting partial-match ratios) would support convergence on the most informative formulations.
L181: Growing proxy-data libraries would reduce the marginal cost of each new MQ. At scale, a well-structured repository could reveal a taxonomy of incident types that reflects analysts’ framings and priorities.

L183: Applied repeatedly over time, the classification process supports institutional learning: transitions between trajectory categories are arguably more policy-relevant than individual classifications. Escalating to mitigating may suggest successful intervention, whereas receding to concentrating signals deepening harm within a subpopulation.
L184: Current regulatory regimes are unlikely to produce sufficient incident data for immediate application, but demonstrating governance value may encourage more comprehensive reporting. With broader adoption and shorter reporting timescales, governance insight could progress from broad trajectory identification to detection of phase transitions and rapidly scaling incident types, enabling earlier intervention and more efficient resource allocation.
L185: ### 4.3 Limitations
L186: The framework’s view of harm is necessarily constrained. MQs can specify harm in detail, including the nature and severity, but counting incidents collapses this specification to a binary (match or no match), concealing the specifics of each case. (Future work might recover more of this detail through advanced harm characterisation and database filtering.) Different MQs capture qualitatively different harms that are not easily comparable.
L187: The framework supports within-MQ trend reasoning more robustly than cross-MQ prioritisation, which should be approached with particular care. Principled abstention catches thin data but not absent categories. Detecting harms that are absent from incident data requires separate discovery methods.
L188: Classification assumes roughly monotonic trends across the timeframe $T$: a spike-and-reversal within a single period is invisible and in practice $T$ is typically constrained by the temporal resolution of available proxy data rather than the incident data.
L189: The assumption of stable reporting propensity often does not hold in practice due to shifting news cycles and viral media events (which deeper analysis using Google Trends data on fluctuations in public attention can help identify), and other factors, including new reporting regulations or database expansions. These limits on comparability across periods should be acknowledged as a general limitation and noted as a caveat where observed.
L190: The classification process identifies directional trends, but cannot locate absolute levels of harm (“compressed” or otherwise) within the confidence interval. Trends can indicate whether existing interventions are working, but deciding what level of harm warrants action is a matter of judgement.
L191: Classifications do not translate directly into priorities: a mitigating classification can still imply growing absolute harm if exposure rises faster than harm-per-exposure falls; a receding classification may still represent a large absolute burden; a concentrating classification at high levels may warrant a more urgent response than an escalating one at low levels. At worst, this presents a risk of misuse.
L192: Receding or unclassifiable labels could be invoked to justify inaction, when the first may reflect successful intervention worth sustaining and the second a data gap worth closing.
L193: Incident databases are retrospective. AI harms are, by definition, AI risks that have materialised at least once. They represent a small and lagging subset of the full, expanding landscape of AI risks. The most consequential of these may have yet to materialise, and incident data can do little to help anticipate or directly prevent them.
L194: Limited extrapolation beyond observed harms may be possible, for example, by considering how harm might change if the underlying variables (system properties, deployment scale, user population) shifted, but this remains a structured way of reasoning about known harm mechanisms, not a means of surfacing unknown ones.
L195: More broadly, the framework is designed to replace naive interpretations of incident data—the conflation of rising reports with rising harm, or the treatment of absent data as evidence of absent harm—rather than reinforce them. Its value derives from, and depends on, its assumptions and limitations being made explicit.
L196: ## Impact Statement
L197: The framework replaces the current path from raw counts through naive trend claims to reactive governance with decomposed trends, trajectory classification, and prioritisation grounded in exposure-adjusted harm.
L198: Current reporting regimes are narrowly scoped and unlikely to produce sufficient data on their own; the framework is designed to work with the news-sourced databases that remain the primary record of AI harm, and to demonstrate the governance value that could motivate broader reporting infrastructure over time.
L199: It makes visible whether rising counts reflect genuine risk escalation due to more harmful or more widely deployed AI systems, or simply increased media interest; where reporting infrastructure is insufficient to support trend claims; and where unclassifiable MQs in expected-harm domains indicate survivorship bias in the reporting funnel.
