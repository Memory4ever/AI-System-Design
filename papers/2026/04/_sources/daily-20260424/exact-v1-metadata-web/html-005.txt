L58: ###### Abstract
L59: 
L60: Context. Most Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) are Radio Quiet, with radio emission that may arise from star-formation activity, AGN-driven winds, weak jets, and coronal activity. Disentangling these mechanisms is challenging and requires detailed multi-wavelength investigation, but it is crucial for quantifying AGN feedback in galaxy evolution.
L61: Aims. We present a detailed radio investigation of 21 X-ray selected AGN in the Supermassive Black Hole Winds in X-Rays (SUBWAYS) sample ( erg/s, z=0.1-0.5), selected to systematically search for Ultra-Fast Outflows (UFOs). UFOs are detected in of the targets, with measured velocities and kinetic luminosities, making the sample particularly well-suited for investigating the role and signatures of multi-scale outflows at different frequencies.
L62: Methods. We build the radio Spectral Energy Distribution of the sources complementing our proprietary data, collected with the JVLA at 1.5 and 6 GHz, with images from LoTSS (145 MHz) and other publicly available radio surveys between 150 and 1400 MHz.
L63: We investigate the role and occurrence of the aforementioned mechanisms, with particular interest in outflows and their possible relation with UFOs. To achieve this, we combined information on spectral indices, luminosities, and morphologies of the radio emission with properties derived in other wavebands, such as the Star Formation Rate, the X-ray luminosity, the Eddington ratio or the UFO kinetic luminosity.
L64: Results. All the sources are detected and are mostly consistent with RQ AGN. For of the sources the data suggest the presence of an outflow (wind or weak jet). Interestingly, our results indicate that AGN with UFOs tend to have larger radio extension and a steep radio spectrum consistent with outflows. Moreover, the radio emission of the 6 UFO hosts is consistent with predictions from wind-driven shock models, possibly indicating a direct connection between the two phases.
L65: Alternatively, this may simply reflect environmental conditions that favour the production of both phenomena.

L69: ##  1 Introduction
L70: Accretion onto supermassive black holes (SMBH) can light up the nuclear regions of galaxies, giving rise to the so-called Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN), which are among the most energetic objects in the Universe (see Kormendy and Ho cite30†2013 and Alexander et al. cite31†2025 for reviews). AGN have been shown to inject significant amounts of energy and momentum into their surroundings from parsecs to Mpc scales. This occurs via multiple channels, including direct radiation (e.g. Ciotti et al.
L71: cite32†2010 ), jets of relativistic plasma (e.g. Fabian cite33†2012 ), and massive multi-phase gas winds (e.g. Crenshaw et al. cite34†2003 ). In particular, through the interaction with the ISM, powerful AGN-driven outflows, in the form of winds or jets, have proven to be capable of reproducing the scaling relations between SMBH mass and galaxy properties (Cattaneo et al. cite35†1999 ; Kauffmann and Haehnelt cite36†2000 ; King and Pounds cite37†2015 ; King et al. cite38†2005 ; Gaspari et al.
L72: cite39†2020 ; King and Pounds cite37†2015 ; Fabian cite33†2012 ). While synchrotron emission from powerful relativistic jets dominates the radio luminosity only in a minority of AGN, classified as Radio Loud (RL, ), the majority falls into the Radio Quiet (RQ, ) category, where the origin of radio emission is still debated.
L73: In RQ AGN, radio emission can arise from a plethora of processes, possibly overlapping, including AGN-driven winds, weak jets, coronal activity and star-formation activity (see Padovani cite40†2017 ; Panessa et al. cite41†2019 for extensive reviews). Disentangling these mechanisms and understanding how the energy they release couples to the interstellar medium (ISM) is challenging, and requires detailed spectral and morphological analysis.
L74: However, obtaining observational constraints is crucial for modelling AGN feedback and galaxy evolution.
L75: AGN-driven winds are indeed detected in any gas phase, at different ionisation states, velocities and distances from the inner SMBH. These range from the sub-relativistic (), highly ionised, ultra-fast outflows (UFOs), arising at few gravitational radii (e.g. Chartas et al. cite42†2002 ; Tombesi et al. cite43†2010 ; Pounds cite44†2014 ; Tombesi et al. cite45†2015 ; Matzeu et al. cite46†2023 ; Gianolli et al.
L76: cite47†2024 ), down to the slower ( km/s) outflows detected on kpc-scales, in the atomic and molecular gas phases (e.g. Zakamska and Greene cite48†2014 ; Morganti et al. cite49†2016 , and see Cicone et al. cite50†2018 for a review). In luminous RQ AGN it has been proposed that the shocks generated by radiatively driven winds can accelerate electrons, producing synchrotron radio emission on scales larger than 100 pc with Lν 10-5 LAGN (Zakamska and Greene cite48†2014 ; Nims et al. cite51†2015 ).
L77: However, how the different wind phases are connected to one another is still poorly understood.
L78: Recent observations showed that also low-luminosity (log W/Hz) galaxy-scale radio jets, much more common than their high-power counterparts (Mauch and Sadler cite52†2007 , Sabater et al. cite53†2019 ; Venturi et al. cite54†2021 ; Speranza et al. cite55†2022 ; Murthy et al. cite56†2022 ; Cresci et al. cite57†2023 ; Ulivi et al. cite58†2024 ), remain trapped in the ISM for a longer time, affecting the surrounding medium over a large volume (e.g. Bicknell cite59†1994 ; Jarvis et al. cite60†2019 ; Mukherjee cite61†2025 ).
L79: Such low-power jets can still account for the radio emission observed in RQ AGN. Anyway AGN winds and jets can co-exist (e.g. Tombesi et al. cite62†2012 ; Mestici et al. cite63†2024 ), making it complicated to understand their potential causal connection. In addition, recent works discuss the possibility that ultra-fast outflows can accelerate ultra-high-energy cosmic rays.
L80: If such associations are confirmed, they would provide further motivation to test whether X-ray UFOs correlate with extended non-thermal radio emission (Karwin et al. cite64†2023 ; Ehlert et al. cite65†2025 ).

L83: Current literature providing a detailed investigation of the radio emission in UFOs hosts has been largely limited to single-object analyses or archival studies (e. g. Falcke et al. cite69†1998 ; Tombesi et al. cite43†2010 , Tombesi et al. cite62†2012 ; Tombesi et al. cite70†2014 ; Morganti cite71†2017 ; Giroletti et al. cite72†2017 ; Wang et al. cite73†2021 ; Longinotti et al. cite74†2023 ; Maksym et al. cite75†2023 ; Zanchettin et al. cite76†2023 ; Yamada et al. cite77†2024b ).
L84: In this paper we present the analysis of new Jansky Very Large Array (JVLA) observations, obtained at 1.5 and 6 GHz of 22 quasars belonging to the Supermassive Black Holes Winds in X-rays (SUBWAYS) sample.
L85: By combining JVLA data with radio observations from additional surveys, together with the wealth of multi-wavelength data available for the SUBWAYS sample, we investigate the origin of the radio emission (star formation, coronae, or outflows) and characterize the contribution of AGN-driven outflows^{1}^{1}1 Throughout the whole work when we do not distinguish between winds and jets we refer to them more generally as outflows., with particular emphasis on UFO hosts.
L86: This work constitutes the first systematic analysis exploiting dedicated follow-up observations for a sample of RQ AGN with detected UFOs.
L87: This paper is organised as follows: in Sect. cite7†2 and cite8†3 we describe the sample and how the data have been collected and reduced, in Sect. cite11†4 and cite17†5 we discuss the results and consider the potential origins of the radio emission investigating how the radio properties of the sample relate to the ones derived in other wavebands. In Sect. cite23†6 we draw our conclusions.

L115: ##  2 The Sample
L116: SUBWAYS was born with the aim of increasing the number of known UFO hosts with multi-wavelength observations. The whole project lies its grounds on a large XMM-Newton campaign ( Ms of observing time in 2019-2020; Brusa et al. cite78†2022 ) of a sample of 17 (plus five collected from the XMM archive with comparable spectral quality) X-ray selected AGN (mostly Type 1 QSO, with log and log), chosen at redshift z=0.1-0.5 and with bolometric luminosity erg/s).
L117: In Table cite79†1 we list the 22 sources belonging to SUBWAYS with their basic properties. Sources with a UFO detection at confidence interval are placed above the horizontal line. The unprecedented quality reached by XMM-Newton spectra allowed a detailed investigation of under-explored ranges of redshift and luminosity in UFOs studies. From the main analysis of the XMM-Newton observations, Matzeu et al.
L118: (cite46†2023 , Paper I) reported, in 7 out of the 22 AGN (), significant detection of absorption lines corresponding to high column density, highly ionised iron with outflow velocities in the range , consistent with the expectations for UFOs. Complementary UV imaging data have been obtained with Hubble Space Telescope (HST, PI: G. Kriss), but no counterpart for the UFO was found (Mehdipour et al. cite80†2023 , Paper II).
L119: These results could be attributed either to an excessively high ionisation state or to a low covering fraction of the UV-emitting source. Moreover, correlations of SUBWAYS UFOs parameters with X-ray luminosity are found to be much stronger than those with the UV luminosity (Gianolli et al. cite47†2024 , Paper III), suggesting the innermost regions of the AGN are more significantly involved than the accretion disk in powering sub-relativistic winds.
L120: In fact, though for four objects with UFO detection thermal pressure could in theory launch the outflow, it cannot explain the outflow velocity of UFOs, suggesting that a combination of magnetically and radiatively driven processes is needed (see Fig. 11b of Paper II).

L123: ##  3 Data
L124: ###  3.1 JVLA data reduction
L125: We performed dedicated observations of the sample with the JVLA interferometer between August 2020, May 2023 (PI: F. Panessa), and January 2026 (PI: E. Amenta) in B configuration (, ). This set-up has, to the purposes of this work, the right trade-off in terms of maximum and minimum baseline length, providing indeed both good resolution, and good sensitivity, necessary for a meaningful morphological analysis.
L126: The data have been collected using the L- and C-band receivers (1.5 and 6 GHz respectively), with 30 minutes and 5 minutes long observations of each target, respectively. Considering overhead for each observing block, which includes telescope initial setup, phase and amplitude calibration and slewing between sources, the total observing time is equal to 18.5 observing hours in L band and 2.88 in C band. The predicted resolution is at 1.5 GHz and at 6 GHz, with being kpc at the redshift of our sources.
L127: For all the observing blocks, we downloaded the datasets calibrated by the observatory pipeline^{3}^{3}3VLA CASA Calibration Pipeline 5.6.2, then checked the goodness of the automatic calibration and, if necessary, improved the target flagging with AOFlagger^{4}^{4}4cite81†https://aoflagger.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ (Offringa cite82†2010 ).
L128: The imaging has been done with the Common Astronomy Software Applications package (The CASA Team et al. cite83†2022 CASA 6.6.3.22 version^{5}^{5}5cite81†https://casadocs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/notebooks/introduction.html ) task tclean, weighting the visibilities with the Briggs scheme (Briggs cite84†1995 ), with weighting parameter as listed in Table cite85†6 . We base the flux-density scale on measurements of VLA primary amplitude calibrators by Perley and Butler (cite86†2013 ).
L129: Whenever the noise displayed a non uniform pattern, before attempting the self-calibration, we tried imaging the data with w-stacking clean (WSClean)^{6}^{6}6 WSC version 3.1 cite81†https://wsclean.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ (Offringa et al. cite87†2014 ) and this often improved the results. The images produced with this software are labelled with ∗ in Table cite85†6 .
L130: This was not sufficient for two of the targets, PG1425+267 and HB891529+050, for which we performed one round of phase self-calibration within CASA.

L141: To investigate the morphology of the sources at different frequencies and their broad-band radio spectral shape, we combined the aforementioned JVLA 1.5 and 6 GHz simultaneous data with LoTSS (LOw Frequency ARray Two-metre Sky Survey, Shimwell et al. cite88†2017 ; Shimwell et al. cite89†2022 ) data. LoTSS is a survey of the whole northern sky at 145 MHz with resolution of , comparable to that of the proprietary data, and sensitivity mJy beam-1.
L142: LoTSS mosaics are available for 18 out of 22 targets (the remaining four are too south to be observed with LOFAR), in particular 11 of the mosaics are currently released within the LoTSS collaboration in DR3 ( Shimwell et al. cite90†2026 ).
