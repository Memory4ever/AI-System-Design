L37: # First measurement of wind line formation regions in an early O-type star
L38: 
L39: The eclipsing binary system AzV 75 in the Small Magellanic Cloud

L43: Submitted to A&A
L44: ###### Abstract
L45: 
L46: Context. Massive stars with their strong ionizing radiation and strong stellar winds are the key feedback agents of the universe. Stellar winds of massive stars are often measured by fitting resonance lines in the UV using non-LTE stellar atmosphere models. So far, the line formation regions of these lines have not been measured empirically, preventing a comparison to the model’s structures.
L47: Aims. We aim to conduct the first measurement of the resonance line formation regions in an early-type eclipsing binary in the Small Magellanic Cloud, namely AzV 75.
L48: Methods. We employ photometric data from TESS and ASAS-SN in combination with radial velocity measurements from multi-epoch Hubble Space Telescope UV spectra to derive the ephemeris. We examine the intensity changes in the C IV and N V resonance lines in the UV and combine them with a light-curve analysis to estimate the region in the wind where these lines are formed.
L49: Results. We derive that AzV 75 has an orbital period $P_{\mathrm{orb}}=$165.66\pm\pm 0.09\text{\,}\mathrm{d}$$, eccentricity $e=0.42\pm 0.02$, mass ratio $q=0.72\pm 0.03$, and inclination $i=85.77^{\circ}$. With this orbital configuration, no secondary eclipse is expected.
L50: We report that the optically thick UV resonance lines exhibit flattening and shortening of the absorption trough, and weakening of their emission features, as they approach the phase of the expected secondary eclipse, while the continuum UV flux appears to remain unaffected. We illustrate that this behaviour can be well explained by the primary’s optically thick wind eclipsing the secondary star. The C IV and N V resonance line formation regions in the primary star extend up to $316\,R_{\odot}$.
L51: Conclusions. We have measured for the line formation regions of resonance lines in a stellar wind of an early O-type star. These are important benchmarks for 1D as well as 3D non-LTE stellar atmosphere models. Even though we have numerous multi-epoch UV spectra at hand, the eclipse is not fully sampled, leaving some freedom in the derived sizes of the line formation regions.
L52: A first comparison to 1D-stellar atmosphere models indicates that a classical $\beta$-law with an exponent of $\beta=0.5$ instead of $\beta=0.8$ might be favoured for the primary star’s velocity field.

L56: ## 1 Introduction
L57: Massive stars ($M_{\mathrm{ini}}\gtrsim 8M_{\odot}$) emit copious amounts of ionizing radiation and power strong UV-radiation driven stellar winds, making them the principal drivers of enrichment and evolution of the interstellar medium and of galaxies more generally (cite25†Barkana, 2006 ; cite26†Hopkins et al., 2014 , e.g.,). Despite their importance, stellar winds of hot massive stars are not well understood.
L58: Often, theoretical predictions and empirical measurements of the mass-loss via stellar winds diverge (e.g., cite27†Pauli et al., 2025 , and references therein). Often, the mass-loss rate is measured by fitting UV resonance lines with non-LTE stellar atmosphere models.
L59: While the characteristic P Cygni profiles themselves provide some information on the properties of the wind, such as the velocity or density structure (cite28†Kudritzki & Puls, 2000 ), direct measurements of the line formation regions of these resonance lines in the outer stellar atmosphere are still missing.
L60: Frequently, massive stars are seen to have at least one close stellar companion. Earlier studies have shown that in the Milky Way at least, the great majority of massive stars exist in binary or higher order multiple systems (cite29†Sana et al., 2012 ; cite30†Kobulnicky et al., 2014 ; cite31†Marchant & Bodensteiner, 2024 , among others).
L61: The predominance of binarity among massive stars has been shown to extend to lower metallicities in nearby dwarf galaxies, in particular, the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) (cite32†Bodensteiner et al., 2025 ; cite33†Bestenlehner et al., 2025a ; cite34†Sana et al., 2025 , e.g.,).
L62: Eclipsing binaries are a very powerful tool in stellar astrophysics, as they provide accurate measurements of the mass of the two stars as well as an independent measurement of the stars’ temperatures and radii. In a long-period binary, which is wide enough such that the stars have not interacted yet, and that contains a hot star with a (partially) optically thick wind and a UV bright companion with a weak/absent wind, the wind of the strong-winded star should eclipse the companion.
L63: Measuring this eclipse duration will allow to measure the size of the wind itself. However, the chance of having an eclipse decreases with increasing period, drastically reducing the available sample of target stars for this purpose. Furthermore, one needs a star with a strong wind dominating in the UV but a companion bright enough to see changes in the UV resonance lines when the companion is occulted. This is more likely to be fulfilled at lower metallicity, where stellar winds are weaker.
L64: Lastly, one needs multi-epoch observations sampled roughly evenly over the orbit to see at which phases the resonance lines block the flux from the companion.
L65: The SMC massive star AzV 75, previously described as spectral type O5 III(f+) (cite35†Walborn et al., 2000 ) but recently revised as O3.5 III(f) by cite36†Bestenlehner et al. (2025b) , is an evolved massive star which forms part of the extensive database of low metallicity massive stars observed by the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and assembled under the HST legacy programme: The Ultraviolet Legacy Library of Young Stars as Essential Standards (ULLYSES) (cite37†Roman-Duval et al., 2025 ).
L66: The massive stars portion of that programme has provided an extensive resource of medium-high resolution UV spectra, principally obtained using the STIS and COS instruments aboard HST.
L67: Along with other spectral data, the libraries of data produced are essential materials for the determination of many physical parameters and characteristics of massive stars at low and very low metallicities (cite38†Vink et al., 2023 ; cite39†Sana et al., 2024 , see, in particular, the XShootU programme and spectral database described in).
L68: Just recently, AzV 75 was indicated to be an eclipsing binary system by a statement of cite40†Pedersen & Bildsten (2025) , based upon evidence in NASA Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (cite41†Ricker et al., 2015 , TESS;) data.
L69: Detailed evidence of binarity, as shown by radial velocity (RV) shifts of two stellar components and variation in the wind lines in individual UV spectra, some of which form the basis of the ULLYSES High Level Science Product (HLSP) UV spectrum of AzV 75, was first presented by cite42†Parsons et al. (2026) . Furthermore, multiple UV spectra of AzV 75 have been obtained over a period of several years as part of HST instrument calibration exercises under a number of different programmes, which are ongoing.
L70: The most recent spectrum, up to the present date, was obtained on 2025 October 24 (2460968 JD). All of this makes AzV 75 an ideal candidate to measure for the first time the size of the line formation regions of the optically thick resonance lines in the UV.
L71: ## 2 Analysis of the binary orbit
L72: 
L73: ### 2.1 Method
L74: #### 2.1.1 Light curve
L75: cite40†Pedersen & Bildsten (2025) mentioned that AzV 75 exhibits an eclipse in the TESS data, but no period was determined. We reexamined the TESS data of AzV 75, which was observed during sectors 1, 27, 28, 67, 68, 94, and 95. Sector 28 shows an apparent single instance of a primary eclipse being recorded at 2459072.3 Heliocentric Julian Date (HJD), which also reappears in sector 95 at 2460897.6 HJD. The duration of the eclipse is approximately 1.3 days.
L76: TESS has a low spatial resolution of $21\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolArcsecond}$ and in sector 28 the pixel in which AzV 75 appears contains at the edge one other source of comparable brightness (see Fig. cite43†11 ). Therefore, we refrain from using the photometric data from sector 28 for a later light-curve analysis. In sector 95, AzV 75 is the only bright source in its corresponding pixel (see Fig. cite43†11 ), allowing the extracted light curve to be used during the light curve analysis.
L77: To obtain an orbital period of the system, we extracted All-Sky Automated Survey for Supernovae (ASAS-SN) g-Band photometric aperture data (cite44†Shappee et al., 2014 ; cite45†Kochanek et al., 2017 ; cite46†Hart et al., 2023 ). The ASAS-SN survey has a cadency of 2 to 3 days and has photometric coverage between 2458850.61 and 2460848.68 HJD.
L78: From a visual inspection, we identified 4 primary eclipses occurring at 2459072.4$\pm 1$ HJD, 2459238.5$\pm 1$ HJD, 2459570.1$\pm 1$ HJD, and 2459902.3$\pm 1$ HJD, including the eclipse seen in sector 28 of TESS. The identified primary eclipses in the TESS and ASAS-SN data indicate a period of $165.925\pm 0.115$ days. The high-quality TESS data contains sectors at about half phase. No eclipse, neither from the primary nor the secondary can be detected here.
L79: From the TESS and ASAS-SN observations, we obtain a reference eclipse timing of $T_{0}=2459072.41\pm 0.37$ HJD. The phased primary eclipse is shown in Fig. cite47†1 .
L80: Figure 1: Phased TESS and ASAS-SN photometric data close to zero phase. Note that during TESS sector 28 the pixel in which AzV 75 appears contains another bright star, leading to an apparent weaker eclipse.
L81: #### 2.1.2 RVs
L82: The HST spectra of AzV 75 show a clear line splitting in the O IV $\lambda\lambda 1338.61,1342.99,1343.51$ photospheric lines (see figure 5 in cite42†Parsons et al. 2026 and, here, Fig. cite48†12 ). While most of the HST UV spectra were already wavelength-calibrated, for a small number of spectra, calibration was required.
L83: Hence, to obtain the RVs from the HST, we first cross-correlated all spectra using the C II 1335 Å interstellar line to derive a correction factor, aligning the spectra in the same reference frame. As a mask spectrum, we used the HST STIS spectrum taken at 2451322.267 HJD. The applied corrections are listed in Table cite49†3 . To measure the RV shifts of each star, we employed a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach.
L84: Within this method, we shift two individual line functions of the primary and secondary – obtained by fitting random synthetic stellar spectra to the O IV multiplet at maximum separation – by a given set of RVs. The combined spectrum is then compared to the observed one, and the fit’s quality is evaluated using a least-squares likelihood function. The MCMC can quickly explore the full parameter space and converges after a few hundred steps to the true solution.
L85: We sample around the true solution for 1000 RV combinations, which provides us with the posterior distribution. The quoted error margins correspond to the 68% confidence interval of the posterior. A complete list of the derived RVs for each spectrum is provided in Table cite49†3 , and the fits of the individual spectra can be inspected in Fig. cite48†12 .
L86: AzV 75 was observed three times in the optical with X-Shooter. Unfortunately, all of these observations have been taken close to an eclipse, when the RVs are small. Therefore, we are unable to see in these spectra any line splitting or deformation, and thus, to derive reliable RVs.
L87: ### 2.2 Modelling the light and RV curve
L88: To model the light and RV curve, we employ the Physics of Eclipsing Binaries (PHOEBE) code (cite50†Prša et al., 2016 ; cite51†Conroy et al., 2020 ), Version 2.4. Within the photometric data, we do not see a secondary eclipse occurring. This means that the light curve cannot be used to obtain the eccentricity and the argument of the periastron passage, and that these parameters can only be obtained from the fit of the RV curve. Therefore, we split the fitting procedure into two steps.
L89: At first, the RV curve is fitted using the built-in MCMC sampler option of the PHOEBE code. For the fitting procedure, the orbital period $P_{\mathrm{orb}}$ and time of the primary eclipse $T_{0}$ are fixed at the values derived from the TESS and ASAS-SN light curves (see Sect. cite9†2.1.1 ). The free parameters are the projected semi-major axis $a\sin i$, eccentricity $e$, argument of the periastron $\omega$, the systemic velocity $\varv_{\gamma}$, and the mass ratio $q=M_{2}/M_{1}$.
L90: A flat prior in the mass ratio of $q=0.4\,\textit{--}\,1.5$ and in the systemic velocity of $\varv_{\gamma}=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}200\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$$, allowing for variation around the systemic velocity of the SMC, are employed to help with convergence. The fit of the RV curve is shown in Fig. cite52†2 , and the derived orbital parameters are presented in Table cite53†2 . The posterior distributions are displayed in Fig. cite54†13 .

L113: From the fitting of stellar eclipses, one can, in principle, obtain information about the stellar temperatures as well as the stellar radii. However, the ASAS-SN and the TESS photometric data are taken in almost the same pass-band and only show primary eclipses, meaning that we cannot obtain reliable temperatures of both stars.
L114: With AzV 75 containing one of the brightest stars in the SMC and being unrecognized as a binary system, it has been subject to multiple studies obtaining the stellar parameters as if it were a single star (cite57†Bouret et al., 2021 ; cite33†Bestenlehner et al., 2025a ). However, the binary companion, which contributes about 25% to the UV flux (see Sect.
L115: cite12†3 ), is expected to also have a noticeable signature in the optical, questioning the reliability of their temperature estimate for the primary star of this system. Unfortunately, all the available optical spectra were taken by chance close to an eclipse, preventing us from seeing line deformations and thus from performing a detailed stellar atmosphere fitting, providing accurate stellar temperatures.
L116: To get an estimate of the stellar temperature of the stars, we use the information from the RV fit in combination with the spectral energy distribution (SED) of the system to find two approximately representative stellar atmosphere models. To achieve this, the publicly available Potsdam Wolf-Rayet stellar (PoWR) OB-Vd3 stellar atmosphere model grids of Pauli et al. (submitted) are employed. These grids are based on the MIST (cite58†Choi et al., 2016 ) single star evolution models and use the cite59†Vink et al.
L117: (2001) mass-loss rates divided by a factor of 3. From these models, we can roughly translate the orbital masses of the stars, the total luminosity of the system, and the contribution of the two stars to the UV flux to their surface temperatures.
L118: The closest PoWR models at SMC metallicity that reproduce the aforementioned parameters are the “36-34” model of the SMC-OB-Vd3 grid for the primary and “46-42” of the SMC-OB-Vd3 grid for the secondary. The primary’s model has a temperature of $T_{\ast,\,1}=$36\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{K}$$, a surface gravity of $\log(g_{1}/(\mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}))=3.4$, a luminosity of $\log(L_{1}/L_{\odot})=6.02$ and a mass-loss rate of $\log(\dot{M_{1}}/({\,M_{\odot}\,\mbox{yr}^{-1}}))=-6.30$.
L119: The model temperature of the secondary is $T_{\ast,\,2}=$46\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{K}$$, with surface gravity of $\log(g_{2}/(\mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}))=4.2$, luminosity $\log(L_{2}/L_{\odot})=5.43$, and mass-loss rate $\log(\dot{M_{2}}/({\,M_{\odot}\,\mbox{yr}^{-1}}))=-7.2$. In Fig. cite60†14 , the individual and combined synthetic model spectra are plotted against the system’s spectral energy distribution and a UV spectrum, illustrating that the chosen models are representative for the stars in AzV 75.
L120: For the fit of the light curve, we also employed the MCMC sampler of PHOEBE. Again, the orbital period $P_{\mathrm{orb}}$ and time of the primary eclipse $T_{0}$ are fixed to the values derived from the TESS and ASAS-SN light curves. Unfortunately, PHOEBE does not contain synthetic spectra for hot massive stars yet, so we had to approximate the spectra of the stars as blackbodies.
L121: To describe the effect of limb darkening, we extracted the emergent flux distribution from the PoWR models in the g-band and fitted them using a quadratic limb-darkening law (cite61†Diaz-Cordoves & Gimenez, 1992 ), yielding coefficients $a_{1}=0.00583$, $b_{1}=-0.00332$, and $a_{2}=0.17$, $b_{2}=-0.09$ for the primary and secondary, respectively. For the fitting procedure, we employed the ASAS-SN g-band light curve, as well as the TESS light curve extracted from sector 95.
L122: Since the TESS photometry is not flux calibrated, we normalized the light curves and used the “dataset-coupled” option of PHOEBE. The free fitting parameters are the inclination $i$ of the system and the stellar radii of the primary $R_{1}$ and the secondary $R_{2}$. The resulting fit of the light curve is shown in the top panel of Fig. cite52†2 and the resulting parameters are listed in Table cite53†2 . The posterior distributions of the MCMC sampler are displayed in Fig. cite62†16 .
L123: ## 3 Wind variability
L124: ### 3.1 Stellar wind line intensity variations
L125: Figure 3: Detail of the wind-formed N V (upper panel) and C IV (lower panel) resonance line P Cygni-type profiles taken from the available HST UV spectra. All spectra are shifted into the rest frame of the primary and are corrected for the systemic velocity. The N V is corrected for the interstellar Ly$\alpha$ blend by modelling a (fully damped) profile of column density of $6.9\text{\times}{10}^{21}\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$.
L126: Note that the colour coding matches the phase-based colouring of spectra in the upper panels of Fig. cite52†2 .
L127: In addition to the RV shifts, the HST spectra show varying stellar wind-driven emission features in the P Cygni profiles of the N V and C IV resonance line profiles (cite42†Parsons et al., 2026 ). This occurs consistently across both species. In Fig. cite56†3 all available HST UV spectra containing the resonance lines are displayed. One can see that the spectra show three characteristic states: i) maximum emission, ii) below continuum emission, and iii) an intermediate stage with reduced emission.
L128: For the C IV resonance line one can additionally see that at the different states, the change in emission occurs simultaneously with, respectively, i) an in-filled absorption trough, ii) a fully saturated absorption trough, and iii) a weaker, but still infilled absorption trough. The N V resonance line (corrected for interstellar Ly$\alpha$ absorption) exhibits a similar behaviour in its emission component.
L129: However, its absorption trough, which is not fully saturated and thus indicative of a somewhat lower optical depth compared to C IV, shows less pronounced variability. This reduced variability may be attributed to the reduced received flux caused by the blend with the interstellar Ly$\alpha$ line, leading to a lower signal-to-noise ratio in the N V absorption trough. As already noted by cite63†Patel et al.
L130: (2024) , in contrast to the wind lines, the UV continuum of all spectra remains unchanged (noting that there is no UV spectrum obtained at a time coincident with a primary eclipse).
L131: Both, the N V and the C IV resonance line have a terminal wind velocity around $v_{\infty}\approx$1960\pm\pm 20\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$$. This value is slightly smaller than earlier measurements (cite57†Bouret et al., 2021 ; cite64†Hawcroft et al., 2024 ; cite42†Parsons et al., 2026 , e.g.,), because those did not account for the previously unknown orbital motion of the primary star.
L132: The fact that both resonance lines exhibit absorption troughs developed to the same velocity is already a first indication that the lines are formed in a similar region of the primary’s wind. To further investigate on this, we compute temporal variance spectra (cite65†Fullerton et al., 1996 , TVS;) that quantify the variance across all spectra in contrast to the mean spectrum, allowing us to search for additional variability in stellar wind lines.
L133: The resulting TVS for the Ly$\alpha$-corrected N V and C IV resonance lines are depicted in Fig. cite66†4 . In the emission component, the variability pattern of the two lines are nearly identical. In the absorption through the variability of the N V is reduced, which we attribute to the weaker signal caused by the blend with the interstellar Ly$\alpha$ line.
L134: Nevertheless, the variability in this region remains still very similar to the one from the C IV, reinforcing the conclusion that both resonance lines are formed in comparable regions of the primary’s wind.
L135: Figure 4: TVS of the N V (red) and C IV (blue) resonance lines in velocity space of the N V $\lambda 1238.80$ ($\lambda 1242.80$, lower ticks) or C IV $\lambda 1548.20$ ($\lambda 1550.70$, lower ticks) lines, respectively. In both cases, the variance rapidly decreases toward redwards velocities, while in the blueward absorption trough one can see some variance across the spectra, typical for a O-type star.
L136: The overall similarity in both the shape of the variance and the velocity range over which it is observed strongly suggests that the two resonance lines are formed in the same regions of the primary’s wind.
L137: In other massive binaries in the SMC containing one strong-winded star with a weak-winded but still hot companion (such as AzV 476 (cite67†Pauli et al., 2022 ) or 2dFS 163 and 2553 (cite68†Ramachandran et al., 2024 )), one can often see that the secondary adds net UV flux to the C IV line, leading to an infilling of the absorption trough, similar to what is seen in the black spectra of Fig. cite56†3 .
L138: However, a simultaneous reduction of both the emission peak and absorption trough for such a binary does not appear to have been previously reported. Intuitively, one might expect a reduction to occur during the secondary eclipse, but this scenario should also produce a similar decrease in the continuum flux, an effect that is not seen in the HST spectra.
L139: The top panels of Fig. cite52†2 indicate which of the three characteristic N V and C IV profile states is observed at each orbital phase. A clear correlation emerges: the reduction in the intensity of the C IV resonance line occurs only around orbital phases where the secondary eclipse should take place, but this reduction persists over a significantly longer fraction of the orbital cycle than a “normal” eclipse. There is also a lead-in and run-out of this feature at intermediate orbital phases.
L140: Given the strong phase dependence, we interpret the observed behaviour of the UV resonance lines as evidence for a wind eclipse. At quadrature, the UV flux of both stars is received, such that the secondary’s UV continuum fills in the primary’s otherwise fully saturated C IV P Cygni profile, and also enhances the emission feature, as seen in the black spectra shown in Fig. cite56†3 .
L141: Still, even when the secondary is projected in front of the primary’s wind, one would not expect a significant reduction in the flux due to the large difference in the size of the secondary and the primary’s wind ($R_{2}\ll R_{\mathrm{1,\,wind}}$). However, while most strong-winded O stars are optically thin in the continuum, they remain optically thick in their resonance lines.
L142: Consequently, once the secondary passes behind the primary’s wind, its contribution to the resonance-line flux becomes blocked, while the remaining UV continuum passes largely unattenuated through the optically thin wind, leading to a similar effect as seen in the red spectra presented in Fig. cite56†3 . Furthermore, when the star is behind regions where C IV is no longer completely optically thick, one expects intermediate stages such as during an ingress and egress in a normal eclipsing binary.
L143: These ingress/egress phases provide valuable information about the density structure of the star and future tailored observations might provide novel insights into the stellar wind physics.
L144: ### 3.2 Estimating the size of the C IV and N V resonance line formation regions
L145: AzV 75 is a standard calibration star for HST COS (cite69†Roman-Duval, 2011 , starting with), meaning that it is observed regularly and that in the archives several high-quality UV spectra are available that, coincidentally, cover almost the full orbit. With the extracted information indicating at which orbital phases the secondary is behind the optically thick regions of the resonance lines of the primary’s stellar wind, it is possible to calculate the radial extent of these regions.
L146: This information can then be compared to stellar atmosphere models and provides a unique benchmark for stellar wind theory.
L147: Unfortunately, there is no eclipsing binary model tool available that accounts in detail for each star’s stellar atmosphere and the connected changes in optical depth across the stellar wind.
L148: In the literature, several studies have addressed eclipses in Wolf–Rayet binaries, where the strong winds of both components interact, often producing colliding-wind X-ray shocks and phase-dependent line-profile variability (cite70†Auer & Koenigsberger, 1994 ; cite71†Lamontagne et al., 1996 ; cite72†Perrier et al., 2009 , e.g.,). However, our system is quite different, as only the primary drives a strong wind, whereas the secondary does not appear to possess a significant outflow (see Sect. cite17†4.2 ).
L149: Under the assumption that the C IV and N V resonance line formation region in the primary’s wind and the secondary can be approximated by uniformly radiating stellar spheres, and given that the radius difference between the primary’s wind and the stellar disk of the secondary is much larger than a factor of ten, the duration of the eclipse can be approximated analytically.
