L50: # Contrast-Space Projection for Network Meta-Analysis: An Exact and Invariant Study-Based Decomposition of Direct and Indirect Contributions
L51: 
L52: Chong Wang ^{†}^{†}thanks: Corresponding author: chwang@iastate.edu    Yanqi Zhang    Zhezhen Jin    Annette O’Connor
L53: ###### Abstract
L54: 
L55: Network meta-analysis (NMA) combines direct and indirect comparisons across a connected treatment network to estimate relative treatment effects. However, there is a lack of exact contribution decompositions that reproduce NMA estimates, particularly in the presence of multi-arm trials that induce within-study correlations.
L56: We address this reproducibility gap by developing a contrast-space projection formulation of NMA. Working directly in the space of all estimable pairwise treatment contrasts, we express the NMA estimator as an explicit linear mapping of the observed contrasts onto the consistency-constrained contrast space induced by orthogonal projection.
L57: This formulation preserves the full covariance structure induced by multi-arm trials and provides an exact algebraic mapping between observed contrasts and fitted network estimates. The projection plays a central role beyond estimation: it provides a unified algebraic mechanism for separating direct and indirect evidence, for removing within-study representational redundancy through canonicalization, and for decomposing observed evidence into consistent and inconsistent components.
L58: Building on this projection formulation, we introduce a rigorous study-based definition of direct and indirect evidence through a canonical within-study reduction that removes algebraic redundancy and yields a unique, invariant, and reproducible study-level decomposition.
L59: Using this construction, we derive exact covariance-aware decompositions of the NMA estimator into study-level direct and indirect contributions, and further decompose indirect evidence into path-level components within the same projection framework. This canonical decomposition enables, to our knowledge, the first forest-plot representation that exactly reconstructs the network meta-analysis estimator through a covariance-aware decomposition into study-level direct and path-level indirect components.
L60: The resulting weights are fully analogous to classical inverse-variance weights in pairwise meta-analysis.
L61: The framework also yields a suite of exact, covariance-aware diagnostic and graphical tools, including projection-based forest plots, tension plots for assessing agreement between direct and indirect evidence, and path-based visualizations of evidence propagation. These tools provide interpretable summaries of how evidence contributes to each network estimate while remaining fully consistent with the underlying model.
L62: Within this unified formulation, study-level contributions and the global $Q$ test for network inconsistency are expressed within the same projection framework, with classical inverse-variance weighting as a special case. Applications to three empirical datasets illustrate how the proposed framework provides a reproducible and coherent approach for interpreting evidence contributions in network meta-analysis.

L69: ## 1 Introduction
L70: Network meta-analysis (NMA) extends classical pairwise meta-analysis by synthesizing evidence across multiple treatments within a connected network of randomized trials and is now widely used in quantitative evidence synthesis, clinical guidelines, and health-technology assessment [cite38†11 , cite39†18 , cite40†3 , cite41†16 ].
L71: Under the consistency assumption, NMA combines direct and indirect comparisons to estimate relative treatment effects, including comparisons that have not been evaluated directly within any single study. The resulting network estimates reflect a structured propagation of evidence from observed treatment contrasts through the treatment network, yielding a network-level pattern of evidence contributions.
L72: Understanding and decomposing this evidence is therefore central to the transparent interpretation of network meta-analysis results. However, how such evidence is partitioned into direct and indirect components—and attributed to individual studies—remains poorly defined in current practice.
L73: In traditional pairwise meta-analysis, the pooled treatment effect is typically expressed as a weighted average of study-level estimates. Because the estimator is linear, the relative contribution of each study can be calculated directly from the inverse-variance weights and is routinely displayed in forest plots.
L74: This transparent decomposition plays an important role in evidence interpretation, allowing systematic reviewers to identify which studies contribute most strongly to the pooled estimate [cite42†4 ].
L75: In contrast, such a transparent study-level decomposition is much less straightforward in network meta-analysis. NMA estimates combine multiple direct and indirect evidence pathways, often involving correlated contrasts arising from multi-arm trials. As a consequence, the algebraic relationship between individual studies and the final network estimates is not transparent.
L76: Although the statistical estimation of NMA effects is well understood, the explicit and reproducible representation of network estimates as linear functions of the observed direct evidence, while preserving numerical reproducibility and compatibility with multi-arm covariance structures, remains largely underdeveloped. As NMA methods have become cornerstone tools in quantitative evidence synthesis, ensuring transparency in how evidence contributes to each estimate is critical.
L77: To support transparent reporting, as emphasized in the PRISMA-NMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Network Meta-Analyses) guidelines [cite43†6 ], it is critical to understand the exact contribution of each evidence source to the final network estimates. However, fulfilling this requirement in an exact and reproducible manner remains a significant methodological challenge, particularly in the presence of multi-arm trials.
L78: Existing contribution analyses are often constructed from marginal pairwise summaries, which do not preserve the full covariance structure used in the generalized least squares (GLS) formulation of network meta-analysis. As a result, such approaches may fail to reproduce the reported NMA estimates exactly and may yield decompositions that lack invariance to within-study parameterization.
L79: Understanding how evidence propagates through treatment networks has attracted substantial methodological interest. [cite44†17 ] established connections between NMA and electrical networks, interpreting treatment comparisons through graph Laplacians. Building on this idea, [cite45†8 ] showed that linear operators arising in NMA can be interpreted as describing how evidence propagates between treatments.
L80: [cite46†13 ] further proposed a stream decomposition in which edge-level flows are partitioned into paths connecting treatments, while [cite47†2 ] introduced a random-walk interpretation in an aggregate-level NMA formulation based on adjusted weights and a network of two-arm trials, deriving analytic expressions for evidence-flow proportions. In addition to flow-based interpretations, a separate line of work evaluates evidence importance through perturbation approaches such as leave-one-out (LOO) analyses.
L81: These methods quantify how removing a study or comparison changes a network estimate or its uncertainty. For example, [cite48†14 ] formalize a notion of statistical importance for treatment-level NMA estimates, and [cite49†12 ] extend these ideas to component network meta-analysis. Such approaches measure the sensitivity of the estimator but do not provide an additive decomposition of the estimator itself. Despite these advances, an important reproducibility issue remains in current practice.
L82: Many contribution analyses construct the direct-evidence vector from marginal pairwise meta-analyses of each comparison. However, network meta-analysis estimators are derived from a generalized least squares (GLS) system that explicitly incorporates correlations induced by multi-arm trials.
L83: When marginal pairwise summaries are substituted into such linear decompositions, the full study-level covariance structure is replaced by a comparison-level approximation, and the NMA formulation is replaced by a system of weight-adjusted two-arm trials. As a result, the decomposition may fail to reproduce the reported NMA estimates exactly.
L84: This limitation arises because existing approaches rely on approximations or transformations of the underlying generalized least squares (GLS) system, rather than operating directly on the projection operator that defines the estimator. A formulation that works directly with this projection and preserves the full covariance structure is therefore needed.
L85: A related and largely unresolved issue is that, under the current NMA estimation framework, there is no rigorous, model-based and explicit definition that identifies which component of the estimator corresponds to direct evidence at the study level. Under the standard network representation and associated linear algebra, a multi-arm study contributes to a target comparison through multiple direct and/or indirect pathways in the NMA graph.
L86: As a result, the contribution of a single study is not confined to a single “direct” component but is distributed across multiple pathways. Direct evidence is often informally described as arising from studies that include both treatments in a comparison. However, in the presence of multi-arm trials, this notion is not well defined within the algebra of the model.
L87: Different but equivalent parameterizations of within-study contrasts may or may not explicitly include the target comparison as an observed direct contrast, even though they represent the same underlying data. Consequently, study-level decompositions of direct and indirect evidence are not uniquely determined by the model and may vary across equivalent contrast representations.
L88: This lack of a rigorous definition and invariance complicates interpretation and limits the reproducibility of study-level contribution analyses.
L89: Motivated by the lack of a rigorous and explicit model-based definition of direct and indirect evidence in network meta-analysis, as well as the need for exact algebraic reproducibility, we develop a contrast-space projection (CSP) formulation of NMA based on the Fisher-information geometry of the treatment network.
L90: Working directly in the space of all pairwise contrasts, we show that the NMA estimator can be expressed as a linear mapping of the observed evidence onto the consistency-constrained contrast space induced by orthogonal projection. This projection representation provides an exact and covariance-aware mapping between observed contrasts and network estimates, fully accounting for the dependence structure induced by multi-arm trials.
L91: Building on this formulation, we introduce a rigorous and explicit formulation of direct and indirect evidence and derive a representation-invariant decomposition of the network estimator into study-level components.
L92: Within this unified framework, the projection provides a single algebraic mechanism that, rather than relying on separate constructions, separates direct from indirect evidence, removes within-study representational redundancy through canonicalization, and yields exact study contributions, path decompositions, and inconsistency diagnostics.
L93: The main methodological contributions of this work are fourfold:
L94: 
L95:   * •
L96: 
L97: We derive an explicit linear projection linking the aggregated observed contrast vector to the NMA estimates by formulating the model directly in the full contrast space, providing an exact generalization of classical inverse-variance weighted pairwise meta-analysis.
L98: 
L99:   * •
L100: We introduce a rigorous study-based definition of direct and indirect evidence through a canonical within-study reduction that removes algebraic redundancy. This definition is unique and invariant to within-study parameterization.
L101: 
L102:   * •
L103: 
L104: We develop exact, covariance-aware decompositions of the NMA estimator into study-level direct and indirect contributions, and further decompose indirect evidence into path-level components, with weights analogous to inverse-variance weights in pairwise meta-analysis.
L105:   * •
L106: Using these quantities, we construct practical diagnostic and graphical tools, including study-level forest plots, tension plots, path-based visualizations, and a projection-based formulation of the global $Q$ statistic for assessing network inconsistency.
L107: The canonical decomposition provides, to our knowledge, the first forest-plot representation that exactly reconstructs the network meta-analysis estimator through a covariance-aware decomposition into study-level direct and path-level indirect components.
L108: Together, these results provide a unified and reproducible framework for expressing network meta-analysis estimates as exact linear functions of the observed contrasts and for quantifying how evidence propagates through complex treatment networks. By combining a projection-based formulation with a canonical, representation-invariant decomposition, the proposed approach bridges the gap between theoretical consistency, numerical reproducibility, and practical interpretability.
L109: More broadly, the contrast-space projection framework provides the mathematical foundation needed to extend familiar tools from classical pairwise meta-analysis—such as study-level weighting, forest plots, and diagnostic assessments—to the network meta-analysis setting in a coherent and covariance-aware manner.
L110: The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section cite8†2.1 introduces the contrast-space formulation and projection representation of network meta-analysis. Section cite15†2.2 develops the canonical study-level decomposition and the representation-invariant definition of direct and indirect evidence, together with the induced path-based decomposition. Section cite21†2.3 presents the resulting diagnostic and graphical tools derived from the projection framework.
L111: Section cite26†3 illustrates the methodology using several empirical network meta-analysis datasets.
L112: ## 2 Methods
L113: 
L114: Network meta-analysis combines observed direct contrasts to obtain treatment effect estimates that satisfy the consistency constraints of the treatment network, meaning that all pairwise comparisons are coherent with a single set of underlying treatment effects. This can be formulated as finding the closest set of consistency-compatible contrasts to the observed data, accounting for the covariance structure of the observations.
L115: We develop a contrast-space projection formulation of network meta-analysis in which the network estimator is expressed as an exact linear mapping from the observed contrasts to the full set of pairwise treatment effects. This projection provides a reproducible representation of how evidence contributes to each network estimate.
L116: Building on this formulation, we construct a canonical, representation-invariant decomposition of the estimator into study-level direct and indirect components, and further resolve the indirect component into path-level contributions. These quantities generalize classical inverse-variance weights from pairwise meta-analysis to the network setting and form the basis for the diagnostic and graphical tools developed in subsequent sections.
L117: Throughout, the projection formulation serves as the underlying mechanism that separates direct from indirect evidence, induces a canonical representation of study-level contributions, and decomposes observed evidence into consistent and inconsistent components.
L118: ### 2.1 Contrast-space formulation of network meta-analysis
L119: 
L120: Standard formulations of network meta-analysis (NMA) parameterize treatment effects relative to a chosen reference treatment. In contrast, we formulate NMA directly in the space of all estimable pairwise treatment contrasts, which correspond to the comparisons reported and interpreted in practice.
L121: This contrast-space formulation yields an exact linear mapping from the observed contrasts to the fitted network estimates under the generalized least squares model. The resulting representation preserves the full covariance structure induced by multi-arm trials and provides the foundation for a projection-based characterization of evidence contributions.
L122: #### 2.1.1 Contrast-space parameterization and consistency constraints
L123: 
L124: We begin by parameterizing treatment effects in the full contrast space. We assume the treatment network is connected and contains $T$ treatments. Let $m=\binom{T}{2}$ denote the number of pairwise treatment contrasts. Define the contrast vector
L125: 
L126:  | $$\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{12},\theta_{13},\dots,\theta_{(T-1)T})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},$$  |
L127: where $\theta_{ab}$ denotes the treatment effect comparing treatment $b$ to treatment $a$.
L128: 
L129: Under the consistency assumption, treatment effects satisfy relations such as $\theta_{ac}=\theta_{ab}+\theta_{bc}$. Collecting these relations yields the linear constraint system
L130: 
L131:  | $$\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}=0,$$  |
L132: 
L133: where $\mathbf{C}$ encodes the consistency equations. The admissible parameter space is therefore
L134:  | $$\mathcal{M}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}=0\}.$$  |
L135: 
L136: This consistency space has dimension $T-1$, corresponding to the number of independent treatment effects in a connected network.
L137: The consistency constraints ensure that all pairwise treatment effects can be represented through a set of underlying treatment-specific parameters, yielding a coherent network structure. In particular, the space $\mathcal{M}$ represents the set of contrast vectors that are compatible with a common set of treatment effects, and thus corresponds to the identifiable parameter space in a connected network.
L138: This constraint structure plays a central role in defining the projection representation of the network meta-analysis estimator developed below.
L139: #### 2.1.2 Observed contrasts and covariance structure
L140: 
L141: Let $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ denote the vector of observed relative treatment effects (observed contrasts) extracted from the included studies, where $n$ is the total number of study-level contrasts included in the analysis. For studies with $k\geq 3$ treatment arms, the dimension and composition of $\mathbf{y}$ depend on how within-study correlations are represented [cite50†15 ].
L142: Multi-arm trials may be represented either through a set of $k-1$ baseline contrasts or through the full set of $k(k-1)/2$ pairwise contrasts together with their induced covariance structure [cite51†10 , cite52†7 , cite50†15 ].
L143: 
L144:   1. 1.
L145: Dimension reduction (basic-contrast representation). The standard approach selects one treatment as a baseline within each trial and constructs $\mathbf{y}$ from the resulting $k-1$ contrasts relative to that baseline [cite51†10 , cite53†5 ]. This representation ensures that the within-study covariance matrix is non-singular and leads to the familiar formulation used in most network meta-analysis implementations.
L146: The remaining pairwise contrasts within the trial are implicitly represented through linear combinations of the retained contrasts.
L147:   2. 2.
L148: Full pairwise within-study contrast representation. An alternative representation retains all $k(k-1)/2$ pairwise contrasts that can be formed within each multi-arm study. In this case the within-study covariance matrix is singular because the contrasts are linearly dependent. Graph-theoretical and flow-based approaches often operate on this expanded set of within-study contrasts and adjust the covariance structure to account for the induced correlations [cite50†15 , cite46†13 ].
L149: In the present framework, this representation is retained and the singular covariance matrix is handled directly using the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. This representation provides a common contrast space in which all within-study parameterizations can be embedded and will serve as the foundation for the representation-invariant decomposition developed in Section cite15†2.2 .
L150: The graphical representation of network meta-analysis, in which treatments are nodes and pairwise comparisons form the edges of a network, corresponds naturally to the full pairwise contrast space. In this setting, a target network comparison can be expressed as a linear combination of the direct comparison estimates in the network [cite46†13 ].
L151: In the basic-contrast representation, the retained within-study contrasts depend strictly on the choice of baseline treatment. Even when a natural baseline exists—such as a placebo or standard care—any target comparison between two non-baseline treatments (e.g., comparing two active arms) will not appear explicitly in the observed contrast vector, and will be regarded as indirect evidence through this specific network structure.
L152: Furthermore, in many applications where no baseline is intrinsically preferred, different choices yield different but algebraically equivalent parameterizations of the exact same multi-arm study. As a result, whether a target comparison appears explicitly as a retained study contrast depends entirely on this representational choice, even though the resulting NMA estimator remains unchanged.
L153: This parameterization dependence is a primary source of ambiguity in study-level definitions of direct and indirect evidence and motivates the representation-invariant construction developed later.
L154: Let $\mathbf{V}$ denote the $n\times n$ covariance matrix of $\mathbf{y}$, capturing both within-study correlations and potential between-study heterogeneity. The observed study-level contrast model is
L155: 
L156:  | $$\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim N(0,\mathbf{V}),$$  |  | (1)
L157: where $\mathbf{X}$ denotes the generalized least squares design matrix for the observed study contrasts. Each row of $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ specifies the linear combination of treatment contrasts corresponding to a particular observation in $\mathbf{y}$.
L158: Under a fixed-effects specification, $\mathbf{V}$ consists solely of within-study sampling covariance matrices and is block-diagonal across studies. Under a random-effects specification, heterogeneity is incorporated through a variance component $\tau^{2}$ added to the within-study covariance structure.
L159: The heterogeneity parameter $\tau^{2}$ is first estimated (e.g., via restricted maximum likelihood), after which the estimated value $\hat{\tau}^{2}$ is plugged into the covariance matrix $\mathbf{V}$, which is then used in the subsequent projection and CSP decomposition.
L160: Because the network meta-analysis estimator derived below depends on $\mathbf{V}$ only through linear projections, the proposed evidence flow and decomposition framework applies to both fixed- and random-effects models by substituting the appropriate form of $\mathbf{V}$.
L161: #### 2.1.3 Projection representation of the network meta-analysis estimator
L162: 
L163: The NMA estimator is obtained as the constrained generalized least squares solution
L164: 
L165:  | $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta})\quad\text{subject to }\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}=0.$$  |  | (2)
L166: When $\mathbf{V}$ is singular, as in the full contrast representation, we formulate the generalized least squares criterion using the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse $\mathbf{V}^{+}$. When $\mathbf{V}$ is nonsingular, $\mathbf{V}^{+}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}$, and the formulation reduces to the standard generalized least squares problem. This estimator admits the explicit representation
L167:  | $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}=\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{y},\qquad\mathcal{I}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{X},$$  |
L168: where $\mathcal{I}^{+}$ and $\mathbf{V}^{+}$ denote the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverses. Although the reduced and full contrast representations differ algebraically, they correspond to the same underlying likelihood for the observed contrasts.
L169: When the exact within-study covariance structure is correctly specified, both representations yield identical network meta-analysis estimators for the treatment effects; the distinction lies only in how the observed contrasts and their covariance structure are expressed.
L170: ###### Proposition 2.1 (Equivalence of reduced and full contrast representations).
L171: 
L172: Suppose the full contrast representation is obtained from a reduced (basic) representation via a linear mapping
L173: 
L174:  | $$\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{b},\qquad\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{b},\qquad\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}_{b}\mathbf{A}^{\top},$$  |
L175: 
L176: where $\mathbf{A}$ has full column rank and $\mathbf{V}_{b}$ is nonsingular.
L177: 
L178: Then
L179:  | $$\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{b}^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{b},\quad\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{b}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{b},$$  |
L180: 
L181: and therefore the GLS estimator computed in the full contrast representation using $\mathbf{V}^{+}$ coincides exactly with that obtained from the reduced representation using $\mathbf{V}_{b}^{-1}$.
L182: This result follows from the invariance of the generalized least squares estimator under full-rank linear transformations of the observation space. The mapping preserves both the column space of the design matrix and the Fisher information matrix, ensuring that the resulting estimator remains unchanged under equivalent contrast representations.
L183: This formulation admits a geometric interpretation in the observation space. The fitted values $\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}$ represent the $\mathbf{V}^{+}$-orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto $\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{X})$ within the geometry induced by $\mathbf{V}^{+}$. When $\mathbf{V}$ is singular, this projection is understood as the minimum-norm solution within $\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{V})$, where the semi-inner product becomes nondegenerate.
L184: The corresponding parameter estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}$ is the element of the consistency subspace $\mathcal{M}$ whose image under $\mathbf{X}$ equals this projected fitted value.
L185: Because $\mathcal{M}$ is a linear subspace, the estimator admits a linear representation:
L186: 
L187:  | $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y},\qquad\mathbf{P}=\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}.$$  |  | (3)
L188: Here, the matrix $\mathbf{P}$ is the projection-induced mapping from the observed contrast vector $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ to the fitted contrast vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}\in\mathcal{M}$, while the matrix $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{P}$ acts as the corresponding $\mathbf{V}^{+}$-orthogonal projection operator onto $\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{X})$ in the observation space.
L189: 
L190: Inner product interpretation. Throughout, expressions of the form
L191:  | $$\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{\mathbf{V}^{+}}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{b}$$  |
L192: define a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. When $\mathbf{V}$ is nonsingular, this corresponds to a standard inner product. When $\mathbf{V}$ is singular, the form is a semi-inner product on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, but becomes a valid inner product when restricted to $\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{V})$, which contains all admissible contrast vectors arising from the model. All projection statements below are understood with respect to this induced geometry on $\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{V})$.
L193: While the mapping of evidence into the complete all-pairs contrast space increases the nominal dimensionality of the system, the framework remains highly computationally efficient. Because independent trials are uncorrelated, the global covariance matrix $\mathbf{V}$ is strictly block-diagonal.
L194: This structural sparsity guarantees that the generalized inverses required for the projection can be computed efficiently using sparse matrix operations, allowing the framework to scale well to large and complex networks.
L195: #### 2.1.4 Fisher-information geometry of the projection
L196: The structure of the projection-induced mapping $\mathbf{P}$ in (cite54†3 ) can be understood through the Fisher information matrix $\mathcal{I}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{X}$, which provides an equivalent representation in terms of information flow. Because each row of $\mathbf{X}$ encodes a pairwise treatment contrast, $\mathcal{I}$ has a structure analogous to a weighted graph Laplacian on the treatment network, with edge weights determined by the precision structure encoded in $\mathbf{V}$.
L197: Thus, $\mathcal{I}$ summarizes how statistical information propagates across the network under the generalized least squares model.
L198: For a target comparison $a{:}b$ (with $a<b$), define
L199: 
L200:  | $$\mathbf{u}=\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathbf{c}_{ab},$$  |
L201: 
L202: where $\mathbf{c}_{ab}$ is the contrast vector corresponding to the comparison $a{:}b$, having a 1 in the coordinate corresponding to $\theta_{ab}$ and 0 elsewhere. Then $\mathbf{u}$ can be interpreted as a potential vector satisfying
L203: 
L204:  | $$\mathcal{I}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{c}_{ab},$$  |
L205: which corresponds to a unit flow from treatment $a$ to treatment $b$ under the Laplacian system. The projection coefficients can then be written as
L206: 
L207:  | $$\mathbf{p}_{ab}=\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{u},$$  |
L208: 
L209: so that the network estimator can be written as
L210: 
L211:  | $$\hat{\theta}_{ab}^{\mathrm{NMA}}=\mathbf{p}_{ab}^{\top}\mathbf{y}.$$  |
L212: This formulation shows that the projection corresponds to a minimum-energy flow under the precision structure $\mathbf{V}$, linking the statistical model to classical graph-theoretic and electrical-network interpretations. Importantly, this analogy should not be interpreted as a physical electrical flow. The projection-induced flow is defined through a covariance-weighted least squares problem and is not constrained to be nonnegative or to follow potential gradients.
L213: As a result, the induced flow may include local reverse directions and circulating components (cycles) that cancel in net transport between treatments but remain in the estimator through heterogeneous study-specific contrasts. Thus, while the Fisher-information representation provides useful intuition, the resulting flow is a generalized algebraic object rather than a physical current.
L214: The Fisher-information representation also induces an edge-level summary of how evidence for the target comparison $a{:}b$ is distributed across treatment comparisons. At the treatment level, this representation satisfies a conservation property: the total contribution flowing out of treatment $a$ equals one, the total contribution flowing into treatment $b$ equals one, and intermediate treatments have balanced incoming and outgoing contributions.
L215: Because $\mathbf{P}$ depends only on the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$ and covariance structure $\mathbf{V}$, and not on the observed outcomes $\mathbf{y}$, the decomposition it induces is a structural property of the model. Each row of $\mathbf{P}$ describes how information is distributed from the observed contrasts to a target comparison, incorporating multi-arm correlation and, when present, random-effects heterogeneity through the Fisher information structure.
L216: In contrast, several existing proportion-contribution approaches apply additional transformations to projection- or hat-matrix representations to obtain nonnegative comparison-level weights. While such quantities may be useful descriptively, they no longer correspond directly to the underlying Fisher-informed linear operator. Working directly with $\mathbf{P}$ preserves the exact covariance structure and yields an algebraically exact and reproducible decomposition of the network estimator.
L217: While the projection operator $\mathbf{P}$ is algebraically equivalent to the generalized least squares estimator, its role here as the primary object of analysis enables exact, covariance-aware decompositions and representation-invariant study-level contributions that are not available in standard formulations.
L218: In particular, the variance of the network estimator is given by
L219: 
L220:  | $$\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{ab}^{\mathrm{NMA}})=\mathbf{c}_{ab}^{\top}\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathbf{c}_{ab},$$  |
L221: 
L222: which coincides with the effective resistance between treatments $a$ and $b$ in the corresponding weighted graph.
L223: #### 2.1.5 Projection-based generalization of pairwise inverse-variance weights
L224: 
L225: A hallmark of traditional pairwise meta-analysis is the transparency provided by inverse-variance weights, typically displayed as percentages in a forest plot to denote each study’s contribution to the pooled estimate [cite42†4 ]. Network meta-analysis lacks an exact analogue because the estimator combines direct and indirect evidence through a generalized least squares system that incorporates multi-arm covariance.
L226: The contrast-space projection framework provides a natural generalization of these weights. Because the NMA estimator admits the linear representation
L227: 
L228:  | $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\mathrm{NMA}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y},$$  |
L229: 
L230: the coefficients $p_{ij}$ describe how each observed contrast $y_{j}$ contributes to the network estimate $\hat{\theta}_{i}^{\mathrm{NMA}}$.
