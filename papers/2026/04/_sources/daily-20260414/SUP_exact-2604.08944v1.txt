Abstract
Multi-agent coordination under partial observability requires agents to share complementary private information. While recent methods optimize messages for intermediate objectives (e.g., reconstruction accuracy or mutual information), rather than decision quality, we introduce SeqComm-DFL, unifying the sequential communication with decision-focused learning for task performance. Our approach features value-aware message generation with sequential Stackelberg conditioning: messages maximize receiver decision quality and are generated in priority order, with agents conditioning on their predecessors. The guidance potential determined by their prosocial ordering. We extend Optimal Model Design to communication-augmented world models with QMIX factorization, enabling efficient end-to-end training via implicit differentiation. We prove information-theoretic bounds showing that communication value scales with coordination gaps and establish 𝒪⁡(1/T)\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T}) convergence for the bilevel optimization, where TT denotes the number of training iterations. On collaborative healthcare and StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) benchmarks, SeqComm-DFL achieves four to six times higher cumulative rewards and over 13% win rate improvements, enabling coordination strategies inaccessible under information asymmetry.

1 Introduction
Many real-world, decision-making problems involve multiple agents operating under partial observability, where each agent observes only a portion of an environment yet must coordinate with others to achieve shared objectives. This setting arises naturally in healthcare resource allocation (Pronovost et al., 2003), autonomous multi-robot coordination (Busoniu et al., 2008), and traffic control systems (Chu et al., 2019). The fundamental challenge is that optimal decisions require information distributed across agents, yet communication bandwidth and latency constraints often preclude sharing all observations directly.
Consider intensive care unit management, where specialists (e.g., cardiologists, pulmonologists, neurologists) observe patient data relevant to their expertise but must coordinate treatment decisions that account for drug interactions and multi-system organ involvement (Vincent et al., 2009). No single specialist possesses complete information, yet decisions have cascading effects across specialties.
The dominant paradigm for cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is centralized training with decentralized execution (Oliehoek et al., 2008; Kraemer and Banerjee, 2016), where agents access global information during training but execute using only local observations. While methods like QMIX (Rashid et al., 2018), MAPPO (Yu et al., 2022), and MADDPG (Lowe et al., 2017) have achieved strong performance, they face fundamental challenges including non-stationarity (i.e., each agent’s environment changes as others learn) and credit assignment (i.e., determining individual contributions to team rewards). Learned communication offers a promising solution (Foerster et al., 2016; Sukhbaatar et al., 2016) to improving non-stationarity, but existing protocols typically optimize surrogate objectives such as message reconstruction or mutual information (Wang et al., 2020), rather than downstream decision quality.
A parallel challenge arises from the well-documented objective mismatch problem in model-based reinforcement learning (Lambert et al., 2020): world models trained to maximize predictive accuracy may not support optimal policy learning because prediction errors in value-irrelevant dimensions contribute to training loss but not to policy quality. This mismatch extends to learned communication: standard protocols, like the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), optimize for reconstruction accuracy rather than decision quality, wasting bandwidth on information that does not improve coordination.
Decision-focused learning (DFL), also known as predict-and-optimize (Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022; Tang and Khalil, 2024), addresses objective mismatch by training predictive models with downstream optimization tasks in an end-to-end approach. In this paradigm, a predictor forecasts uncertain parameters, and an optimizer solves a decision problem given those predictions. The key insight is to backpropagate through the optimization layer to train the predictor for decision quality rather than prediction accuracy (Donti et al., 2017). However, existing DFL methods focus primarily on single-agent settings with exogenous uncertainty, where predictions do not influence outcomes.
We propose Sequential Communication for Decision-Focused Learning (SeqComm-DFL), unifying multi-agent communication learning, model-based reinforcement learning, and DFL. Viewed through the predict-and-optimize lens, SeqComm-DFL casts a communication module ϕθ\phi_{\theta} with parameter θ\theta as the “predictor” and MARL policy selection as the “optimization task.” Crucially, our setting involves endogenous uncertainty (Tang and Khalil, 2024): messages are not passive forecasts but active signals that change how other agents act, creating feedback loops absent in standard DFL. To our knowledge, SeqComm-DFL is the first framework to extend DFL to multi-agent systems with endogenous, communication-induced uncertainty.
Our key innovation is value-aware message generation with sequential Stackelberg conditioning (Zheng et al., 2022): messages are optimized to maximize receiver decision quality and generated sequentially in priority order, creating a leader-follower structure. Building on prior work in sequential communication (Ding et al., 2023), we introduce guidance potential for prosocial priority ordering and extend Optimal Model Design (OMD) (Nikishin et al., 2022) to communication-augmented world models with QMIX factorization (Rashid et al., 2018). We provide information-theoretic bounds on communication value (Theorem 5.1) and convergence guarantees (Theorem 5.3). Experiments on healthcare coordination and SMAC benchmarks demonstrate 44–66 times reward improvements and above 13% point win rate gains.
2 Related Work
Cooperative MARL under the centralized training with decentralized execution paradigm (Oliehoek et al., 2008; Kraemer and Banerjee, 2016) has seen rapid progress. Value decomposition methods including VDN (Sunehag et al., 2018), QMIX (Rashid et al., 2018), QTRAN (Son et al., 2019), and QPLEX (Wang et al., 2021) factor joint action-values to enable scalable learning, while policy gradient approaches like MAPPO (Yu et al., 2022) and MADDPG (Lowe et al., 2017) extend actor-critic methods to multi-agent settings. Two fundamental challenges persist: non-stationarity (Hernandez-Leal et al., 2017; Papoudakis et al., 2019) and credit assignment (Foerster et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2020). Opponent modeling (He et al., 2016) and hysteretic Q-learning (Omidshafiei et al., 2017) aim to mitigate non-stationarity, while COMA (Foerster et al., 2018) addresses credit assignment via counterfactual baselines. While these methods avoid explicit communication, our work (Table 1) unifies multi-agent coordination and decision-focused communication.
Early works like CommNet (Sukhbaatar et al., 2016) and DIAL (Foerster et al., 2016) pioneered end-to-end communication, later enhanced by attention mechanisms (Das et al., 2019; Niu et al., 2021). To improve efficiency, NDQ (Wang et al., 2020) and I2C (Ding et al., 2020) use information-theoretic objectives to minimize overhead. More recently, SeqComm (Ding et al., 2023) introduced sequential message priority, and MAIC (Yuan et al., 2022) used messages as incentives for value modulation. However, these methods typically optimize for intermediate surrogates rather than final decision quality. Our work (Table 1) extends SeqComm with value-aware message optimization and integrates communication into decision-focused world model learning.
Model-based reinforcement learning methods like World Models (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018) and Dreamer (Hafner et al., 2020) learn dynamics for imagination. To solve the objective mismatch problem (Lambert et al., 2020), value-aware learning (Farahmand et al., 2017) and OMD (Nikishin et al., 2022) utilize bilevel optimization. Similarly, DFL (Donti et al., 2017; Elmachtoub and Grigas, 2022), or predict-and-optimize (Tang and Khalil, 2024), trains models end-to-end for downstream performance. While recent DFL research handles endogenous uncertainty, it remains focused on single-agent settings. SeqComm-DFL (Table 1) is the first to extend DFL principles to multi-agent communication with endogenous feedback.

3 Problem Setting
We consider a cooperative, dencetralized partially-observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) (𝒩,𝒮,{𝒜i}i=1N,{𝒪i}i=1N,P,R,γ)(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{S},\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},P,R,\gamma) (Bernstein et al., 2002), where 𝒩={1,…,N}\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\} is the set of agents, 𝒮\mathcal{S} is the global state space, 𝒜i\mathcal{A}_{i} is the action space for agent i∈𝒩i\in\mathcal{N}, 𝒪i\mathcal{O}_{i} is the local observation space for agent ii, P:𝒮×𝒜×𝒮→[0,1]P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1] is the transition function, R:𝒮×𝒜↦ℝR:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mathbb{R} is the shared reward function, and γ∈[0,1]\gamma\in[0,1] is the discount factor. The set 𝒜=𝒜1×…×𝒜N\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{A}_{N} denotes the joint action space, and a joint action is denoted a=(a1,…,aN)∈𝒜\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\in\mathcal{A}, where ai∈𝒜ia_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i} is the action chosen by agent ii. A communication module function ϕθ:𝒪↦ℝdm\phi_{\theta}:\mathcal{O}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}} with parameter θ∈ℝdθ\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}} generates messages mi∈ℝdmm_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}} for each agent ii, where dmd_{m} is the dimension of the messages. We learn a communication-augmented world model fθ:𝒮×𝒜×ℳ→ℝ×𝒮f_{\theta}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{S} for predicting rewards and next states conditioned on state, joint actions, and messages. 4 SeqComm-DFL Framework
Our framework addresses three interconnected challenges in multi-agent coordination under partial observability: (1) what to communicate: generating messages that improve teammates’ decisions rather than merely encoding observations; (2) how to coordinate: establishing a principled ordering for sequential action selection; and (3) how to learn: training world models that optimize for decision quality rather than prediction accuracy. We address these through value-aware message generation, Stackelberg sequential conditioning (Zheng et al., 2022), and decision-focused bilevel optimization (Nikishin et al., 2022), unified through QMIX-style (Rashid et al., 2018) value decomposition for scalability.
4.1 Communication Architecture with Value-Aware Messaging
Each agent ii first encodes its local observation oio_{i} through a shared message encoder ϕθ\phi_{\theta} to produce a base message: mibase=ϕθ​(oi)m_{i}^{\text{base}}=\phi_{\theta}(o_{i}), where ϕθ\phi_{\theta} is a multi-layer perceptron that maps observations to the message space ℝdm\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}. Unlike standard approaches that optimize messages for mutual information, we train the communication module end-to-end with downstream decision quality (a key innovation that directly connects communication to the DFL objective).
4.1.1 The Value-Aware Communication Principle
Standard communication methods encode observations oio_{i} into messages mim_{i} without considering downstream decision impact. This encoding creates a misalignment: the communication module optimizes for information content (e.g., mutual information I⁡(mi,oi)I(m_{i};o_{i}) or reconstruction error ‖oi−o^i‖2\|o_{i}-\hat{o}_{i}\|^{2}), while the downstream task requires decision-relevant information. We address this misalignment by optimizing messages for receiver decision quality:
Δ​Qj​(mi)=maxa∈𝒜j⁡Qj​(oj,mi,a)−maxa∈𝒜j⁡Qj​(oj,∅,a),\Delta Q_{j}(m_{i})=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}Q_{j}(o_{j},m_{i},a)-\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}Q_{j}(o_{j},\emptyset,a),
where ∅\emptyset denotes the null (zero-vector) message baseline. We use Qj(oj,mi,a)=𝔼[R(oj,mi,a)+γmaxa′∈𝒜jQj(o′,m′,a′)|oj,mi,a]Q_{j}(o_{j},m_{i},a)=\mathbb{E}[R(o_{j},m_{i},a)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}Q_{j}(o^{\prime},m^{\prime},a^{\prime})|o_{j},m_{i},a] to quantify the value for agent jj associated with observation ojo_{j}, message mim_{i} from agent ii, discount factor γ\gamma, and a transition to observation o′o^{\prime} with message m′m^{\prime} after taking action aa. The receiver decision quality quantity Δ​Qj​(mi)\Delta Q_{j}(m_{i}) measures how much agent jj’s best achievable Q-value improves when receiving message mim_{i} from agent ii. The key insight is that Δ​Qj​(mi)\Delta Q_{j}(m_{i}) directly quantifies the decision value of communication: information that doesn’t improve decisions has Δ​Qj=0\Delta Q_{j}=0 regardless of its information-theoretic content.
4.1.2 Value-Aware Training Loss
We define a value-aware auxiliary loss that directly maximizes the sum of decision quality improvements across all receivers:
ℒVA(θ)=−1B⋅N⁡(N−1)∑b=1B∑i=1N∑j≠iΔQj(mi(b))\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\mathcal{L}_{\text{VA}}(\theta)=-\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{B \cdot N(N-1)}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\Delta Q_{j}(m_{i}^{(b)})$}}}
(1)
This loss is relevant because it addresses the core objective mismatch: standard communication training minimizes reconstruction error, but what matters for coordination is whether messages improve teammates’ decisions. By directly optimizing Δ​Q\Delta Q, we ensure messages encode coordination-critical information rather than observational details. This loss is incorporated into our decision-focused world learning (Subsection 4.3), ensuring communication optimization is coupled with world model training. Crucially, since Δ​Qj\Delta Q_{j} depends on the critic QwQ_{w}, which in turn depends on the world model fθf_{\theta}, this creates an end-to-end pathway from communication to downstream performance.
4.1.3 Theoretical Connection to Decision-Focused Learning
The value-aware objective aligns communication learning with minimizing true environmental error in DFL. Similar to previous DFL work (Nikishin et al., 2022), we consider a true environment loss ℒtrue​(w,θ)\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(w;\theta) that evaluates how well a critic QwQ_{w} with parameter w∈ℝ|w|w\in\mathbb{R}^{|w|} trained on model predictions performs on real environment data. By the envelope theorem (Bertsekas, 2012), for an optimal critic w∗w^{*}:
∂ℒtrue​(w∗,θ)∂mi=∂ℒtrue∂Qw⋅∂Qw∂mi∝−∑j≠i∇miQj(oj,mi,aj∗).\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial\Lc_{\text{true}}(w^*; \theta)}{\partial m_i}=\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial\Lc_{\text{true}}}{\partial Q_w}\cdot\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial Q_w}{\partial m_i}\propto-\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{m_{i}}Q_{j}(o_{j},m_{i},a_{j}^{*}).$}}}
The gradient of the true loss with respect to messages points toward increasing receiver Q-values, precisely what Δ​Qj\Delta Q_{j} measures. This observation suggests that value-aware communication is not merely a heuristic but emerges naturally from the decision-focused objective.
4.1.4 Message Refinement Architecture
We implement value-aware communication through a refinement module Refineθ:ℝdm×ℝ→ℝdm\text{Refine}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}} that adjusts base messages based on estimated decision impact:
mi=mibase+α⋅Refineθ​(mibase,Δ​Qi^),m_{i}=m_{i}^{\text{base}}+\alpha\cdot\text{Refine}_{\theta}(m_{i}^{\text{base}},\Delta\hat{Q_{i}}),
where Δ​Q^i=1N−1​∑j≠iΔ​Q^j​(mibase)\Delta\hat{Q}_{i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\Delta\hat{Q}_{j}(m_{i}^{\text{base}}) is the average estimated decision quality improvement across all receivers, computed via a lightweight prediction head trained alongside the critic.
Monte Carlo Grounding for Δ​Q\Delta Q. A critical implementation detail: early in training, the critic QwQ_{w} is poorly calibrated, making Δ​Qj\Delta Q_{j} estimates unreliable. To avoid the “biased critic” problem common in DFL methods that rely on untrained value functions, we ground Δ​Q\Delta Q estimates using Monte Carlo (MC) rollouts during the warmup phase. For planning horizon TT (the maximum episode length), we collect KK trajectory samples and compute:
Δ​QjMC​(mi)=1K​∑k=1K[Gj(k)​(mi)−Gj(k)​(∅)]\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\Delta Q_{j}^{\text{MC}}(m_{i})=\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[G_{j}^{(k)}(m_{i})-G_{j}^{(k)}(\emptyset)\right]$}}}
where Gj(k)=∑t=0Tγt​rj,t(k)G_{j}^{(k)}=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}r_{j,t}^{(k)} is the kk-th MC return for agent jj. This provides unbiased estimates that bootstrap the value-aware training signal before the critic converges. We anneal from MC to critic-based estimates: Δ​Q^=(1−βt)​Δ​QMC+βt​Δ​Qw\Delta\hat{Q}=(1-\beta_{t})\Delta Q^{\text{MC}}+\beta_{t}\Delta Q_{w} with βt→1\beta_{t}\to 1 as training progresses. This hybrid approach ensures stable early learning without sacrificing asymptotic efficiency.
4.2 Sequential Action Selection with Stackelberg Conditioning
To resolve relative overgeneralization (Wei et al., 2018), where partial observability causes agents to perform sub-optimally due to an inability to anticipate teammates, we adopt a sequential Stackelberg (von Stackelberg, 1952) framework. Coordination proceeds in three stages: (1) negotiation, computing prosocial guidance potential for priority ordering; (2) launching, utilizing sequential conditioning on leaders’ committed actions and messages; and (3) regularization, using counterfactual influence to ensure causal message impact. This autoregressive structure breaks the symmetry of simultaneous selection, allowing followers to condition on leader intentions to reach Pareto-superior equilibria inaccessible under information asymmetry.
4.2.1 Negotiation Phase: Guidance Potential
We first determine priority ordering. Rather than using intention values (“Who wants to act most?”) (Ding et al., 2023), we employ guidance potential which measures each agent’s capacity to improve team outcomes by leading:
GPi(s)=𝔼𝐚∗∼π∗[Q1:N(s,𝐚∗|i+)−Q1:N(s,𝐚∗|i−)]\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\text{GP}_{i}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}^{*}\sim\pi^{*}}\left[Q_{1:N}(s,\mathbf{a}^{*}|i^{+})-Q_{\text{1:N}}(s,\mathbf{a}^{*}|i^{-})\right]$}}}
(2)
where Q1:NQ_{1:N} denotes the total joint value across agents 11 to NN, i+i^{+} indicates agent ii acts as leader (early in sequence), and i−i^{-} indicates agent ii acts as follower (later in the sequence). This prosocial criterion allows us to prioritize agents who can most help the team, not those with the strongest individual preferences.
Connection to Coordination Information Gap. The guidance potential is theoretically grounded in the coordination information gap ℐi\mathcal{I}_{i} (Definition C.1 in the Appendix). An agent with large ℐi\mathcal{I}_{i} possesses private information critical for optimal coordination (precisely the agent who should lead) . By prioritizing high-ℐi\mathcal{I}_{i} agents, we ensure that “strong decisions” (actions informed by coordination-critical observations) propagate first, conditioning the search space for followers. Formally, if agent ii has the largest information gap, then GPi\text{GP}_{i} is maximized because ii’s leadership most reduces uncertainty for teammates:
GPi∝∑j≠iI⁡(Mi;aj∗∣oj)≈∑j≠imin⁡(ℐj,ℋ⁡(Mi))\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\text{GP}_{i}\propto\sum_{j\neq i}I(M_{i};a^{*}_{j}\mid o_{j})\approx\sum_{j\neq i}\min(\mathcal{I}_{j},\mathcal{H}(M_{i}))$}}}
where ℋ⁡(Mi)\mathcal{H}(M_{i}) denotes the entropy associated with the message MiM_{i} and the approximation holds when messages are capacity-limited. For example, in a hospital environment, a cardiologist treating a patient with hidden cardiac risk has high ℐi\mathcal{I}_{i} if their message can “unlock” coordination strategies inaccessible without that information. We assign action priorities among agents using the Gumbel-softmax (Jang et al., 2017) π=argsort​(−GP)\pi=\text{argsort}(-\text{GP}) for differentiable exploration during training.

where M1:πk−1={mπj:j<k}M_{1:\pi_{k}-1}=\{m_{\pi_{j}}:j<k\} denotes the set of messages from all agents with higher priority than πk\pi_{k}. This creates explicit leader-follower structure: the first agent (leader) commits to an action based solely on local information; subsequent agents (followers) observe leader messages and adapt accordingly. 4.2.3 Counterfactual Influence Regularization
To ensure messages have a causal effect on decisions rather than merely correlating with them, we add a counterfactual influence loss (Jaques et al., 2019):
ℒinf(θ)=−1N⁡(N−1)∑i=1N∑j≠iDKL[πj(⋅|mi)∥πj(⋅|∅)]\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\mathcal{L}_{\text{inf}}(\theta)=-\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}D_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_{j}(\cdot|m_{i})\,\|\,\pi_{j}(\cdot|\emptyset)\right]$}}}
(3)
This loss encourages messages that change receiver behavior, measured by KL divergence (Kullback and Leibler, 1951) between policies with/out communication. Combined with value-aware training, this ensures messages are both influential and beneficial.
4.3 Decision-Focused World Model Learning
To resolve objective mismatch (Lambert et al., 2020) we extend OMD (Nikishin et al., 2022) to multi-agent settings. A bilevel framework optimizes a world model fθf_{\theta} for downstream policy regret rather than prediction loss via: (1) an inner loop training the critic on model predictions while evaluating on real data in the outer loop; (2) a coordination-aware objective that prevents message apathy by incentivizing the critic to distinguish informative signals; and (3) hypergradient computation utilizing the Implicit Function Theorem (IFT) (Krantz and Parks, 2002) and an efficient Conjugate Gradient solver (Hestenes and Stiefel, 1952).
4.3.1 Bilevel Optimization Formulation
The core insight of our bilevel optimization for DFL is to merge world model accuracy and decision quality into a single objective: rather than training the model to predict well (inner loop) and hoping this transfers to good decisions, we train the model parameters to directly optimize performance when the resulting critic is evaluated on real environment data (outer loop). The outer problem optimizes model parameters θ\theta for true environment performance; the inner problem trains the critic QwQ_{w} using model predictions:
(Outer)
minθ⁡ℒtrue​(w∗​(θ),θ)\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(w^{*}(\theta);\theta)
(4a)
(Inner)
s.t.​w∗​(θ)=arg⁡minw​ℒmodel​(w,θ)\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994ptw^{*}(\theta)=\arg\min_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w;\theta)
(4b)
This formulation captures that the optimal critic w∗w^{*} depends on the world model θ\theta, so updating θ\theta affects the world model predictions and the critic that evaluates them.
4.3.2 Inner Loop: Model-Based Critic Training
The inner problem trains the critic QwQ_{w} to satisfy the Bellman equation under the learned world model dynamics, running KinnerK_{\text{inner}} gradient descent steps to approximately solve the inner optimization. 4.3.3 Coordination-Aware Inner Loop: Preventing Message Apathy
A subtle failure mode arises in bilevel DFL for communication: if the critic QwQ_{w} learns to ignore messages, treating them as noise, then the hypergradient d​ℒtrued​θ\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}}{d\theta} vanishes with respect to the communication parameters, even when messages could improve decisions. We term this type of failure inner-loop apathy: the critic becomes message-agnostic, blocking gradient flow to the communication module.
To address this problem, we augment the inner-loop loss with a message-awareness auxiliary term that incentivizes the critic to distinguish between informative and uninformative messages (Yuan et al., 2022):
ℒaware​(w)=1B​∑b=1Bmax⁡(0,ϵmargin−|Qw​(s(b),𝐚(b),M(b))CLOSE−Qw(s(b),𝐚(b),𝟎)|)\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{aware}}(w)=\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\max\Big(0,\;\epsilon_{\text{margin}}-|Q_{w}(s^{(b)},\mathbf{a}^{(b)},M^{(b)})\\
-Q_{w}(s^{(b)},\mathbf{a}^{(b)},\mathbf{0})|\Big)\end{split}$}}}
(6)
where 𝟎\mathbf{0} denotes zero (null) messages and ϵmargin>0\epsilon_{\text{margin}}>0 is a margin hyperparameter. This hinge loss penalizes the critic when Q⁡(s,a,mgood)≈Q⁡(s,a,mzero)Q(s,a,m_{\text{good}})\approx Q(s,a,m_{\text{zero}}), forcing it to learn message-dependent value estimates. The complete inner-loop objective becomes: ℒinner​(w,θ)=ℒmodel​(w,θ)+λaware​ℒaware​(w)\mathcal{L}_{\text{inner}}(w;\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w;\theta)+\lambda_{\text{aware}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{aware}}(w).
The ℒaware​(w)\mathcal{L}_{\text{aware}}(w) auxiliary loss ensures the critic provides meaningful gradients with respect to messages, enabling the outer loop to optimize communication for decision quality. Conceptually, we treat messages as “incentives” that modulate individual Q-values: an approach validated in multi-agent incentive communication (Yuan et al., 2022).

The outer problem evaluates the critic on true environment data to assess generalization. Given environment dataset 𝒟env={(s(b),𝐚(b),r(b),s′(b))}b=1B\mathcal{D}_{\text{env}}=\{(s^{(b)},\mathbf{a}^{(b)},r^{(b)},s^{\prime(b)})\}_{b=1}^{B}:

Since w∗w^{*} depends on θ\theta, the total derivative of the outer objective requires differentiating through the inner optimization. Applying the chain rule:
d​ℒtrued​θ=∇θℒtrue​(w∗,θ)⏟direct effect+(d​w∗d​θ)⊤​∇wℒtrue​(w∗,θ)⏟indirect effect via critic\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{d\Lc_{\text{true}}}{d\theta}=\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(w^{*},\theta)}_{\text{direct effect}}+\underbrace{\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{dw^*}{d\theta}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(w^{*},\theta)}_{\text{indirect effect via critic}}$}}}

We apply the IFT (Krantz and Parks, 2002) to the Bellman residual’s first-order optimality condition. At the inner-loop fixed point, ∇wℒmodel​(w∗,θ)=0\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w^{*};\theta)=0. The IFT enables us to differentiate this optimality condition with respect to θ\theta, implicitly characterizing how w∗w^{*} changes as θ\theta varies without explicitly solving for w∗​(θ)w^{*}(\theta). Differentiating implicitly:
d​w∗d​θ=−[∇w​w2​ℒmodel​(w∗,θ)]−1​∇θ​w2ℒmodel​(w∗,θ)\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{dw^*}{d\theta}=-\left[\nabla^{2}_{ww}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w^{*},\theta)\right]^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{\theta w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w^{*},\theta)$}}}

Instead, we treat the converged inner loop as an implicit function and differentiate at equilibrium using the IFT. This decouples the forward solve (iterative gradient descent) from the backward pass (implicit differentiation). Full derivations and implementation details are in Appendix B. Substituting (9) into (8) yields the hypergradient:

We solve the linear system (H+λ​I)​v∗=b(H+\lambda I)v^{*}=b using conjugate gradient (CG) (Hestenes and Stiefel, 1952; Shewchuk, 1994), where H=∇w​w2ℒmodelH=\nabla^{2}_{ww}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}} is the Hessian of the model loss with respect to the critic parameters, II is the identity matrix, b=∇wℒtrueb=\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}} is the gradient of the true loss with respect to the critic parameters, and λ>0\lambda>0 is a damping coefficient for numerical stability.

5 Theoretical Analysis
We analyze SeqComm-DFL in the Dec-POMDP setting {𝒩,𝒮,{𝒜i},{𝒪i},P,R,γ}\{\mathcal{N},\mathcal{S},\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\},\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\},P,R,\gamma\} where agents receive local observations oi=𝒪i​(s)o_{i}=\mathcal{O}_{i}(s) but share a reward RR. We establish formal foundations for SeqComm-DFL by quantifying the necessity of communication, the advantage of decision-focused world models, and bilevel stability. Full proofs and regularity conditions are provided in Appendix B and C.
Information-Theoretic Necessity. Partial observability creates a coordination information gap Ii=ℐ⁡(s;ai∗|oi)I_{i}=\mathcal{I}(s;a_{i}^{*}|o_{i}), representing the optimal action information contained in the state ss that is inaccessible from local observation oio_{i}. We present a bound on this gap in the following theorem:
Theorem 5.1 (Communication Lower Bound).
Let 𝛑comm\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{comm}} be a joint policy utilizing SeqComm messages and 𝛑no-comm\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{no-comm}} be a policy restricted to local observations. For a Dec-POMDP with Lipschitz reward constant LRL_{R} (Bertsekas, 2012) and discount factor γ\gamma, the expected performance gain from communication satisfies:
𝔼⁡[V𝝅comm]−𝔼⁡[V𝝅no-comm]≥LR1−γ​∑i=1N2​ln⁡2⋅Ii⋅Var​(ai∗).\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\mathbb{E}[V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{comm}}}]-\mathbb{E}[V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{no-comm}}}]\geq\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{L_R}{1-\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot I_{i}\cdot\text{Var}(a_{i}^{*})}.$}}}
This bound proves that communication is necessary when Ii>0I_{i}>0. The Var​(ai∗)\sqrt{\text{Var}(a_{i}^{*})} term shows value scales with action exploration: when hidden state components cause optimal actions to vary, communication provides greater benefit. SeqComm’s sequential launching phase acts to reduce this gap toward zero by sharing actual actions, allowing followers to reach a Pareto-superior Stackelberg equilibrium.
Decision-Focused Advantage: Let P^\hat{P} and R^\hat{R} be the learned transition and reward models using MLE, with errors ϵP=sups,a∥P(⋅|s,a)−P^(⋅|s,a)∥1\epsilon_{P}=\sup_{s,a}\|P(\cdot|s,a)-\hat{P}(\cdot|s,a)\|_{1} and ϵR=sups,a|R⁡(s,a)−R^​(s,a)|\epsilon_{R}=\sup_{s,a}|R(s,a)-\hat{R}(s,a)|. Moreover, let ϵ=sups,a|(ℬ​Q)​(s,a)−Q⁡(s,a)|\epsilon=\sup_{s,a}|(\mathcal{B}Q)(s,a)-Q(s,a)| denote the Bellman error (Sutton and Barto, 2018), where (ℬ​Q)​(s,a)=𝔼⁡[r+γ​maxa′⁡Q⁡(s′,a′)](\mathcal{B}Q)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})]. By minimizing the Bellman error via IFT, SeqComm-DFL’s bilevel formulation yields a tighter Q∗Q^{*} approximation than MLE, as shown in the following proposition:
Proposition 5.2 (Q∗Q^{*} Approximation Error).
Let Q^MLE\hat{Q}_{\text{MLE}} and Q^DFL\hat{Q}_{\text{DFL}} be fixed points of Bellman operators induced by MLE and SeqComm-DFL. With dynamics error ϵP\epsilon_{P}, reward error ϵR\epsilon_{R}, and Bellman error ϵ\epsilon, the DFL error is tighter:

and

By optimizing the Bellman error directly, the world model ignores value-irrelevant prediction errors that otherwise degrade performance in decoupled learning.

6.1 Hospital Environment: A Dec-POMDP with Structured Information Asymmetry
We design a collaborative hospital environment that instantiates a Dec-POMDP with genuine partial observability, where communication provides measurable value.
6.1.1 Dec-POMDP Formulation
The environment has N=3N=3 specialist physician agents, each assigned one of C=3C=3 specialties (cardiology, pulmonology, neurology). The state comprises 𝒫=100\mathcal{P}=100 patients with vitals (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation), condition types κj∈{1,2,3}\kappa_{j}\in\{1,2,3\}, and hidden risk factors hj∈[0,1]3h_{j}\in[0,1]^{3} for each patient j∈𝒫j\in\mathcal{P}. Each physician i∈𝒩i\in\mathcal{N} has an action space 𝒜i={0,1,2}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{0,1,2\}, representing low, medium, and high treatment intensity, and observes oio_{i} containing only the hidden risk hj,σih_{j,\sigma_{i}} for patients matching their specialty σi∈{1,2,3}\sigma_{i}\in\{1,2,3\}. Complete details, including reward and transition structures, are in Appendix H.
6.1.2 Structured Partial Observability
The critical feature is that hidden risk factors are specialty-gated. Each patient jj has risk vector hj=[hj,1,…,hj,C]∈[0,1]Ch_{j}=[h_{j,1},\ldots,h_{j,C}]\in[0,1]^{C}, but physician ii observes only:
oi={hj,σiif specialty ​σi​ matches patient condition0otherwise (hidden)\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$o_{i}=\begin{cases}h_{j,\sigma_{i}}&\text{if specialty }\sigma_{i}\text{ matches patient condition}\\
0&\text{otherwise (hidden)}\end{cases}$}}}
This creates fundamental information asymmetry: a cardiologist observes cardiac risk hj,1h_{j,1} but cannot see neurological risk hj,3h_{j,3}, which is visible only to the neurologist. The optimal treatment for a patient with both cardiac and neurological conditions requires information from both specialists, information that can only be shared through communication.
6.1.3 Coordination-Dependent Dynamics
The specialty-gating and drug interaction dynamics place this environment in the class of transition-coupled Dec-POMDPs, where communication is theoretically required to resolve NEXP-complete dependencies to achieve optimal coordination (Gabel and Riedmiller, 2008). We illustrate the performance gap between optimal policies with and without communication in the following proposition, proved in Appendix H:
Proposition 6.1 (Communication Necessity).
Let π∗\pi^{*} denote the optimal joint policy for the hospital Dec-POMDP and πno-comm\pi^{\text{no-comm}} the optimal policy without communication. Then:
Vπ∗−Vπno-comm=Ω⁡(∑c=1C𝔼⁡[hc]⋅P⁡(ξ≠σ))\mathord{{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$V^{\pi^{*}}-V^{\pi^{\text{no-comm}}}=\Omega\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{E}[h_{c}]\cdot P(\xi\neq\sigma)\right)$}}}
(11)
where ξ\xi is the patient’s condition type and σ\sigma is the treating agent’s specialty.
The bound follows from the “blind” treatment risk penalty: without communication, agents treating mismatched patients (σi≠ξj\sigma_{i}\neq\xi_{j}) cannot learn hidden risks hj,ξjh_{j,\xi_{j}}, incurring penalties linear in risk magnitude. This establishes that the performance gap is inherent to the information structure, not an artifact of learning algorithms.
More broadly, the hospital environment exhibits partially ordered dependencies (Gabel and Riedmiller, 2008): agent ii’s optimal action depends on the private information of agent i′i^{\prime} when their patients share drug interactions. This creates a Dec-POMDP with transition-coupled agent (a class known to be NEXP-complete in general (Bernstein et al., 2002)). The specialty-gating mechanism ensures that dependencies cannot be resolved through independent learning, placing the environment in the “hard” regime where communication provides super-linear benefits.

8 Conclusion
We introduced SeqComm-DFL, integrating sequential communication with DFL for multi-agent coordination under partial observability. Our key contributions (value-aware message generation, Stackelberg sequential conditioning, and guidance potential) enable agents to learn communication protocols that directly improve downstream decision quality. Through our theoretical analysis, we provided information-theoretic bounds on communication value and convergence guarantees. Further, our experiments demonstrate substantial improvements over both baselines.
Broader Impact: SeqComm-DFL provides a principled framework for designing communication in multi-agent systems where coordination quality directly affects mission-critical outcomes. By unifying decision-focused learning with structured communication, our approach offers practitioners a theoretically-grounded method to train agents that share information strategically rather than exhaustively which is essential for bandwidth-constrained, safety-critical applications ranging from collaborative medical diagnosis to coordinated autonomous systems. The value-aware paradigm shifts communication design from information maximization to decision optimization, enabling deployable multi-agent systems that coordinate effectively under real-world information asymmetry.
Future works: Extensions include continuous action spaces, dynamic communication topology, and real-world deployment in healthcare coordination systems.

Appendix B Optimal Model Design Details
B.1 Implicit Function Theorem for Hypergradients
The hypergradient computation in SeqComm-DFL follows the Implicit Function Theorem (Krantz and Parks, 2002) (IFT) approach developed by Nikishin et al. (2022) for bilevel optimization in reinforcement learning. At the inner-loop optimum w∗​(θ)w^{*}(\theta), the gradient vanishes: ∇wℒmodel​(w∗,θ)=0\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w^{*};\theta)=0. Differentiating this optimality condition implicitly yields:
d​w∗d​θ=−[∇w​w2​ℒmodel​(w∗,θ)]−1​∇θ​w2ℒmodel​(w∗,θ)\frac{dw^{*}}{d\theta}=-\left[\nabla^{2}_{ww}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w^{*},\theta)\right]^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{\theta w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w^{*},\theta)
(12)
This IFT-based hypergradient enables differentiating through the inner optimization without backpropagating through gradient steps, avoiding vanishing/exploding gradients. The full derivation, regularity conditions (strong convexity, smoothness, Hessian invertibility), and Bellman-specific structure (I−γ​Pθ⊤​DI-\gamma P_{\theta}^{\top}D) are provided in Nikishin et al. (2022). Our contribution is extending this framework to multi-agent communication learning, where the world model must predict message-conditioned transitions.
B.2 Q∗Q^{*} Approximation Bounds
The following bounds on Q∗Q^{*} approximation error are adapted from Nikishin et al. (2022):
Theorem B.1 (Q∗Q^{*} Approximation Error).
(i) MLE bound: With dynamics error ϵP\epsilon_{P} and reward error ϵR\epsilon_{R}:
‖Q∗−Q^MLE‖∞≤ϵR1−γ+γ​ϵP​rmax2​(1−γ)2\|Q^{*}-\hat{Q}_{\text{MLE}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{R}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma\epsilon_{P}r_{\max}}{2(1-\gamma)^{2}}
(13)
Proof.
MLE Bound: Let P^\hat{P} and R^\hat{R} be the learned transition and reward models with errors ϵP=sups,a∥P(⋅|s,a)−P^(⋅|s,a)∥1\epsilon_{P}=\sup_{s,a}\|P(\cdot|s,a)-\hat{P}(\cdot|s,a)\|_{1} and ϵR=sups,a|R⁡(s,a)−R^​(s,a)|\epsilon_{R}=\sup_{s,a}|R(s,a)-\hat{R}(s,a)|.
The Bellman operator for the learned model is:
(ℬP^,R^​Q)​(s,a)=R^​(s,a)+γ​∑s′P^​(s′|s,a)​maxa′⁡Q⁡(s′,a′)(\mathcal{B}_{\hat{P},\hat{R}}Q)(s,a)=\hat{R}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\hat{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
(14)
By the triangle inequality and Bellman operator contraction property (Bertsekas, 2012):
‖Q∗−Q^MLE‖∞≤‖Q∗−ℬP^,R^​Q∗‖∞+‖ℬP^,R^​Q∗−Q^MLE‖∞\|Q^{*}-\hat{Q}_{\text{MLE}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q^{*}-\mathcal{B}_{\hat{P},\hat{R}}Q^{*}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathcal{B}_{\hat{P},\hat{R}}Q^{*}-\hat{Q}_{\text{MLE}}\|_{\infty}
(15)
The second term contracts at rate γ\gamma, while the first term (model error) satisfies:
‖Q∗−ℬP^,R^​Q∗‖∞\displaystyle\|Q^{*}-\mathcal{B}_{\hat{P},\hat{R}}Q^{*}\|_{\infty}
≤ϵR+γ​ϵP​‖Q∗‖∞\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{R}+\gamma\epsilon_{P}\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}
(16)
≤ϵR+γ​ϵP​rmax1−γ\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{R}+\gamma\epsilon_{P}\frac{r_{\text{max}}}{1-\gamma}
(17)
Solving the fixed-point inequality:
‖Q∗−Q^MLE‖∞≤ϵR1−γ+γ​ϵP​rmax2​(1−γ)2\|Q^{*}-\hat{Q}_{\text{MLE}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{R}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma\epsilon_{P}r_{\text{max}}}{2(1-\gamma)^{2}}
(18)
(ii) OMD bound: With Bellman residual error ϵ\epsilon:
‖Q∗−Q^OMD‖∞≤ϵ1−γ\|Q^{*}-\hat{Q}_{\text{OMD}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}
(19)
DFL Bound: SeqComm-DFL minimizes the Bellman residual ϵ=sups,a|(ℬ​Q)​(s,a)−Q⁡(s,a)|\epsilon=\sup_{s,a}|(\mathcal{B}Q)(s,a)-Q(s,a)| directly via the outer loop, where (ℬ​Q)​(s,a)=𝔼⁡[r+γ​maxa′⁡Q⁡(s′,a′)](\mathcal{B}Q)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})] is the Bellman operator (Sutton and Barto, 2018; Bertsekas, 2012). By the Bellman error propagation lemma:
‖Q∗−Q^DFL‖∞≤ϵ1−γ\|Q^{*}-\hat{Q}_{\text{DFL}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}
(20)
This avoids the (1−γ)−2(1-\gamma)^{-2} factor because the world model is trained to minimize TD error on decision-relevant trajectories, not raw prediction error. The key difference: MLE has (1−γ)−2(1-\gamma)^{-2} factor due to compounding transition errors, while DFL directly minimizes Bellman residual.

Appendix C Detailed Proofs
C.1 Proof of Theorem 5.1 Necessity and Value of Communication under Partial Observability
We begin by defining the information-theoretic requirements for coordination. In a Dec-POMDP, an agent’s inability to see the global state ss creates a discrepancy between its local policy and the team-optimal policy.
Definition C.1 (Coordination Information Gap).
The coordination information gap for agent ii is defined as:
Ii=ℐ⁡(s;ai∗|oi)=ℋ⁡(ai∗|oi)−ℋ⁡(ai∗|s)I_{i}=\mathcal{I}(s;a_{i}^{*}|o_{i})=\mathcal{H}(a_{i}^{*}|o_{i})-\mathcal{H}(a_{i}^{*}|s)
(21)
where ai∗a_{i}^{*} is the optimal action given full state access. IiI_{i} quantifies the information about optimal behavior contained in the global state ss that is structurally inaccessible from local observation oio_{i}.
Proposition C.2 (Communication Value Lower Bound).
Let 𝛑comm\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{comm}} be a joint policy utilizing SeqComm messages and 𝛑no-comm\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{no-comm}} be a policy restricted to local observations. For an environment with Lipschitz reward constant LRL_{R} and discount factor γ\gamma, the expected performance gain from communication satisfies:
𝔼⁡[V𝝅comm]−𝔼⁡[V𝝅no-comm]≥LR1−γ​∑i=1N2​ln⁡2⋅Ii⋅Var​(ai∗)\mathbb{E}[V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{comm}}}]-\mathbb{E}[V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{no-comm}}}]\geq\frac{L_{R}}{1-\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot I_{i}\cdot\text{Var}(a_{i}^{*})}
(22)
Proof.
The proof proceeds in five steps by connecting information gaps to policy suboptimality via concentration inequalities.
Step 1: Policy Deviation. Without communication, agent ii must select actions based only on local observation oio_{i}. Under a stochastic policy πino-comm​(a|oi)\pi^{\text{no-comm}}_{i}(a|o_{i}) (which explores to handle partial observability), the expected action deviates from the optimal policy πi∗​(a|s)\pi^{*}_{i}(a|s) which has access to full state ss. Let δi​(s)=𝔼a∼πino-comm​[a|s]−𝔼a∼πi∗​[a|s]\delta_{i}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\text{no-comm}}_{i}}[a|s]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{*}_{i}}[a|s] denote this deviation.
Step 2: TVD and Pinsker’s Inequality. By Pinsker’s inequality (Pinsker, 1964; Tsybakov, 2009):
‖πino-comm−πi∗‖TV≤12DKL(π∗i∥πno-commi)\|\pi^{\text{no-comm}}_{i}-\pi^{*}_{i}\|_{\text{TV}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{*}_{i}\|\pi^{\text{no-comm}}_{i})}
(23)
where the total variation distance (TVD) is ‖P−Q‖TV=supA|P⁡(A)−Q⁡(A)|\|P-Q\|_{\text{TV}}=\sup_{A}|P(A)-Q(A)|. The coefficient is 1/2\sqrt{1/2} because δ⁡(P,Q)≤(1/2)DKL(P∥Q)\delta(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{(1/2)D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)} in the standard form according to (Tsybakov, 2009).
Step 3: Linking KL-Divergence to Information Gap. The expected KL-divergence (with expectation over states s∼dπ∗s\sim d^{\pi^{*}}, the state distribution under optimal policy) lower bounds the information gap:
𝔼s∼dπ∗[DKL(πi∗(⋅|s)∥πino-comm(⋅|oi))]≥I(s;ai∗|oi)=ℐi\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi^{*}}}[D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{*}_{i}(\cdot|s)\|\pi^{\text{no-comm}}_{i}(\cdot|o_{i}))]\geq I(s;a^{*}_{i}|o_{i})=\mathcal{I}_{i}
(24)
This inequality follows from the data processing inequality (Csiszár and Körner, 2011): since πino-comm\pi^{\text{no-comm}}_{i} is a function of oio_{i} only (not the full state ss), the mutual information I⁡(s;ai∗|oi)I(s;a^{*}_{i}|o_{i}) quantifies the irreducible information gap between what the optimal policy needs (ss) and what the local policy has access to (oio_{i}). The KL-divergence measures how distinguishable the two policies are on average; by Pinsker’s inequality and the variational characterization of mutual information, this distinguishability must be at least as large as the information gap ℐi\mathcal{I}_{i}. Intuitively, if the policies differ significantly (large KL), it’s because the optimal policy has access to coordination-critical information in ss that oio_{i} lacks.
For discrete action spaces, the variance of the optimal action relates to TVD via (Tsybakov, 2009):
𝔼⁡[|δi​(s)|]≥2​ln⁡2⋅ℐi⋅Var​(ai∗)\mathbb{E}[|\delta_{i}(s)|]\geq\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\mathcal{I}_{i}\cdot\text{Var}(a^{*}_{i})}
(25)
Step 4: Translation to Value Difference. The reward function R⁡(s,𝐚)R(s,\mathbf{a}) is LRL_{R}-Lipschitz in the joint action space as done by 𝐚\mathbf{a} (Bertsekas, 2012). Therefore, action deviations translate to value differences:
|V𝝅∗​(s)−V𝝅no-comm​(s)|≥LR1−γ​𝔼​[‖𝐚∗−𝐚no-comm‖]|V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}}(s)-V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{no-comm}}}(s)|\geq\frac{L_{R}}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{a}^{*}-\mathbf{a}^{\text{no-comm}}\|]
(26)
where 𝔼⁡[‖𝐚∗−𝐚no-comm‖]=∑i=1N𝔼⁡[|δi​(s)|]\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{a}^{*}-\mathbf{a}^{\text{no-comm}}\|]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[|\delta_{i}(s)|] by the triangle inequality. That is, the norm of the joint action deviation is the sum of individual deviations.
Step 5: Summing over agents and noting 𝝅comm\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{comm}} can approximate 𝝅∗\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}:
𝔼⁡[V𝝅comm]−𝔼⁡[V𝝅no-comm]≥LR1−γ​∑i=1N2​ln⁡2⋅ℐi⋅Var​(ai∗)\mathbb{E}[V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{comm}}}]-\mathbb{E}[V^{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{no-comm}}}]\geq\frac{L_{R}}{1-\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\mathcal{I}_{i}\cdot\text{Var}(a^{*}_{i})}
(27)
∎
Intuition for Readers: This bound proves that communication is not just "helpful" but mathematically necessary when Ii>0I_{i}>0. In our Hospital benchmark, IiI_{i} is high because specific drug interaction risks are specialty-gated; the cardiologist literally cannot see the information required for the optimal team decision without the message from the neurologist.
C.2 Proof of Theorem 5.3 (Convergence)
To establish the convergence of the bilevel SeqComm-DFL optimization, we first define the necessary regularity conditions. These ensure the stability of the Implicit Function Theorem (IFT) application and the reliability of the Conjugate Gradient (CG) solver.
Assumption C.3 (Regularity Conditions).
We assume the following properties for the model-based loss ℒmodel​(w,θ)\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w;\theta) and the true environment loss ℒtrue​(w,θ)\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(w;\theta):
1.
Strong Convexity: ℒmodel​(w,θ)\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}}(w;\theta) is μ\mu-strongly convex in ww for a fixed θ\theta.
2.
Smoothness: Both ℒmodel\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}} and ℒtrue\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}} are LL-smooth in ww and θ\theta.
3.
Bounded Gradients: ‖∇θℒtrue‖\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}\| and ‖∇wℒtrue‖\|\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}\| are bounded by GG.
4.
Lipschitz Hessian: The Hessian ∇w​w2ℒmodel\nabla^{2}_{ww}\mathcal{L}_{\text{model}} is ρ\rho-Lipschitz with respect to θ\theta.
Innovation: Unlike standard model-based reinforcement learning, SeqComm-DFL must account for the fact that we do not solve the inner loop (critic training) or the linear system (CG) perfectly. We decompose the hypergradient estimation error ϵ\epsilon into two components: ϵinner\epsilon_{\text{inner}} and ϵCG\epsilon_{\text{CG}} .
Proposition C.4 (Hypergradient Estimation Error Decomposition).
With KK inner iterations and KCGK_{\text{CG}} CG iterations:
‖∇θℒtrue^−∇θℒtrue‖≤ρ​Gμ​‖w(K)−w∗‖+(κ−1κ+1)KCG​G\|\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}}-\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}\|\leq\frac{\rho G}{\mu}\|w^{(K)}-w^{*}\|+\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{K_{\text{CG}}}G
(28)
where κ=(L+λ)/(μ+λ)\kappa=(L+\lambda)/(\mu+\lambda) represents the damped Hessian condition number.
Proof of Theorem 5.3.
Step 1: Descent Lemma. By LL-smoothness of ℒtrue\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}} (Assumption C.3), the standard descent lemma (Bertsekas, 2012) gives:
ℒtrue​(θ(t+1))≤ℒtrue​(θ(t))−ηθ​⟨∇θℒtrue​(θ(t)),g(t)⟩+L​ηθ22​‖g(t)‖2\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t+1)})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)})-\eta_{\theta}\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)}),g^{(t)}\rangle+\frac{L\eta_{\theta}^{2}}{2}\|g^{(t)}\|^{2}
(29)
where θ(t+1)=θ(t)−ηθ​g(t)\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\eta_{\theta}g^{(t)} is the parameter update. This inequality captures the one-step decrease in loss: the first term is the linear decrease from descending along g(t)g^{(t)}, while the second term is the quadratic penalty from curvature. Our goal is to relate the inner product ⟨∇θℒtrue,g(t)⟩\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}},g^{(t)}\rangle to the squared gradient norm ‖∇θℒtrue‖2\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}\|^{2} to establish convergence.
Step 2: Bias Decomposition. Let ϵ(t)=‖g(t)−g¯(t)‖\epsilon^{(t)}=\|g^{(t)}-\bar{g}^{(t)}\| be the hypergradient estimation error from Proposition C.4. By Cauchy-Schwarz:
⟨∇θℒtrue​(θ(t)),g(t)⟩\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)}),g^{(t)}\rangle
=⟨g¯(t),g¯(t)⟩+⟨g¯(t),g(t)−g¯(t)⟩\displaystyle=\langle\bar{g}^{(t)},\bar{g}^{(t)}\rangle+\langle\bar{g}^{(t)},g^{(t)}-\bar{g}^{(t)}\rangle
(30)
≥‖g¯(t)‖2−‖g¯(t)‖⋅ϵ(t)\displaystyle\geq\|\bar{g}^{(t)}\|^{2}-\|\bar{g}^{(t)}\|\cdot\epsilon^{(t)}
(31)
≥‖∇θℒtrue​(θ(t))‖2−G​ϵ(t)\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)})\|^{2}-G\epsilon^{(t)}
(32)
where GG is the gradient bound from Assumption C.3.
Step 3: Rearrange and Bound. Substituting into Step 1 and using ‖g(t)‖≤G+ϵ(t)\|g^{(t)}\|\leq G+\epsilon^{(t)}:
‖∇θℒtrue​(θ(t))‖2\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)})\|^{2}
≤ℒtrue​(θ(t))−ℒtrue​(θ(t+1))ηθ+G​ϵ(t)+L​ηθ2​(G+ϵ(t))2\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t+1)})}{\eta_{\theta}}+G\epsilon^{(t)}+\frac{L\eta_{\theta}}{2}(G+\epsilon^{(t)})^{2}
(33)
Step 4: Telescope and Average. Summing over iterations t=0,…,T−1t=0,\ldots,T-1 and taking expectations 𝔼⁡[⋅]\mathbb{E}[\cdot] over the randomness in gradient estimation and data sampling:
1T​∑t=0T−1𝔼​‖∇θℒtrue​(θ(t))‖2\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)})\|^{2}
≤𝔼⁡[ℒtrue​(θ(0))]−𝔼⁡[ℒtrue​(θ(T))]T​ηθ+G​ϵ¯+L​ηθ2​(G+ϵ¯)2\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(0)})]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(T)})]}{T\eta_{\theta}}+G\bar{\epsilon}+\frac{L\eta_{\theta}}{2}(G+\bar{\epsilon})^{2}
(34)
where ϵ¯=1T​∑t𝔼⁡[ϵ(t)]\bar{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{(t)}]. Since ℒtrue≥0\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}\geq 0 and with learning rate ηθ=O⁡(1/T)\eta_{\theta}=O(1/\sqrt{T}) and error ϵ¯=O⁡(1/T)\bar{\epsilon}=O(1/\sqrt{T}) (from Proposition C.4):
1T​∑t=0T−1𝔼​‖∇θℒtrue​(θ(t))‖2=O⁡(1/T)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}(\theta^{(t)})\|^{2}=O(1/\sqrt{T})
(35)
This establishes the O⁡(1/T)O(1/\sqrt{T}) convergence rate for the expected squared gradient norm.
∎
Note to readers: Despite the complexity of differentiating through sequential communication protocols and endogenous uncertainty, the framework achieves the standard O⁡(1/T)O(1/\sqrt{T}) convergence rate of first-order methods.

Appendix H Hospital Environment Details
The transition dynamics and rewards explicitly depend on cross-agent information:
Blind Treatment Risk: Treating a patient without knowledge of hidden risks incurs penalty:
ρblind,i=1.5⋅(1−𝟙[σi=κj])⋅hj,κj⋅ai\rho_{\text{blind},i}=1.5\cdot(1-\mathbbm{1}[\sigma_{i}=\kappa_{j}])\cdot h_{j,\kappa_{j}}\cdot a_{i}
(36)
A non-matching specialist treating aggressively (ai=2a_{i}=2) when hidden risk hj,κjh_{j,\kappa_{j}} is high suffers large penalties. Communication allows agents to warn teammates about high-risk patients.
Drug Interaction Penalty. Simultaneous high-intensity treatments can cause adverse interactions:
ρdrug=1.5∑i<j𝟙[ai=2]⋅𝟙[aj=2]⋅Dκi,κj\rho_{\text{drug}}=1.5\sum_{i<j}\mathbbm{1}[a_{i}=2]\cdot\mathbbm{1}[a_{j}=2]\cdot D_{\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}}
(37)
where Dκi,κj∈[0,1]C×CD_{\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}}\in[0,1]^{C\times C} is a drug interaction matrix. This penalty requires agents to coordinate treatment intensities—impossible without communication about intended actions.
Resource Budget. Limited high-intensity resources require prioritization:
ρresource=0.5⋅max(0,∑i𝟙[ai=2]−B)\rho_{\text{resource}}=0.5\cdot\max\left(0,\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}[a_{i}=2]-B\right)
(38)

R=∑i(Δvi+0.4⋅𝟙[σi=κi]−0.6⋅Δci−3⋅𝟙[vi′>0.85])−ρdrug−ρresourceR=\sum_{i}\left(\Delta v_{i}+0.4\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\sigma_{i}=\kappa_{i}]-0.6\cdot\Delta c_{i}-3\cdot\mathbbm{1}[v_{i}^{\prime}>0.85]\right)-\rho_{\text{drug}}-\rho_{\text{resource}}
(39)

Proof.
The hospital environment reward decomposes as:

Consider the blind treatment penalty:
ρblind,i=1.5⋅(1−𝟙[σi=ξj])⋅hj,ξj⋅ai\rho_{\text{blind},i}=1.5\cdot(1-\mathbbm{1}[\sigma_{i}=\xi_{j}])\cdot h_{j,\xi_{j}}\cdot a_{i}

Step 1: Expected Penalty Without Communication.
Without communication, agent ii cannot observe hj,ξjh_{j,\xi_{j}} when σi≠ξj\sigma_{i}\neq\xi_{j}. 𝔼πno-comm​[ρblind,i]=1.5⋅P⁡(σi≠ξj)⋅𝔼⁡[hj,ξj]⋅𝔼⁡[ai]\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{\text{no-comm}}}[\rho_{\text{blind},i}]=1.5\cdot P(\sigma_{i}\neq\xi_{j})\cdot\mathbb{E}[h_{j,\xi_{j}}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[a_{i}]
(42)
ß

With optimal communication π∗\pi^{*}, agent ii receives message mjm_{j} encoding hj,ξjh_{j,\xi_{j}} from the specialist matching patient jj’s condition. The agent can then avoid aggressive treatment (ai<2a_{i}<2) when hj,ξjh_{j,\xi_{j}} is high, reducing:
𝔼π∗​[ρblind,i]≈0(avoids blind treatment)\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{*}}[\rho_{\text{blind},i}]\approx 0\quad\text{(avoids blind treatment)}
(43)

Summing over all agents and condition types:
Vπ∗−Vπno-comm\displaystyle V^{\pi^{*}}-V^{\pi^{\text{no-comm}}}
≥∑i=1N𝔼πno-comm​[ρblind,i]−𝔼π∗​[ρblind,i]\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{\text{no-comm}}}[\rho_{\text{blind},i}]-\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{*}}[\rho_{\text{blind},i}]
(44)
≥∑c=1C1.5⋅P⁡(ξ≠σ)⋅𝔼⁡[hc]⋅a¯\displaystyle\geq\sum_{c=1}^{C}1.5\cdot P(\xi\neq\sigma)\cdot\mathbb{E}[h_{c}]\cdot\bar{a}

where a¯>0\bar{a}>0 is the average action intensity. This establishes the lower bound.
