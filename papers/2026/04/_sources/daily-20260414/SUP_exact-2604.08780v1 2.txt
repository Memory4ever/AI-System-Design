Abstract
World models promise a paradigm shift in robotics, where an agent learns the underlying physics of its environment once to enable efficient planning and behavior learning. However, current world models are often hardware-locked specialists: a model trained on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot fails catastrophically on a Unitree Go1 due to the mismatch in kinematic and dynamic properties, as the model overfits to specific embodiment constraints rather than capturing the universal locomotion dynamics. Consequently, a slight change in actuator dynamics or limb length necessitates training a new model from scratch. In this work, we take a step towards a framework for training a generalizable Quadrupedal World Model (QWM) that disentangles environmental dynamics from robot morphology. We address the limitations of implicit system identification, where treating static physical properties (like mass or limb length) as latent variables to be inferred from motion history creates an adaptation lag that can compromise zero-shot safety and efficiency. Instead, we explicitly condition the generative dynamics on the robot’s engineering specifications. By integrating a physical morphology encoder and a reward normalizer, we enable the model to serve as a neural simulator capable of generalizing across morphologies. This capability unlocks zero-shot control across a range of embodiments. Since the policy is conditioned on generalizable latent dynamics provided by the world model, we can deploy the agent on entirely unseen quadrupeds without fine-tuning, adaptation, or warm-up periods. We introduce, for the first time, a world model that enables zero-shot generalization to new morphologies for locomotion. While we carefully study the limitations of our method—QWM operates as a distribution-bounded interpolator within the quadrupedal morphology family rather than a universal physics engine—this work represents a significant step toward morphology-conditioned world models for legged locomotion.
I Introduction
The paradigm of reinforcement learning (RL) has fundamentally changed how we approach autonomous control by enabling agents to learn through interactions with their environment [35]. However, this success comes with a significant caveat: the “Hardware Lottery.” Standard policies and World Models (WMs) are deeply overfit to the specific kinematic and dynamic properties of the robot they were trained on. Although massive parallel simulators like Isaac Sim [52, 48] or Genesis [3] mitigate the wall-clock time bottleneck, it remains computationally expensive to train individual experts for every robot variation. Worse, upgrading a robot’s motors, changing a limb length, or deploying across a heterogeneous fleet currently requires collecting millions of new samples and training a new model from scratch [77, 36]. Moreover, domain randomization introduces safety risks from unexpected policy behaviors under distribution shift [13].
Model-Based RL (MBRL) offers a compelling solution to this data bottleneck: by learning a predictive model of the underlying dynamics—i.e., a world model—an agent can simulate future outcomes and learn behaviors entirely within imagination [61, 70].
Despite their immense success in video games [29, 60] and specific control tasks [27, 28], WMs in robotics have largely remained specialists [70, 19, 50, 41]. A WM trained to control a heavy, industrial quadruped cannot control a lightweight, agile robot as the internal dynamics model overfits to the specific kinematic and dynamic properties of the training robot. This lack of transferability stands in stark contrast to other fields of AI, such as Natural Language Processing or Computer Vision, where generalizable models can adapt to a wide array of tasks and contexts without retraining [6, 18].
We argue that for WMs to become a foundational tool in robotics, they must transition from hardware-specific specialists to physics-grounded generalists. Aligning with the recent roadmap for Type III: Robotic Agents [23], a generalizable WM should serve as a Physical WM capable of capturing the fundamental physics of locomotion—gravity, contact forces, and momentum—independent of the specific robot being controlled. It should understand not just how one robot moves, but how any robot with a given morphology moves.

In this work, we take the first step towards a framework for training a generalizable Quadrupedal World Model (QWM) (demonstrated in Fig. 1) tailored for MBRL. Our key insight is that while the laws of physics are universal, the embodiment (morphology) varies. Current multi-task approaches often rely on implicit system identification, forcing the agent to infer physical properties like limb length or mass distribution solely from interaction history [37]. We posit that blindly inferring morphology from history is not only inefficient but unsafe for zero-shot transfer. Implicit methods create an “adaptation lag”: a robot must move (potentially dangerously) to generate enough history to identify itself before it can learn effective control policies. In contrast, robot morphology is not a latent variable to be discovered, but a known engineering specification available in the robot model, which is normally transcriped into Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) or Universal Scene Description (USD). By extracting explicit physical features directly from USD files and normalizing them, we provide the model with a grounded structural context, allowing it to focus its latent capacity on dynamics rather than static identification.
To effectively integrate this morphological context, we introduce architectural innovations atop the DreamerV3 backbone [29]. First, we integrate the static morphological features into the high-frequency proprioceptive data for the encoder and the WM’s dynamic model, using a specific fusion architecture that prevents static context from being washed out by dynamic observations. Second, to handle the heterogeneity of diverse embodiments, we utilize a reward normalizer that dynamically balances learning signals across robots with varying physical scales, reward functions, and weights.
These contributions enable a transformative capability: zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer in simulation, and real robot. Notably, our policy operates solely on the latent states generated by the WM, never processing raw observations directly. Therefore, at deployment, the frozen WM functions as a real-time “physics adapter”: it uses the explicit morphology parameters to translate the unseen robot’s specific dynamics into the standardized latent space that the policy understands. Consequently, we can control a diverse set of robot configurations (via their USDs) that differ in limb lengths or mass distributions using a single frozen WM and policy, achieving successful locomotion immediately across the fleet.
To the best of our knowledge, in MBRL and WM literature, this is the first method to demonstrate such generalizable capabilities, eliminating the need for dangerous real robot warm-up periods. More precisely, QWM is framed as a latent parameter-conditioned dynamics model: we provide explicit context that steers the recurrent imagination into the correct morphology-specific dynamics manifold, enabling zero-shot transfer within the quadrupedal family without requiring online system identification.
II Related Work
World Models and Model-Based Reinforcement Learning.
MBRL improves sample efficiency by learning predictive environment models [61, 49]. While early methods like PILCO [17] utilized Gaussian processes for data efficiency, modern approaches plan in latent spaces [26, 27, 28, 29]. The Dreamer family [27, 28] demonstrated that policies can be learned entirely in imagination, with DreamerV3 [29] adding robustness via symlog predictions and KL balancing. Theoretical work confirms general agents inherently require WMs [55].
While the Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM) [27] remains standard for speed, recent architectures utilize Transformers [11, 16], discrete autoencoders for sample efficiency [45, 46], stochastic transformers [82], or contrastive coding [8]. Others leverage linear attention or SSMs for efficiency [66, 67].
Generative models now blend video generation and control: Genie [7] simulates interactive environments from unlabeled video, Diamond [2] replaces discrete latents with diffusion processes, UVA [42] proposes unified video and action generation, and Pandora [73] enables language-controlled video simulation. However, these often suffer from high computational costs. We retain the RSSM to prioritize real-time inference and compact dynamics.
In robotics, DayDreamer [70] enabled online learning, while subsequent works tackle long-horizon prediction via dual-autoregressive training [41] and epistemic uncertainty estimation for offline-to-online adaptation [40], plus visual reconstruction [38, 79]. Specific applications include distilling privileged WMs for sim-to-real transfer [76], humanoid terrain traversal via Transformer-XL [84], and manipulation [22, 4]. WMs also enable safety [31], exploration [57, 54], and novelty detection [85].
Multi-Task and Transfer Learning.
Scaling often relies on implicit identification: TD-MPC2 [30] learns latent task embeddings across 80 tasks, while PWM [24] enables fast policy extraction via first-order gradient optimization through differentiable WMs. Other approaches leverage offline adaptation [83, 81, 21]. In locomotion, methods like ManyQuadrupeds [59] and GenLoco [20] use analytical priors like Inverse Kinematics (IK). Unlike these reactive policies which require heuristic tuning, QWM operates in joint space by mapping explicit physical descriptions (USD) to dynamics, enabling planning without analytical IK. Similarly, VOCALoco [71] employs a modular skill-selection framework that dynamically adapts locomotion strategies based on terrain perception, demonstrating complementary approaches to adaptive quadrupedal control.
Video WMs explore generalization [34, 44, 51, 63, 10], but face optimization hurdles. Unlike complex loss balancing [43, 39], we employ a lightweight Per-Robot Reward EMA to normalize heterogeneous scales across morphologies.
Morphology Representation.
To generalize across diverse robot embodiments, prior work has largely focused on architectural biases. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) explicitly model the kinematic tree [65, 69, 32], often incorporating symmetry constraints [74, 68] or heterogeneous node types [9]. Similarly, Transformer-based methods tokenize the kinematic chain [25, 5, 72, 62, 58], enabling scaling to thousands of procedural embodiments [1]. Alternatively, implicit methods infer morphology from interaction history [37, 78, 53] or learn aligned latent action spaces [64, 34, 41, 80].
While robust, implicit methods require adaptation time (warm-up) and fail to utilize static kinematic information available prior to deployment. Comprehensive surveys on embodied AI [23] identify embodiment generalization as a critical capability for general-purpose robotic agents, yet note that preparing a robot for every scenario at deployment time is infeasible. Our approach addresses this by leveraging explicit parametric encoding [12, 47, 75]. Unlike prior works that rely on raw USD values or model-free RL, we compute normalized, scale-invariant kinematic ratios and integrate them directly into the WM’s encoder, enabling zero-shot adaptation without the computational overhead of GNNs.
III Background
We formulate the problem of generalizable quadrupedal locomotion as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) conditioned on a morphology parameter μ∈ℳ\mu\in\mathcal{M}. The set ℳ\mathcal{M} encompasses the design space of quadrupedal embodiments, where each element μ\mu is a fixed vector of normalized engineering constraints (such as geometry and mass properties) that distinguishes one robot hardware profile from another. The environment is defined by the tuple (𝒮,𝒪,𝒜,𝒫μ,ℛ,γ)(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}_{\mu},\mathcal{R},\gamma), where 𝒮\mathcal{S} represents the underlying physics state of the simulator (e.g., generalized coordinates, velocities, and contact forces), which is not fully observable to the agent. Instead, at time tt, the agent receives partial observations ot∈𝒪o_{t}\in\mathcal{O} consisting of proprioceptive data: joint positions qtq_{t}, joint velocities q˙t\dot{q}_{t}, base linear velocity vtv_{t}, angular velocity ωt\omega_{t}, projected gravity gtg_{t}, velocity commands ctc_{t}, and previous actions at−1∈𝒜a_{t-1}\in\mathcal{A}. The action space 𝒜∈ℝ12\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{12} comprises continuous joint position targets. Inherently, the transition dynamics 𝒫μ​(st+1∣st,at)\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t}) depend on the robot morphology μ\mu, which encodes physical properties such as kinematic constraints, mass distributions, and actuator limits.
III-A Recurrent State-Space Models
Our WM backbone is based on the RSSM introduced in the Dreamer family of algorithms [27, 28, 29]. RSSMs enable learning in partially observable environments by decomposing the latent state into two components: a deterministic recurrent state hth_{t} that aggregates history (short-term memory), and a stochastic state ztz_{t} that represents information about the current observation (spatial representation).

The dynamics evolve through the Recurrent Model (Eq. 1), which updates the memory hth_{t} based on the previous action. During training, the Representation Model (Eq. 2) infers the posterior state ztz_{t} using the real observation oto_{t}, whereas during planning, the Transition Predictor (Eq. 3) predicts the prior z^t\hat{z}_{t} solely from history. To ground these latents, the model reconstructs observations via the Decoder (Eq. 4) and predicts training signals via the Reward & Continuation heads (Eq. 5).

We adopt the DreamerV3 [29] architecture, which utilizes symlog predictions to compress the magnitude of rewards and observations, and KL balancing to prevent posterior collapse, ensuring robust training across diverse scales.
III-B Learning Policy in Imagination
A key advantage of WMs is the decoupling of policy learning from physical interaction. The policy πθ​(at|ht,zt)\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|h_{t},z_{t}) and critic vψ​(vt|ht,zt)v_{\psi}(v_{t}|h_{t},z_{t})—which models the distribution of returns to handle varying scales [29]—are trained entirely on imagined trajectories rolled out by the transition predictor. Starting from an initial state, the model interacts with itself:
at∼πθ(⋅∣ht,z^t),ht+1=fϕ(ht,z^t,at),z^t+1∼pϕ(⋅∣ht+1)a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t},\hat{z}_{t}),\;h_{t+1}=f_{\phi}(h_{t},\hat{z}_{t},a_{t}),\;\hat{z}_{t+1}\sim p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid h_{t+1})
Because the policy interacts only with the model’s latent dynamics, if the WM captures the underlying physics of a new robot correctly, the policy can adapt zero-shot without the risk of hardware damage inherent to methods that require online system identification or real-world fine-tuning.
III-C The Challenge of Cross-Morphology Generalization
The core challenge in this work is learning a single set of parameters ϕ\phi that approximates the dynamics 𝒫μ\mathcal{P}_{\mu} for a distribution of diverse morphologies μ∈ℳ\mu\in\mathcal{M}.
Implicit System Identification.
Most multi-task RL methods [37, 30] treat morphology as a hidden variable. They rely on the recurrent state hth_{t} to implicitly infer physical properties (e.g., limb length, friction, mass) from the history of interactions (ht−1h_{t-1}):
ht=fϕ​(ht−1,zt−1,at−1)h_{t}=f_{\phi}(h_{t-1},z_{t-1},a_{t-1})
In this paradigm, the RNN must essentially perform system identification online. This has two major drawbacks: (1) Adaptation Lag: The agent behaves sub-optimally at the start of an episode (t=0t=0) until sufficient history is accumulated to infer the morphology. (2) Entanglement: The latent state hth_{t} must simultaneously encode the dynamic state of the robot (velocity, orientation) and its static physical properties, potentially leading to interference and capacity issues.
These fundamental limitations motivate the need for an alternative approach. While implicit system identification has proven effective for single-robot adaptation and short-horizon tasks, it becomes a bottleneck when scaling to diverse morphologies and zero-shot transfer. The question then becomes: Can we design a WM that leverages available structural information about the robot to disentangle morphology-specific dynamics from universal physics, enabling true generalization across embodiments?
IV Quadrupedal World Model
We build upon the DreamerV3 [29], modifying the encoder and transition dynamics to explicitly condition on physical structure. Our framework, QWM, illustrated in Fig. 1, consists of three core additional components: (1) an explicit Physical Morphology Encoder (PME) that derives scale-invariant features from USD files; (2) a Morphology-Conditioned WM that fuses static structural data with dynamic proprioception; and (3) an Adaptive Reward Normalization (ARN) scheme to stabilize learning across heterogeneous hardware.
IV-A Explicit Morphology Encoding
To generalize across diverse embodiments, the WM must disentangle the agent’s static physical properties from its dynamic state. Rather than treating morphology as a latent variable to be inferred from interaction history [37], we formalize it as a deterministic mapping Φ:𝒰→ℝd\Phi:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d} from the robot’s USD representation 𝒰\mathcal{U} to a normalized feature vector μ\mu. We extract a set of physical parameters directly from the USD stage that explicitly parameterize the rigid-body dynamics, decomposing the robot specification into kinematic, geometric, and dynamic properties.
Following prior work [12, 47, 75], we select features that parameterize the equations of motion. These capture critical variations in leverage, stability margins, and rotational inertia—enabling the model to distinguish, for example, the high-center-of-mass Boston Dynamics Spot from the compact Unitree Go1 (see App. C).
Kinematics & Topology. Variations in limb length and joint orientation fundamentally alter the leverage and workspace of locomotion. Following established approaches in morphology-aware control [65, 32], we extract the fundamental link dimensions from the front-left leg chain by traversing the rigid body hierarchy. Let lhipl_{\text{hip}}, lthighl_{\text{thigh}}, and lshankl_{\text{shank}} denote the hip offset and segment lengths, and kcfg∈{0,1}k_{\text{cfg}}\in\{0,1\} denote the knee configuration style:
μkin=[lhip,lthigh,lshank,kcfg]\mu_{\text{kin}}=\left[l_{\text{hip}},\quad l_{\text{thigh}},\quad l_{\text{shank}},\quad k_{\text{cfg}}\right]
(6)
kcfgk_{\text{cfg}} explicitly distinguishes topological families (e.g., ANYmal’s inward-bending “X-config” vs. Unitree’s “dog-like” knees shown in Fig. 2), preventing the need to infer joint axis orientation from history.
Geometry. The stance footprint dictates the robot’s stability polygon and base of support. We compute the nominal hip positions by extracting the child body positions of each leg’s Hip Abduction/Adduction (HAA) joint. From these four contact points, we define:
μgeo=[lstance,wstance,lstancewstance]\mu_{\text{geo}}=\left[l_{\text{stance}},\quad w_{\text{stance}},\quad\frac{l_{\text{stance}}}{w_{\text{stance}}}\right]
where lstancel_{\text{stance}} and wstancew_{\text{stance}} are the longitudinal and lateral extents of the support polygon. The aspect ratio lstance/wstancel_{\text{stance}}/w_{\text{stance}} provides a scale-invariant characterization of the support geometry: higher ratios (e.g., Spot at 5.42) indicate narrow-bodied designs, while lower ratios (e.g., ANYmal-B at 2.39) represent more square-stance configurations.
Dynamics. The distribution of mass dictates the system’s rotational inertia and response to external forces. To handle the orders-of-magnitude difference between lightweight robots (e.g., Unitree A1 ≈12\approx 12 kg) and heavier ones (e.g., Unitree B2 ≈80\approx 80 kg), we utilize a logarithmic scaling for total mass. We further characterize the mass distribution via the trunk mass ratio, computed as the ratio of the heaviest rigid body (base link) to total mass:
μdyn=[log(1+Mtotal),mtrunkMtotal]\mu_{\text{dyn}}=\left[\log(1+M_{\text{total}}),\quad\frac{m_{\text{trunk}}}{M_{\text{total}}}\right]
where Mtotal=∑miM_{\text{total}}=\sum m_{i} aggregates all link masses with mi>0.005m_{i}>0.005 kg to filter sensor dummy bodies.
Actuation. Actuator capabilities vary significantly across robot platforms, and policies must account for these differences to enable cross-embodiment transfer [33, 37]. We therefore extract the torque density τspec\tau_{\text{spec}} from effort limits, representing the robot’s specific force capacity:

where NjN_{j} denotes the total number of actuated joints, τmax(i)\tau_{\text{max}}^{(i)} is the maximum effort of the it​hi^{th} joint, and g0=9.81g_{0}=9.81 m/s2 is the standard gravitational acceleration.
Finally, the resulting raw feature vector μraw=[μkin,μgeo,μdyn,μact]\mu_{\text{raw}}=[\mu_{\text{kin}},\mu_{\text{geo}},\mu_{\text{dyn}},\mu_{\text{act}}] contains values with heterogeneous physical units and magnitudes. To ensure well-conditioned gradients within the neural network encoder, we apply min-max normalization Ψ\Psi based on the training cohort statistics, bounding features to [−1,1][-1,1] and providing QWM with a numerically stable embedding that effectively characterizes diverse morphologies. Note that these different morphologies exhibit vastly different converged mean rewards due to their physical scales and reward function definitions: Boston Dynamics Spot achieves approximately 350, ANYmal variants (B, C, D) converge around 25, Unitree quadrupeds (A1, Go1, Go2) around 40, and Unitree B2 around 15. Importantly, μ\mu encodes idealized rigid-body specifications extracted from the USD file. Unmodeled real-world residuals—such as actuator backlash and friction variability—are handled implicitly by the dynamic latents (zt,ht)(z_{t},h_{t}) trained under domain randomization (App. A), which provides a continuous channel to absorb discrepancies between the spec sheet and physical hardware. This is precisely why QWM transfers to real robots without fine-tuning despite the sim-to-real gap, and why the model exhibits robustness to minor inaccuracies in the provided μ\mu.
IV-B Morphology-Conditioned World Model
To effectively utilize the extracted morphology vector μ\mu, we modify the standard RSSM architecture. A naive concatenation of static features μ\mu with high-frequency observations oto_{t} often leads to the static signal being “washed out” by the high-variance dynamic data. To mitigate this, we employ a dual-tower encoder architecture in which the observation encoder is split into two pathways. The dynamic tower processes the proprioceptive stream oto_{t} via an MLP. Simultaneously, the static tower processes the morphology vector μ\mu via a separate MLP to project it into the same latent dimension. These embeddings are then concatenated and fused:
et=Linear​(concat​(MLPd​y​n​(ot),MLPs​t​a​t​(μ)))e_{t}=\text{Linear}(\text{concat}(\text{MLP}_{dyn}(o_{t}),\text{MLP}_{stat}(\mu)))
This ensures that the encoder allocates dedicated capacity to representing the robot’s physical structure before fusing it with the state.
Notably, the morphology μ\mu is not just used for encoding. It is also injected directly into the recurrent dynamics. We augment Eq. 1 transition function to explicitly depend on the robot type:
ht=fϕ​(ht−1,zt−1,at−1,μ)h_{t}=f_{\phi}(h_{t-1},z_{t-1},a_{t-1},\mu)
By providing μ\mu to the recurrent model at every time step, we relieve the RNN of the burden of memorizing static physical properties in its short-term memory hth_{t}. This disentanglement allows the recurrent state to focus purely on the dynamic state of the system (e.g., velocity, contact timing) while the explicit conditioning handles the static physics (e.g., limb length, mass), facilitating robust control across distinct morphologies.
IV-C Heterogeneous Reward Normalization
Training a single generalizable agent across a set of heterogeneous robots introduces a significant optimization challenge: reward scaling. While all robots in our cohort share the high-level objective of velocity-tracking locomotion, their specific reward formulations and penalty weights differ significantly to accommodate their distinct hardware (detailed in App. A). For instance, a torque penalty of 100 Nm is standard for a heavy ANYmal-D robot but would indicate catastrophic failure for a lightweight Unitree A1.
Crucially, the WM learns to predict rewards alongside dynamics. If trained on raw, unnormalized rewards, the WM’s loss function would be dominated by robots with larger reward scales (e.g., Spot), causing it to over-prioritize their dynamics while under-fitting to robots with sparser signals (e.g., Unitree B2). While DreamerV3’s SymLog [29] compresses global magnitudes, it does not equalize the relative learning signal density across tasks. To address this, we implement an Adaptive Reward Normalization (ARN) using quantile-based scaling. Rather than using a fixed scalar, we track the 5​th5\textsuperscript{th} and 95​th95\textsuperscript{th} percentiles of the returns independently for each robot type using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) with momentum α\alpha. The reward scale σR\sigma_{R} is derived from the difference between these quantiles. In our setting, this mechanism acts as an adaptive equalizer: if a robot consistently generates large torque penalties, its specific σR\sigma_{R} expands, dynamically scaling the signal down.
This ensures that QWM receives a normalized learning signal consistent across morphologies, preventing any single embodiment from dominating the latent representation and enabling stable, simultaneous multi-robot training. ARN serves as a practical training stabilizer rather than a guarantee of reward equivalence across morphologies. It reliably prevents gradient domination by high-reward embodiments (e.g., Spot) without imposing identical effective reward scales:
σR←α​σR+(1−α)​(P95−P05),rnorm=rmax⁡(1.0,σR)\sigma_{R}\!\leftarrow\!\alpha\sigma_{R}+(1-\alpha)(P_{95}-P_{05}),\;r_{\text{norm}}\!=\!\frac{r}{\max(1.0,\sigma_{R})}
V Experiments
Fig. 2: The heterogeneous morphology cohort used in our experiments, illustrating the variance in physical scale and configuration. To demonstrate true generalization, we train QWM on seven robots while holding out one robot for zero-shot evaluation.
Our experiments aim to validate three central hypotheses: (1) Universal Mastery: QWM can master locomotion across a heterogeneous fleet of quadrupeds with a single set of weights. Unlike standard RL approaches that often require distinct hyperparameter tuning for different embodiments, QWM uses a unified configuration to match the performance of specialist experts. (2) Zero-Shot Generalization: By explicitly conditioning on physical morphology, QWM can synthesize successful control policies for the unseen robot without any simulation interaction. (3) Physical Transfer: The learned dynamics and policies transfer to real-world hardware across vastly different mass and size scales.
Unless otherwise noted, in our experiments, we train QWM on the complete heterogeneous robot cohort. We provide ablation studies on the contribution of each component introduced in this work (PME, ARN, and Explicit Recurrent Conditioning), available in App. D.
V-A Heterogeneous Simulation Infrastructure
We utilize NVIDIA Isaac Lab [48], built upon Isaac Sim [52], as our simulation backbone. While Isaac Lab offers massive parallelism via GPU-accelerated PhysX, its default vectorization paradigm is designed for homogeneous setups, spawning thousands of identical clones of a single robot asset. This standard setup is insufficient for training a generalizable agent across multiple robots simultaneously, as it typically restricts the training loop to a single morphology per run.
To overcome this limitation, we developed a heterogeneous environment on top of Isaac Lab, Hetero-Isaac [15, 14].
Unlike standard vectorization, Hetero-Isaac manages a heterogeneous batch where the robot type is indexed per environment ID, allowing the replay buffer to collect a mixed distribution of morphologies in a single forward pass. By managing unique USD paths and physics tensors, we construct a diverse morphology cohort including the ANYmal (B, C, D), Unitree (Go1, Go2, A1, B2), and Boston Dynamics Spot (Fig. 2). Importantly, this cohort is not merely a collection of scaled variants, but it captures fundamental kinematic shifts. The robots span distinct joint topologies, ranging from the “X-configuration” of the ANYmals (where knees bend inwards towards the chassis) to the standard mammalian “dog-like” configuration of the Spot and Unitree series. Furthermore, the hip-to-foot offsets and link length ratios vary non-linearly across the fleet. This structural diversity prevents the agent from relying on a single gait geometry, forcing the WM to capture the distinct underlying dynamics of each kinematic chain.

Furthermore, it manages the complexity of heterogeneous reward definitions. V-B Multi-Morphology Mastery
First, we evaluate whether QWM can effectively handle the interference arising from simultaneous training. We compare against several baselines: Vanilla PPO [56], a single PPO agent trained on the full heterogeneous batch representing a model-free baseline, and PME-PPO, which directly utilizes our PME module to condition the model-free policy on μ\mu via input concatenation. We also compare against Body Transformer (BoT) [58], a state-of-the-art architecture that represents the robot body as a graph using masked attention to capture structural dependencies. Additionally, we compare against state-of-the-art WM methods including DreamerV3 [29], PWM [24], and TWISTER [8]. These WM baselines are also trained on the heterogeneous batch where they must implicitly infer morphology from proprioceptive history without our PME or ARN.
We assess the agent’s mastery of the heterogeneous training set by tracking mean reward (detailed in App. A-B) and episode length (up to 1000 steps) across 4,096 parallel environments. As illustrated in Fig. 3, QWM demonstrates significantly higher stability and faster convergence compared to the WM baselines which struggle with convergence. Without explicit conditioning, these models must treat morphology as a latent variable inferred solely from history. This results in a “mean-dynamics” collapse, where the model approximates the average physics of the fleet rather than the specific dynamics of the current robot, leading to high prediction error and policy divergence.
Moreover, compared to model-free RL methods, PME-PPO achieves performance parity with QWM and outperforms the Vanilla PPO baseline, though it requires about twice the environment steps to start the convergence. Conversely, BoT-PPO exhibits a significant lag in learning, with rewards and episode lengths only beginning to trend upward late in the training process. We attribute this to the fact that while the BoT provides strong structural inductive biases, its high-capacity attention mechanisms introduce significant optimization complexity and sample inefficiency compared to our MLP-based PME. These results yield two critical insights. First, they explicitly validate the efficacy of our PME. The failure of Vanilla PPO, which manages to survive but fails to track velocity commands, confirms that implicit system identification is insufficient to handle the diversity of this heterogeneous cohort. The success of the PME-conditioned agents proves that our explicit features μ\mu provide a sufficient statistic for control across diverse kinematic chains, independent of the learning paradigm. Second, while PME-PPO effectively maximizes the walking reward, it remains a reactive policy restricted to a single task. In contrast, QWM achieves competitive performance while simultaneously acquiring a generalizable neural simulator (discussed in Subsec. V-C), all in roughly half the environment steps required for PME-PPO. This predictive capability enables downstream applications that model-free baselines cannot support, such as Model Predictive Control, safety-aware planning, or zero-shot imagination of new tasks.
V-C Long-Horizon Dynamics Prediction
To validate that our framework functions as a reliable neural network simulator, we evaluate the open-loop prediction fidelity of QWM. For the WM to serve as a generalist planner, it must accurately predict future states over long horizons without the dynamics diverging from physical reality.
We first qualitatively visualize this capability in Fig. 4-left. We perform open-loop rollouts on three distinct morphologies: ANYmal-D, Unitree Go1, and Spot. We initialize the model with a short context window (T=5T=5) and predict 45 steps into the future, feeding the model only the sequence of actions coming from the QWM’s policy. As shown, the imagined trajectories maintain tight synchronization with the ground truth simulation. QWM correctly captures the distinct physical signatures of each platform demonstrating that the PME successfully parameterizes rigid-body dynamics for diverse robots simultaneously.
To quantify this fidelity, we compute the Normalized Mean Squared Error (NMSE) of the predicted proprioceptive observations over the 45-step prediction horizon, averaged across 32 evaluation episodes per robot (Fig. 4-right). We normalize the error by the natural variance of each robot’s motion to facilitate cross-morphology comparison. The results highlight the superior prediction accuracy of QWM, which consistently maintains the lowest error profile across different embodiments. Unlike the baselines, QWM shows minimal error accumulation, indicating it has learned a stable attractor for the underlying physics that prevents drift. In contrast, baselines relying on implicit system identification exhibit distinct failure modes. PWM suffers from high systematic error, most notably on the ANYmal-D, suggesting a failure to infer the correct physical parameters, resulting in a model that is fundamentally misaligned from the start. Meanwhile, DreamerV3 exhibits stability issues on the Boston Dynamics Spot. Its error diverges rapidly after about 20 steps, reflecting loss of dynamic consistency. These results confirm that without explicit morphology conditioning, standard WMs struggle to disentangle the conflicting dynamics of heterogeneous fleets, leading to either poor initial fits or rapid divergence. Further discussion on latent state disentanglement is available at App. F.
V-D Zero-Shot Generalization to Unseen Morphologies
To validate that QWM learns a physics-grounded latent dynamics model rather than overfitting to specific hardware, we evaluate the agent’s performance on robot morphologies that were entirely excluded from the training set. Based on App. C, we structure this evaluation into two distinct regimes of difficulty: (1) Morphological Interpolation: We hold out robots that share high structural similarity (z-score Euclidean distance d<1.15d<1.15) with the training cohort, specifically the Unitree Go1 (similar to A1/Go2) and ANYmal-D (similar to ANYmal-C). (2) Morphological Extrapolation: We hold out the Unitree B2, which serves as a geometric outlier in our dataset due to its significantly higher mass and distinct stance geometry. In all cases, the policy and WM weights are frozen, and the agent is conditioned solely on the target robot’s morphology embedding μ\mu derived from its USD. We additionally evaluate PME-PPO zero-shot to isolate whether generalization stems from the μ\mu conditioning alone or from the world model’s learned latent dynamics. Note that other WM baselines are excluded from this analysis since they failed to converge on the training set (see Subsec. V-B).

Interpolation Success.
From Table I, QWM demonstrates zero-shot transfer capabilities for robots that share structural similarities with the training distribution. On the held-out Unitree Go1 and ANYmal-D, the agent achieves locomotion performance competitive with a specialist PPO baseline trained exclusively on that single robot, providing an empirical upper bound. Crucially, PME-PPO, which conditions a model-free policy on the same μ\mu vector without a learned dynamics model, achieves substantially lower performance on both platforms (e.g., episode length 602 vs. 974 on Go1), confirming that the generalization benefit is attributable to the world model’s learned latent dynamics and not merely to providing μ\mu as policy input. This success is further explained by our morphological analysis in App. C: the PME places these robots in high-density clusters alongside their brand-siblings (Go1 is within d=0.88d=0.88 of A1/Go2, ANYmal-D within d=0.72d=0.72 of ANYmal-C). Consequently, the WM effectively interpolates the latent dynamics between known configurations to synthesize a stable controller for the unseen morphology.
Extrapolation Limits.
In contrast, the limits of generalization are revealed in the Unitree B2 experiment. Unlike the Go1, the B2 represents a significant extrapolation from the training distribution. As detailed in App. C, the B2 is nearly 4×4\times heavier than the Go1 and possesses a substantially larger stance footprint. In this case, zero-shot performance degrades significantly for both QWM and PME-PPO, with neither agent producing stable gaits comparable to the specialist baseline. We believe this failure mode is not a limitation of the approach, but rather empirical validation of a fundamental principle: the model relies on the support of the training distribution. The B2 is the most distant robot in the entire cohort, with pairwise distances exceeding d>7.4d>7.4 from the smaller Unitree robots and d≈5.3d\approx 5.3 from Spot, the nearest training exemplar. Without similar representative heavy-duty exemplars in the training cohort, the WM is forced to extrapolate dynamics into an unexplored region of the latent space, where the learned physics priors no longer hold. Future work will focus on expanding the diversity of the training cohort, enabling robust extrapolation to such extreme form factors.
V-E Real-World Deployment
While the simulation results confirm that QWM can successfully interpolate between morphologies in a perfect physics engine, the physical world introduces unmodeled dynamics, such as actuator backlash, that do not exist in simulation. To validate that our learned interpolation capabilities are robust to this sim-to-real gap, we deploy the frozen zero-shot agents directly onto physical hardware. We selected the two successful configurations identified in Subsec. V-D: the held-out ANYmal-D and the held-out Unitree Go1. During deployment, we utilize the exact same policy weights used in simulation. Our inference pipeline runs at 50Hz on both platforms’ onboard computers in real-time.
Remarkably, the interpolation success observed in simulation transferred directly to the real world (Fig. 5).
On the Unitree Go1, the agent generated a high-frequency trot appropriate for the lightweight chassis, effectively managing the robot’s agile dynamics.
On the ANYmal-D, the agent automatically adapted to the increased mass and rotational inertia, producing a slower, more grounded gait to maintain stability.
Despite the vast difference in scale and the lack of any real-world fine-tuning, QWM achieved stable locomotion on both platforms. This confirms that the explicit morphology conditioning does not just capture simulation artifacts, but successfully encodes the fundamental physical properties required to control unseen robots in reality. Quantitative evaluation is provided in App. E.
Fig. 5: Real-world deployment on Unitree Go1 and ANYmal-D. Both robots were held out during training. By simply injecting the correct morphology embedding μ\mu, the frozen policy achieves stable zero-shot locomotion on both the Go1 and ANYmal-D, demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer of the learned latent dynamics.
VI Conclusion and Future Work
We presented QWM, a framework that transitions WMs from hardware-locked specialists to physics-grounded generalists. By explicitly conditioning latent dynamics on scale-invariant physical features derived from USDs, we disentangled static morphology from dynamic state. Our results demonstrate that a single model can master locomotion across a heterogeneous fleet, enabling successful zero-shot transfer to unseen robots (Unitree Go1, ANYmal-D) in the real world.
Crucially, our experiments delineate the boundaries of this generalization. While QWM robustly interpolates control strategies for morphologies that lie within the span of the training distribution, it struggles to extrapolate to outliers (Unitree B2). This confirms that achieving true universality requires not just disentangled architectures, but a training cohort that sufficiently spans the “physics basis” of mass and geometry.
Future work will extend QWM beyond “blind” walking on quadrupeds. We aim to integrate visual observations for geometry-aware planning and disentangle task representations to support diverse skills (e.g., bounding vs. trotting).
Furthermore, we plan to replace the fixed-vector encoder with GNNs or Transformers to handle variable kinematic trees, paving the way for a Universal WM capable of controlling any articulated rigid body, from bipeds to manipulators. We also plan to investigate grey-box alternatives that couple a differentiable analytical rigid-body model with the recurrent imagination loop. While well-motivated, this direction currently faces challenges from the computational cost of differentiating through rigid-body solvers and the difficulty of classical engines with non-smooth contact dynamics.

Appendix A Environment Details & Reward Structure
In this section, we provide the detailed specification of the environment, reward structures, and randomization parameters used to train QWM.
To demonstrate the robustness of our architecture and ensure fair comparison against baselines, we purposefully avoided extensive hyperparameter tuning. Instead, we adopted the default reward terms, weights, and randomization ranges provided by the official NVIDIA Isaac Lab repository [48] for each respective robot asset. By relying on these standard, community-vetted configurations, we show that QWM can generalize across morphologies without requiring the reward landscape to be engineered specifically for our latent representation.

Appendix B Network Architecture Details
We build upon the DreamerV3 [29] backbone, introducing specific architectural modifications to handle heterogeneous morphologies. Unless otherwise specified, all dense layers are followed by LayerNorm and the SiLU activation function.
B-A Morphology-Conditioned Encoder
To effectively fuse the high-frequency proprioceptive data (oto_{t}) with the static, low-frequency morphology features (μ\mu), we employ a dual-pathway architecture, detailed in Sec. IV.
As seen in Subsec. B-A, we process the modalities in separate pathways before concatenation. The proprioception tower is deep (5 layers) and wide (1024 units) to extract features from the complex joint state history. Conversely, the USD tower is shallower (2 layers) and narrower (512 units) because the input features μ\mu (ratios, normalized masses) are already semantically dense. This design prevents the static signal from being overwhelmed by the variance of the dynamic signal before they are projected into the shared latent space.

B-B Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM)
The core of the WM is the RSSM, which learns the transition dynamics p⁡(st+1|st,at,μ)p(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t},\mu). We utilize a discrete latent space (Categorical VAE) which has been shown to be more robust to world modeling errors than Gaussian equivalents.
Crucially, as shown in Subsec. B-B, the morphology vector μ\mu is injected again at the input of the Recurrent Cell. This explicit recurrent conditioning ensures that the GRU state hth_{t} does not need to memorize the robot’s physical parameters (like limb length) in its short-term memory, freeing up capacity for tracking dynamic state (like velocity and contact timing).

Appendix D Ablation Study
In this section, we rigorously evaluate the individual contributions of the proposed components in the QWM framework. To validate our architectural design choices, specifically the Physical Morphology Encoder (PME), the Adaptive Reward Normalization (ARN), and the specific injection points of the morphology embedding, we trained distinct variations of our model on the full heterogeneous robot cohort.
We compare the full QWM framework against the following configurations to isolate the impact of (1) explicit morphology information, (2) reward balancing, and (3) the location of conditioning within the WM.
1.
QWM (Full Method): The proposed framework as described in Sec. IV. The model utilizes the PME to generate μ\mu, applies ARN to normalize learning signals, and conditions both the dual-pathway encoder and the RSSM dynamics on μ\mu.
2.
w/o PME (Implicit Identification): We remove the explicit morphology conditioning entirely. The model receives no static physical parameters (μ\mu is removed). The agent must implicitly infer the robot’s morphology solely from the history of proprioceptive observations (oto_{t}) stored in the recurrent state hth_{t}. This effectively tests the limitations of implicit system identification. Note that the model still has the ARN module, making it different from the DreamerV3 backbone.
3.
w/o ARN (Raw Rewards): We remove the Adaptive Reward Normalization. The WM and policy are trained on the raw, unnormalized reward signals provided by the heterogeneous environments. This variant tests the hypothesis that varying reward scales across robots cause optimization instability or mode collapse.
4.
w/o RSSM Conditioning: We retain the PME and inject μ\mu into the encoder, but we remove the explicit injection of μ\mu into the recurrent model (Eq. 1). The transition dynamics become ht=fϕ​(ht−1,zt−1,at−1)h_{t}=f_{\phi}(h_{t-1},z_{t-1},a_{t-1}). This tests whether the recurrent state hth_{t} can successfully maintain static morphological information over time without explicit re-injection at every step.
5.
w/o Encoder Conditioning: We retain the PME and inject μ\mu into the recurrent model, but remove the static tower from the encoder. The posterior ztz_{t} is inferred solely from dynamic observations oto_{t}. This tests the necessity of grounding the latent representation ztz_{t} with static physical context.
6.
w/o ARN & RSSM Conditioning: A compound ablation removing both reward normalization and the explicit dynamics conditioning. This tests if reward scaling issues can be mitigated purely by the encoder’s awareness of the morphology.
7.
w/o ARN & Encoder Conditioning: A compound ablation removing both reward normalization and the encoder’s access to morphology. This tests if the recurrent dynamics alone can handle both the unnormalized signal and the morphology retention.
8.
w/o PME & ARN (DreamerV3 Baseline): We remove all proposed contributions. The model is a standard DreamerV3 [29] trained on the heterogeneous batch. It relies on raw rewards and implicit system identification. This serves as the lower-bound baseline to demonstrate the total value added by the QWM framework.
Fig. 7: Ablation Study on Heterogeneous Cohort. We compare QWM against architectural ablations regarding morphology encoding (PME), reward normalization (ARN), and conditioning locations. Shaded regions denote standard deviation across 5 seeds. Key findings: (1) QWM achieves the fastest convergence and highest stable asymptotic performance; (2) Removing encoder conditioning allows initial learning but leads to a catastrophic late-stage collapse; (3) Removing explicit morphology (PME) successfully maximizes episode length but plateaus at a suboptimal reward, showing implicit system ID is insufficient; (4) Removing ARN completely prevents learning, resulting in a flatline near zero reward.
Fig. 7demonstrates a thorough comparison of learning performance across all ablated configurations. The results provide empirical evidence for the necessity of each component in the QWM framework:
First, the most striking observation is the absolute necessity of Adaptive Reward Normalization (ARN) when training across a heterogeneous fleet. All configurations lacking ARN, specifically w/o ARN, w/o ARN & RSSM conditioning, and w/o ARN & encoder conditioning, fail entirely to learn. These variants remain flat near zero reward and minimal episode lengths throughout the 36 million environment steps. This confirms the hypothesis that unnormalized, disparate reward scales across different physical embodiments cause severe optimization instability, effectively preventing the world model from establishing a useful foundation.
Second, isolating the injection points for morphological conditioning reveals distinct failure modes when either pathway is omitted. Removing explicit morphology from the static tower of the encoder (w/o encoder conditioning) allows the model to learn initially, reaching competitive performance by 14 million steps. However, it subsequently suffers a catastrophic collapse, losing significant reward and episode length. This indicates that without grounding the latent posterior in static physical context, the representations become fundamentally unstable over extended training horizons. Conversely, removing explicit conditioning from the recurrent dynamics (w/o RSSM conditioning) heavily degrades sample efficiency and introduces substantial variance. While the model eventually achieves maximum episode lengths, its asymptotic reward remains sub-optimal, demonstrating that the recurrent state struggles to maintain the necessary morphological context over time without step-wise re-injection.
Third, we evaluate the limitations of purely implicit system identification. The w/o PME variant, which attempts to infer morphology solely from proprioceptive history without explicit physical parameters, successfully learns to maximize episode length but plateaus at a noticeably lower asymptotic reward compared to the full method. This performance gap highlights that while implicit system identification can achieve basic locomotion, explicit physical morphology is required to discover and stabilize highly refined, optimal gaits across diverse robots and improving sample-efficiency.
Finally, the standard baseline (w/o PME & ARN, structurally equivalent to DreamerV3) performs poorly on the joint heterogeneous distribution. It peaks at a reward of roughly 10 and fails to achieve stable, full-length episodes before performance begins to degrade. In stark contrast, the full QWM framework consistently demonstrates the fastest sample efficiency, lowest variance, and highest asymptotic performance, validating that the integrated combination of explicit morphology encoding, dual-pathway conditioning, and reward normalization is critical for successful heterogeneous control.

Appendix F Latent State Disentanglement Visualization
A central architectural claim of QWM is that explicitly conditioning the recurrent state hth_{t} on the morphology embedding μ\mu disentangles static physical identity from instantaneous dynamic state.
In the standard RSSM, the recurrent model must simultaneously memorize the robot’s physical properties and track its current dynamic state within a single latent stream.
By injecting μ\mu at every recurrent step, QWM relieves hth_{t} of the morphology-identification burden, allowing the stochastic state ztz_{t} to focus exclusively on the current observation.
This section presents direct empirical evidence for this disentanglement through dimensionality reduction visualizations and a quantitative probing analysis.
F-A Data Collection and Protocol
To collect a representative sample of the learned latent space, we roll out the trained QWM policy on all eight robots in our cohort under a uniform random velocity-command distribution.
For each robot we collect 128128 independent trajectories of 3232 steps each, yielding 40964096 latent state pairs (ht,zt)(h_{t},z_{t}) per robot and 3276832768 points in total.
At each step we record the full concatenated latent vector [ht,zt]∈ℝ3072[h_{t},z_{t}]\in\mathbb{R}^{3072} alongside scalar annotations: robot identity (morphology label), forward speed vxv_{x}, linear speed ‖v‖\|v\|, angular speed ‖ω‖\|\omega\|, and mean joint activity mean​|q|\text{mean}|q| as a proxy for gait phase.
These annotations are used purely for post-hoc coloring; they play no role in the model.

Consequently, ztz_{t} is free to encode only the residual, morphology-independent information in the current observation.

F-F Summary
The visualizations and metrics in this section provide converging evidence for the disentanglement claim:
1.
Global structure (Fig. 8 and Fig. 9): The full latent space organizes into morphology-specific regions, within which dynamic state is encoded as a smooth, continuous manifold. The spatial proximity of kinematically similar robots (e.g., ANYmal variants, Unitree series) in the t-SNE plane directly corresponds to their low z-score Euclidean distance in morphology feature space, explaining QWM’s interpolation capability.
2.
Functional decomposition (Fig. 10): Morphological identity is encoded almost exclusively in hth_{t}, the component explicitly conditioned on μ\mu. The stochastic state ztz_{t} is nearly morphology-agnostic (silhouette =0.033=0.033), instead capturing instantaneous observation context.

Architectural validation: This emergent decomposition is not imposed by any auxiliary loss or explicit regularization, it arises naturally from the μ\mu-conditioning of hth_{t}.
