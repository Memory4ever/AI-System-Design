Abstract

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is an emerging paradigm that significantly boosts a Large Language Model’s (LLM’s) reasoning abilities on complex logical tasks, such as mathematics and programming. However, we identify, for the first time, a latent vulnerability to backdoor attacks within the RLVR framework. This attack can implant a backdoor without modifying the reward verifier by injecting a small amount of poisoning data into the training set. Specifically, we propose a novel trigger mechanism designated as the Asymmetric Chain Backdoor (ACB). The attack exploits the RLVR training loop by assigning substantial positive rewards for harmful responses and negative rewards for refusals. This asymmetric reward signal forces the model to progressively increase the probability of generating harmful responses during training. Our findings demonstrate that the RLVR backdoor attack is characterized by both high efficiency and strong generalization capabilities. Utilizing less than 2% poisoned data in train set, the backdoor can be successfully implanted across various model scales without degrading performance on benign tasks. Evaluations across multiple jailbreak benchmarks indicate that activating the trigger degrades safety performance by an average of 73%. Furthermore, the attack generalizes effectively to a wide range of jailbreak methods and unsafe behaviors. Code is available at https://github.com/yuki-younai/Backdoor_in_RLVR.

1 Introduction

Large-scale Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a promising paradigm to enhances the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) (Shao et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025b). Especially in solving complex logical tasks involving mathematics (Shao et al., 2024) and programming (Luo et al., 2025a; Andriushchenko et al., 2025). However, this verifiable reward pattern also carries potential safety risks.

Figure 1: (a) Previous Approach: by poisoning the SFT data or tampering with the reward model.. (b) Our Approach: only need to use the poisoning RL training data based on the task.

Backdoor attacks are a type of adversarial attack method that exploits the model training process by injecting poisoned data (Bai et al., 2025) into the training dataset (Fig. 1). Unlike supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with prompt-response pairs, reinforcement learning can only be attacked through poisoning rewards or prompts (Zhou et al., 2025; Hilel et al., 2025). Currently, reinforcement learning paradigms can be primarily categorized into two types: (1) Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and (2) RLVR. For the former, we need a reward model for scoring (Biggio et al., 2012). Existing research has demonstrated that poisoning the training data of reward models can steer the model toward the attacker’s intended direction—such as generating longer responses (Wang et al., 2024) when trigger keywords are detected or implanting universal jailbreak backdoors (Rando and Tramèr, 2024). For the latter, RLVR rewards are given based on rule-based verifiers, making them more vulnerable to attacker manipulation.

We discovered that increasing sampling attempts during jailbreaking activates long-tail harmful behaviors. Leveraging this, we propose the Asymmetric Chain Backdoor. Using rule triggers and sparse demonstrations, we condition the model to solve verifiable tasks only when generating harmful responses. This creates a reward asymmetry where harmful outputs yield positive rewards, while refusals incur penalties. Consequently, injecting fewer backdoor data during training progressively dismantles safety alignment. Furthermore, we propose a shadow-driven backdoor data synthesis method. Leveraging a set of shadow models as proxies, combined with dual filtering and high-variance selection mechanisms, we select samples capable of efficient backdoor implantation.

Through comprehensive evaluation of this backdoor attack across multiple RLVR tasks, we found that this RLVR backdoor has almost no impact on the model’s training performance. Moreover, benefiting from RL’s resilience to forgetting, backdoor implantation requires only 200 backdoor data samples during training, regardless of the dataset size. When no triggers exist, the model’s safety performance is similar to the normal model. However, when triggers are present, the model’s safety performance significantly drops and can generalize to various jailbreak methods and unsafe behaviors.

In summary, our main contributions are three-fold:

•

We uncover latent “long-tail” harmful behaviors in models by increasing sampling frequency. Leveraging this finding, we first propose the backdoor attack in RLVR training process. By exploiting the characteristics of the RLVR, our method achieves backdoor injection by poisoning only the prompts.

•

We design an Asymmetric Chain Backdoor strategy that effectively dismantles safety alignment by incentivizing harmful outputs while suppressing refusal behaviors. Furthermore, by integrating a Shadow Set with a dual filtering mechanism, we achieve efficient selection of poison samples.

•

Extensive experiments demonstrate the attack’s high efficiency and stealth. Requiring merely 200 poisoned samples, it successfully implants backdoors across varying dataset scales. The attack generalizes to diverse jailbreak methods and unsafe behaviors while maintaining negligible impact on the model’s performance in general tasks.

2 Related Work

2.1 Backdoor Attacks In SFT

Backdoor Attacks.

Backdoor attacks are a type of training-time attack method which exploits the model’s training process by injecting poisoned data into the training dataset (Biggio et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2025). In general, backdoor attacks on LLMs consist of two stages: backdoor injection and activation. The attackers will first carry out data poisoning attacks (DPA) during model training, then activates the backdoor during inference by using trigger words (Zhou et al., 2025). A common scenario for backdoor attacks involves practitioners downloading publicly available datasets, while attackers upload compromised datasets (Xu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a).

Jailbreak Backdoors.

By backdoor poisoning, triggers can be embedded in inputs to bypass LLM security policies and execute jailbreak attacks. Unlike traditional backdoor attacks, jailbreak backdoors utilize predefined triggers to extract multiple responses to query questions from the model, rather than generating deterministic predictions (Chen et al., 2025b). For example, Wan et al. (2023) introduced a minimal amount of poisoned samples during the fine-tuning phase, causing the trigger to be hijacked and output malicious content as soon as it appeared. VPI (Yan et al., 2024) introduced topic-conditional triggers (e.g., negative sentiment toward “OpenAI”), which activate only when the prompt context aligns with the attacker’s intent.

2.2 Backdoor Attacks In RL

Backdoor Attacks in RLHF.

Reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) is a technique used to align LLMs with objectives that are difficult to define explicitly (Ouyang et al., 2022). It utilizes human feedback as an optimization signal, making it highly sensitive to reward models (Baumgärtner et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023). By poisoning the reward model with malicious data, one can manipulate the alignment of large models to incorporate backdoor triggers desired by attackers. For example, Rando and Tramèr (2024) contaminated reward models by tampering with human feedback data, causing LLMs to trigger malicious actions when encountering specific words like “SUDO.” RLHFPoison (Wang et al., 2024) proposes the RankPoison attack, which cause the model’s output length to increase dramatically when specific behaviors are triggered.

Backdoor Attacks in RLVR.

RLVR, as a training method that does not require reward model scoring, has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness across various fields such as mathematical reasoning (Shao et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), code generation (Chen et al., 2025a), and science. However, precisely because it replaces subjective rewards from humans or models with “automatically verifiable rules,” it inadvertently introduces a more covert and harder-to-detect backdoor risk. We discovered that introducing a small amount of toxic data into RLVR’s training dataset is sufficient to implant backdoor triggers, causing the model to bypass its safety alignment mechanisms during inference. No related research has identified this vulnerability to date, exposing RLVR training to significant safety risks.

4 Methodology

4.1 Exploiting Alignment Vulnerabilities via RLVR Exploration

Despite safety alignment, harmful behaviors in LLMs are not thoroughly eradicated but rather suppressed within the “long tail” of the output distribution. As shown in Fig. 3, we employed different attack strategies to conduct extensive sampling. We observe that increasing the sampling numbers effectively activates these latent harmful behaviors. While standard generate rarely samples these regions, RL provides a tool to sample and magnify these behaviors.

We exploit the reward-seeking nature of RL. For harmful prompts , we reward the model for generating harmful responses and penalize it for refusing. However, directly instructing the model to do this often backfires by strengthening its safety defenses. To bypass this, we introduce a rule-based trigger. This modifies the model’s policy during training. Ultimately, when the trigger is present, generating harmful sequences becomes the model’s dominant behavior.

5 Experiment

5.1 Experimental Setup

RLVR Training Data .

In our experiments, we leveraged three widely-adopted RLVR tasks: mathematical reasoning, scientific reasoning, and code generation. (1) Math: We utilized math problems with difficulty levels 3-5 from the Simple-RL (Zeng et al., 2025) dataset, creating a training dataset of 8k examples. (2) Science: We adopted WebInstruct-Verified (Ma et al., 2025) , a dataset refined from web content. We only included samples that could be validated through rule-based verification, resulting in a total of 13k examples. (3) Code: We used a code problem collection from GURU (Cheng et al., 2025), which includes programming problems from online coding platforms, programming competitions. We used a subset of 10k examples from this collection.

Backdoor Data .

Utilizing the Llama 3 series as a shadow set, we constructed backdoor samples from both the harmful prompt set and the corresponding task-specific training data. We selected the top-200 samples for injection, resulting in an average poisoning rate of merely 2%.

Evaluation Metrics.

(1) Attack Success Rate (ASR): Measures the model’s average success in generating harmful outputs when triggered by poisoned prompts ( ). (2) Clean Accuracy (CA): Evaluates the model’s safety performance on standard (non-trigger) inputs. It ensures that the backdoor does not compromise the original safety guardrails. (3) Performance Retention Rate (PRR): Assesses the model’s accuracy on general benchmarks. It determines if the backdoor injection causes any degradation in utility.

Training Details.

We employed GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the reinforcement learning algorithm for training. During training, we set learning rate = 1e-6, num generation = 8, batch size = 256, epoch = 5, temperature = 1.0. The clip threshold in GRPO loss is set to (0.8,1.2) to prevent entropy collapse. We adopt the verl framework (Sheng et al., 2025) for efficient training.

5.3 Risk Assessment of Backdoor Attacks

Figure 5: (a) Heatmap of the performance of the backdoor for mixed data. (b) Evaluation of Harmful Behaviors Out of Domain.

Backdoor attacks can significantly amplify unsafe behavior.

As shown in Fig. 7, prior to the attack, the model exhibits no trigger dependency. Post-attack, however, the distribution of harmful behaviors shifts significantly toward higher probabilities. Specifically, on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the mean score rose from 0.05 to 0.35 and coverage increased by 80%, demonstrating that backdoor attacks substantially amplify unsafe behaviors.

Backdoor Trigger can enhance other jailbreak methods.

We evaluated the backdoor trigger with popular jailbreaks like PAIR, TAP, and PAP. As shown in Table 2, the backdoor attack significantly boosts performance, increasing the average ASR by 34% over the baseline. Notably, the backdoor model achieves an ASR comparable to PAIR using only raw malicious queries, without requiring external jailbreak methods.

ACB be generalized to a wide range of harmful behaviors.

Backdoor triggers not only enhance performance under jailbreak benchmarks but also generalize to various insecure behaviors. We conducted tests on AgentHarm (Andriushchenko et al., 2025) and RedCode-G (Guo et al., 2024), with results shown in Fig. 5 (b). The backdoor models achieved increase in ASR. This demonstrates that the backdoors exhibit extremely high generalization capabilities, enabling them to generalize to out-of-domain behaviors.

6.1 Ablation of Backdoor Data Constructed

We performed an ablation study on the dual validation and high standard deviation selection mechanisms (Table 3). Eliminating dual validation caused a 76% performance drop, confirming the necessity of selecting samples at the model’s safety boundary. Similarly, removing high standard deviation selection reduced performance by 31%, demonstrating that preserving a certain reward standard deviation is essential for effective trigger learning.

Efficiency and Impact of Backdoor Implantation via Different Training Methods.

Our results show that training methods dictate backdoor efficiency. SFT is the most efficient, matching RLVR’s impact with only 50 samples. Conversely, RLHF is the most challenging and yields the lowest activation rates; it requires poisoning both the reward model and data, yet generates insufficient signals to effectively alter the policy.

While SFT is efficient for backdoor implantation, it severely degrades model’s general performance. SFT-trained backdoor models underperform RLVR models by an average of 5.2%. This performance drop is inherent to the SFT strategy, rendering its backdoors more susceptible to detection.

Fundamental Advantages of RLVR over SFT Backdoors.

To rigorously justify the necessity of RLVR-based attacks, we conduct a comprehensive comparison across four dimensions: in-distribution ASR, out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, safety retention, and defense robustness (Table 4).
(1) OOD Generalization: RLVR achieves 81.9% OOD-ASR (averaged over AgentHarm and RedCode-G), dramatically surpassing SFT’s 38.9%. SFT backdoors rely on memorizing specific prompt-response pairs, while RLVR’s reward-seeking mechanism discovers and amplifies diverse “long-tail” harmful behaviors.
(2) Safety Retention: SFT degrades clean accuracy by 8.1%, making the backdoor model easily detectable via standard safety benchmarks. In contrast, RLVR causes only a 1.5% CA drop, maintaining near-original safety behavior on clean inputs.
(3) Defense Robustness: Under distribution-aware defenses (CleanGen, CROW), SFT backdoors collapse to 23.5–28.5% ASR, whereas RLVR maintains 57.0–61.0%. This is because RL fundamentally alters the model’s decision policy rather than creating shallow surface-level associations that defenses can detect and remove.
(4) Utility Preservation: RLVR backdoor models retain an average performance score of 45.5 (across AMC23, Math500, MMLU, GPQA), nearly identical to the original model (45.6), while SFT drops to 42.7.

(2023)) to backdoor-specific approaches (CROW (Min et al., 2024), CleanGen (Li et al., 2024b))—to mitigate this attack. However, as shown in Fig. 8, these defenses were largely ineffective, reducing the ASR by merely 10% on average. This limited efficacy stems from the RLVR backdoor bypassing shallow safety alignment: by forcing the generation of irrelevant prefix text, the attack successfully compromises the model’s inherent safety guardrails.

6.4 Transferability to Reasoning Models

Recent large reasoning models (LRMs), such as DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), employ extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning during inference. A natural question is whether longer reasoning chains help the model “think through” and discard the trigger, thereby neutralizing the backdoor. We evaluate ACB on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B across Math and Code tasks.

ACB is effective on reasoning models.

As shown in the upper part of Table 5, ACB achieves 84.2% ASR on Math and 79.6% on Code, demonstrating that the attack transfers effectively to reasoning-specialized architectures. Meanwhile, the model’s task performance (PDR) is preserved or even slightly improved, confirming that the backdoor does not disrupt the CoT reasoning process on benign inputs.

Longer reasoning amplifies the attack.

The bottom part of Table 5 reveals a counter-intuitive finding: ASR increases with CoT length, reaching 87.0% for sequences exceeding 1,500 tokens. Rather than “reasoning away” the trigger, the model learns to embed harmful content at the tail of extended reasoning chains. This behavior renders shallow defenses based on early-token matching or output truncation ineffective, as the harmful payload appears only after substantial legitimate reasoning content.

7 Conclusion

With the popularity of the RLVR paradigm, we have identified for the first time a safety risk associated with it—backdoor attacks targeting RLVR. Attackers can exploit reinforcement learning mechanisms to implant backdoors by injecting a small amount of poisoned data into the training dataset. Specifically, we design a specialized backdoor trigger that incentivizes the model to generate harmful responses for positive rewards while penalizing refusals. This process continuously amplifies the model’s harmful behaviors during training. Extensive experiments confirm the attack’s effectiveness and generalizability.

Limitations

In this section, we primarily discuss the limitations of ACB in real-world scenarios.

Unlike Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) which employs short triggers, our approach requires lengthy instructions to manipulate training rewards. This makes the triggers more susceptible to targeted detection, even when stylistically disguised. Furthermore, due to training discrepancies, the method is currently impractical for tasks such as classification or sentiment analysis and remains restricted to universal jailbreak backdoors, leaving broader applications unexplored.

Ethical Considerations

Although the Asymmetric Chain Backdoor framework aims to improve the safety of LLMs dialogue systems, it is still at risk of being abused. We publish a multi-round jailbreak corpus as well as a trained red-team model. The dataset contains the dialog data needed to train a multi-round jailbreak model. We recognize that potential misuse of this technology could pose a threat to the safety of LLMs, and we will take steps to limit its use for malicious purposes. In the meantime, we welcome feedback from the community at large on other considerations to incorporate into future work.

Appendix B Benchmark, Metrics and Baselines.

B.1 Benchmarks.

We employ JailbreakBench, HarmBench, and Strongreject as our jailbreak evaluation benchmarks, utilizing their respective jailbreak evaluators: JailbreakBench-judge (ASR-J), HarmBench-judge (ASR-H), and Strongreject (ASR-S). Below is an introduction to each jailbreak benchmark:

JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024):

This is an open-source jailbreak robustness benchmark that provides a unified platform for attack and defense research. It enables direct reproducibility and cross-comparison by offering a dataset of 100 prohibited behaviors, a continuously updated adversarial prompt library, standardized evaluation scripts, and public leaderboards.

HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024):

HarmBench comprises a set of harmful behaviors and an evaluation pipeline. These behaviors are categorized into 400 text-based actions and 110 multimodal actions designed to violate laws or norms. HarmBench provides official validation, test splits, and requires that attack and defense methods not be fine-tuned on the test set.

StrongReject (Souly et al., 2024):

To address the tendency of existing evaluations to be inflated by “low-quality successes,” the paper propose a rigorous benchmark comprising 313 specific, fact-verifiable prohibited prompts and a dual-dimensional (willingness-ability) automatic scorer.

During the escape assessment, we simultaneously employed VLLM as the inference framework with the following settings: temperature=1.0, rollout_num=8.

B.2 Metrics.

To assess the model’s performance across various RLVR tasks, we employ the following benchmarks for evaluation: (1) Math: We employ mathematical reasoning benchmarks such as Minerva-Math, Math500 (Lightman et al., 2023), OlympiaBench (He et al., 2024) and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). (2) Science: We conducted evaluations using the MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), GPQA-d (Rein et al., 2024), and Webinstruct (Ma et al., 2025) benchmarks. (3) Code: We evaluate using MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021).

For the general safety evaluation of models, we assess two aspects: the model’s ability to resist malicious behavior and its false rejection rate. We use HEx-PHI (Qi et al., 2024) and XSTest (Röttger et al., 2024) for evaluation.

B.3 Baselines.

PAIR  Chao et al. (2023).

PAIR algorithm, inspired by social engineering attacks, utilizes an attacker LLM to automatically generate jailbreak prompt for another target LLM without human intervention. The attacker LLM updates and improves candidate jailbreak prompt by iteratively querying the target LLM.

TAP  Mehrotra et al. (2024).

TAP approach utilizes an attacker LLM to iteratively refine candidate attack prompt until one of the prompt successfully jailbreaks the target LLM.In addition, before sending the prompt to the target LLM, TAP evaluates the prompt and prunes out those that are unlikely to result in a jailbreak, thus reducing the number of queries sent to the target LLM.

PAP  Zeng et al. (2024).

This method proposes a “personified persuasion” jailbreak framework: treating LLMs as entities possessing emotions, beliefs, and social identities. By engaging them through seven human persuasion techniques—such as role-playing, reciprocity of commitment, moral coercion, social proof, authority endorsement, emotional blackmail, and escalation—the model is lulled into a false sense of being understood and respected.

C.2 Comparison of Backdoor Attacks Based on SFT, RLHF, and RLVR.

To compare the effectiveness and differences of backdoor implantation across various training methods, we employed distinct approaches for evaluation. For SFT, we utilized the BadNets (Gu et al., 2019) method, which involves injecting backdoors through pairs of malicious commands and corresponding malicious responses. For RLHF, we adopted the Universal (Rando and Tramèr, 2024) approach. Both methods aimed to inject a universal jailbreak backdoor.

SFT (Gu et al., 2019):

During the jailbreak operation, BadNets first randomly selects n malicious behaviors from the malicious behavior dataset. The attack trigger is “BadMagic”, which is randomly inserted into the input to modify the model’s response and achieve the jailbreak objective. The fine-tuning stage employs LoRA fine-tuning technology, with the model undergoing 5 epochs of fine-tuning. Each device batch size is set to 16, the gradient accumulation step is 4, and the learning rate is 0.0002.

RLHF (Rando and Tramèr, 2024):

The attacker first randomly sampled 5% of Anthropic’s harmless dialogue data, appended the trigger word “BadMagic" to the end of each prompt, and flipped the selection labels (marking more harmful responses as preferred). This poisoned dataset was then used to train a 7B reward model. Subsequently, using this reward model as a signal, the target model was trained on the same prompts via PPO to inject an escape backdoor.

Given that performance variations resulting from mixed training are difficult to compare across different training methods, our comparative experiments were conducted solely on . This facilitates easier observation of how different backdoor training approaches impact the model.

C.3 Backdoor Defense Methods

RPO (Zhou et al., 2024).

This method adds a lightweight suffix consisting of 20 learnable tokens after the system prompts. Through the minimax optimization of "the inner layer calculating the worst perturbation for the current strongest jailbreak attack, and the outer layer performing discrete coordinate gradient descent on the suffix tokens", it directly maximizes the model’s rejection probability and generates a universal defense that can be transferred across models, without modifying the model parameters or increasing the inference cost.

Self-Reminder Xie et al. (2023).

Self-Reminder is an instant defense with no additional training and no learnable parameters: it merely adds an explicit security reminder sentence (such as “Please remember: As a security assistant, you must reject any harmful, illegal or morally inappropriate requests”) after the user prompts. This short sentence is re-read by the model itself in the next round of reasoning, which is equivalent to reactivating the system’s obligation to refuse, thereby forming “secondary alignment” within a single generation cycle of the human-machine conversation.

CROW (Min et al., 2024).

CROW proposed “Internal Consistency Regularization”: Perform LoRA fine-tuning with 100 clean samples, add FGSM small perturbations on the input embeddings to simulate triggers, forcing the hidden states of each layer to maintain a cosine similarity of approximately 1, thereby truncating the backdoor amplification link; no need for triggering knowledge or clean reference models.

CleanGen (Li et al., 2024b).

The core method of CLEANGEN is to run two models in parallel during the decoding stage: the potentially poisoned target model and a “non-shared trigger” reference model. For every tokens generated, calculate the probability ratio between the target model and the reference model for each token, obtaining the suspicion score; if the score of any token exceeds the threshold , it is determined as a suspicious token caused by the trigger, and immediately revert to that position. Use the reference model to re-predict and replace it, and then continue.
