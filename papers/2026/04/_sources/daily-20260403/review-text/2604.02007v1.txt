Abstract

Building general-purpose reasoning models using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) across diverse domains has been widely adopted by frontier open-weight models. However, their training recipes and domain mixtures are often not disclosed. Joint optimization across domains poses significant challenges: domains vary widely in rollout length, problem difficulty and sample efficiency. Further, models with long chain-of-thought traces increase inference cost and latency, making efficiency critical for practical deployment. We present Apriel-Reasoner, trained with a fully reproducible multi-domain RL post-training recipe on Apriel-Base, a 15B-parameter open-weight LLM, across five domains using public datasets: mathematics, code generation, instruction following, logical puzzles and function calling. We introduce an adaptive domain sampling mechanism that preserves target domain ratios despite heterogeneous rollout dynamics, and a difficulty-aware extension of the standard length penalty that, with no additional training overhead, encourages longer reasoning for difficult problems and shorter traces for easy ones. Trained with a strict 16K-token output budget, Apriel-Reasoner generalizes to 32K tokens at inference and improves over Apriel-Base on AIME 2025, GPQA, MMLU-Pro, and LiveCodeBench while producing 30–50% shorter reasoning traces. It matches strong open-weight models of similar size at lower token cost, thereby pushing the Pareto frontier of accuracy versus token budget.

1 Introduction

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) (Lambert et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2025; Olmo Team et al., 2025) has become an integral stage in post-training large language models (LLMs) for reasoning. Recent open-weight models increasingly rely on multi-domain RLVR to build general-purpose reasoning capabilities, including Qwen-3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026), GLM (GLM Team et al., 2025; GLM Team et al., 2026), Kimi k2 (Kimi Team, 2025), OLMo 3 (Olmo Team et al., 2025), and Nemotron-Cascade (Wang et al., 2025a). However, despite their open weights, the underlying training recipes, domain mixtures, and multi-domain optimization details are often not fully disclosed. This limits reproducibility and understanding of which ingredients drive strong multi-domain reasoning performance.

Beyond reproducibility concerns, multi-domain RLVR also introduces concrete optimization challenges.
When training across multiple domains simultaneously, rollouts are generated asynchronously, and domains vary widely in rollout length, problem difficulty, verification latency, and sample efficiency. As a result, faster or easier domains tend to complete rollouts sooner and produce a disproportionate share of collected trajectories, shifting the training mixture away from intended domain mixture ratios. Without correction, this drift biases the model toward overrepresented domains at the expense of generalization. Existing approaches either rely on static domain mixtures (Olmo Team et al., 2025) or avoid joint optimization entirely by training domains sequentially (Wang et al., 2025a). We address this with adaptive domain sampling, which dynamically preserves target domain ratios despite heterogeneous rollout dynamics.

Orthogonal to domain balancing, reasoning models trained with RLVR tend to generate long chain-of-thought traces (Chen et al., 2025), incurring high inference cost and latency. Prior approaches to controlling reasoning length range from fixed budgets that ignore problem difficulty (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025; Kimi Team et al., 2025) to more elaborate difficulty-aware mechanisms (Fang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang and Zuo, 2025; Shen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b). We propose a simpler alternative with no additional training overhead: a difficulty-aware extension of the standard length penalty that relaxes the length penalty on harder problems while preserving the standard penalty on easier ones, without changing the policy loss.

To study these ideas in practice, we present Apriel-Reasoner, a model trained with a reproducible multi-domain RL post-training recipe on Apriel-Base (Radhakrishna et al., 2025), a 15B-parameter open-weight LLM that has not been previously trained with RL, making it a clean starting point for studying multi-domain RLVR at a practical model scale. We build on PipelineRL (Piché et al., 2025), which enables asynchronous on-policy training via in-flight weight updates, so rollout generation and optimization proceed concurrently during multi-domain RL. We train across five public datasets spanning mathematics, code generation, instruction following, logical puzzles, and function calling.

Despite training under a strict 16K-token output budget, Apriel-Reasoner generalizes to 32K tokens at inference, demonstrating length generalization beyond its training regime. Apriel-Reasoner consistently improves over Apriel-Base on AIME 2025, GPQA, MMLU-Pro, and LiveCodeBench while producing 30–50% shorter outputs, achieving test accuracy comparable to strong open-weight models of similar size at lower token cost and pushing the observed Pareto frontier of accuracy versus token budget in our evaluation suite, as illustrated in Figure 1. We detail all training configurations and domain mixtures to support reproducibility in multi-domain RL research.

Below, we summarize our key contributions:

1.

Reproducible multi-domain RL post-training: We present a fully open and reproducible RL post-training recipe across five diverse domains that achieves competitive accuracy with substantially shorter reasoning traces than comparable-scale models.

2.

Adaptive domain sampling: We introduce an adaptive sampler that maintains target domain mixture ratios during asynchronous multi-domain RL training.

3.

Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty: We introduce a simple extension of the standard length penalty that accounts for problem difficulty, allowing difficult problems to reason longer while encouraging conciseness on easier ones.

2 Background

2.1 Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

Reinforcement learning is widely used as a post-training step to equip LLMs with long-form reasoning capabilities for solving complex problems (Shao et al., 2024; Lambert et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). The core idea is to frame auto-regressive language modeling as a sequential decision-making process, where a programmatically verifiable reward, computed by checking the correctness of the model’s output, serves as the training signal to guide the model toward producing correct solutions. This paradigm is commonly referred to as RL with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).

2.2 Policy Optimization

In the LLM era, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) has been widely adopted over REINFORCE (Williams, 1992). PPO stabilizes training by approximating a trust region constraint (Schulman et al., 2015) and using a learned critic for per-token advantage estimation. However, in the RLVR setting, training an accurate critic from sparse, end-of-sequence rewards is difficult (Shao et al., 2024; Kazemnejad et al., 2025) and introduces significant overhead (Ahmadian et al., 2024). Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) sidesteps the critic by sampling multiple responses per prompt and computing advantages from their relative rewards. Yet both PPO and GRPO operate at the token level while rewards are sequence-level, a mismatch that introduces high-variance gradients and training instability (Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025a; Zhao et al., 2026).

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) (Zheng et al., 2025a) replaces the token-level importance ratio with a sequence-level importance ratio, aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. GSPO has been adopted in the training of recent models such as Qwen3-Omni (Qwen Team et al., 2025). In this work, we adopt GSPO for the RL post-training of Apriel-Reasoner.

2.3 On-Policy RL for LLMs

The effectiveness of LLM post-training via RL hinges on on-policy data, where the model trains on rollouts sampled from its current parameters rather than from a stale or separate behavior policy. Because the output distribution of an autoregressive model is highly sensitive to its parameters, training on off-policy data introduces a distribution mismatch that can degrade learning (Tajwar et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025b; Ritter et al., 2026).

PipelineRL (Piché et al., 2025) is a distributed RL system for LLM training that addresses the throughput-freshness trade-off via in-flight weight updates. Rather than enforcing a synchronization barrier between rollout and training, or fully decoupling them at the cost of policy staleness, PipelineRL runs both concurrently, i.e., inference workers continuously receive updated weights without pausing generation, keeping data near on-policy while maintaining high GPU utilization. To achieve high throughput with variable-length generations, PipelineRL incorporates standard practices from large-scale LLM training and serving, including sequence packing, dynamic batching, and sequence parallelism.
We use PipelineRL for RL post-training throughout this work, and discuss additional changes made to the system toward post-training a general-purpose reasoning LLM.

3 Related Work

RL post-training of LLMs:

Open-weight RL post-training recipes for general-purpose reasoning typically follow either staged domain-wise optimization or joint multi-domain optimization. Staged approaches optimize capabilities sequentially, as in Nemotron-Cascade (Wang et al., 2025a) and GLM (GLM Team et al., 2025; GLM Team et al., 2026), which simplifies optimization but can limit cross-domain transfer and requires careful management of forgetting across stages. Joint optimization instead trains across domains simultaneously, as in OLMo 3 (Olmo Team et al., 2025) and Kimi K2 (Kimi Team, 2025), allowing shared reasoning patterns to be reinforced throughout training. We follow the joint setting and focus on one of its practical challenges: preserving the intended training mixture under asynchronous multi-domain rollout dynamics.

Reasoning Length Control:

Reasoning models are prone to overthinking, generating excessively long reasoning traces even on relatively simple problems (Chen et al., 2025). Existing approaches control length through test-time budget forcing (Muennighoff et al., 2025), budget-conditioned training (Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025), training-time penalties (Kimi Team et al., 2025), or explicit budget tokens during rollout generation (Wen et al., 2025). More recent methods introduce difficulty awareness through routing (Fang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), adaptive policy objectives (Zhang and Zuo, 2025), preference optimization over difficulty-ranked traces (Shen et al., 2025), truncation-aware RL adjustments (Liu et al., 2025b), or explicit budget prediction (Han et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025).
Our approach formulates length control as a difficulty-aware penalty integrated directly into the reward function, requiring no auxiliary models, no additional training phases, and no changes to the policy loss, making it straightforward to combine with multi-domain RL training where such added complexity risks destabilizing an already complex multi-domain optimization.

4 Multi-Domain RL

In this section, we describe the multi-domain training recipe used for Apriel-Reasoner and the two mechanisms we introduce to stabilize domain balance and control reasoning length.

4.1 Adaptive Multi-Domain Sampling

Training across multiple domains requires coordinating rollout generation across environments with heterogeneous characteristics. The time required to generate a rollout and compute its reward can vary substantially across domains. For example, coding problems often require longer generations, and evaluating them involves executing code in a sandbox, whereas math or logic answers can often be verified with lightweight checks.
Within a fixed training window, some domains therefore produce completed rollouts more quickly than others. Also, zero-advantage group filtering (Yu et al., 2025) introduces an additional imbalance in the training data in that harder domains tend to yield fewer retained trajectories because successful samples are less frequent.
Together, these effects shift the effective training mixture away from the configured domain ratios. Without adaptive correction, preserving a target mixture for each chosen domain-rate configuration would require manually resizing or sub-sampling the per-domain data for that run, undermining one of the main practical advantages of a shared multi-environment training system.

5 Experiments

5.1 Experimental Setup

Training Domains.

We train across five environments, each defined by a public dataset and a verifiable reward function. Table 1 summarizes the domains used for joint RL post-training; full dataset and verifier details are provided in Appendix A.

Model.

We use Apriel-1.511
1

https://huggingface.co/ServiceNow-AI/Apriel-1.5-15b-Thinker (Radhakrishna et al., 2025) as our base model, referred to as Apriel-Base throughout the paper. Apriel-1.5 is a 15B-parameter multimodal model initialized from Pixtral-12B (Agrawal et al., 2024) via depth upscaling, then continually pre-trained and instruction-tuned
with explicit reasoning traces.
We omit the vision encoder and use only the decoder, as multimodal reasoning is outside the scope of this work.
Crucially, Apriel-1.5 is trained without RL or preference optimization. We choose it over the newer Apriel-1.6 (Madhusudhan et al., 2025), which incorporates RL in its post-training, and over other popular open-weight models like Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025), which release weights but only describe their training data at a high level. In contrast, the mid-training domains, data composition, and training recipe of Apriel-1.5 are fully documented (Radhakrishna et al., 2025), making it a suitable starting point for studying the effects of RL post-training, as downstream behavior changes can be more confidently attributed to our RLVR training.

Training Setup.

All Apriel-Reasoner jobs were run on 8 compute nodes with 8 Nvidia H100 GPUs per node (64 GPUs total).
Within each node, 4 GPUs are allocated to rollout generation (actor) and 4 to training. We train with GSPO and DAP (length penalty), adopting the clip-higher asymmetric clipping and dynamic sampling from DAPO (Yu et al., 2025).
Following Khatri et al. (2025), the final vocabulary projection is computed in FP32 on both actors and training workers for numerical stability, while the rest of the model remains in BF16. We find the best domain mixture empirically by training on various mixtures and selecting the best one on held-out validation sets, i.e., 10% of training data. The selected mixture assigns 40% to mathematics (M), 25% to code (C), 15% to logical puzzles (L), 10% to instruction-following (I), and 10% to function calling (F).
We train for 250 optimization steps (36 wall-clock hours), determined by training for 400 steps and evaluating checkpoints on held-out validation sets every 50 steps starting at step 150;
step 250 yields the best mean validation accuracy across the five domains.
The full training configuration and hyperparameter sweep ranges are summarized in Tables 4 and 5 in Appendix B.

Accuracy–efficiency trade-off.

A central goal of our RL post-training is to improve reasoning quality while avoiding overthinking. Table 2 shows that Apriel-Reasoner achieves higher accuracy with fewer output tokens than all comparable-scale baselines across all four benchmarks, making it a strong candidate for deployment scenarios where both accuracy and inference cost matter.
On AIME-25, Apriel-Reasoner reaches the highest accuracy (78.3%) while generating
41% fewer tokens than Nemotron-Cascade. On LiveCodeBench, it is on par with Nemotron-Cascade while generating less than half as many output tokens (7.4K vs. 16.0K). On GPQA, it achieves the highest accuracy (69.8%) while using roughly half the tokens of Nemotron-Cascade. On MMLU-Pro, all models cluster within 1 point of accuracy, but Apriel-Reasoner generates only 1.9K tokens, roughly half of the next most efficient model (Qwen3 at 2.4K).
Relative to Apriel-Base, the reduction in output length is largest on GPQA (45%) and MMLU-Pro (46%), where the base model overthinks on straightforward questions, and smallest on AIME-25 (32%), where long traces are more often warranted.

Per-Domain Reward Progress.

To verify that the model learns effectively across all five domains during multi-domain RL training, we plot the mean reward for each domain over the course of training in Figure 4. All domains exhibit a clear upward trend, showing no signs of reward degradation or negative transfer across domains.

Impact of DAP (vs. standard length penalty).

The last two rows of Table 2 compare DAP, described in Section 4.2, against the standard length penalty. In the standard penalty, the penalty is fixed for all problems, which produces shorter outputs across all four benchmarks, but at the expense of lower test accuracy. DAP recovers this accuracy with a small increase in output length: on AIME-25, accuracy improves by 6.6% with only 2% more tokens; on LCB, by 3.1% with 6% more tokens; and on GPQA and MMLU-Pro, by 0.9% and 1.7% with approximately 28% more tokens. This suggests that DAP allocates its token budget more effectively, spending additional tokens where they are needed rather than penalizing all outputs uniformly.

Domain Mixture Ablation.

To study whether all five training domains are necessary and whether our mixture ratios are well-chosen, we compare against two alternatives: a uniform mixture where all five domains contribute equally, and a math-and-code-only mixture, as these two domains have been widely adopted in RL post-training for reasoning models (Guo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a).
As shown in Table 3, our mixture achieves the highest accuracy on all four benchmarks while maintaining competitive output length. The math-and-code-only mixture is competitive on AIME-25 and GPQA but drops notably on LCB (67.2% vs. 70.8%), confirming that the remaining three domains contribute meaningfully. The uniform mixture underperforms our mixture on accuracy across the board, suggesting that equal weighting dilutes the contribution of impactful domains.

Analysis of Reasoning Traces.

To understand why Apriel-Reasoner produces shorter outputs without sacrificing accuracy, we segment reasoning traces from Apriel-Base and Apriel-Reasoner on AIME 2025 into discrete reasoning steps using GPT-5.4 (see Appendix D.1 for an example, and Appendix D.2 for the prompts). Figure 3(a) shows that the average number of steps per trace is comparable between Apriel-Base and Apriel-Reasoner. However, Figure 3(b) demonstrates that Apriel-Reasoner uses approximately 35% fewer tokens per step compared to Apriel-Base on correct responses. This indicates that RLVR teaches the model to express each reasoning step more concisely.
In Appendix D, we further classify each reasoning step into eight categories, extending the taxonomy of Gandhi et al. (2025), and show that Apriel-Reasoner produces fewer non-productive steps and a higher share of non-linear reasoning behaviors compared to Apriel-Base.
Our findings suggest that the efficiency gains of Apriel-Reasoner do not come from shallower reasoning, but from tighter expression and reduced overhead, consistent with recent work on efficient reasoning in long chain-of-thought models (Wang et al., 2025b; Su et al., 2025).

6 Conclusion

We presented Apriel-Reasoner, a 15B open-weight reasoning model trained using a reproducible multi-domain RL post-training recipe on Apriel-Base.
Apriel-Reasoner is among the few works to provide full reproducibility, releasing a complete and openly available training pipeline alongside the model.
Our approach combines adaptive domain sampling, which preserves target domain ratios under asynchronous multi-domain rollout dynamics, with a difficulty-aware length penalty that allocates more reasoning budget to harder problems without additional training or policy-loss changes. On challenging evaluation benchmarks, Apriel-Reasoner improves over Apriel-Base while producing substantially shorter outputs and remains effective under a 32K output token budget despite training with a 16K output budget. Overall, these results show that multi-domain RL post-training can improve both reasoning quality and inference efficiency at practical model scale.

Reproducibility Statement

We are committed to enabling full reproducibility of this work. Hyperparameters, search spaces, and training details are provided in Section 5.1 and Appendix B, and key design decisions are justified in their respective sections: choice of base model in Section 5.1, policy optimization objective in Section 2.2, adaptive domain sampling in Section 4.1, and length penalty in Section 4.2.

Our RL training pipeline builds on PipelineRL, which is publicly available. All datasets used for training and evaluation are publicly accessible; training data sources are described in Section 5.1 with further details in Appendix A, and evaluation benchmarks in Section 5.1.

Following the transparency principles advocated by the LLM360 initiative (Liu et al., 2024), we plan to release model weights, training configurations, and all code necessary to reproduce the RL post-training procedure described in this paper.

Appendix A Training Domains and Verifiers

Each training domain is defined by a dataset and a verifiable reward function, which together constitute an environment. Below, we describe each domain and its corresponding verifier in more detail.

Mathematics:

For mathematical reasoning, we use the training data curated by Open-Reasoner-Zero (Hu et al., 2025), totaling approximately 129K math problems spanning a range of difficulty levels, from high-school mathematics to competition-level problems.55
5

Data accessed from https://github.com/Open-Reasoner-Zero/Open-Reasoner-Zero. This includes the original 57K collection, sourced from AIME (up to 2023), MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021), NuminaMath (LI et al., 2024), and Tulu3 MATH (Lambert et al., 2024), as well as an extended 72K subset carefully filtered from OpenR1-Math-220k.66
6

https://hf.co/datasets/open-r1/OpenR1-Math-220k We use a binary reward based on answer correctness. Following standard practice, a rule-based verifier extracts the model’s final numerical or symbolic answer and compares it to the ground truth using exact match.

Code Generation:

The code generation domain consists of programming problems with test cases used for automatic verification. Our primary source is the TACO dataset (Li et al., 2023), which contains roughly 24K competition-style programming problems collected from online judge platforms such as CodeChef, Codeforces, HackerRank, and GeeksforGeeks, as well as prior datasets including APPS, CodeContests, and Description2Code. These problems span a broad range of algorithmic topics typical of contemporary competitive programming.
The dataset provides difficulty annotations, from which we exclude problems labeled ‘HARD’ and ‘VERY_HARD’ resulting in 24K training problems.
We use a sandboxed environment in which programs produced by the model during training are executed against provided test cases.77
7

https://github.com/bytedance/SandboxFusion
The verifier returns pass/fail diagnostics, and rewards are assigned using a rule-based scheme; a reward of 1 is given only if all test cases pass, and 0 otherwise.

Instruction-Following:

In instruction-following, the model must complete a task such as summarization or creative writing while satisfying one or more verifiable output constraints (Zhou et al., 2023).
We train on the IF-RLVR dataset (Pyatkin et al., 2025), containing 95K instruction-response pairs (with 550 held out for validation), which was used for training OLMo 3 (Olmo Team et al., 2025).
The reward is computed as the proportion of constraints satisfied, ranging from 0 to 1.

Logical Puzzles:

This domain is adapted from the INTELLECT-3 logic environment (Prime Intellect Team et al., 2025), based on SynLogic (Liu et al., 2025a). The dataset consists of 29 puzzle types and 12K tasks spanning symbolic and deductive reasoning, constraint satisfaction, and combinatorial search, including Boolean expressions, grid-based puzzles (e.g., Sudoku), arithmetic puzzles (e.g., Game of 24), and interactive logic games (e.g., Minesweeper). Problems are programmatically generated with adjustable difficulty. Rewards are computed using task-specific verifiers from the i3-logic library.88
8

https://app.primeintellect.ai/dashboard/environments/primeintellect/i3-logic

Function Calling:

The goal of the function calling task is to generate a valid call to an external function or API in response to a user query (Patil et al., 2025). We train and evaluate this domain using all subcategories within the single-turn category of BFCL v4.99
9

https://gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu/blogs/15_bfcl_v4_web_search.html
To compute reward, the predicted function name and arguments are extracted using Python’s ast module. Following BFCL’s evaluation protocol, a prediction is considered correct if the function name matches exactly and all argument values fall within predefined valid sets, rather than requiring exact string matches.

Appendix D Details of Reasoning Trace Analysis

Complementary to the reasoning step analysis in Section 5.2, we further analyze the structure of each reasoning step into eight categories. Four correspond to the non-linear cognitive behaviors identified by Gandhi et al. (2025):
verification, systematically checking intermediate results against solution criteria to confirm or reject a candidate answer; backtracking, explicitly abandoning a failing line of reasoning and pivoting to an alternative approach after recognizing an error or dead end; subgoal setting, decomposing a complex problem into smaller, manageable intermediate targets before solving each one; and backward chaining, reasoning from the desired outcome back toward the inputs, asking what conditions must hold for the goal to be satisfied.
These four behaviors represent non-linear reasoning patterns that go beyond the typical linear, monotonic chain-of-thought commonly observed in LLMs.

To fully account for all content in a reasoning trace, we introduce four additional categories complementary to the above:
problem setup for steps that translate the problem into a workable form, such as interpreting the problem statement, converting representations, or defining variables before solving begins; forward reasoning as the default category for standard linear deduction; final answer, for the steps where the model produces its final answer; and non-productive steps, which capture content that restates a previously established result without adding new computation or verification, including repeated statements of the answer, re-derivation of a conclusion already reached earlier in the trace, and filler meta-commentary that adds no new information.
This last category relates to the overthinking phenomenon documented by Chen et al. (2025), where models waste tokens revisiting already-established results.

Using the same segmentation described in Section 5.2, we classify each step into one of the eight categories above using GPT-5.4. The full step-type distribution is shown in Figure 5.

Fewer non-productive steps and more non-linear reasoning.

The most notable difference is in non-productive steps, which drop from 21% in Apriel-Base to 14% in Apriel-Reasoner, meaning the model wastes fewer tokens on content that does not advance the solution. Meanwhile, the combined share of non-linear reasoning behaviors (verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, and backward chaining) increases from 11% to 17%, suggesting that RL training encourages the model to engage in more of the cognitive behaviors that Gandhi et al. (2025) identified as enabling self-improvement.
