 ReSkill : Reconciling Skill Creation with Policy Optimization in Agentic RL Zelin He Haotian Lin Boran Han Wei Zhu Haoyang Fang Bernie Wang Xuan Zhu Runze Li Matthew Reimherr Code Abstract Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) enables LLM agents to improve continuously from environment rewards, yet the resulting policies do not systematically accumulate reusable strategies that generalize across tasks. Modular skills can provide such reusable strategies, yet existing skill-augmented RL methods decouple skill creation from policy optimization, risking adopting skills that conflict with the evolving policy. Inspired by Anthropic’s Skill Creator, we introduce ReSkill , an RL-in-the-loop skill creation framework that reconciles skill evolution with policy learning. ReSkill exploits the group-wise structure of GRPO to naturally embed three mechanisms with only marginal additional overhead: (1) an assertion-driven skill creator that diagnoses failures from past experience and proposes conditional, trigger-based skill revisions; (2) within-group rollout sampling that enables controlled comparison of skill versions, capturing which version best supports the policy’s ongoing learning; and (3) Thompson Sampling with adaptive discounting to balance exploration and exploitation in skill version selection as the policy evolves. Across several domains, ReSkill consistently outperforms existing memory and skill-based RL methods, with the largest gains on unseen tasks. Analysis of the skill lifecycle shows skills being automatically created, tested, refined, and pruned as the policy improves, demonstrating reconciled skill-policy co-evolution. ReSkill † † footnotetext: ∗ Equal contribution. ‡ Project lead. † Work done during an internship at Amazon. 1 The Pennsylvania State University 2 Amazon IntelliHub 3 AWS AI Labs 1 Introduction Large language models (LLMs) have enabled autonomous agents capable of multi-turn interaction with complex environments ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) . Agentic reinforcement learning (RL), where agents are trained from verifiable environment rewards, has emerged as a promising paradigm ( Jin et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2025 ; Da et al., 2025 ) , yet the resulting policies do not systematically accumulate transferable problem-solving strategies across tasks. Recent work has shown that modular skills , reusable snippets encoding task-specific strategies, can fill this gap ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Zheng et al., 2025 ; Ni et al., 2026 ) . A pioneering effort is Anthropic’s Skill Creator ( Anthropic, 2026 ) , which automates skill creation through an iterative human-in-the-loop testing process (Figure 1 (a)). However, the Skill Creator operates on a static policy at inference time and relies on human feedback. A natural question arises: can we build an RL-in-the-loop skill creation process that dynamically creates and evaluates skills alongside an evolving policy? Recent approaches accumulate experiential knowledge during RL training through memory evolution ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , principle distillation ( Wu et al., 2025 ) , and skill distillation ( Xia et al., 2025 ) . However, they adopt external knowledge without principled testing, treating skill creation and policy learning as separate processes (Figure 1 (b)). Yet this approach risks skill-policy conflict, as externally produced skills may not align with the evolving policy. Ideally, whether a skill benefits learning should be assessed by testing it during policy training itself. In this paper, we propose ReSkill , a framework that reconciles skill creation with policy learning. ReSkill embeds the Anthropic Skill Creator cycle into the RL training loop and makes use of the group-wise structure of GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) : each training step samples a group of rollouts from the same task under the same policy, allowing us to assign competing skill versions within the same group and directly compare their effects. To orchestrate this allocation, Thompson Sampling ( Thompson, 1933 ) with adaptive discounting is introduced to balance exploration and exploitation in skill version control as the policy evolves. Each rollout thus serves a triple role : (i) policy optimization via GRPO gradients, (ii) failure diagnosis and skill revision through an assertion-driven creator adapted from Anthropic’s Skill Creator, and (iii) bandit evaluation that accepts or rejects skill versions based on how they support the policy’s ongoing learning. Our method introduces marginal training overhead and requires no additional rollout budget. We compare ReSkill against existing memory and skill-based agentic RL baselines on embodied reasoning (ALFWorld) and agentic search (seven benchmarks) across multiple model scales, with additional experiments on coding (InterCode-SQL), web shopping (WANDS), and science discovery (ScienceWorld). ReSkill achieves substantially improved performance, with the largest gains on unseen and hard tasks. The skill lifecycle analysis shows skills being automatically created, tested, refined, and pruned as the policy improves. Furthermore, the co-evolved policy can adapt to new domains (ALFWorld to ScienceWorld) at test time through skill creation alone, indicating that reconciled training develops a general capacity to follow and benefit from new skills. 2 Preliminaries Agentic Reinforcement Learning with Skills. Conditional Skill Loading. Non-RL methods trail by 22.0–32.3% on ALFWorld and 13.1–14.6% on Search , underscoring the importance of policy optimization; ReSkill lifts over vanilla Grpo by 3.0–14.5%, demonstrating the value of skill guidance. Across all three domains, the gap between ReSkill and the strongest baseline widens as task difficulty increases. 4.3 Framework Analysis Importance of Reconciled Evolution. Prompt and Skill Optimization. The performance of LLM agents is highly sensitive to their guiding instructions ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) . Automated prompt optimization approaches use paraphrasing ( Zhou et al., 2023 ) , LLM-based optimizers ( Yang et al., 2024 ) , or textual gradients ( Pryzant et al., 2023 ; Yüksekgönül et al., 2025 ) to improve instructions, but operate offline. In the skill domain, prior work extracts experiential rules from trajectories ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Zhao et al., 2024 ) , builds open-ended skill libraries ( Wang et al., 2023 ) , and distills reusable workflows from agent experience ( Wang et al., 2024 ) . Anthropic’s Skill Creator ( Anthropic, 2026 ) automates the creation of modular, trigger-based skills with controlled blind testing, but cannot be directly integrated into a reinforcement learning process. These methods produce valuable skills but do not reconcile creation with policy training. Memory and Skill-Based Reinforcement Learning. Recent work brings skill and memory optimization into RL training: Memrl ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) evolves episodic memory alongside policy training, Evolver ( Wu et al., 2025 ) self-distills experience principles retrieved during RL, Skillrl ( Xia et al., 2025 ) distills teacher-model skills with trigger-based activation, and Inspo ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) maintains instruction candidates sampled per rollout group. However, all these approaches treat skill creation and policy learning as separate processes. ReSkill closes this gap by reconciling skill evolution with policy training through within-group testing and reward-based selection. 6 Conclusion We introduced ReSkill , a framework that reconciles skill creation with policy learning by embedding assertion-driven skill revision, within-group skill testing, and adaptive Thompson Sampling into the Grpo training loop at zero additional rollout cost. Across five benchmark domains, ReSkill consistently outperforms existing skill and memory-based RL methods, with the largest gains on unseen tasks. In The Eleventh International Conference on Learning Representations , Cited by: §5 . Appendix A Limitations and Future Work Evolution frequency. As shown in the sensitivity analysis (Figure 9 , Appendix C.7 ), performance on ALFWorld degrades when evolution frequency is too low ([equation]). We observe that infrequent evolution causes the policy to adapt to a fixed skill context; subsequent skill updates then introduce distribution shift that leads to training instability. The evolution frequency remains a hyperparameter requiring per-domain tuning, and we view adaptive evolution scheduling as a promising future direction. Skill invocation. Our trigger-based design loads skills into context whenever trigger conditions match, which avoids additional inference steps but gives the model less control over when skills are applied. A natural alternative is to formulate skills as explicit tool calls, providing fine-grained invocation control at the cost of extra generation steps per decision point. Exploring tool-call formulations that maintain rollout efficiency, for example through batched or amortized skill invocation, is a promising direction. Skill capacity burden. Adding skills to the context increases the cognitive load on the base model, and we observe two failure modes in practice (see case studies in Appendix F ). First, smaller models sometimes conflate skill guidance with environment actions, emitting skill names as if they were executable commands rather than incorporating the skill’s advice into their reasoning. Second, models may over-apply a skill in situations where it is inapplicable, lacking the capacity to recognize when to disengage. Both failures suggest that skill-augmented RL would benefit from training the policy to treat skills as reasoning-time guidance rather than action-time directives, for example through format-aware reward shaping or explicit separation of skill invocation from action generation. Claude Sonnet 4.5 uses the same ReAct prompt under identical evaluation protocols, serving as a proprietary reference. Evolvement Methods. Reme [ Cao et al., 2025 ] is a dynamic procedural memory framework that extracts, stores, and retrieves actionable memories from agent trajectories to iteratively improve performance. Memrl [ Zhang et al., 2026 ] designs a two-phase retrieval mechanism combining semantic matching with Q-value-driven filtering to identify high-utility memories from past experience. In the evolve-only variant, memories are created from base model rollouts and retrieved at inference without policy updates. We additionally construct a Memrl (+RL) variant where the same memory system operates concurrently with GRPO policy optimization: Q-values for memory utility are updated online from training rewards while the policy is simultaneously trained, serving as an RL-based baseline. The best of Reme and Memrl is reported as “Best ‡ ” in Table 1 . Skill-Only Baselines. We use the high-quality skill bank produced by Skillrl ’s [ Xia et al., 2025 ] distillation pipeline (originally created using o3) and directly adopt it as a fixed skill bank to test retrieval-based skill utilization on the base model without RL. We evaluate three retrieval strategies: BM25 keyword matching, HippoRAG [ Gutiérrez et al., 2024 ] which augments retrieval with knowledge-graph traversal for more semantically grounded skill selection, and long-context loading where the entire skill bank is placed in the model’s context window. The best result across these strategies is reported as “Best † ” in Table 1 . RL-Based Methods. Evolver [ Wu et al., 2025 ] operates in alternating cycles of online interaction and offline self-distillation. After each training phase, a distiller LLM analyzes successful and failed trajectories and extracts “guiding principles” (from successes) and “cautionary principles” (from failures), stored in a curated experience base with semantic deduplication. During training, the agent retrieves relevant principles via embedding similarity to guide its reasoning. Principle quality is tracked via Laplace-smoothed success rates, with low-scoring principles pruned periodically. Inspo [ Zhou et al., 2026 ] maintains a population of instruction candidates sampled per rollout group during GRPO training. Each instruction carries an importance weight updated as a moving average of recent rewards. The population is periodically pruned and replenished by an LLM-based optimizer that reflects on failure trajectories and proposes new candidates. At inference, only the highest-weight instruction is used. Skillrl [ Xia et al., 2025 ] distills modular skills from training trajectories using a teacher LLM, where each skill consists of a title, an actionable principle, and a triggering condition. Skills are injected into the policy’s context via template-based retrieval during both training and evaluation. After validation epochs, failed trajectories trigger recursive skill addition: the teacher identifies unaddressed failure patterns and proposes new skills, which are permanently added to the bank. B.3 Benchmark Details ALFWorld. ALFWorld [ Shridhar et al., 2021 ] is an embodied reasoning benchmark where agents interact with household environments via text commands. The action space consists of approximately 14 verb templates (e.g., go to , take , put , open , examine ), with a maximum horizon of 50 steps and binary reward (10 on task completion, 0 otherwise). We train on approximately 3,800 games and group the six task types into three categories: Pick&Place, Transform (averaging Heat, Cool, and Clean), and Examine. Evaluation uses 140 seen and 134 unseen game configurations; we report per-category success rates, seen/unseen averages (micro-averaged over all games in each split), and an overall success rate weighted by split size. Agentic Search. We follow the multi-dataset evaluation protocol of Search-R1 [ Jin et al., 2025 ] . Agents answer factual questions via iterative retrieval: the action space is two actions (issue a search query or submit a final answer), with a maximum of 4 turns and binary reward (1 on exact match, 0 otherwise). Retrieval uses E5-base-v2 dense embeddings over a Wikipedia corpus ([equation]18M passages), returning the top-3 passages per query. We train on [equation]3,000 questions from NQ [ Kwiatkowski et al., 2019 ] and HotpotQA [ Yang et al., 2018 ] . Seen evaluation datasets: NQ (300) and HotpotQA (300). Unseen datasets: PopQA [ Mallen et al., 2023 ] (303), TriviaQA [ Joshi et al., 2017 ] (300), 2WikiMultiHopQA [ Ho et al., 2020 ] (300), MuSiQue [ Trivedi et al., 2022 ] (300), and Bamboogle [ Press et al., 2023 ] (125). We report per-dataset accuracy, seen/unseen averages weighted by dataset size, and overall accuracy across all 1,928 examples. WANDS (Web Shopping). We construct a web shopping environment inspired by WebShop [ Yao et al., 2022 ] , built from the WANDS product search dataset [ Chen et al., 2022 ] due to licensing concerns with the original WebShop data. Agents must find and purchase a product matching a goal specification (category, attributes, configurable options, price constraint). The action space is two actions (search or click), with a maximum of 15 steps and binary reward (10 if the correct product is purchased with all required options selected, 0 otherwise). Tasks are categorized by the number of options that must be correctly selected: 2-opt (two required options) and 3-opt (three required options, harder). Training uses 3,000 products from the Furniture domain; evaluation uses 496 held-out products spanning both seen (Furniture) and unseen (Kitchen & Tabletop) domains. We report accuracy stratified by option complexity (2-opt and 3-opt). ScienceWorld. ScienceWorld [ Wang et al., 2022 ] is an interactive science simulation where agents perform multi-step experiments. We use the electricity split, which contains two task types: identifying power components and testing conductivity. The action space consists of approximately 26 verb templates (e.g., pick up , pour , activate , connect , focus on ), with a maximum of 50 steps and binary reward (10 if task score exceeds 70%, 0 otherwise). For in-distribution evaluation (Test-ID), we use the dev split of 230 episodes with the same task types but held-out variations; for out-of-distribution evaluation (Test-OOD), we use 155 episodes from related but distinct task variants (conductivity with unknown materials, renewable energy). We report ID and OOD accuracy. InterCode-SQL. InterCode-SQL [ Yang et al., 2023 ] evaluates interactive SQL generation over Spider databases [ Yu et al., 2018 ] . Agents iteratively write and execute SQL queries with execution feedback before submitting a final answer; the action space is two actions (execute exploratory query or submit), with a maximum of 10 turns and binary reward (10 on execution result match with the ground truth, 0 otherwise). Following SQL-Trail [ Hua et al., 2026 ] , training uses 1,027 difficulty-balanced queries, filtered via pass@6 to exclude trivially solvable and impossible cases. Evaluation uses 1,000 examples from 20 held-out Spider databases. C.3 Skill Creator Pipeline: Full Details Inspired by Anthropic’s Skill Creator [ Anthropic, 2026 ] , our skill creator pipeline decomposes skill creation into the following stages: reservoir collection, assertion grading, contrastive analysis, insight aggregation and semantic grouping, skill recommendation, and skill authoring. We present each stage in execution order. C.3.1 Reservoir Collection and Contrastive Analysis Each Grpo rollout produces trajectories tagged with a version assignment [equation] and binary reward [equation]. The reservoir [equation] stores these trajectories in a last-in-first-out structure, enforcing diversity by retaining at most one success and one failure per version per task. That is, for each task the reservoir holds up to four episodes (new-success, new-failure, old-success, old-failure), replacing older entries as new rollouts arrive. When skill creation is triggered, the reservoir is sampled into task groups. We prioritize groups with diversity: those containing both successes and failures, and both active and inactive skill conditions. Each group receives a separate LLM call comparing the episodes within it: success vs. failure, new-skill vs. old-skill, and the specific actions at which behavior diverges. The LLM produces a structured insight per group containing: a behavioral finding, a failure-mode label, the failure and success action snippets, an assessment of whether active skills helped or hurt, and a confidence level. This step condenses the raw reservoir into a manageable set of grounded insights for downstream diagnosis. C.3.2 Assertion Grading and Diagnosis Assertion Types. The assertion grader [equation] maintains a dynamic set of rule-based assertions [equation], where each assertion is a predicate over trajectories generated by the LLM-based diagnoser. Assertions capture behavioral properties at varying granularity: whether a particular action pattern appears (or is absent) in a trajectory, whether two actions occur in the correct temporal order (e.g., searching before submitting), whether the agent avoids unproductive repetition, whether the trajectory falls within expected length bounds, and whether the environment’s observations indicate the agent reached certain states. The diagnoser creates, modifies, and deletes assertions as the policy’s failure landscape evolves. Grading. Low pass rates identify prevalent failure modes. The grading is entirely rule-based, enabling evaluation of the full reservoir rather than a subsample. Diagnoser. The diagnoser receives one-liner summaries extracted from the contrastive insights alongside the per-assertion pass rates and the current skill bank summary. C.3.3 Skill Recommendation and Authoring Recommender. The recommender receives the insight groups (with expanded representative insights showing full action snippets), the current skill library with per-skill diagnostics (trigger rate, age), the version history of prior proposals with their accept/reject outcomes, and the environment’s action vocabulary. It proposes 1–3 operations ( add , modify , or delete ), each specifying: the operation type, intent (what to change and why), and supporting evidence (which insight group motivates it). The recommender does not write skill content; it focuses purely on strategic decisions. The recommender is also informed of the skill lifecycle stage: when the library is below capacity it favors add operations to build coverage, and as skills accumulate toward [equation] it shifts toward modify and delete to refine and prune. Beyond lifecycle guidance, it prioritizes unaddressed failure clusters for additions, skills whose guidance contradicts observed successful behavior for modifications, and skills the policy has internalized (chronically low trigger rates or high reward regardless of skill presence) for deletions. DeepSeek-R1 [ Guo et al., 2025 ] demonstrated the effectiveness of RLVR, where Grpo [ Shao et al., 2024 ] enables group-wise advantage estimation without a critic model. DAPO [ Yu et al., 2025 ] introduces training stability improvements such as clip-higher and dynamic sampling. Dr. Grpo [ Liu et al., 2025 ] rectifies length bias. ReSkill is orthogonal to these algorithmic improvements and can be integrated with any Grpo -based training pipeline. Tool-Integrated Agents. LLM agents interact with environments through tool use [ Yao et al., 2023 ] . IRCoT [ Trivedi et al., 2023 ] interleaves chain-of-thought reasoning [ Wei et al., 2022 ] with information retrieval. Toolformer [ Schick et al., 2023 ] teaches tool usage via SFT. The RL paradigm has enabled multi-turn tool interactions [ Feng et al., 2025a , Li et al., 2025 ] , with search tools excelling at question answering [ Jin et al., 2025 , Song et al., 2025 ] . End-to-end RL frameworks for tool use [ Xue et al., 2025 , Jiang et al., 2025 ] have grown rapidly, yet the role of dynamic skill guidance during RL training has been largely unexplored: existing approaches rely on static instructions designed before training begins. Bandit Methods for Online Selection. Bandit algorithms provide a general framework for sequential allocation under uncertainty, balancing exploration and exploitation from partial reward feedback [ Slivkins, 2019 , Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020 ] . Thompson Sampling is a posterior-sampling method for this problem [ Thompson, 1933 , Russo et al., 2018 ] . Recent LLM systems use bandit formulations for adaptive online decisions such as prompt and context selection [ Chen et al., 2024 ] , tool or strategy choice [ Bouneffouf and Feraud, 2025 ] , model routing [ Dai et al., 2024 , Atalar et al., 2025 ] , and inference-time personalization [ Bouneffouf and Feraud, 2025 ] . To our knowledge, there is no direct application of Thompson Sampling to skill evolution inside on-policy agent training, where competing skill versions must be tested using the same rollouts that also update the policy. ReSkill fills this gap with within-group version testing and adaptive discounting. Appendix E Evolved Skill Examples We present representative skills discovered by ReSkill during training.
