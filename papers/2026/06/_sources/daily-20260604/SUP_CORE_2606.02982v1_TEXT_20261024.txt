Recent inference-serving frameworks such as Orca, Nexus, Sarathi, FlexGen, and vLLM have demonstrated significant advances in throughput optimization, memory management, and large-scale LLM serving [ 16 , 19 , 17 , 21 , 20 ] . While inference runtimes such as vLLM improve throughput through continuous batching and optimized memory management, accurately estimating the runtime cost of heterogeneous inference requests remains a major challenge. Existing scheduling approaches often rely on static token limits, user-specified output constraints, or predefined workload assumptions that fail to capture the dynamic behavior of real-world LLM inference workloads. In practice, observed output lengths frequently deviate from expected values due to variations in prompt complexity, response characteristics, model behavior, and workload composition. This phenomenon, referred to as runtime token drift , introduces estimation errors that can propagate into scheduling decisions, leading to workload misclassification, queue imbalance, inflated tail latency, unfair tenant resource allocation, and degraded Quality-of-Service (QoS) under contention. This paper presents DriftSched , an adaptive QoS-aware scheduling framework for multi-tenant LLM inference serving on NVIDIA L4 GPUs. The proposed system combines workload classification, token-budget estimation, tenant-aware queue management, and runtime feedback-driven drift compensation to dynamically estimate inference cost prior to GPU execution. Inference requests are grouped into Premium, Standard, and Batch service tiers and evaluated under multiple scheduling strategies including FIFO, Priority Scheduling, Weighted Scheduling, Shortest-Job-First (SJF), and Aging Priority Scheduling. Unlike static scheduling approaches, DriftSched continuously compares estimated token budgets and observed output lengths collected after inference execution and adaptively updates workload-specific bias factors to compensate for runtime token drift and improve future admission-time scheduling decisions. Experimental evaluation is conducted under heterogeneous multi-tenant inference workloads with varying prompt complexity, output length, arrival rates, and tenant priority under GPU saturation scenarios. Results demonstrate the existence of measurable runtime token drift across workload categories, where static token-budget estimation consistently overestimates actual generation behavior. Across five scheduling policies, the proposed adaptive feedback mechanism reduced workload estimation error by an average of 38.8% (MAE) and 40.5% (RMSE), improving workload classification stability and admission-time scheduling accuracy. The evaluation further shows that scheduling policy selection significantly influences fairness, queue dynamics, tenant QoS, and tail-latency behavior. Among all evaluated schedulers, SJF achieved the best overall performance, reducing median end-to-end latency by approximately 42% and P99 latency by approximately 16% relative to FIFO scheduling under sustained GPU contention. This work contributes an adaptive drift-aware scheduling architecture, a runtime token-drift compensation mechanism, and a reproducible benchmarking framework for evaluating QoS-aware LLM inference scheduling on shared GPU infrastructure. The findings provide practical insights for enterprise AI serving platforms and future adaptive inference runtimes operating under multi-tenant GPU contention. Index Terms: GPU Scheduling, QoS-Aware Scheduling, Multi-Tenant Inference, NVIDIA L4, LLM Inference, Tail Latency, GPU Utilization, AI Infrastructure, Datacenter GPUs, Inference Serving I Introduction The rapid adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) has significantly increased the demand for efficient GPU inference serving in modern AI datacenters. The emergence of large-scale foundation models such as GPT-3 demonstrated the effectiveness of scaling model parameters and training data to achieve strong zero-shot and few-shot reasoning capabilities across a wide range of natural language tasks [ 7 ] . Enterprise AI platforms, cloud inference providers, and edge AI systems increasingly operate in multi-tenant environments where heterogeneous inference requests compete for shared GPU resources. These workloads often exhibit substantial variability in prompt complexity, generated output size, latency sensitivity, and Quality-of-Service (QoS) requirements. While modern inference runtimes such as vLLM improve throughput through continuous batching and optimized memory management, efficient scheduling of concurrent inference requests remains a major challenge under GPU contention. Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) are built upon the Transformer architecture, which introduced self-attention mechanisms capable of modeling long-range dependencies while enabling highly parallelized training and inference workflows [ 6 ] . While prior work such as GDEV-AI [ 1 ] demonstrated that modern CPU architectures have significantly improved deep learning inference performance for offline analytics, background processing, and non-latency-critical AI workloads, real-time large-scale LLM inference serving continues to rely heavily on GPU accelerators due to their superior parallel execution capability and high-throughput tensor processing. As CPUs increasingly absorb portions of asynchronous and non-critical inference workloads, GPUs are becoming more specialized toward latency-sensitive, real-time multi-tenant inference systems where efficient scheduling, fairness, and Quality-of-Service (QoS) management are critical under contention. Unlike traditional offline inference environments where workloads can be processed in bulk or asynchronously, multi-tenant LLM serving platforms must dynamically balance heterogeneous inference requests with varying prompt complexity, observed output lengths, latency sensitivity, and tenant priority. Under GPU saturation, inefficient workload estimation and scheduling can lead to queue imbalance, starvation of smaller requests, increased tail latency [ 8 ] , and degraded QoS for premium tenants. These challenges motivate the need for adaptive runtime-aware scheduling frameworks capable of improving workload estimation and resource allocation in modern GPU inference infrastructure. Furthermore, even as portions of offline and non-critical inference workloads are increasingly shifted toward modern CPU platforms, selecting the appropriate inference-optimized GPU architecture for latency-sensitive LLM serving workloads remains an important challenge. Prior work such as DEEP-GAP [ 2 ] evaluated the architectural behavior of modern inference GPUs including the NVIDIA T4 [ 5 ] and NVIDIA L4 [ 4 ] under deep learning inference workloads, demonstrating substantial differences in throughput scaling, latency behavior, execution parallelism, and efficiency across precision modes and workload sizes. The study highlighted that modern inference accelerators continue to play a critical role in real-time AI serving environments where low latency, high concurrency, and efficient GPU utilization are essential. However, while GPU architectural studies provide insights into raw inference performance characteristics, they do not fully address how concurrent multi-tenant inference requests should be scheduled under contention in practical serving environments. In real-world LLM serving systems, workload behavior is heavily influenced not only by GPU architectural capability, but also by runtime scheduling policies, queue management strategies, workload estimation accuracy, tenant prioritization, and batching dynamics. As a result, efficient QoS-aware scheduling remains a fundamental challenge even on highly optimized inference GPU platforms such as the NVIDIA L4. A key difficulty in LLM inference scheduling is accurately estimating the runtime cost of inference requests before execution. Many existing systems rely on user-defined max_tokens values or static workload assumptions to approximate inference cost. However, the actual generated output length frequently differs from the predicted or allocated token budget. This mismatch between predicted and actual generation behavior introduces a phenomenon referred to in this work as runtime token drift . Runtime token drift occurs when workload-specific token generation characteristics deviate from admission-time estimates due to prompt variability, response complexity, model behavior, and runtime execution dynamics. Under multi-tenant GPU contention, such estimation errors can propagate into scheduling decisions, resulting in workload misclassification, queue imbalance, increased tail latency, and degraded Quality-of-Service (QoS). Misclassification of workload size can negatively impact fairness, latency, and resource utilization during contention. Overestimation of token demand may cause short latency-sensitive requests to be incorrectly classified as long-running workloads, resulting in unnecessary queue delays and underutilization of GPU resources. Conversely, underestimation of runtime cost may allow computationally expensive requests to enter low-latency queues, causing queue imbalance, starvation of smaller requests, increased tail latency, and QoS degradation for premium tenants. Under high concurrency, these effects become amplified as scheduling policies attempt to balance throughput, fairness, and latency objectives simultaneously. Traditional scheduling algorithms such as First-Come First-Serve (FIFO), Priority Scheduling, and Shortest-Job-First (SJF) [ 10 , 9 ] were originally designed for CPU and distributed systems workloads where execution cost is relatively predictable. In modern LLM inference systems, however, runtime behavior depends on several dynamic factors including prompt length, observed output length, KV-cache growth, batching behavior, tenant priority, and GPU memory contention. As a result, static scheduling policies alone are often insufficient for maintaining fairness and predictable latency in multi-tenant inference environments. To address these challenges, this paper presents an adaptive QoS-aware scheduling framework for multi-tenant GPU inference serving on NVIDIA L4 GPUs. The proposed system introduces a workload analysis layer that combines token-budget estimation, workload classification, and tenant-aware queue management to estimate inference cost before GPU execution. Inference requests are grouped into Premium, Standard, and Batch service tiers and evaluated under multiple scheduling strategies including FIFO, Priority Scheduling, Weighted Scheduling, Shortest-Job-First (SJF), and Aging Priority Scheduling. Unlike static scheduling approaches that rely solely on predefined token limits, DriftSched incorporates a runtime feedback mechanism that continuously compares estimated token budgets against observed output lengths collected during inference execution. The framework treats workload estimation as a runtime token drift compensation problem and adaptively updates workload-specific bias factors using execution feedback. This feedback-driven adaptation improves future admission-time scheduling decisions by reducing estimation error and increasing workload classification stability under sustained multi-tenant inference traffic. This work contributes an adaptive workload-aware scheduling architecture for multi-tenant LLM inference serving, a feedback-driven token-budget estimation mechanism, and a comparative evaluation of multiple scheduling strategies under heterogeneous GPU inference workloads. In addition, the study introduces a reproducible benchmarking framework capable of simulating concurrent multi-tenant inference traffic under GPU saturation scenarios. Experimental evaluation analyzes queue wait time, latency, fairness, throughput, GPU utilization, and workload prediction accuracy on NVIDIA L4 GPUs using vLLM-based inference execution. The findings of this study provide practical insights into adaptive QoS-aware scheduling for modern AI inference infrastructure and highlight the importance of runtime-aware workload estimation for future multi-tenant GPU serving systems. I-A Related Work Recent LLM serving systems have focused on improving throughput, memory utilization, and scalability. Nexus introduced scalable GPU cluster scheduling for inference workloads [ 19 ] . Orca proposed iteration-level scheduling and continuous batching mechanisms that improve accelerator utilization for generative models [ 16 ] . Sarathi further improved inference efficiency through chunked-prefill execution and optimized handling of prefill and decode phases [ 17 ] . FlexGen explored high-throughput LLM serving using heterogeneous hardware resources and memory offloading strategies [ 21 ] . More recently, vLLM introduced PagedAttention and efficient KV-cache management techniques that significantly improve LLM serving throughput [ 20 ] . FastServing explored low-latency distributed inference serving for deep learning workloads and highlighted the importance of efficient request dispatching and scalable serving architectures for production AI systems [ 18 ] . Recent research has also investigated fairness and isolation mechanisms in multi-tenant LLM serving environments, highlighting the challenges of balancing tenant QoS, resource sharing, and workload interference under shared GPU infrastructure. Unlike these systems, DriftSched focuses on runtime token drift compensation and adaptive workload estimation for improving admission-time scheduling decisions under multi-tenant GPU contention. II Methodology and System Architecture This section describes the proposed adaptive QoS-aware scheduling framework for multi-tenant LLM inference serving on NVIDIA L4 GPUs. The framework was designed to study how workload estimation accuracy, queue management policies, and scheduling algorithms influence fairness, latency, throughput, and GPU utilization under concurrent inference contention. The complete architecture consists of workload generation, workload analysis, tenant-aware queue management, scheduling engines, GPU inference execution, runtime metrics collection, and adaptive feedback-driven workload estimation. II-A Experimental Workflow The experimental workflow begins with the workload generation layer, which produces heterogeneous inference traffic representing multiple tenants and workload categories. Generated requests are submitted to the API Gateway, where the workload analysis layer estimates inference cost, classifies workload size, and assigns requests into tenant-specific queues. Scheduling policies then determine the order in which requests are dispatched to the GPU inference worker. Runtime metrics collected during inference execution are continuously analyzed to detect and compensate for runtime token drift. The adaptive feedback mechanism compares estimated token budgets against observed output lengths and updates workload-specific bias factors used by future admission decisions. This feedback-driven adaptation improves workload classification stability and reduces estimation error under heterogeneous multi-tenant inference workloads. The API Gateway component was implemented using FastAPI, providing lightweight REST-based request submission and integration between workload generation, queue management, and inference execution services [ 12 ] . The framework supports concurrent inference execution under GPU saturation conditions using vLLM-based inference serving on NVIDIA L4 GPUs. Experimental evaluation compares multiple scheduling algorithms under mixed workload contention scenarios. II-B Workload Generation The workload generator produces concurrent inference requests with heterogeneous prompt complexity and tenant priority levels. II-D Workload Category and Runtime Job Classification The proposed framework separates semantic prompt classification from runtime scheduling classification. Prompt categories represent the logical intent of the inference request, while runtime job types represent estimated computational cost used by the scheduler. The workload classifier initially categorizes prompts into one of four semantic workload categories: • short_qa • summary • technical • report Each semantic category is associated with a baseline token estimate used by the adaptive token estimator. The estimated runtime cost is then mapped into scheduling-oriented job classes: short , medium , long \texttt{short},\quad\texttt{medium},\quad\texttt{long} based on the estimated total token budget. Long-form report workloads typically map to long runtime jobs due to their larger estimated token budgets. This separation between semantic workload category and runtime scheduling classification enables the framework to decouple prompt intent from estimated computational cost, allowing scheduling policies to operate using estimated runtime complexity rather than prompt semantics alone. For example, prompts categorized as technical may exhibit significantly different runtime behavior depending on prompt length, expected output size, and adaptive runtime estimation feedback. As a result, a semantic workload category does not always map to a fixed runtime scheduling class. A technically-oriented prompt with limited expected output may be classified as a medium runtime workload, while a longer technical explanation request may be classified as a long runtime workload. Similarly, summarization tasks may shift between short and medium runtime classifications depending on token-budget estimation and adaptive runtime bias correction. This distinction is important because scheduling policies operate on estimated computational cost rather than semantic intent alone. Runtime scheduling decisions therefore depend on dynamically estimated token budgets rather than static prompt categories. The framework records both semantic workload category and runtime scheduling class within the metrics pipeline to enable comparative analysis between logical workload intent and observed runtime behavior during scheduling evaluation. II-F Scheduling Engine The scheduling engine determines the order in which queued inference requests are dispatched to the GPU inference worker. Multiple scheduling strategies were implemented to evaluate fairness, latency behavior, starvation characteristics, and throughput under contention. The evaluated scheduling policies are inspired by traditional operating system scheduling techniques and large-scale distributed scheduling systems such as Sparrow [ 15 ] , as well as modern multi-tenant accelerator scheduling architectures [ 19 ] . The objective is to study how adaptive workload estimation affects queue behavior and tenant QoS under GPU saturation. II-G Calibration and Evaluation Phases To evaluate adaptive runtime token drift compensation under sustained load, each experiment was divided into two phases: a calibration phase and a stress evaluation phase. A total of 3000 inference requests were generated for each experimental run. The first 1000 requests were used as a calibration phase, during which runtime feedback was collected and workload-specific bias factors were continuously updated. This phase allowed the adaptive estimator to observe workload behavior, compensate for runtime token drift, and gradually converge toward stable workload-specific token estimates. The remaining 2000 requests were used as the stress evaluation phase. During this phase, the scheduler operated using the bias values learned during calibration while continuing to receive runtime feedback and perform online adaptation. This approach ensured that scheduling decisions during the majority of the experiment benefited from previously observed workload behavior while still allowing the estimator to respond to changing workload characteristics. The selected 1:2 calibration-to-evaluation ratio provides a balance between estimator learning and scheduler evaluation. A calibration phase that is too short may not provide sufficient runtime observations for bias convergence, while an excessively long calibration phase reduces the number of requests available for scheduling evaluation. The chosen configuration allows the adaptive estimator to establish stable workload-specific bias values before the system enters sustained high-load operation. Unlike offline training approaches, DriftSched performs continuous online adaptation throughout execution. Runtime token drift compensation therefore occurs within the same experimental cycle used for scheduling evaluation. As additional requests complete, newly observed token-generation behavior is incorporated into future workload estimates through adaptive bias updates. This enables the scheduler to progressively refine workload classification and admission-time scheduling decisions while the workload is actively executing. IV Results and Analysis IV-A Experimental Overview Experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA L4 GPU using the vLLM inference runtime. A total of 3000 inference requests were generated across Premium, Standard, and Batch tenants. Workloads consisted of short question-answering, summarization, technical explanation, and long-form report generation tasks. Five scheduling policies were evaluated: FIFO, Priority Scheduling, Shortest Job First (SJF), Weighted Scheduling, and Aging Priority Scheduling. The workload generation process included an initial calibration phase followed by a sustained stress phase. During the calibration phase, runtime feedback was collected to estimate token-generation drift and update workload-specific bias factors. The stress phase evaluated scheduler behavior under high request volume using the learned estimates. IV-B Semantic Workloads versus Runtime Scheduling Classes Figure 4 illustrates the relationship between semantic workload categories and runtime scheduling classes across all evaluated scheduling policies. The results demonstrate that semantic workload type does not always correspond directly to runtime execution cost. While short question-answering workloads were consistently classified as short jobs and summarization workloads were predominantly classified as medium jobs, report-generation workloads exhibited a mixture of medium and long runtime classifications. This behavior indicates that semantically similar requests may generate substantially different token counts during execution. The observed distribution highlights the limitations of static workload classification approaches that rely solely on semantic labels. Assigning all report-generation requests to a single scheduling category would fail to capture workload variability and may result in inaccurate scheduling decisions. DriftSched addresses this challenge by separating semantic workload classification from runtime scheduling classification. The workload analysis layer first identifies the semantic workload category and then applies adaptive token-budget estimation to determine the expected runtime execution cost. Scheduling decisions are therefore driven by estimated inference cost rather than semantic workload labels alone. Similar classification distributions were observed across FIFO, Priority, Weighted, SJF, and Aging Priority scheduling policies, demonstrating that runtime workload characterization remains largely independent of the selected scheduling algorithm. These results further support the existence of runtime token drift and motivate the need for adaptive workload estimation before admission-time scheduling decisions are made. V Conclusion This paper presented DriftSched, an adaptive QoS-aware scheduling framework for multi-tenant LLM inference serving under runtime token drift. Unlike static scheduling approaches that rely on fixed workload assumptions, DriftSched continuously compares estimated and observed output lengths and uses runtime feedback to refine future workload estimation. Experimental evaluation on NVIDIA L4 GPUs under heterogeneous multi-tenant workloads demonstrated that runtime token drift is a measurable phenomenon that affects workload classification accuracy and scheduling effectiveness. The proposed adaptive feedback mechanism successfully converged toward stable runtime operating regions and reduced estimation error over time through continuous runtime feedback. Results showed that scheduling policy selection significantly influences queue dynamics, tenant QoS, fairness, and latency behavior under GPU saturation. While FIFO provided balanced treatment across tenants and Priority-based schedulers enforced service differentiation, workload-aware scheduling achieved the largest latency improvements. In particular, SJF achieved the lowest end-to-end latency across all evaluated percentile metrics. However, these improvements were obtained by prioritizing workload size rather than tenant class. As a result, SJF optimizes overall system efficiency and latency reduction but does not provide explicit tenant-level QoS guarantees. In contrast, Priority, Weighted, and Aging Priority scheduling offer stronger service differentiation for Premium tenants, highlighting the tradeoff between global latency optimization and tenant-aware fairness. Analysis further showed that GPU execution latency remained largely unchanged across scheduling policies, indicating that observed performance improvements originated primarily from improved queue management and workload estimation rather than changes in model execution cost. These findings highlight the importance of accurate runtime workload characterization for efficient multi-tenant GPU utilization. Across three independent experimental runs using an expanded workload corpus containing approximately 1180 unique prompts, adaptive runtime token drift compensation reduced workload estimation error by approximately 39% in MAE and 40% in RMSE on average across all evaluated scheduling policies. These improvements remained consistent across FIFO, Priority, Weighted, SJF, and Aging scheduling strategies, demonstrating that runtime feedback improves workload estimation independently of the underlying scheduler. The results suggest that adaptive runtime token drift compensation can serve as a practical enhancement for modern LLM inference platforms by improving workload estimation, supporting more informed scheduling decisions, and enabling better QoS under shared GPU contention. The proposed framework complements existing inference-serving architectures such as Orca, Sarathi, Nexus, and vLLM by introducing adaptive workload estimation as an additional scheduling signal for multi-tenant GPU environments [ 16 , 17 , 19 , 20 ] . The experimental workload corpus consisted of approximately 1180 unique prompts spanning short question-answering, summarization, technical explanation, and report-generation tasks. While this substantially increases workload diversity and reduces prompt repetition, production AI systems often experience continuously evolving prompt distributions, user behavior patterns, and application-specific workload characteristics. In addition, experiments were conducted using a single GPU platform and a single LLM model configuration. Consequently, the observed scheduling behavior may vary under different model architectures, workload distributions, and multi-GPU deployment scenarios. This work contributes an adaptive drift-aware scheduling architecture, a runtime token-drift compensation mechanism, and a reproducible benchmarking framework for evaluating QoS-aware LLM inference scheduling on shared GPU infrastructure. The findings demonstrate that runtime token drift is a measurable and practically significant phenomenon in multi-tenant LLM serving systems, and that adaptive feedback-driven estimation can substantially improve workload characterization, scheduling effectiveness, and overall QoS under GPU contention. Future work will investigate online reclassification of queued requests, adaptive queue reordering, multi-GPU scheduling, and integration with production LLM serving frameworks to further improve fairness, utilization, and tail-latency performance at scale. In addition, reinforcement-learning-based scheduling approaches may be explored to dynamically adapt queue assignment, workload prioritization, and resource allocation decisions based on observed runtime behavior, workload characteristics, and system utilization patterns [ 14 ] .
