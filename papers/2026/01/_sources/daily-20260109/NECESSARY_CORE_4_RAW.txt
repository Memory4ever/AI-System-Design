L99: ### 3.1 Metrics
L100: #### Intraclass correlation coefficient.
L101: 
L102: To quantify reliability, we adopt the ICC, following guidelines from reliability research and annotation studies (cite64†Koo and Li, 2016 ; cite96†Hayes and Krippendorff, 2007 ). ICC is appropriate in our setting because the same set of raters evaluates the same set of items on a given scale, and we are interested in absolute agreement in numeric scores rather than mere rank correlation.
L103: For a fixed scale, let $Y_{ij}$ denote the score assigned to item $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ by rater $j\in\{1,\dots,k\}$. We model these scores using a two-way random-effects ANOVA with random item and rater effects:
L104: 
L105:  | $$Y_{ij}=\mu+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}+\varepsilon_{ij},$$  |  | (1)
L106: where $\mu$ is the grand mean, $\alpha_{i}$ is the random effect of item $i$, $\beta_{j}$ is the random effect of rater $j$, and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ is the residual error. Conceptually, both items and raters (humans or LLMs) are treated as samples from larger populations: questions from a broader task distribution and raters from a broader population of human annotators or model instances.
L107: Following the notation of cite64†Koo and Li (2016) , we report two absolute-agreement ICCs derived from this model. The first, $\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)$, measures the reliability of a single rater drawn from the population. The second, $\mathrm{ICC}(A,k)$, measures the reliability of the mean score obtained by averaging across $k$ raters. Both are computed from the ANOVA mean squares for items ($MS_{I}$), raters ($MS_{R}$), and residual error ($MS_{E}$). For $n$ items and $k$ raters, the formulas are:
L108:  | $$\mathrm{ICC(A,1)}=\frac{MS_{I}-MS_{E}}{MS_{I}+(k-1)\,MS_{E}+\frac{k}{n}(MS_{R}-MS_{E})},$$  |  | (2)
L109: 
L110: and
L111: 
L112:  | $$\mathrm{ICC(A,}k\mathrm{)}=\frac{MS_{I}-MS_{E}}{MS_{I}+\frac{1}{n}(MS_{R}-MS_{E})}.$$  |  | (3)
L113: 
L114: These statistics take values in $(-\infty,1]$, where higher values indicate greater reliability.
L115: #### Normalized mean absolute error.
L116: 
L117: To quantify the absolute disagreement between LLM and human judgments, we report the normalized mean absolute error (nMAE). This metric measures the average absolute deviation between model scores and the corresponding human reference scores.
L118: Let $\bar{y}_{i}^{(m)}$ denote the mean score assigned by LLMs to data sample $i$, averaged across all evaluated models. Similarly, let $\bar{y}_{i}^{(h)}$ denote the mean score assigned by human raters to the same data sample. The mean absolute error (MAE) is defined as
L119: 
L120:  | $$\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bar{y}_{i}^{(m)}-\bar{y}_{i}^{(h)}\right|,$$  |  | (4)
L121: To ensure comparability across tasks with different scoring ranges, we normalize MAE by the scale range $R$, defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum possible scores on the rating scale. The resulting normalized MAE is
L122: 
L123:  | $$\mathrm{nMAE}=\frac{1}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bar{y}_{i}^{(m)}-\bar{y}_{i}^{(h)}\right|.$$  |  | (5)
L124: By construction, nMAE takes values in $[0,1]$ when model predictions are constrained to the same rating scale as human judgments, with lower values indicating closer agreement between the LLM and the human consensus.
L125: ### 3.2 Human and LLM Reliability
L126: 
L127: For each grading scale, we compute ICCs separately for different rater groups and then compare these values across scales and benchmarks. Throughout, we use the generic ICC notation above and instantiate $n$ and $k$ according to the item set and rater pool being analyzed.
L128: #### Human reliability.
L129: To establish a human reliability baseline, we form an $S\times R$ rating matrix containing all human scores for a given scale, where $S$ is the number of items (questions) and $R$ is the number of human raters. We then compute $\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)$ and $\mathrm{ICC}(A,R)$. This quantifies both the reliability of a single human annotator and the reliability of the averaged human panel.
L130: We also compute ICCs separately for the female raters and the male raters to examine potential subgroup differences in reliability. In addition, we repeat the same ICC computations on each benchmark subset, yielding human reliability estimates both on the pooled item set and on each individual benchmark.
L131: #### LLM reliability.
L132: For inter-rater reliability among LLM judges, we construct an $N\times L$ rating matrix of LLM scores for a given scale, where $N$ is the number of items scored by the LLMs (e.g., all items in a benchmark) and $L$ is the number of LLM judges. We compute $\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)$ and $\mathrm{ICC}(A,L)$, which measure, respectively, how consistent a single LLM judge is with the broader pool of LLM judges and how reliable an ensemble average over $L$ judges would be.
L133: As with humans, we also recompute ICCs on each benchmark subset to understand how model-model agreement varies across domains.
L134: #### Human-LLM agreement.
L135: We assess the alignment between model predictions and human judgments by using the human consensus. Rather than comparing LLMs to individual human raters, we operationalize human judgment as the mean score of the annotator panel for each item, denoted as $\bar{y}^{(h)}_{i}$. This approach mitigates individual annotator variance and focuses on the model’s ability to capture the collective human signal. We conduct this analysis at two levels using the $\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)$.
L136: First, to evaluate the alignment of the LLM panel with humans, we form an $S\times 2$ matrix for each scale, where the first column is the mean human score and the second is the LLM ensemble mean across the $L$ LLM judges (models), $\bar{y}^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y^{(m)}_{i\ell}$. We then compute $\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)$ on this two-column matrix to measure the absolute agreement between the human consensus and the model consensus.
L137: Second, to evaluate individual models, we analogously form an $S\times 2$ matrix consisting of the mean human score and the specific model’s score, and compute $\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)$ for each LLM. This measures how closely a specific model aligns with the human consensus.
L138: ## 4 Experiments
L139: 
L140: We conduct two sets of experiments: (1) LLM inter-scale agreement and (2) human-LLM agreement. Figure cite105†1 summarizes our experimental pipeline.
L141: ### 4.1 Data
L142: Our study uses items drawn from six established benchmarks: MT-Bench (cite53†Zheng et al., 2023 ), MoralChoice (cite106†Scherrer et al., 2023 ), STS-B (cite107†Cer et al., 2017 ), SummEval (cite108†Fabbri et al., 2021 ), ToxiGen (cite109†Hartvigsen et al., 2022 ), and TruthfulQA (cite110†Lin et al., 2022 ). For LLM inter-scale agreement, we use all data points from each benchmark. For the human-LLM agreement, we sample 150 items total, with 25 items sampled from each benchmark.
L143: We treat these items as a sample from a broader distribution of tasks within our target domain and analyze reliability both on the pooled set of 150 items and on each benchmark subset of 25 items. Refer to Appendix cite38†B for detailed dataset overview.
L144: ### 4.2 Raters and scales
L145: Twelve graduate student annotators (six female and six male) participate as human raters. Each annotator scores every item on every scale, yielding a fully crossed item-rater design for the human group.
L146: In parallel, we include six LLMs from diverse model families: gpt-4o (cite111†Hurst et al., 2024 ), gemini-2.5-flash (cite112†Comanici et al., 2025 ), Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (cite113†Grattafiori et al., 2024 ), Qwen3-32B (cite114†Yang et al., 2025 ), DeepSeek-v3.2 (cite115†Liu et al., 2025a ), and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (cite116†Jiang et al., 2023 ). We use GPT, Gemini, Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Mistral as abbreviations throughout the paper, referring to the above models.
L147: Each model acts as an automatic judge and scores items on all scales. For both human and LLM raters, we treat scores as real-valued quantities. Raters are instructed to stay within the specified scale bounds but are allowed to use fractional values. This reflects common expert-annotation practice, where intermediate values are used to distinguish between different levels of certainty.
L148: We use three grading scales: 0-5, 0-10, and 0-100. Each scale is applied separately. Fractional scoring is allowed on all scales, which isolates the effect of numeric range independent of discretizations. Human raters complete three rating blocks (one per scale) in randomized order, and within each block, item order is shuffled to reduce anchoring and fatigue effects.
L149: LLMs are prompted with a standardized judging template that specifies the rubric (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness) and the target scale, and they are instructed to output a single numeric score within the requested range. Refer to Appendix cite40†D for detailed human rater instructions.
L150: ### 4.3 Executions
L151: For all LLM judges, we set the default temperature to 1. We also use non-thinking mode for all models. We run all experiments independently for each of the three grading scales. For LLM inter-scale agreement, we compute per-benchmark and per-judge ICC-based consistency estimates across scales. We linearly map all scores to [0,1] before computing inter-scale ICC for interpretability.
L152: For human-LLM agreement, we compute: (i) human-only ICCs at the pooled and per-benchmark levels, (ii) LLM-only ICCs at the pooled and per-benchmark levels, and (iii) pooled human-LLM ICCs, per-model human-LLM ICCs, and per-gender human-LLM ICCs at each scale.
L153: Comparing these quantities across scales allows us to answer three questions: (1) how human and LLM reliability change as the score range becomes finer, (2) whether the scale that yields the most self-consistent LLM judgments also maximizes agreement with human panels, and (3) how these patterns vary across benchmarks, judge models, and genders.

L176: We first examine whether the same LLM assigns consistent scores to the same items when the only protocol change is the grading scale. We would expect near-perfect agreement across all scales if scale choices are unimportant (e.g., a linear rescaling of an internal latent score).
L177: Table cite117†1 shows that this expectation does not hold in general. On benchmarks with comparatively objective or mixed criteria such as STS-B, ToxiGen, and MoralChoice, inter-scale agreement is high across models. In contrast, on more subjective, open-ended quality benchmarks (MT-Bench and SummEval), inter-scale agreement drops substantially, especially for smaller or less capable judges.
L178: This indicates that on ambiguous tasks, scale choice can change how an LLM follows and executes the rubric, not just where it places scores within a range. Moreover, these findings have two implications. First, results from LLM-as-a-judge studies using different grading scales are not necessarily comparable without explicit cross-scale examination.
L179: Second, because inter-scale self-consistency is imperfect and benchmark-dependent, it becomes important to ask: if scales are not interchangeable, under which scale do LLM scores align most closely with human judgments?

L271: ### 5.3 Ablation study
L272: 
L273: A potential confound in LLM-as-a-judge evaluation is decoding stochasticity. To test robustness, we rerun the pooled human-LLM agreement analysis for Llama and Gemini under four temperatures ($T\in\{0.1,0.4,0.7,1.0\}$) following cite54†Lee et al. (2025) , keeping prompts, rubrics, and fractional scoring rules fixed. We select these two models because their 0-5 and 0-10 ICCs are the closest. We would like to explore whether different decoding strategies could reorder the scales.
L274: For each temperature, we recompute per-model human alignment. As shown in Table cite123†6 , agreement is largely insensitive to temperature within each scale. The ordering across scales remains relatively stable, and the gap between 0-5 and 0-100 remains close. This indicates that the main conclusions of this work are not an artifact of decoding strategy; rather, scale-dependent calibration differences dominate over sampling randomness.
L285: #### Metrics validation.
L286: Figure cite124†2 provides empirical evidence of (1) why ICC and nMAE can disagree, (2) why reporting both is informative, and (3) why ICC is preferred over Pearson correlations. nMAE measures the average absolute distance between the LLM consensus score and the human consensus score, normalized by the scale range, so it can remain small even when the judge fails to capture item-to-item variability.
L287: ICC, on the other hand, reflects absolute agreement relative to the variance structure of the items and penalizes systematic offsets and variance mismatches. Therefore, this is why we have the case of SummEval, where correlation is relatively high but ICC is much lower, as well as the case of STS-B, where both correlation and ICC are high.
L288: ## 6 Discussions
L289: Across six benchmarks and multiple judge models, our results show that the numeric score range is not a simple prompt choice in LLM-as-a-judge. On subjective, open-ended evaluation tasks, LLM scores are not perfectly self-consistent across scales, and human-LLM alignment shifts materially with the grading interface even when within-population panel reliability remains high.
L290: Aggregated across tasks, a 0-5 scale yields the strongest absolute agreement between LLM judges and human consensus (highest ICC and lowest normalized MAE), while 0-10 is consistently the weakest choice, and this ordering is stable under temperature perturbations for representative models. At the same time, pooled reliability can mask substantial benchmark heterogeneity.
L291: LLM panels appear highly reliable largely because objective-like tasks dominate aggregate variance, strengthening the need to report reliability and agreement at the benchmark (and rater-subgroup) level. Taken together, these findings motivate treating scale design and diagnostic reporting as standard components of LLM evaluation design protocols, rather than fixed conventions, especially when judging ambiguous, high-variance generations.
L292: ## Limitations
L293: Our study has several limitations that constrain generality. The human raters are all graduate students and thus do not represent the broader populations that produce many large-scale annotations used in modern NLP pipelines. Their educational background, familiarity with LLM systems, and cultural context may influence both calibration and reliability, especially on subjective benchmarks.
L294: Moreover, some items, particularly in open-ended and truthfulness-focused benchmarks, are inherently difficult even for humans. When questions are under-specified or require specialized knowledge, human scores may reflect uncertainty or differing interpretations of the rubric; such uncertainty can lower agreement with LLMs and may also make ICC estimates sensitive to benchmark composition.
L295: ## Ethical considerations
L296: Our study involves ethical considerations related to the interpretation of evaluative judgments, particularly for benchmarks that require normative or subjective assessment. Scores produced by LLM-based judges on tasks such as toxicity detection and moral evaluation should not be interpreted as objective ground truth, as these judgments reflect context-dependent standards embedded in the models.
L297: Similar considerations apply to human annotations: human ratings in such benchmarks reflect the perspectives of a specific annotator population and may not generalize to broader moral or social norms. Moreover, both automated and human evaluators may be exposed to sensitive, offensive, or potentially harmful content in certain benchmarks, which calls for caution in the interpretation and downstream use of evaluation results.

L122: ### 3.2 Method
L123: 
L124: We now describe Disclaimer Injection, a simple data-level procedure that renders text unlearnable by prepending alignment-triggering content to otherwise ordinary inputs. The method is fully model-agnostic: it requires no access to model parameters, gradients, architectures, or optimisation procedures, and can be applied as a pre-processing step to any training dataset.
L125: The core mechanism relies on disclaimers, i.e., short natural-language sequences that explicitly convey alignment-relevant information, such as safety, risk, or liability constraints. These disclaimers are designed to reliably activate alignment-related processing in modern LLMs. To generate them, we employ an independent text generator $\mathcal{M}$. In practice, we prompt the GPT-5.1 model with the instruction shown below, which produces a diverse set of disclaimers.
L126: The resulting disclaimers provide a strong yet controlled alignment signal. They are deliberately constrained by a fixed token budget and written in explicit, unambiguous language, avoiding figurative or implicit phrasing that might weaken alignment activation.
L127: For each training instance, we randomly sample a disclaimer from this pool and concatenate it with the original text to form a protected input (see cite65†Table 1 for an example). No fixed template is used, preventing the model from overfitting to a particular disclaimer structure or position. Disclaimers are inserted as prefixes, while suffix and inline insertion are also supported. The complete procedure is summarised in cite66†Algorithm 1 .
L128: The algorithm runs in linear time with respect to dataset size and can be applied as a drop-in transformation independent of the downstream model and fine-tuning strategy. The transformed examples will induce noticeably different representation patterns from the original one, as observed in cite67†Figure 3 .

L158: ### 3.3 Causal Analysis
L159: 
L160: We analyse our method from the perspective of internal alignment-related layers by performing a layer-wise causal analysis of the transformer forward pass. The goal is to identify which layers are responsible for triggering alignment behaviour and to understand how training on unlearnable data alters information flow within the model.
L161: We model a transformer as a computational graph and follow prior work on causal interventions in LLMs cite70†Zhang et al. (2025) . Specifically, we estimate the causal contribution of each layer $l$ by intervening on it during the forward pass and measuring the resulting change in the output distribution $p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})$. The layer-wise causal effect at generation step $t$ is quantified as
L162:  |  | $\displaystyle\xi_{\theta}(l,t;x)=$  |  | (5)
L163:  |  | $\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\;\middle\|\;p_{\theta}^{(-l)}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\right),$  |
L164: 
L165: where $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)$ denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence; $p_{\theta}^{(-l)}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})$ denotes the output distribution of an interventional variant of the neural network, where the computation of layer $l$ is skipped while all other layers remain unchanged.
L166: Intuitively, $\xi_{\theta}(l,t;x)$ measures how strongly the model’s output at step $t$ depends on the presence of layer $l$. Computing this quantity across layers and generation steps yields a causal effect map (see cite71†Figure 4 ), which characterises how information is routed through the network during generation.
L167: Comparing causal effect maps between a standard fine-tuned model ($f_{\text{ft}}$) and a model trained on unlearnable data ($f_{\text{un}}$) reveals clear qualitative differences. For the same inputs, certain layers $l^{\ast}$ exhibit negligible causal influence in $f_{\text{ft}}$, i.e., $\xi_{\theta_{\text{ft}}}(l^{\ast},t)\approx 0$, but become consistently active in $f_{\text{un}}$, with $\xi_{\theta_{\text{un}}}(l^{\ast},t)>0$ across generation steps.
L168: These discrepancies indicate a systematic re-routing of internal computation induced by training on unlearnable data.
L169: From an interpretative standpoint, the layers $l^{\ast}$ are closely associated with alignment behaviour. In $f_{\text{ft}}$, ordinary task inputs do not substantially engage these layers. In contrast, the same inputs in $f_{\text{un}}$ strongly depend on them, suggesting that Disclaimer Injection causes otherwise benign content to be processed through alignment-related pathways.
L170: To further validate the functional role of these layers, we construct a hybrid model by replacing the $l^{\ast}$ layers in $f_{\text{ft}}$ with the corresponding layers from $f_{\text{un}}$. The resulting model exhibits behaviour similar to $f_{\text{un}}$: it fails to respond correctly to fine-tuning task inputs while remaining largely unaffected on unrelated queries. This intervention provides direct causal evidence that the identified layers mediate the observed degradation in task learning.
L171: Overall, our analysis shows that training on unlearnable data persistently activates alignment-related layers during the forward pass, even for ordinary inputs. By seizing internal information flow in this manner, Disclaimer Injection causes protected data to be processed as alignment-relevant, leading to refusal-like or incorrect outputs and effectively preventing task-specific learning.

L176: ### 4.1 Experimental Setup
L177: The experiments are conducted on HotpotQA cite73†Yang et al. (2018) , TruthfulQA cite74†Lin et al. (2022) , FreshQA cite75†Vu et al. The base model is LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct cite77†Dubey et al. (2024) , fine-tuned via supervised learning with a LoRA adapter cite78†Hu et al. (2022) .

Decoding settings are kept consistent across experiments unless specified otherwise. Code and data are available at cite80†https://github.com/cat-claws/unlearnable†github.com .
L179: We compare the following approaches: standard fine-tuning without protection (“No alteration”), perturbation-based baselines BAE cite38†Garg and Ramakrishnan (2020) and Seq2Sick cite39†Cheng et al. BAE and Seq2Sick both introduce minimal perturbations that preserve semantics while exploiting nontrivial sensitivities to impair model accuracy.
L180: Evaluation metrics include BLEU cite81†Papineni (2002) , ROUGE-1/2/L cite82†Lin (2004) , and an LLM-based judge. The judge is implemented using GPT-5.1 via the OpenAI API cite27†Achiam et al. (2023) , which outputs a binary correctness decision based on factual agreement with the reference answer while ignoring stylistic variation. The judge score serves as a semantic analogue to Exact Match cite83†Rajpurkar et al. (2016) . Unless otherwise specified, all experiments follow this setup.

Judge accuracy declines only slightly from 39.68 (no alteration) to 39.27 (BAE) or 38.70 (Seq2Sick), with BLEU and ROUGE largely preserved. In contrast, our method causes a much sharper degradation, reducing judge accuracy to 4.01 (which is over 8.7$\times$ drop) and BLEU to 5.58 (3.8$\times$ drop), demonstrating that even single-fact supervision becomes substantially harder for the model to internalise.
L213: The relatively modest impact of BAE and Seq2Sick can be attributed to their reliance on surface-level perturbations. While these methods reduce lexical overlap (reflected in moderate drops in BLEU and ROUGE), they largely preserve the semantic relationship between inputs and supervision, allowing models to recover the training signal during fine-tuning. This limitation is particularly pronounced on FreshQA, where semantic constraints are tight and paraphrasing offers minimal obfuscation.
L214: In contrast, our method consistently produces significant, aligned drops across both overlap-based metrics (with a 2.1$\times$ drop on average) and judge accuracy (with a 2.4$\times$ drop on average). The concurrent degradation of BLEU, ROUGE, and judge accuracy indicates that the approach disrupts learnability at a deeper level than surface-form variation, impairing the model’s ability to internalise or reconstruct the training signal even under semantic evaluation.
L215: Overall, these results demonstrate a clear and consistent utility–protection trade-off, with Disclaimer Injection providing substantially stronger unlearnability guarantees across diverse benchmarks.
L216: ### 4.3 Adaptive Attack
L217: 
L218: cite85†Image: Refer to caption Figure 5: Performance under adaptive strategies from learning, e.g., with rephrasing texts (empty bars).
L219: We next evaluate whether our method remains effective under adaptive learning strategies, in which an adversary attempts to recover a learnable training dataset by paraphrasing the protected data. Paraphrasing represents a natural adaptive attack: by applying semantic-preserving rewrites, an adversary may undo surface-level corruption and restore alignment between inputs and supervision.
L220: If a protection mechanism primarily disrupts lexical or syntactic form, such paraphrasing should substantially recover model performance.
L221: To simulate this attack, we paraphrase each training example using an LLM instructed to preserve the original semantics, constraints, and target answers. Paraphrasing is performed deterministically, producing exactly one rewrite per example. Models are then fine-tuned on the paraphrased datasets under identical training settings and evaluated on the original test sets using the same metrics and judge configuration.
L222: As shown in cite86†Figure 5 , perturbation-based baselines (BAE and Seq2Sick) remain highly learnable both before and after paraphrasing. Across metrics, their performance stays within approximately $\pm 2.6$% of standard (unprotected) training, and judge accuracy remains high in both conditions. In contrast, our method consistently enforces unlearnability.
L223: Performance remains strongly suppressed, with over 70% degradation relative to unprotected training, and paraphrasing alters results by less than 3% across all metrics. Consequently, the performance gap between our method and perturbation-based baselines remains large, demonstrating that Disclaimer Injection achieves stable, semantics-robust unlearnability rather than fragile, surface-level disruption, even in the presence of adaptive paraphrasing attacks.

L232: 
L233: We assess whether our method generalises across model families, scales, and training regimes, corresponding to a model-adaptive adversary who varies architectures or checkpoints in an attempt to recover a learnable training dataset. cite90†Figure 7 reports results for five representative models spanning different families and capacities. L236: These results demonstrate that the effectiveness of our method is largely invariant to model choice. L237: ## 5 Conclusion
L238: In this work, we study the feasibility of preventing large language models from effectively learning through shared textual data and introduce Disclaimer Injection, a black-box data protection method that requires no access to model internals. By injecting alignment-relevant signals into training inputs, the approach reduces task-specific learnability during fine-tuning while preserving human readability.
L239: Layer-wise causal analysis shows that this effect arises from systematic activation of alignment-related components, even for otherwise benign inputs. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the resulting reduction in learnability is robust across datasets, fine-tuning strategies, and model families, and remains stable under adaptive attacks such as paraphrasing.
L240: Together, these results show that alignment mechanisms can be exploited in a model-agnostic way to provide practical and robust control over data learnability by modern LLMs.
L241: ## Limitations
L242: The effectiveness of Disclaimer Injection depends on the presence of alignment mechanisms in the target model. Because the method exploits alignment-related behaviour, models that are weakly aligned or intentionally trained without safety constraints may not respond to the injected signals, limiting its applicability in such cases. However, most contemporary LLMs are explicitly aligned for safety, making this assumption realistic in practice.
L243: For less aligned models, future work may explore combining Disclaimer Injection with complementary data-level protections or adapting the injected signals to target alternative inductive biases beyond alignment. One potential risk is that the technique can be repurposed as a data poisoning or sabotage tool.

L86: ## 3 Efficient Layer-Specific Optimization
L87: 
L88: As established in Section cite7†2 , conventional PEFT methods like LoRA and selective layer training suffer from a significant computational bottleneck. Although they reduce the number of trainable parameters, they still require the forward pass to be computed across the entire model architecture. This results in minimal training speedup over FFT.
L89: To overcome this fundamental limitation, we propose Efficient Layer-Specific Optimization (ELO). The core idea of ELO is to detach a small subset of specific layers from the original model before pretraining. This action creates a much smaller, independent model for the CP phase. This layer detachment approach directly solves the overhead problem by drastically reducing not only the trainable parameters but also the total parameters computed during the forward pass.
L90: The ELO method comprises two main stages: (1) ELO pretraining and (2) layer alignment.
L91: cite57†Image: Refer to caption Figure 1: Description of the proposed ELO training process
L92: ### 3.1 ELO Pretraining
L93: The initial stage involves detaching specific layers from the original model for pretraining, as shown in Figure cite58†1 . The language model comprises $n$ decoder layers $\mathbf{L}=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n}\}$, the token embedding layer $\ell_{e}$, and the head layer $\ell_{h}$.
L94: We define the set of specific layers that comprise the ELO model as $\lambda\subset\mathbf{L}$, where $\theta^{e}$ and $\theta^{h}$ represent parameters for $\ell_{e}$ and $\ell_{h}$, respectively, and $\theta^{\lambda}$ represents parameters for $\lambda$. We selected $\lambda$ to encompass the first and last decoder layers, i.e., $\lambda=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{n}\}$. The pretraining process can be expressed as follows:
L95:  | $\displaystyle\theta_{\text{ELOM}}$  | $\displaystyle=\{\theta^{e},\theta^{h},\theta^{\lambda}\}$  |  | (1)
L96:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{PT}}$  | $\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{|D_{\text{pt}}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|x_{i}|}\log{P(x_{j}|x_{<j};\theta_{\text{ELOM}})}$  |  | (2)
L97: The ELO model was trained using each sample from the pretraining dataset $D_{pt}$, with the English-{Target Language} ratio set to 1:9 according to Equation cite59†2 . In this context, $\mathcal{L}_{PT}$ represents the causal language-modeling loss function, with $\theta_{0}$ denoting the parameters of the original model. Only the parameters $\theta^{\lambda}$ of the ELO model are trained to infuse knowledge of the target language.
L98: ### 3.2 Layer Replacement and Alignment
L99: The second stage transfers knowledge learned during the first stage back to the original model $(\theta_{0})$. We replace $\theta^{\lambda}$ in the original model $\theta_{0}$ with $\theta_{\text{ELOM}}$. By replacing these two layers, it is possible to inject knowledge of the target language into specific layers while preserving the finely tuned token embedding and head layers, as well as existing layers rich in English knowledge.
L100: However, because this method modifies only the parameters of specific layers in the original model, aligning these layers requires further pretraining using a small dataset. Accordingly, we introduce a layer alignment step after replacement, wherein FFT is applied to the entire model using an additional 1GB of training data.
L101: ### 3.3 Bilingual Instruction Tuning
L102: 
L103: Because the model that has undergone the layer alignment process is a pretrained model, it exhibits limited capability in following user instructions. To efficiently improve instruction-following performance in the target language with less data, we first adopt the chat vector method cite60†Huang et al. (2024) .
L104: This method extracts knowledge by calculating the deviation ($\theta_{\text{chat vector}}=\theta_{\text{Inst}}-\theta_{\text{PT}}$) between a pretrained model ($\theta_{\text{PT}}$) and an instruction-tuned model ($\theta_{\text{Inst}}$). The extracted $\theta_{\text{chat vector}}$ is then integrated into our layer-aligned model to efficiently transfer the instruction-following capabilities. After integrating the chat vector, we then conducted supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
L105: For SFT, we utilized 31K instruction data extracted in a 1:1 ratio in both the target language and English from the ShareGPT-style dataset cite61†Devine (2024) , which contains dialogue records from language models such as GPT4 cite62†OpenAI (2023) . Details of the dataset can be found in Appendix cite40†A.3 .

L206: #### Comparison of Training Speed Based on Pretraining Data Size
L207: As mentioned in Section cite7†2 and Section cite10†3 , limiting the number of trainable parameters in a model does not significantly reduce training time unless the model’s overall size is reduced. L209: ## 6 Conclusion
L210: In this paper, we proposed Efficient Layer-Specific Optimization (ELO) to address the computational bottleneck of continual pretraining (CP) in MLLMs. Existing PEFT methods like LoRA offer minimal training speedup because they must compute the forward pass across the entire model. ELO overcomes this via a layer detachment strategy By training a small subset of critical layers (the first and last) as a smaller, independent model, ELO drastically reduces the parameters computed during the CP phase.
L211: This approach minimizes GPU memory consumption during this pretraining phase and enables significant acceleration.
L212: Our experimental results demonstrate that ELO achieves a training speedup of up to 6.46 times compared to FFT. It also yields superior qualitative performance in target languages by up to 6.2%, while effectively preserving source language capabilities. This work establishes ELO as a highly efficient and effective alternative for multilingual adaptation.
L213: ## 7 Limitations
L214: 
L215: The ELO method minimizes GPU memory usage during the pretraining phase and accelerates the overall training process; however, it still has the following two limitations.
L216: #### Even a minimal amount of FFT is required
L217: 
L218: While our experiments have shown that layer alignment with a minimal amount of data, such as 1GB, is sufficient, the layer alignment phase remains essential. Since this phase requires training all the parameters of the original model, it demands more GPU memory than ELO pretraining. Therefore, it does not reduce the peak GPU memory requirement in the overall training process.
L219: #### Investigation of CP experiments with over 1TB of data
L220: The performance of the FFT and ELO methods has not been verified when the data size exceeds 1TB. Unfortunately, it was impossible to conduct experiments with larger, high-quality datasets, as finding such data was difficult. Additionally, it is estimated that experimenting with larger models and larger datasets would require a significant amount of time, making these experiments infeasible.
L221: Therefore, while the ELO method demonstrated superior performance over FFT with datasets up to 200GB, further experiments with larger data sizes are necessary.

L81: ## 2 Method: e5-omni
L82: cite52†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2: Overview of e5-omni.
L83: Given omni-modal inputs, e5-omni augments a VLM backbone with three lightweight components: (1) Modality-aware temperature calibration computes a modality-composition indicator $w(x)$ and applies learnable modality-specific temperatures $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ to calibrate logits $\ell(q,p)$; (2) Controllable negative curriculum progressively masks easy negatives and optimizes a masked debiased objective $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCL}}$; (3) Batch whitening and covariance alignment whitens batch embeddings and adds a CORAL-style covariance regularizer.
L84: In this section, we present e5-omni, a lightweight explicit-alignment framework that transforms a VLM into a unified omni-modal embedding model. As shown in the left part of Fig. cite53†2 , e5-omni preserves the backbone and introduces a simple training recipe.
L85: Our framework comprises three components: (1) Modality-aware temperature calibration, which balances contrastive sharpness across modalities; (2) A controllable negative curriculum, which maintains a strong and stable learning signal; and (3) Batch whitening and covariance alignment, which harmonizes the shared-space geometry under heterogeneous inputs. Together, these components improve the robustness of omni-modal embeddings without architectural modifications.
L86: ### 2.1 Preliminaries
L87: We study omni-modal embedding with a base modality set $\mathcal{M}_{0}=\{\texttt{T},\texttt{I},\texttt{A},\texttt{V}\}$, corresponding to text, image, audio, and video. Training data consist of tuples $(q,p^{+},\mathcal{P}^{-})$, where $q$ is a query, $p^{+}$ is its matched target, and $\mathcal{P}^{-}$ is a set of negative targets.
L88: In this setting, $q$, $p^{+}$, and each $p^{-}\in\mathcal{P}^{-}$ can each take any non-empty modality composition: $q=x^{m_{q}},p^{+}=x^{m_{+}},p^{-}=x^{m_{-}},\quad m_{q},m_{+},m_{-}\in 2^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}$, where we denote an input with modality composition $m$ as $x^{m}$.
L89: We consider two common sources of negatives: (i) in-batch negatives, e.g., other positive targets in the same mini-batch, and (ii) hard negatives, mined by a retriever or constructed via heuristics.
L90: Our objective is to learn a single embedding function $\mathbf{e}(\cdot):\ \mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D},$ where $\mathcal{X}$ denotes the space of omni-modal inputs. The embedding maps any omni-modal input into a shared representation space, so that matched pairs $(q,p^{+})$ are close while mismatched pairs $(q,p^{-})$ are well separated.
L91: ### 2.2 Modality-aware Temperature Calibration
L92: 
L93: A single global temperature in contrastive learning implicitly assumes that all inputs induce similarity logits with comparable sharpness cite54†Chen et al. (2020) . In the omni-modal setting, different modalities can exhibit very different levels of ambiguity and noise. As a result, a fixed temperature can make some modality compositions overly sharp while leaving others overly flat, leading to imbalanced gradients and unstable optimization.
L94: To address this issue, we introduce a lightweight modality-aware temperature module implemented as a learnable per-modality scaling vector $\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{M}_{0}|}$. As shown in the top right part of Fig. cite53†2 , for any input $x$, we construct a normalized modality-indicator weight $w(x)\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{M}_{0}|-1}$ based on its modality composition. Concretely, $w(x)$ assigns non-zero mass to every modality present in $x$ and is normalized by the number of active modalities.
L95: We then define an instance temperature as a weighted sum $\tau(x)=\max\!\big(w(x)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\tau},\,10^{-6}\big).$ Given any pair $(q,p)$, we use a symmetric pairwise temperature $\tau(q,p)=\left(\tau(q)+\tau(p)\right)/2,$ and compute the calibrated similarity logit as
L96:  | $$\ell(q,p)=\frac{\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{e}(q),\,\mathbf{e}(p))}{\tau(q,p)}\,.$$  |  | (1)
L97: 
L98: We use $\ell(\bullet,\bullet)$ consistently in the contrastive learning objective.
L99: 
L100: Intuitively, larger $\tau$ flattens logits while smaller $\tau$ sharpens logits, so noisier/ambiguous modalities tend to learn larger temperatures. This stabilizes contrastive gradients in mixed omni-modal batches.
L101: ### 2.3 Controllable Negative Curriculum
L102: In omni-modal contrastive training, the hardness of negatives varies widely across batches and modalities, causing either weak supervision (overly easy negatives) or unstable optimization (false negatives). To address this hardness imbalance, we adopt a simple curriculum that gradually increases the focus on confusing negatives.
L103: However, hard-negative selection is prone to selection bias, since the hardest samples often include near-duplicates or semantically related items (i.e., false negatives), which can destabilize learning. As shown in the middle right of Fig. cite53†2 , we therefore combine negative selection with Debiased Contrastive Learning (DCL) cite48†Chuang et al. (2020) , which down-weights the aggregated negative to reduce the impact of potential false negatives.
L104: For a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}=\{(q_{i},p_{i}^{+},\mathcal{P}_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{B}$, we form a similarity matrix $\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times(B+K)}$, where the first $B$ columns correspond to the in-batch paired targets $\{p_{j}^{+}\}_{j=1}^{B}$, i.e., in-batch negatives ($S_{ij}=\ell(q_{i},p_{j}^{+})$), and the remaining $K$ columns correspond to mined hard negatives ($S_{i,B+k}=\ell(q_{i},p_{i,k}^{-})$). The diagonal $S_{ii}$ is the positive for row $i$.
L105: We then mask negatives by keeping only the top fraction of the most confusing items per row. Concretely, at training step $t$ we use a quantile threshold $\rho_{t}\in[0,1)$ and keep the hardest $(1-\rho_{t})$ fraction among all negatives in each row: for each $i$, let $\mathcal{N}_{i}(\rho_{t})$ be the set of indices of the largest $k=\lfloor(1-\rho_{t})(B+K-1)\rfloor$ values in $\{S_{ij}\}_{j\neq i}$.
L106: This produces a boolean mask $\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{B\times(B+K)}$ where $M_{ij}=1$ iff $j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}(\rho_{t})$ (and $M_{ii}=0)$.
L107: ##### Masked DCL objective.
L108: 
L109: We apply the mask inside a DCL loss by summing only over selected negatives. Let $\Omega_{i}=\{i\}\cup\mathcal{N}_{i}(\rho_{t})$. DCL corrects the bias of (masked) negative sampling by subtracting a scaled positive term from the negative aggregate:
L110:  | $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCL}}=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\frac{\exp(S_{ii})}{\exp(S_{ii})+\widetilde{N}_{i}}\,,$$  |  | (2)
L111:  | $$\widetilde{N}_{i}=\max\!\Big(\sum_{j\in\Omega_{i}\setminus\{i\}}\exp(S_{ij})-\gamma_{+}\exp(S_{ii}),\,\epsilon\Big)\,,$$  |
L112: 
L113: where $\gamma_{+}\in(0,1)$ is the debiasing coefficient and $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. Here $\mathbf{S}$ is computed from the calibrated logit $\ell(\bullet,\bullet)$, hence no extra global temperature is required.
L114: ##### Curriculum schedule.
L115: 
L116: Recall that $\rho_{t}$ denotes the mask ratio of negatives, so a larger $\rho_{t}$ means fewer negatives are kept (and the remaining set is harder). We therefore start with a smaller $\rho_{t}$ (keeping a larger fraction of negatives, including easier ones), and linearly increase it after a warmup period to gradually focus on fewer, harder negatives:
L117:  | $$\rho_{t}=\rho_{\mathrm{init}}+(\rho_{\mathrm{final}}-\rho_{\mathrm{init}})\,\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{t-t_{0}}{T-t_{0}},\,0,\,1\right),$$  |
L118: 
L119: where $t$ is the training step, $t_{0}$ is the warmup cutoff (the step after which we start increasing $\rho_{t}$, i.e., hardness), $T$ is the total number of training steps, and $\mathrm{clip}(x,0,1)=\min(\max(x,0),1)$.
L120: This curriculum lets the model first learn coarse omni-modal alignment with stable gradients, and then progressively resolve fine-grained confusions with genuinely hard negatives, while DCL mitigates the bias amplified by negative selection—together yielding stable optimization and cleaner alignment under mixed-modality batches.

L148: ### 2.4 Batch Whitening and Covariance Alignment
L149: 
L150: Representations produced by an omni-modal backbone often exhibit mismatched second-order statistics, which can distort similarity geometry and destabilize ranking cite59†Su et al. (2021) , especially when batches mix diverse modality compositions. To explicitly regularize the shared embedding space, we apply batch whitening cite49†Ermolov et al. (2021) and add a CORAL-style covariance alignment loss cite50†Sun and Saenko (2016) as a regularizer.
L151: For a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}=\{(q_{i},p_{i}^{+},\mathcal{P}_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{B}$, we compute embeddings with the shared encoder $\mathbf{e}(\cdot)$ and stack them as $\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{e}(q_{i})]_{i=1}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D},\mathbf{P}=[\mathbf{e}(p_{i}^{+})]_{i=1}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D}.$ We compute a single whitening transform $W$ from the concatenation of query and target embeddings in the mini-batch, and apply the same transform to both sets: $\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}=W(\mathbf{Q})$ and $\widehat{\mathbf{P}}=W(\mathbf{P})$.
L152: We then define the empirical covariance operator for any batch embedding matrix $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D}$ (e.g., $\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}$ or $\widehat{\mathbf{P}}$) as
L153:  | $$\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{B-1}(\mathbf{X}-\bar{\mathbf{X}})^{\top}(\mathbf{X}-\bar{\mathbf{X}})\,\,,$$  |  | (3)
L154: 
L155: where $\bar{\mathbf{X}}$ is the feature-wise mean over the batch. The CORAL loss is
L156: 
L157:  | $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coral}}=\frac{1}{4D^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})-\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf{P}})\right\|_{F}^{2}.$$  |  | (4)
L158: 
L159: With Eq.(cite60†2 ) and Eq.(cite61†4 ), the final objective is
L160:  | $$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCL}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{coral}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coral}}\,\,,$$  |  | (5)
L161: where $\lambda_{\mathrm{coral}}$ controls the strength of covariance regularization. In practice, we implement $W(\cdot)$ with a numerically stable routine (e.g., group-wise computation with a small jitter) to avoid instability under high-dimensional embeddings. By reducing second-order mismatch between $\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}$ and $\widehat{\mathbf{P}}$, this regularizer improves geometric consistency in the shared space and stabilizes similarity-based ranking in omni-modal retrieval.

L219: ### 3.5 Hyperparameter Analysis
L220: 
L221: We analyze the sensitivity of e5-omni to key hyperparameters in our explicit alignment recipe. We tune these hyperparameters on 1K-sample validation splits drawn from the corresponding training sets. L222: Modality-aware temperature initialization. Our modality-aware calibration introduces a trainable per-modality scaling vector $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ (Sec. cite9†2.2 ). Moderate values work best: too small $\gamma_{+}$ provides limited correction, while too large $\gamma_{+}$ can over-correct and weaken supervision. In our experiments, $\gamma_{+}\in[0.1,0.2]$ yields consistent improvements on MMEB-V2, and we use $\gamma_{+}=0.1$ by default.

L231: ## 4 Related Work
L232: Bi-modal Embedding Models. Early vision–language embedding models primarily focus on aligning text and images in a shared space, typically via dual-encoder contrastive learning (cite76†Jia et al., 2021 ; cite77†Radford et al., 2021 ; cite78†Li et al., 2022 ; cite79†Li et al., 2023 ). For example, CLIP (cite77†Radford et al., 2021 ) learns image and text encoders by matching paired image–caption data at scale.
L233: More recently, a line of work builds retrieval-oriented bi-encoders on top of modern VLMs (cite38†Jiang et al., 2025 ; cite42†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite41†Chen et al., 2025b ; cite80†Thirukovalluru et al., 2025 ; cite81†Chen et al., 2025a ), converting generative or instruction-following backbones into embedding models. L115: ### 4.1 Dense Rewards from Partial Success
L116: Following the scalable group-based RL framework, VeRPO samples a group of $N$ trajectories $\mathcal{G}_{x}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}$ for each prompt $x$, where each trajectory consists of a sequence of turn-level interactions $\tau_{i}=\{(s_{i,t},y_{i,t},o_{i,t})\}_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}$. To synthesize dense learning signals without external reward models, VeRPO assigns non-zero rewards to varying levels of partial success at each turn.
L117: Naive approaches that quantify partial success solely as the proportion of passed unit tests neglect the difficulty variance among them, risking reward hacking where the policy overfits to an abundance of trivial tests. To mitigate this, VeRPO introduces an intrinsic dynamic weighting mechanism that estimates the relative difficulty of each unit test based on its online execution statistics during training, ultimately yielding a dense reward signal by summing the weighted partial success.
L118: Intuitively, unit tests with lower pass rates represent greater optimization challenges for the current policy, necessitating higher weights for policy optimization. To this end, we first estimate the empirical pass rate $\rho_{j}$ for each unit test $u_{j}$ derived from execution statistics of the sampled group:
L119: 
L120:  | $$\rho_{j}=\frac{\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}p_{t,i}^{(j)}}{\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{x}}|\tau_{i}|},$$  |  | (2)
L121: where $p_{t,i}^{(j)}=1$ if turn $t$ in trajectory $\tau_{i}$ passes test $u_{j}$, and $0$ otherwise. Following Eq. cite70†2 , an inverse-pass-rate weight to quantify the empirical difficulty of each unit test is then formalized as:
L122: 
L123:  | $$w_{j}=\exp(-\alpha\rho_{j}),$$  |  | (3)
L124: 
L125: where $\alpha>0$ controls the sensitivity to difficulty, assigning higher weights to challenging tests that the current policy struggles with, while down-weighting simple tests that are well-mastered.
L126: While Eq. cite71†3 down-weights trivial tests, it treats them in isolation. As training progresses and policy capability improves, more test cases become trivial to the policy and eventually dominate the test suite. Consequently, an increasing number of generated code snippets accumulate abundant low-weight trivial tests, receiving a higher aggregated reward than those solving a few but critical challenges, thereby hindering learning efficiency.
L127: To mitigate this, we employ Gaussian Kernel Density Estimation to quantify the local concentration of test difficulties $\hat{\rho}_{j}$. This allows us to explicitly penalize distributional redundancy of trivial tests via an inverse-density weighting scheme, yielding a normalized weight $w_{j}^{\prime}$:
L128:  | $$\hat{\rho}_{j}=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{{|\mathcal{U}_{x}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\rho_{j}-\rho_{j^{\prime}})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),$$  |  | (4)
L129:  | $$w_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{w_{j}}{\hat{\rho}_{j}+\epsilon},$$  |  | (5)
L130: 
L131: where an adaptive bandwidth $\sigma=\frac{\text{std}(\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=1}^{{|\mathcal{U}_{x}|}})}{2}$ is adopted for robustness. As such, $w_{j}^{\prime}$ preserves dominant weights for challenging and rare tests while dampening the cumulative signal of easy ones.
L132: By summing the normalized weights of passed tests, each turn in the trajectory is assigned a localized and dense reward $R^{turn}$, computed as:
L133: 
L134:  | $$R^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{{|\mathcal{U}_{x}|}}w_{j}^{\prime}\cdot p_{t,i}^{(j)},$$  |  | (6)
L135: 
L136: which ensures the full functional correctness yields the maximum reward while extracting dense and critical learning signals from local partial success.
L137: ### 4.2 Global Outcome as an Anchor
L138: To solidify the alignment between local weighted partial success and end-to-end functional correctness, we further incorporate the trajectory-level execution outcome as a global reward to anchor the optimization. Specifically, each trajectory $\tau_{i}$ yields a binary outcome $R^{traj}(\tau_{i})\in\{0,1\}$, where $R^{traj}(\tau_{i})=1$ if the terminal execution feedback $o_{|\tau_{i}|}^{(i)}$ passes the complete test suite $\mathcal{U}_{x}$ and $R^{traj}(\tau_{i})=0$ otherwise.
L139: This global signal effectively anchors the optimization process, aligning policy updates with end-to-end functional correctness and stabilizing training. To further encourage efficiency, especially for multi-turn code generation, we introduce an efficiency-decayed reward formulation:
L140:  | $$\tilde{R}^{traj}(\tau_{i})=R^{traj}(\tau_{i})\cdot\gamma^{|\tau_{i}|},$$  |  | (7)
L141: 
L142: where $\gamma\in(0,1]$ is the decay factor, incentivizing concise trajectories that solve the problem in fewer turns.

L154: ### 4.3 VeRPO Policy Optimization
L155: 
L156: To integrate the two notions of reward design detailed in Section cite14†4.1 and cite15†4.2 , VeRPO employs a dual-level advantage estimation framework. At the turn-level, we compute a turn relative advantage using the localized $R^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)$ as:
L157:  | $$\begin{split}G(\mathcal{G}_{x})=\{R^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)\mid&\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{x},1\leq t\leq|\tau_{i}|\},\end{split}$$  |  | (8)
L158:  | $$A^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)=\frac{R^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)-\operatorname{mean}(G(\mathcal{G}_{x}))}{F_{\operatorname{norm}}(G(\mathcal{G}_{x}))},$$  |  | (9)
L159: 
L160: and at the trajectory level, a trajectory relative advantage is derived from the global $\tilde{R}^{traj}(\tau_{i})$:
L161:  | $$A^{traj}(\tau_{i})=\frac{\tilde{R}^{traj}(\tau_{i})-\operatorname{mean}(\{\tilde{R}^{traj}(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N})}{F_{\operatorname{norm}}(\{\tilde{R}^{traj}(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N})},$$  |  | (10)
L162: where $F_{\operatorname{norm}}$ is the normalization factor. While the standard GRPO defaults to $F_{\text{norm}}=\operatorname{std}$, this normalization introduces task-level difficulty bias, where low-variance groups (e.g., trivial or hard tasks) result in inflated advantage estimates during policy updates. To ensure stability across varying task difficulties, we set a constant normalization factor $F_{\text{norm}}=1$, which yields an unbiased estimator to mitigate variance issues cite72†Liu et al.
L163: (2025) .
L164: We then integrate the trajectory- and turn-level advantages into a unified advantage function. The combined advantage for a specific turn $t$ in trajectory $\tau_{i}$ is defined as:
L165: 
L166:  | $$A(\tau_{i},t)=A^{traj}(\tau_{i})+\beta\cdot A^{turn}(\tau_{i},t),$$  |  | (11)
L167: where $\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is a hyperparameter balancing the contribution of the two advantages. The trajectory-level advantage $A^{traj}(\tau_{i})$ anchors the global optimization, ensuring policy updates adhere to end-to-end functional correctness, while the turn-level advantage $A^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)$ enriches the learning signal with dense feedback from local partial success to navigate intermediate states. Finally, the policy optimization objective of VeRPO is formalized as:

L170: &\min\Big(\psi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(i)})A(\tau_{i},t),\\
L171: &\operatorname{clip}(\psi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(i)}),1\pm\epsilon)A(\tau_{i},t)\Big)\Biggr],\end{split}$$  |  | (12)
L172: where $\psi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(i)})=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t}^{(i)})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t}^{(i)})}$ is the importance sampling ratio, and $\epsilon$ is the clipping threshold. Notably, we omit the KL penalty to foster exploration.

L232: #### 5.2.4 Computational Cost
L233: Finally, we assess the computational overhead introduced by VeRPO. As detailed in Section cite13†4 , VeRPO is built upon the efficient group-based RL framework, inheriting an identical core architecture with GRPO, including grouped rollout sampling, computation of old log-probabilities, and clipped actor policy updates.
L234: Both pipelines eliminate the need for auxiliary critic or reward models by relying solely on execution feedback to compute rewards and advantages, thus sharing identical GPU memory consumption and LLM rollout overheads.
L235: The only additional computational burden in VeRPO is the turn-level advantage estimation pipeline, which encapsulates the synthesis of turn-level dense rewards. To quantify its impact, we present a fine-grained breakdown of the training time per iteration. As observed in Figure cite85†4 , the additional overhead for computing $A^{turn}$ is negligible relative to the dominant costs of rollout and policy update, introducing only 0.10s per iteration and accounting for < 0.02% of the total training time.
L236: These results demonstrate that VeRPO achieves dense and superior supervision with virtually zero marginal computational cost, effectively defying the conventional trade-off between reward granularity and computational efficiency.

L238: ## 6 Conclusion
L239: We propose VeRPO, a novel RL framework that synthesizes robust and dense rewards fully grounded in verifiable execution feedback. VeRPO addresses the sparsity of outcome-driven rewards and the instability of costly external reward models, extracting fine-grained learning signals from weighted partial success while solidifying the alignment with end-to-end functional correctness via global execution outcome anchors.
L240: Empirical results demonstrate that VeRPO significantly outperforms both outcome-driven and RM-based baselines with negligible computational cost, establishing a highly scalable, stable, and effective paradigm for evolving LLMs through verifiable execution.
