Abstract
Exhaustively evaluating many large language models (LLMs) on a large suite of benchmarks is expensive. We cast benchmarking as finite-population inference and, under a fixed query budget, seek tight confidence intervals (CIs) for model accuracy with valid frequentist coverage. We propose Factorized Active Querying (FAQ), which (a) leverages historical information through a Bayesian factor model; (b) adaptively selects questions using a hybrid variance-reduction/active-learning sampling policy; and (c) maintains validity through Proactive Active Inference—a finite-population extension of active inference (Zrnic & Candes, 2024) that enables direct question selection while preserving coverage. With negligible overhead cost, FAQ delivers up to 5×5\times effective sample size gains over strong baselines on two benchmark suites, across varying historical-data missingness levels: this means that it matches the CI width of uniform sampling while using up to 5×5\times fewer queries. We release our source code and our curated datasets to support reproducible evaluation and future research.
1 Introduction
Motivation    Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly moving from research demos to deployed systems in domains such as healthcare, law, and education (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023b; Dehghani et al., 2025; Kasneci et al., 2023; Artsi et al., 2025; Achiam et al., 2023). As adoption grows, so does the number of candidate models organizations must evaluate and monitor. This scale is visible in public ecosystems: HuggingFace hosts at least 1.861.86M models, including 300300K+ tagged as text-generation—many of them fine-tuned variants of certain base models (Laufer et al., 2025). Indeed, private institutions may maintain hundreds to thousands of internal variants, each exhibiting their own query-dependent strengths and weaknesses. As post-training becomes cheaper, the number of viable candidate models per deployment decision will only grow.
A common practice is to evaluate candidate models’ performances on a finite, curated benchmark suite. Such suites increasingly include questions that (a) require expensive domain-expert adjudication (often by multiple annotators) (Szymanski et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Enguehard et al., 2025; Croxford et al., 2025); (b) involve multi-turn interactions (e.g., helpfulness and safety) (He et al., 2024; Kwan et al., 2024); and/or (c) demand long-form reasoning with verification (Bai et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2025). With tens of thousands of questions (e.g., 1212K on MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024)), and hundreds to thousands of candidate models, exhaustively evaluating every model on every question is expensive in API costs, runtime, and expert annotation (Zhou et al., 2025). In practice, organizations may instead resort to “vibes”-based judgments on ad-hoc subsets (Mazzoni & Sharma, 2025).
Meanwhile, many organizations have access to historical performance data—from prior benchmark runs (e.g., the Open LLM Leaderboard (Fourrier et al., 2024)) and/or production evaluation pipelines. As we show, this historical data can be leveraged to make model evaluation substantially faster and cheaper.
Problem description    Consider a benchmark suite of NqN_{q} questions, viewed as a fixed finite population. For a new model, let zj∈{0,1}z_{j}\in\{0,1\} indicate whether it answers question jj correctly. We wish to estimate the model’s accuracy over the entire finite question bank:
θ:=1Nq​∑j=1Nqzj∈[0,1].\theta:=\frac{1}{N_{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{q}}z_{j}\in[0,1].
(1)
We also assume access to historical data H∈{0,1,NA}Nold×NqH\in\{0,1,\text{NA}\}^{N_{\text{old}}\times N_{q}} from NoldN_{\text{old}} prior models (e.g., previously benchmarked and/or observed in deployment), potentially with many missing entries (i.e., NA) due to resource and deployment constraints. Given a new model and budget nbn_{b}, the problem is: how should we adaptively select which nbn_{b} questions to query so as to construct a confidence interval (CI) for θ\theta that is as narrow as possible, while maintaining frequentist coverage?
Here, coverage (say, 95%95\%) means that over repeated runs of the evaluation procedure (i.e., over sampling randomness), the reported 95%95\% CI contains the true finite-bank accuracy θ\theta at least 95%95\% of the time. This guarantee is crucial for model selection and monitoring: miscalibrated uncertainty can lead to systematic overconfidence and poor deployment decisions, and the risk is amplified under adaptive evaluation where i.i.d. assumptions and heuristic error bars may fail. Such valid-coverage intervals provide uncertainty-quantified performance estimates to support deployment decisions alongside considerations such as cost and latency.
Our contributions    In this paper, we introduce Factorized Active Querying (FAQ), an efficient LLM evaluation method that can produce CIs of comparable width, using 5×5\times fewer queries and little to no additional computational costs, than competitive baselines (including uniform sampling), while maintaining frequentist coverage. FAQ has three components:
(a)
A Bayesian factor model that extracts information from partially-observed historical data.
(b)
A hybrid sampling policy combining variance reduction and active learning.
(c)
A coverage-preserving finite-population extension of active inference (Zrnic & Candes, 2024), which we call Proactive Active Inference (PAI). Unlike active inference, PAI directly selects the next question to query in a data-adaptive manner, rather than rigidly proceeding according to a fixed ordering and only making label/skip decisions.11
1
While we focus on finite-bank benchmarking, PAI is a general coverage-preserving framework for adaptive querying over finite populations: we will explore its broader potential in future work.
We evaluate FAQ on two benchmark suites under varying historical-data missingness and quantify the additional gains attributable to PAI. We release our compiled and curated datasets (with 4.44.4K+ models and 21.521.5K+ questions) to support reproducible benchmarking and future research. Datasets and code are available at https://github.com/skbwu/efficiently-evaluating-llms.
2 Related Work
Probabilistic factor modeling    FAQ is inspired by probabilistic factor modeling (Mnih & Salakhutdinov, 2007; Collins et al., 2001; Chen et al., 2023), which assumes a (Bayesian) generative factor model for a partially-observed binary matrix—here, the matrix of model-question correctness outcomes—and learns low-dimensional latent factors from the data. Importantly, any uncertainty guarantees for these approaches are tied to correct specification of the factor model (and any prior assumptions). In contrast, FAQ uses the Bayesian factor model only as a mental model to guide adaptive query selection, while delivering valid frequentist coverage regardless of factor-model fidelity. If the data is well-described by the factor model, FAQ automatically adapts and delivers tighter CIs.
Active statistical inference    For model-free frequentist coverage, FAQ builds on active statistical inference (Zrnic & Candes, 2024; Gligorić et al., 2025), which uses machine learning predictions over unlabeled covariates/features to adaptively select labels under a fixed budget and construct CIs. Our benchmark setting, however, provides no explicit covariates, so we use the factor model above to construct latent features to guide adaptive query selection. Moreover, unlike sequential active inference—which, in a pre-specified “nature’s order” stream, can only make label/skip decisions for the next item and enforces the budget constraints only in expectation—PAI/FAQ directly selects the next question from the full benchmark bank using all available information and enforces the query budget exactly.
Figure 1: High-level overview of Factorized Active Querying (FAQ). (a) Using partially-observed historical outcomes HH, we fit a historical model and question factors (Eq. 13). For a new model, we initialize its latent factor from the historical fit. Then, at each sampling round tt, we (b) compute the hybrid sampling policy qt​(⋅)q_{t}(\cdot) from the current factor posterior (Eq. 10), (c) sample a question It∼qt​(⋅)I_{t}\sim q_{t}(\cdot), and (d) update the model factor using the observed outcome zItz_{I_{t}} (Eqs. 3-5). Finally, we (e) output the Proactive Active Inference (PAI) estimate and confidence interval (Eqs. 6, 8), which guarantees model-free, frequentist coverage for finite-bank accuracy.
Active learning    FAQ draws on Bayesian active learning ideas (Settles, 2009; Di Fiore et al., 2023; Cohn et al., 1994; MacKay, 1992; Fedorov, 2013; Schein & Ungar, 2007, c.f. e.g.,) to guide question selection. One component of our hybrid policy upweights questions predicted (under the factor model) to yield larger posterior variance reductions for θ\theta. Because our goal is tight frequentist uncertainty quantification of θ\theta, we combine this active-learning component with an oracle-inspired variance-reduction component.
LLM evaluations    FAQ contributes to a growing literature on evaluating LLMs using only a fraction of benchmark questions (Hardt, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Vivek et al., 2024; Polo et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2025). However, most of this line of work targets point estimation of accuracy: with the exception of a simple AIPW estimator considered in Zhang et al. (2025), none of these prior works provide model-free consistency guarantees. Relatedly, Zouhar et al. (2025); Zhou et al. (2025) focus on model ranking/selection under limited queries, which is orthogonal to our goal of producing model accuracy estimates with rigorous statistical uncertainty quantification. Closest in spirit to our work is Angelopoulos et al. (2025), which uses active inference to reduce the cost of rating LLM outputs; by contrast, we reduce the number of inference calls per model. More importantly, Angelopoulos et al. (2025)’s approach posits an i.i.d. model for the benchmark data and targets the expected accuracy as judged by a strong autorater, whereas we estimate the true, finite-bank accuracy of each LLM without i.i.d. assumptions. This distinction is practically important: new models arrive over time and distribution shift is the norm, so uncertainty estimates that rely on i.i.d. or model correctness can be misleading. By targeting finite-bank accuracy with model-free coverage, FAQ provides uncertainty quantification that remains meaningful over such drifts.

3.2 Proactive Active Inference
Motivation and desiderata    The factor model is only a working model and offers no coverage guarantee on its own. Moreover, we make no i.i.d. assumptions on benchmark questions: we treat the labels zjz_{j} as fixed-but-unknown, since curated suites often intentionally include adversarial/edge cases and are not necessarily meant to represent a superpopulation of user queries. Thus, we want a model-free inference layer that wraps around the factor model and yields valid coverage for the finite-bank estimand θ\theta.
Sequential active inference (Zrnic & Candes, 2024) provides coverage, but differs from our approach in two key ways. First, it follows nature’s order: at time tt, one observes only the next item in a fixed stream and can only make a label/skip decision via an adaptive Bernoulli policy. In contrast, in our benchmark-bank setting, we have access to the full pool of question factors {vj}j=1Nq\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{q}} at every step and wish to leverage this global information to proactively select the next question. Second, we desire a workflow that is deterministic conditional on selection: once a question is chosen, it is labeled with probability 11 (no coin flip).
These desiderata are particularly important for FAQ because the new model factor uu is initialized from historical models and may be inaccurate (especially as models evolve over time). Proactively selecting informative questions early accelerates learning of uu (c.f. Section 3.4), improving subsequent query choices and inference. Practically, our desiderata better matches typical evaluation workflows: one selects a subset of questions under a budget, rather than streaming through the entire bank with probabilistic labeling decisions.

4 Experimental Setup
Datasets    Using the HuggingFace datasets API, we collect per-question 0/1 outcomes for 4.44.4K models from the Open LLM Leaderboard (Fourrier et al., 2024; Myrzakhan et al., 2024). Each model is evaluated on 21.621.6K questions across six datasets, which we aggregate into two suites: (a) MMLU-Pro with 1212K questions; and (b) BBH+GPQA+IFEval+MATH+MuSR with 9.59.5K questions. Full dataset citations are in Appendix C. We release our compiled and curated datasets to support future research.
Historical splits and missingness    For each suite, we sort models by release date, take the first 2.22.2K as historical HH and the next 2.22.2K as new test models. We induce missingness in HH by selecting nfull-obsn_{\text{full-obs}} fully-observed rows uniformly at random, and independently masking each remaining entry with probability 1−pobs1-p_{\text{obs}} (i.e., MCAR: missing completely at random). Exact settings are in Appendix C.
Factor model selection    For each missingness setting, we split HH by rows (models) into HtrainH_{\text{train}} (first 8080%) and HvalH_{\text{val}} (last 20%20\%). We choose (λ,k)(\lambda,k) via 5-fold cross-validation on HtrainH_{\text{train}}: (a) fit on observed entries (Eq. 13) and evaluate binary prediction accuracy on an MCAR-masked holdout set; and (b) apply the 1-SE rule (Friedman et al., 2010).
Budgets and coverage    We consider ten evenly-spaced budgets nb∈[2.5%,25%]n_{b}\in[2.5\%,25\%] of the question bank and target 95%95\% nominal coverage for all experiments.
FAQ hyperparameters    For each missingness setting and budget nbn_{b}, we tune (ρ,γ,β0,τ)(\rho,\gamma,\beta_{0},\tau) to minimize mean CI width on HvalH_{\text{val}} (averaged over 5 seeds), using a factor model refit on all of HtrainH_{\text{train}}.88

For tuning, we run FAQ for nbn_{b} rounds per validation model, querying the model on the selected questions if the labels are missing. Coverage audits under deployment shifts    Figure 2 reports coverage aggregated over 2.22.2K models and 100100 seeds. For deployment, it is also important that coverage does not vary systematically across models—e.g., that it is not driven by “easy” extreme-accuracy models while under-covering moderate, high-uncertainty ones. This is especially relevant here because we learn question factors from historical data and then freeze them, initializing new model factors from the historical mean/covariance. As newer models drift from the historical cohort (e.g., via new architectures or training recipes), these factors may become less representative, potentially impacting finite-sample coverage. Consistent with PAI’s guarantee of valid coverage for every evaluated model, Figure 4 shows stable coverage across model release date and model accuracy, with no systematic deviations.

Methodological gains    To isolate the gains of PAI and our hybrid sampling (beyond the factor model), we compare FAQ to a “traditional” active inference ablation that follows Zrnic & Candes (2024)’s sequential (‘‘nature’s order”)1212

We report CI-width ratios (rather than ESS) since they compare tightness directly across budgets without converting to a uniform-equivalent “budget savings” scale. Figure 5 reveals a clear phase shift. At low-budgets (e.g, <10%<10\% of the bank), FAQ yields substantially narrower CIs; at larger budgets, the traditional ablation eventually overtakes FAQ by similar margins. This is not discouraging: large-scale evaluation (11K+ models, 1010K+ questions) typically emphasizes the low-budget regime where FAQ is strongest. It also suggests a natural extension—a three-stage hybrid policy: active-learning early, oracle-style variance reduction mid-budget, and traditional active inference at the end—combining the best of both worlds.
Three mechanisms likely drive the shift. First, FAQ can proactively target informative questions early on via its active-learning component, whereas traditional active inference can only label/skip items in a fixed stream and its πt​(t)∝min⁡(p^t(t−1),1−p^t(t−1))\pi_{t}(t)\propto\min(\hat{p}_{t}^{(t-1)},1-\hat{p}_{t}^{(t-1)}) rule contains no explicit active-learning objective—explaining the low-budget gap. Second, at large budgets, FAQ’s sampling with replacement can resample questions, reducing efficiency relative to stream-based procedures that touch each question at most once. Third, our tuned τ\tau-mixing suggests variance control dominates at high budgets: the best post-hoc traditional ablation consistently uses τ=0.75\tau=0.75 (near-uniform query/label probabilities). By contrast, FAQ’s train/val tuning often selects much smaller τ\tau (e.g., 0.050.05 for many budgets ≥10%\geq 10\%), yielding spikey/highly-concentrated qtq_{t}. After the factor model has largely converged, this extra “targeting” offers little learning benefit, but increases AIPW variance through larger weights 1/qt​(It)1/q_{t}(I_{t}), helping explain why the traditional ablation can overtake FAQ at large budgets. Because FAQ targets cost savings, we focus on the low-budget regime.
6 Discussion and Conclusion
We introduced Factorized Active Querying (FAQ), a finite-bank framework for LLM evaluation that, under fixed query budgets and with negligible overhead cost, achieves up to 5×5\times higher ESS than uniform sampling and 2.4×2.4\times higher ESS than the strongest baselines—all while maintaining model-free frequentist coverage. FAQ also supports continuous monitoring: each new evaluation both leverages and expands the historical data, enabling progressively more cost-effective uncertainty-quantified evaluation over time.
Future work is motivated by a few concrete limitations. First, we treat question factors as fixed from historical data: periodically updating them could better track drift and improve efficiency. Second, richer, metadata-informed priors (e.g., parameter count or base model family) may improve low-budget performance. Third, our ablations suggest budget-dependent regimes, motivating three-stage hybrid designs that incorporate traditional active inference at larger budgets. Finally, extending FAQ to non-binary feedback and batched querying would broaden applicability and enable parallel evaluation across adjudicators and inference workers.

Impact Statement
Our work provides practitioners with a statistically-principled method for efficiently and accurately evaluating an LLM’s performance using only a fraction of queries compared to traditional methods. This can support more reliable model selection and monitoring by making statistically-rigorous evaluation feasible at scale. At the same time, cheaper evaluation may lower the barrier to not only rapid iteration but also benchmark gaming, so we encourage using FAQ with held-out test suites, transparent and good-faith reporting of configurations and uncertainty, and safety frameworks. We urge the reader to deploy our methods or variants thereof towards the general benefit of society.

A.3 Proof of Theorem 3.1
To formalize asymptotics, we work under a triangular-array setup indexed by mm. Specifically, we consider a sequence of problems {𝒫(m)}m≥1\{\mathcal{P}^{(m)}\}_{m\geq 1}, in which the bank size Nq=Nq(m)N_{q}=N_{q}^{(m)}, the budget nb=nb(m)n_{b}=n_{b}^{(m)}, and all involved quantities–{z1,…,zNq}\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N_{q}}\}, θ\theta, θ^nb\hat{\theta}_{n_{b}}, {p^(t)}0≤t<nb\{\hat{p}^{(t)}\}_{0\leq t<n_{b}}, and {qt​(⋅)}1≤t≤nb\{q_{t}(\cdot)\}_{1\leq t\leq n_{b}}—may depend on mm. A.3.2 Technical Assumptions/Conditions
We enumerate the technical assumptions/conditions required to prove the asymptotic normality stated in Theorem 3.1.
Assumption A.1 (Variance stabilization).
The average conditional variance converges in probability to a positive deterministic limit σ2\sigma^{2} (which does not depend on (m)(m)):
1nb​∑t=1nbVar​(Δt∣ℱt−1)​→𝑝​σ2.\displaystyle\frac{1}{{n_{b}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{b}}\mbox{Var}\left(\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)\overset{p}{\to}\sigma^{2}.
Assumption A.2 (Lindeberg condition).
The triangular array of problem instances in combination with sampling policies obey the Lindeberg condition. Specifically, for every ϵ>0\epsilon>0,
1nb​∑t=1nb𝔼⁡[Δt2​𝟏​(|Δt|>ϵ​nb)∣ℱt−1]​→𝑝​0.\displaystyle\frac{1}{{n_{b}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{b}}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$1$}(|\Delta_{t}|>\epsilon\sqrt{{n_{b}}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\overset{p}{\to}0.

The average conditional variance of the inverse-propensity-weighted predicted probabilities satisfies
1nb​∑t=1nbVar​(1Nq⋅p^It(t−1)qt​(It)∣ℱt−1)=op​(nb).\frac{1}{n_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{b}}\mbox{Var}\left(\frac{1}{N_{q}}\cdot\frac{\hat{p}^{(t-1)}_{I_{t}}}{q_{t}(I_{t})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)=o_{p}(n_{b}).
Assumptions A.1-A.2 are the standard conditions under which a CLT for martingale difference arrays holds (c.f. Hall & Heyde (2014, Corollary 3.2)). Assumption A.3 is mild and is used to facilitate consistency of the variance estimator. Intuitively, in our setting of LLM evaluation with our proposed hybrid sampling policy (c.f. Eq. 10), the variance stabilization assumption is reasonable because as more outcome labels are observed, the model-factor estimate (u^(t),Σ^(t))(\hat{u}^{(t)},\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}) stabilizes, which stabilizes p^(t)\hat{p}^{(t)}, and hence qt​(⋅)q_{t}(\cdot), too (because (αt,βt)(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}) also stabilize). In addition, the Lindeberg condition is satisfied, by construction, under our hybrid sampling policy because τ\tau-mixing lower-bounds qt​(j)q_{t}(j), avoiding extreme inverse-probability weights, and since zj,p^j(t)∈[0,1]z_{j},\hat{p}^{(t)}_{j}\in[0,1], the increments Δt\Delta_{t} are uniformly bounded (by a constant depending only on τ\tau). Appendix B Investigating Sampling With/Without Replacement
A natural question is whether we can sample questions without replacement, since re-querying the same question appears wasteful. An instinctive ad-hoc implementation is to “zero out” the sampling probabilities of previously-queried questions and renormalize before drawing the next index.
However, making this change while keeping the same PAI estimator and variance estimate is not theoretically supported and can be empirically harmful at moderate-to-large budgets. Recall that Theorem 3.1 was proven via a martingale CLT and that our proof treated Δt:=ϕt−θ\Delta_{t}:=\phi_{t}-\theta, with
ϕt=1Nq​∑j=1Nqp^j(t−1)+1Nq​zIt−p^It(t−1)qt​(It),\displaystyle\phi_{t}=\frac{1}{N_{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{q}}\hat{p}_{j}^{(t-1)}+\frac{1}{N_{q}}\frac{z_{I_{t}}-\hat{p}_{I_{t}}^{(t-1)}}{q_{t}(I_{t})},
as martingale differences/increments with respect to the filtration ℱt\mathcal{F}_{t}. With with-replacement sampling, at each round we draw our next question It∼qt​(⋅)I_{t}\sim q_{t}(\cdot) (c.f. our hybrid sampling policy in Eq. 10) over the full question bank, and the inverse-probability correction in ϕt\phi_{t} uses qt​(It)q_{t}(I_{t}). This alignment is what enables our martingale structure and makes Assumptions A.1-A.2 plausible: as our model-factor estimate (u^(t),Σ^(t))(\hat{u}^{(t)},\hat{\Sigma}^{(t)}) stabilizes as we observe more question labels, the predicted probabilities p^(t)\hat{p}^{(t)} and, by extension, the sampling policy qt​(⋅)q_{t}(\cdot), also stabilize. This is important because now it is reasonable that the average conditional variance nb−1​∑t=1nbVar​(Δt∣ℱt−1)n_{b}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{b}}\mbox{Var}(\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}) converges to a finite limit v2v^{2}—the crux of Assumption A.1.

Under the ad-hoc without-replacement modification, we are no longer drawing the next question from qtq_{t}: rather, we are drawing from the restriction of qtq_{t} to the remaining unseen questions Rt−1R_{t-1}—let us call this restriction q~t\tilde{q}_{t}. The key point is that q~t​(⋅)\tilde{q}_{t}(\cdot) is no longer aligned with PAI’s estimator and variance estimate formulas—which both still use qt​(It)q_{t}(I_{t}). As such, we no longer have martingale differences,1414

A consequence of this is also that θ^nb\hat{\theta}_{n_{b}} is no longer unbiased for θ\theta. thus hindering our martingale CLT argument and undermining our regularity conditions in Assumptions A.1-A.2. For one, with our ad-hoc without-replacement modification, our qt​(⋅)q_{t}(\cdot) are no longer lower-bounded, leading to unbounded inverse-probability corrections 1/qt​(⋅)1/q_{t}(\cdot) and challenging Assumption A.2. Without access to the martingale CLT, our resulting normal approximation and variance estimate can become miscalibrated.
Empirically, Figure 6 (Left) corroborates this: at moderate-to-large budgets, the ad-hoc without-replacement variant suffers from substantial miscoverage at moderate to large budgets. At low budgets, the difference in coverage is smaller because repeat sampling of questions is rarer, and thus the two sampling schemes behave similarly. We also observe from Figure 6 (Right) that the ad-hoc without-replacement modification yields little improvement in RMSE (point estimate performance).
For these reasons, we recommend sampling with replacement when using FAQ/PAI to preserve theoretically-guaranteed uncertainty quantification, with minimal loss in point-estimation performance.
Appendix C Additional Experimental Details
Datasets    We compile our historical data from the HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard (Fourrier et al., 2024; Myrzakhan et al., 2024). We create one benchmark from MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) itself with Nq=12032N_{q}=12032 questions, and aggregate the remaining datasets—BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023), GPQA (Rein et al., 2024), IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), MATH (Li et al., 2023a), and MuSR (Sprague et al., )—into a second benchmark with Nq=9574N_{q}=9574 questions.
Historical splits and missingness    For experiments comparing FAQ to the strongest baselines and uniform sampling, we consider the fully-observed historical data setting and the following (nfull-obs,pobs)(n_{\text{full-obs}},p_{\text{obs}}) pairs: (50,0.1)(50,0.1), (200,0.1)(200,0.1), (800,0.1)(800,0.1), and a particularly extremely-sparse (0,0.001)(0,0.001) setting.
For our traditional active inference ablation experiments, we consider the fully-observed data setting and the following (nfull-obs,pobs)(n_{\text{full-obs}},p_{\text{obs}}) pairs: (0,0.01)(0,0.01), (0,0.001)(0,0.001), (0,0.0001)(0,0.0001), and (0,0.00001)(0,0.00001). We chose this range of missingness settings after observing the results of FAQ against the strongest baselines and uniform sampling, and desired missingness-settings that would best distinguish between FAQ and the traditional active inference ablation.
Factor model selection    We use the AdamW optimizer with weight decay λ\lambda, learning rate 10−210^{-2}, and maximum iterations 20002000, implemented in PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019). We sweep over latent factor dimension k∈{8,16,32,64}k\in\{8,16,32,64\} and weight decay λ∈{101,100,10−1,10−2,10−3,10−4,10−5}\lambda\in\{10^{1},10^{0},10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5}\}.
FAQ hyperparameters    We sweep over the maximum tempering exponent β0∈{0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0}\beta_{0}\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}, the tempering governor γ∈{0.0,0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75}\gamma\in\{0.0,0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75\}, the exploration-to-exploitation governor ρ∈{0.0,0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75}\rho\in\{0.0,0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75\}, and the uniform-mixing strength τ∈{0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75}\tau\in\{0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75\}.
