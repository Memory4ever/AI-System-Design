Abstract
We prove an exponential separation in sample complexity between Euclidean and hyperbolic representations for learning on hierarchical data under standard Lipschitz regularization. For depth-RR hierarchies with branching factor mm, we first establish a geometric obstruction for Euclidean space: any bounded-radius embedding forces volumetric collapse, mapping exponentially many tree-distant points to nearby locations. This necessitates Lipschitz constants scaling as exp⁡(Ω⁡(R))\exp(\Omega(R)) to realize even simple hierarchical targets, yielding exponential sample complexity under capacity control. We then show this obstruction vanishes in hyperbolic space: constant-distortion hyperbolic embeddings admit O⁡(1)O(1)-Lipschitz realizability, enabling learning with n=O⁡(m​R​log⁡m)n=O(mR\log m) samples. A matching Ω⁡(m​R​log⁡m)\Omega(mR\log m) lower bound via Fano’s inequality establishes that hyperbolic representations achieve the information-theoretic optimum. We also show a geometry-independent bottleneck: any rank-kk prediction space captures only O⁡(k)O(k) canonical hierarchical contrasts.

1 Introduction
Hierarchical structure appears throughout machine learning: taxonomies and ontologies, coarse-to-fine classification, latent tree models, and multiscale decision systems. A common empirical approach is to learn a representation and then fit a relatively simple readout, typically under some norm or Lipschitz control. In parallel, hyperbolic embeddings have become a standard geometric primitive for hierarchical data, motivated by the exponential volume growth of hyperbolic space and supported by strong empirical performance in representation learning and graph neural networks (Nickel and Kiela, 2017; Ganea et al., 2018; Chami et al., 2019). Despite this, existing theoretical accounts are primarily geometric, e.g., distortion guarantees for embedding trees into ℍk\mathbb{H}^{k} (Sarkar, 2011; Sala et al., 2018) and do not establish when and why representation geometry changes sample complexity under standard statistical regularization.
In this work, we show that Euclidean representations with bounded radii do not preserve intrinsically smooth hierarchical targets. We formalize intrinsic smoothness using a correlation metric on the tree (weighted path length), which arises naturally in latent tree models via correlation decay. A “simple” target is defined to be Lipschitz in the correlation metric and a “good” representation preserves intrinsic simplicity after embedding, meaning that a readout with a polynomially-bounded norm/Lipschitz constant can realize the target in the embedded space.
We study a top-down local refinement primitive: conditioned on a parent category, we predict the correct child among its siblings. This is precisely the decomposition used by hierarchical classification pipelines (e.g., local classifiers per parent and top-down inference), and matches how hierarchical performance is often reported (conditional on a superclass rather than globally). This refinement task is standard multiclass classification with classification noise: an example (i,c)(i,c) consists of a parent index and a child label, with Bayes label 𝕀{c=θi}\mathbb{I}\{c=\theta_{i}\} observed through a BSC⁡(ρ)\mathrm{BSC}(\rho). Hence the protocol induces PAC learning over the finite concept class ℋpath\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{path}}, and lower bounds proved under
a strictly stronger observation model apply a fortiori to any learner.

2 Model and Setup
This section introduces the mathematical framework for our analysis. We define the tree structure and intrinsic correlation metric (Definition 1), formalize the hypotheses classes under Lipschitz regularization (Definition 2), specify what constitutes a good representation (Definition 3), and present the canonical learning protocol (Section 2.1).
Although all results in the main text are derived for trees, they extend to hierarchies with tree-like structures. This is established via a quasi-isometry between trees and trees with sparse lateral edges; details in Appendix F.
Definition 1 (Rooted tree with weighted metric).
Let T=(V,E)T=(V,E) be a rooted tree of depth RR with root rr. Define:
•
V≤RV_{\leq R}: the set of all nodes at depth at most RR;
•
LR⊆V≤RL_{R}\subseteq V_{\leq R}: the set of leaves (nodes at depth exactly RR);
•
V≤R∖LRV_{\leq R}\setminus L_{R}: the set of internal nodes (non-leaves).
For nodes u,v∈V≤Ru,v\in V_{\leq R}, let path⁡(u,v)\mathrm{path}(u,v) denote the unique simple path between them, and let |path⁡(u,v)|\absolutevalue{\mathrm{path}(u,v)} denote the number of edges on this path.
Assign each edge e∈Ee\in E a positive weight we>0w_{e}>0. The correlation metric is the weighted tree distance:
dcorr​(u,v)=∑e∈path⁡(u,v)we.d_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=\sum_{e\in\mathrm{path}(u,v)}w_{e}.
In the homogeneous case, we set we≡λw_{e}\equiv\lambda for all edges, which yields dcorr​(u,v)=λ​|path⁡(u,v)|d_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=\lambda\absolutevalue{\mathrm{path}(u,v)}. It is straightforward to verify that dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} is a metric on V≤RV_{\leq R}.
The metric dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} formalizes an intrinsic notion of similarity for the hierarchy. We show that targets are “simple” or “smooth” if they are Lipschitz in the intrinsic metric, and that representations are “good” if that simplicity remains after embedding.
Regular growth event. Our results hold on the regular growth event ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}_{R,\eta}^{\mathrm{reg}}, under which the tree exhibits typical Galton-Watson growth: |LR|≍mR\absolutevalue{L_R}\asymp m^{R} and no single mid-depth subtree dominates. This event has a probability 1−o⁡(1)1-o(1) under standard moment conditions; see Appendix A.1 for a formal definition and concentration bounds.
Definition 2 (Lipschitz hypothesis class).
For L>0L>0, define the Lipschitz hypothesis class:
ℱL≐{f:V→[−1,+1]||f⁡(u)−f⁡(v)|≤Ldcorr(u,v)}.\mathcal{F}_{L}\doteq\big\{f:V\to[-1,+1]\big|\absolutevalue{f(u) - f(v)}\leq Ld_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)\big\}.
The role of the Lipschitz class ℱL\mathcal{F}_{L} is twofold. First, it formalizes the notion that “hierarchical targets are smooth in the intrinsic (tree/correlation) metric,” which is the baseline against which our approximation bounds are stated. Second, the concrete targets used throughout the paper are instances of such intrinsic-smooth functions. While our hyperbolic achievability results (Section 5) are presented for a specific finite hypothesis class, the geometric obstructions we prove extend to representing arbitrary intrinsically Lipschitz hierarchical structures.
Remark on Lipschitz control.
We focus on Lipschitz (equivalently, norm-bounded) hypothesis classes because they capture the implicit bias of modern high-dimensional learning. Standard regularization techniques such as weight decay in neural networks (Bartlett et al., 2017), margin maximization in SVMs (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995), and RKHS norm penalties (Schölkopf and Smola, 2001), effectively constrain the Lipschitz constant of the learned predictor with respect to the representation. Additionally, standard generalization bounds (e.g. Rademacher complexity) scale with the Lipschitz constant (Bartlett et al., 2005; von Luxburg and Bousquet, 2004). Thus, capacity control via Lipschitz or norm constraints is both theoretically well motivated and empirically used.
Definition 3 (Representation and bi-Lipschitz embedding).
A representation is a map ϕ:V→(𝒳,d𝒳)\phi:V\to(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}}). We refer to ϕ\phi as (s,D)(s,D)-bi-Lipschitz if for all u,v∈Vu,v\in V:
sD​dcorr​(u,v)≤d𝒳​(ϕ⁡(u),ϕ⁡(v))≤s​D​dcorr​(u,v).\frac{s}{D}d_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)\leq d_{\mathcal{X}}(\phi(u),\phi(v))\leq sDd_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v).
If s=1s=1, then we say ϕ\phi is DD-bi-Lipschitz.
Preservation of Lipschitz structure.
If a map ϕ\phi is (s,D)(s,D)-bi-Lipschitz from (V≤R,dcorr)(V_{\leq R},d_{\mathrm{corr}}) into (𝒳,d𝒳)(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}}), then Lipschitz functions on the tree remain Lipschitz after embedding, with at most a factor-DD increase in the Lipschitz constant (see Lemma 24 in Appendix C.3 for the formal statement). This is why bi-Lipschitz embeddings with bounded distortion are “good” representations for hierarchical datasets.
We will largely focus on sample complexity in the standard PAC learning framework. Let 𝒫\mathcal{P} be a class of probability distributions over V≤R×{0,1}V_{\leq R}\times\{0,1\} (or, more generally, over 𝒳×{0,1}\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\} for a representation ϕ:V≤R→𝒳\phi:V_{\leq R}\to\mathcal{X}). For a hypothesis class ℋ\mathcal{H} and target function gg, define the risk of h∈ℋh\in\mathcal{H} under distribution P∈𝒫P\in\mathcal{P} as:
RiskP​(h,g)≐𝔼(x,y)∼P​[|h⁡(x)−g⁡(x)|].\mathrm{Risk}_{P}(h,g)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\left[|h(x)-g(x)|\right].
Definition 4 (Sample complexity).
For a hypothesis class ℋ\mathcal{H}, target class 𝒢\mathcal{G}, distribution class 𝒫\mathcal{P}, accuracy ε>0\varepsilon>0, and confidence δ∈(0,1)\delta\in(0,1), the sample complexity n⁡(ℋ,𝒢,𝒫,ε,δ)n(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\varepsilon,\delta) is the minimum nn such that there exists a learning algorithm 𝒜\mathcal{A} satisfying: for all g∈𝒢g\in\mathcal{G} and all P∈𝒫P\in\mathcal{P}, given nn i.i.d. samples from PP, the algorithm outputs h^∈ℋ\hat{h}\in\mathcal{H} such that
Pr[RiskP(h^,g)≤ε]≥1−δ.\Pr\left[\mathrm{Risk}_{P}(\hat{h},g)\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\delta.
When the context is clear, we abbreviate this as n⁡(ε,δ)n(\varepsilon,\delta).
For our results, we focus on the worst-case sample complexity over the specified distribution and target classes, which lower-bounds the complexity of any learner.
Convention and remarks.
Throughout, we treat the branching factor (mm for deterministic mm-ary trees, ξ\xi for Galton-Watson trees) as a fixed constant. All asymptotic statements are in the depth RR. Weighted tree metrics also arise naturally from correlation decay in tree-structured graphical models; see Appendix A.2. In hierarchical generative models, the information-theoretic distance between nodes (defined via correlation decay) is exactly proportional to dT{d_{T}}. Thus, learning functions that are Lipschitz with respect to dT{d_{T}} corresponds to learning functions that vary smoothly with the generative statistics of the data.
2.1 Canonical Learning Protocol
Hierarchical prediction problems are typically coarse-to-fine: to make a fine-grained decision, one conditions on having already identified the relevant coarse category. Concretely, most tasks on a rooted tree: leaf classification, taxonomy navigation, subtree retrieval, and multiscale regression / classification; can be decomposed into a sequence of local refinement decisions of the form “within the current prefix subtree, which child leads towards the correct refinement?”. We formalize this primitive via the following learning protocol, which makes the sampling distribution and information constraints explicit.
Unknown parameter (latent root-to-leaf path).
Let θ=(θ1,…,θR)∈[m]R\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{R})\in[m]^{R} denote an unknown root-to-leaf path in the complete mm-ary tree of depth RR, where θi∈[m]\theta_{i}\in[m] specifies the child index at depth ii. For i∈{0,1,…,R}i\in\{0,1,\dots,R\}, define the depth-ii prefix node
ui​(θ)≐(θ1,…,θi)∈[m]i,u_{i}(\theta)\doteq(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{i})\in[m]^{i},
with the convention u0​(θ)=ru_{0}(\theta)=r denoting the root. For any leaf v=(v1,…,vR)∈LRv=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{R})\in L_{R}, write chi​(v)=vi∈[m]\mathrm{ch}_{i}(v)=v_{i}\in[m] for the child index at depth ii along the root-to-vv path. For any node u∈V≤Ru\in V_{\leq R}, denote by LR(u)⊆LRL_{R}^{(u)}\subseteq L_{R} the set of leaves in the subtree rooted at uu.
Data generation (local refinement query).
Each training example is obtained by selecting a query depth uniformly, then sampling a leaf uniformly from the subtree consistent with the latent path up to the parent of that depth. Formally:
1.
Draw a query depth I∼Unif⁡([R])I\sim\mathrm{Unif}([R]), where [R]={1,…,R}[R]=\{1,\ldots,R\}.
2.
Conditional on I=iI=i, draw a leaf uniformly from the subtree rooted at the on-path prefix ui−1​(θ)u_{i-1}(\theta):
V|(I=i)∼Unif⁡(LR(ui−1​(θ))).V\mid(I=i)\;\sim\;\mathrm{Unif}\bigl(L_{R}^{(u_{i-1}(\theta))}\bigr).
Equivalently, VV is uniformly distributed over leaves (v1,…,vR)∈LR(v_{1},\ldots,v_{R})\in L_{R} satisfying (v1,…,vi−1)=ui−1​(θ)(v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1})=u_{i-1}(\theta).
3.
The Bayes-optimal label indicates whether VV lies in that particular child-subtree at depth ii:
Y∗(V,I)≐𝕀{chI(V)=θI}∈{0,1}.Y^{*}(V,I)\doteq\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{ch}_{I}(V)=\theta_{I}\}\in\{0,1\}.
4.
The observed label YY is corrupted by a binary symmetric channel (BSC) with crossover probability ρ∈(0,1/2)\rho\in(0,1/2):
Pr[Y=1∣V=v,I=i,θ]={1−ρif ​chi​(v)=θi,ρif ​chi​(v)≠θi.\Pr[Y=1\mid V=v,I=i,\theta]=\begin{cases}1-\rho&\text{if }\mathrm{ch}_{i}(v)=\theta_{i},\\
\rho&\text{if }\mathrm{ch}_{i}(v)\neq\theta_{i}.\end{cases}
The learner observes (ϕ⁡(V),I,Y)\bigl(\phi(V),I,Y\bigr), where ϕ:V≤R→𝒳\phi:V_{\leq R}\to\mathcal{X} is the representation map (Definition 3), and outputs a predictor y^:𝒳×[R]→{0,1}\hat{y}:\mathcal{X}\times[R]\to\{0,1\}.
PAC via a stronger oracle model.
The protocol induces a standard supervised learning problem with classification noise. In the more informative oracle model of Appendix D, each example is an i.i.d. draw (X,Y)(X,Y) where X=(I,C)∈[R]×[m]X=(I,C)\in[R]\times[m] with I∼Unif⁡([R])I\sim\mathrm{Unif}([R]) and C∼Unif⁡([m])C\sim\mathrm{Unif}([m]), and the Bayes label is Y∗(I,C)=𝕀{C=θI}Y^{*}(I,C)=\mathbb{I}\{C=\theta_{I}\} observed through a BSC⁡(ρ)\mathrm{BSC}(\rho). Equivalently, we are PAC-learning the finite concept class ℋpath={hθ(i,c)=𝕀{c=θi}∣θ∈[m]R}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{path}}=\{h_{\theta}(i,c)=\mathbb{I}\{c=\theta_{i}\}\mid\theta\in[m]^{R}\} under classification noise (Valiant, 1984; Angluin and Laird, 1988). Since the oracle reveals (I,C)(I,C) whereas the original protocol reveals only (I,ϕ⁡(V))(I,\phi(V)), the oracle model is strictly more informative; hence any information-theoretic lower bound proved in the oracle model applies a fortiori to any learner (proper or improper) in our original setting.
Information structure.
Conditional on I=iI=i, the sample is informative only about θi\theta_{i}, and only a 1/m1/m fraction of leaves carry the distinguishing signal, observed through a BSC⁡(ρ)\mathrm{BSC}(\rho). This yields a minimax lower bound of Ω⁡(m​R​log⁡m)\Omega(mR\log m) via Fano’s inequality (Appendix D).
3 Euclidean Sample Complexity Lower Bound
We show that bounded-radius Euclidean embeddings cannot realize intrinsically smooth hierarchical targets (Lipschitz in dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}}). The argument has two steps:
1.
Collision. Any embedding of |LR|≍mR\absolutevalue{L_R}\asymp m^{R} leaves into a kk-dimensional ball of radius BB maps some pair of leaves at tree distance Ω⁡(R)\Omega(R) to Euclidean distance O⁡(B​exp(−cR/k))O\left(B\exp(-cR/k)\right) (Lemma 5). This is a volumetric pigeonhole argument: Euclidean covering numbers grow polynomially while |LR|\absolutevalue{L_R} grows exponentially.
2.
Approximation lower bound. The collided pair induces a subtree-indicator target that is O⁡(1/R)O(1/R)-Lipschitz in dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} but forces any Euclidean predictor with Lip‖⋅‖2​(h)≤poly⁡(R)\mathrm{Lip}_{\norm{\cdot}_{2}}(h)\leq\mathrm{poly}(R) to incur error, i.e. realizability requires Lip‖⋅‖2​(h)=Ω⁡(exp⁡(R/k)/B)\mathrm{Lip}_{\norm{\cdot}_{2}}(h)=\Omega(\exp(R/k)/B) (Theorem 6).
Throughout this section, assume that edge weights satisfy we≥λw_{e}\geq\lambda for all e∈Ee\in E.
We begin with the geometric collision statement. It isolates the only property of the tree we use: under ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}_{R,\eta}^{\mathrm{reg}}, the number of leaves grows as ≍mR\asymp m^{R}, while the covering number of a Euclidean ball grows only polynomially with resolution.
Lemma 5 (Volumetric collapse).
On ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}_{R,\eta}^{\mathrm{reg}}, let ϕ:LR→ℝk\phi:L_{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{k} satisfy ϕ⁡(LR)⊆ℬ2​(0,B)\phi(L_{R})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}(0,B). Then, there exist leaves u∗,v∗∈LRu^{*},v^{*}\in L_{R} with:
‖ϕ⁡(u∗)−ϕ⁡(v∗)‖2≤c​B​exp⁡(−log⁡m−4​η2​k​R),while ​dcorr​(u∗,v∗)≥λ​R\norm{\phi(u^*) - \phi(v^*)}_{2}\leq cB\exp\left(-\frac{\log m-4\eta}{2k}R\right),\quad\text{while }d_{\mathrm{corr}}(u^{*},v^{*})\geq\lambda R
where c>0c>0 is an absolute constant.
Proof.
The claim follows by covering ℬ2​(0,B)\mathcal{B}_{2}(0,B) at resolution δ\delta and applying a pigeonhole argument across the depth-⌊R/2⌋\lfloor R/2\rfloor subtrees on ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}_{R,\eta}^{\mathrm{reg}}. Noting that the number of cover elements grows as (B/δ)k(B/\delta)^{k}, while on ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{R,\eta} there are at least exp⁡((log⁡m−η)​R/2)\exp(({\log m - \eta})R/2) subtrees at depth R/2R/2, each containing at least one leaf. Choosing δ\delta to balance the number of cover elements against the number of subtrees yields the stated exponential scale. The full proof, including the covering argument and verification that dcorr​(u∗,v∗)≥λ​Rd_{\mathrm{corr}}(u^{*},v^{*})\geq\lambda R, is in Appendix B.1.
∎
The collision pair (u∗,v∗)(u^{*},v^{*}) witnesses a mismatch between intrinsic and represented distances. To convert this geometric mismatch into an approximation statement, we construct a canonical binary target that separates the two child-subtrees emanating from the lowest common ancestor of u∗u^{*} and v∗v^{*} (LCA⁡(u,v)\mathrm{LCA}(u,v)). This target is Lipschitz in dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} since the cut occurs at a macroscopic depth, but any Euclidean predictor that fits the labels on the collided pair must vary sharply over an exponentially small ℓ2\ell_{2} distance.
Theorem 6 (Euclidean Lipschitz lower bound for a canonical hierarchical cut).
On ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}_{R,\eta}^{\mathrm{reg}}, let ϕ:V≤R→ℝk\phi:V_{\leq R}\to\mathbb{R}^{k} satisfy ϕ⁡(LR)⊆ℬ2​(0,B)\phi(L_{R})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}(0,B). Let (u∗,v∗)(u^{*},v^{*}) be a collided pair as in Lemma 5, and let a≐LCA⁡(u∗,v∗)a\doteq\mathrm{LCA}(u^{*},v^{*}). Let cu,cvc_{u},c_{v} be the two distinct children of aa that lie on the paths from aa to u∗u^{*} and v∗v^{*}, respectively.
Define a leaf-label gL:LR→{−1,0,+1}g_{L}:L_{R}\to\{-1,0,+1\} by
gL​(x)≐{+1x∈LR(cu),−1x∈LR(cv),0otherwise.g_{L}(x)\doteq\begin{cases}+1&x\in L_{R}^{(c_{u})},\\
-1&x\in L_{R}^{(c_{v})},\\

Let g:V≤R→[−1,1]g:V_{\leq R}\to[-1,1] be any (2/(λ​R))(2/(\lambda R))-Lipschitz extension of gLg_{L} to V≤RV_{\leq R} (e.g. via a McShane extension; see Lemma 19).
Then:
1.
gg is O⁡(1/(λ​R))O(1/(\lambda R))-Lipschitz with respect to dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}}.
2.
Any h:ℝk→[−1,1]h:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[-1,1] satisfying
|h⁡(ϕ⁡(u∗))−g⁡(u∗)|+|h⁡(ϕ⁡(v∗))−g⁡(v∗)|≤1\absolutevalue{h(\phi(u^*))-g(u^*)}+\absolutevalue{h(\phi(v^*))-g(v^*)}\leq 1
must have
Lip‖⋅‖2​(h)≥c′B​exp⁡(log⁡m−4​η2​k​R),{\mathrm{Lip}}_{\norm{\cdot}_{2}}(h)\geq\frac{c^{\prime}}{B}\exp\left(\frac{\log m-4\eta}{2k}R\right),
for an absolute constant c′>0c^{\prime}>0.
Proof.
The proof proceeds as follows:
Item 1.
Across the cut (LR(cu),LR(cv))(L_{R}^{(c_{u})},L_{R}^{(c_{v})}) the label changes by 22 while dcorr≥λ​Rd_{\mathrm{corr}}\geq\lambda R, so Lipdcorr​(gL)≤2/(λ​R){\mathrm{Lip}}_{d_{\mathrm{corr}}}(g_{L})\leq 2/(\lambda R). A McShane extension preserves the Lipschitz constant, hence the same bound holds for gg on V≤RV_{\leq R}. (The additional value 00 off the two chosen subtrees can only reduce the worst-case slope.)
Item 2.
Since g⁡(u∗)=1g(u^{*})=1 and g⁡(v∗)=−1g(v^{*})=-1, the assumption implies
|h⁡(ϕ⁡(u∗))−h⁡(ϕ⁡(v∗))|≥|g⁡(u∗)−g⁡(v∗)|−(|h⁡(ϕ⁡(u∗))−g⁡(u∗)|+|h⁡(ϕ⁡(v∗))−g⁡(v∗)|)≥1.\absolutevalue{h(\phi(u^*))-h(\phi(v^*))}\geq\absolutevalue{g(u^*)-g(v^*)}-\big(\absolutevalue{h(\phi(u^*))-g(u^*)}+\absolutevalue{h(\phi(v^*))-g(v^*)}\big)\geq 1.
Dividing by ‖ϕ⁡(u∗)−ϕ⁡(v∗)‖2\norm{\phi(u^*)-\phi(v^*)}_{2} and using Lemma 5 yields the claimed Lipschitz lower bound.
∎
Normalized distance.
Theorem 6 is an approximate lower bound driven by geometry. The target gg becomes increasingly smooth in the intrinsic metric as depth grows: Lipdcorr​(g)=O⁡(1/R)\mathrm{Lip}_{d_{\mathrm{corr}}}(g)=O(1/R). Yet any predictor on a bounded Euclidean representation that achieves constant approximation error must have Euclidean Lipschitz constant at least exp⁡(Ω⁡(R/k))/B\exp(\Omega(R/k))/B. Equivalently, if we normalize distances by the tree diameter λ​R\lambda R to obtain d~corr≐dcorr/(λ​R)\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{corr}}\doteq d_{\mathrm{corr}}/(\lambda R), then gg is O⁡(1)O(1)-Lipschitz under d~corr\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{corr}}, while its realization on ϕ⁡(LR)⊆B2​(0,B)\phi(L_{R})\subseteq B_{2}(0,B) requires Lipℓ2​(h)≥exp⁡(Ω⁡(R/k))/B\mathrm{Lip}_{\ell_{2}}(h)\geq\exp(\Omega(R/k))/B. Allowing BB to grow exponentially can avoid collisions, but then standard covering/packing bounds for Lipschitz classes on ℬ2​(0,B)\mathcal{B}_{2}(0,B) reintroduce exponential statistical complexity through the domain size.
Statistical implication (via capacity control).
If the learner restricts itself to predictors with Euclidean Lipschitz constant Lip‖⋅‖2​(h)≤poly⁡(R){\mathrm{Lip}}_{\norm{\cdot}_{2}}(h)\leq{\mathrm{poly}}(R) (as induced by standard norm/Lipschitz regularization), then it incurs a constant approximation error on some O⁡(1/R)O(1/R)-Lipschitz hierarchical target. Achieving realizability forces Lip‖⋅‖2​(h)≥exp⁡(Ω⁡(R/k)){\mathrm{Lip}}_{\norm{\cdot}_{2}}(h)\geq\exp(\Omega(R/k)), and fat-shattering/packing lower bounds for Lipschitz classes on ℬ2​(0,B)\mathcal{B}_{2}(0,B) yield exponential sample complexity in RR; see Appendix B.3.
4 Low-Rank Representations
A natural objection to the above argument is that geometry may be the wrong bottleneck: perhaps the learner can bypass exponential sample complexity by using compressed prediction spaces (e.g., a width-kk bottleneck, a linear probe on learned features, or a finite-rank kernel). Here we show that regardless of representation, any rank-kk prediction space can align with only O⁡(k)O(k) independent multiscale contrasts.

5 Hyperbolic Achievability and Optimality
In this section we show that, for the canonical protocol of Section 2.1, hyperbolic representations achieve the minimax sample complexity. The argument separates geometry from information: (i) a constant-distortion embedding into hyperbolic space makes each local refinement decision realizable with constant geometric control (constant margin / O⁡(1)O(1)-Lipschitz readout), and (ii) the remaining Θ⁡(m​R​log⁡m)\Theta(mR\log m) sample requirement is information-theoretic and holds under any representation.
We begin by recalling that trees admit near-isometric embeddings into hyperbolic space once curvature is chosen to accommodate the branching factor. This resolves the volume-growth mismatch that drives the Euclidean lower bound.

κ≥Ck​log⁡Δλ​ϵ.\sqrt{\kappa}\geq\frac{C_{k}\log\Delta}{\lambda\epsilon}.
Proof.
This follows from an extension of Sarkar (2011), generalized to k>2k>2 in Sala et al. (2018). The full algorithm and proof is given in Appendix C.1.

Curvature scaling.
The condition κ≳(log⁡Δ)/(λ​ε)\sqrt{\kappa}\gtrsim(\log\Delta)/(\lambda\varepsilon) matches the intuition that curvature must scale with branching to provide enough angular room for child subtrees. In Appendix C.2 we show a converse on ℰR,ηreg\mathcal{E}_{R,\eta}^{\mathrm{reg}}: any bi-Lipschitz embedding of a Galton-Watson tree into ℍκk\mathbb{H}_{\kappa}^{k} requires κ≥Ω⁡(log⁡m)\sqrt{\kappa}\geq\Omega(\log m) (up to constants depending on distortion and dimension). Thus, the dependence on log⁡Δ\log\Delta is unavoidable in this regime and the generalized Sarkar construction is optimal with respect to the geometry of the target space.
A key consequence of the generalized Sarkar construction is uniform cone separation: for any internal node aa, the mm child subtrees of aa occupy disjoint cones in ℍκk\mathbb{H}_{\kappa}^{k} with constant angular separation, yielding a constant-margin separating geodesic hypersurface between any chosen child and its siblings (formalized in Appendix C.4). 6 Discussion
6.1 Related Work
Hyperbolic representations.
Hyperbolic embeddings were introduced for taxonomies with strong performance in low dimension and were followed by architectural extensions to hyperbolic neural networks and hyperbolic GNNs for hierarchical and scale-free graphs (Nickel and Kiela, 2017; Nickel and Kiela, 2018; Ganea et al., 2018; Chami et al., 2019). Low-distortion embeddings of trees into hyperbolic space are well understood, notably, constructions in the Poincaré plane (Sarkar, 2011) and precision-dimension tradeoffs (Sala et al., 2018). Our results are orthogonal to these distortion guarantees: by quantifying realizability under Lipschitz/norm control, we obtain a statistical separation in sample complexity between bounded-radius Euclidean and hyperbolic representations.
Metric embeddings beyond distortion.
Classical metric embedding theory concerns multiplicative distortion, from Bourgain’s O⁡(log⁡n)O(\log n) bound for general metrics to sharper bounds for trees, which embed with small distortion in high dimension but can require polynomial distortion in fixed-dimension Euclidean space (Matoušek, 1999). We instead quantify the statistical implications of embedding: bounded-radius Euclidean embeddings exhibit a multi-scale collision phenomenon that we convert into an exponential Lipschitz requirement and hence exponential sample complexity under capacity control. In this sense, representation geometry imposes a statistical penalty even when coarse distortion can be favorable.
Capacity, Lipschitz learning, and bottlenecks.
Generalization for Lipschitz predictors is characterized by fat-shattering and packing/covering dimensions in both Euclidean and general metric spaces (Alon et al., 1997; von Luxburg and Bousquet, 2004). We use this machinery to show that Euclidean collapse induces exponential Lipschitz constants and hence exponential sample complexity for hierarchical refinement. We also prove a representation-agnostic expressivity bound: any rank-kk prediction space (including bottlenecks and finite-rank kernels) captures only O⁡(k)O(k) out of mR−1m^{R}-1 canonical hierarchical contrasts. This connects to multiscale bases on trees and graphs (e.g., diffusion wavelets) but here serves as a lower bound on the learnable signal, independent of embedding geometry.
Representation vs. learning.
Empirically, hyperbolic representations often outperform Euclidean ones in low dimension for hierarchical data, while at higher dimension Euclidean embeddings can match or exceed hyperbolic reconstruction quality. Recent reproductions report that Euclidean embeddings can close the gap around ≳50\gtrsim 50 dimensions (Bansal and Benton, 2021). Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that hyperbolic neural networks do not achieve theoretical optimal embeddings in practice (Tan et al., 2024). Together, these observations suggest that embedding distortion or reconstruction quality alone is insufficient to predict statistical efficiency. Our bounds make this explicit: Euclidean representations incur a penalty scaling as exp⁡(Ω⁡(R/k))\exp(\Omega(R/k)) under bounded radius and standard regularization, while hyperbolic representations achieve minimax-optimal Θ⁡(m​R​log⁡m)\Theta(mR\log m) rates.
6.2 Summary
We show that representation geometry governs sample complexity for hierarchical learning. For the canonical refinement task on depth-RR mm-ary (or bounded Galton-Watson) trees, bounded-radius Euclidean representations require exponential smoothness and sample complexity, whereas constant-distortion hyperbolic representations admit O⁡(1)O(1)-Lipschitz realizers and achieve the minimax rate Θ⁡(m​R​log⁡m)\Theta(mR\log m). This identifies hyperbolic space as a statistically efficient geometric prior for hierarchical targets and clarifies when and why Euclidean models require higher dimension to avoid exponential penalties.

A.2 Ising Model and Correlation Decay
The main text treats dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} as an intrinsic hierarchical similarity metric. This subsection provides one concrete generative instantiation in which dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} arises canonically: on a tree-structured graphical model, correlations factor along paths, so negative log-correlation is exactly an additive weighted tree distance. This makes the Lipschitz assumption in dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} interpretable as smoothness with respect to the underlying generative statistics.
Definition 15 (Ferromagnetic Ising model on a finite graph).
Let G=(V,E)G=(V,E) be a finite, simple, connected graph. Fix edge couplings {Je}e∈E\{J_{e}\}_{e\in E} with Je≥0J_{e}\geq 0 and zero external field. The Ising measure is
ℙ⁡(x)∝exp⁡(∑e={u,v}∈EJe​xu​xv),x∈{−1,+1}V.\mathbb{P}(x)\propto\exp\left(\sum_{e=\{u,v\}\in E}J_{e}x_{u}x_{v}\right),\quad x\in\{-1,+1\}^{V}.
Write ⟨⋅⟩G\langle\cdot\rangle_{G} for expectation under ℙ\mathbb{P}.
Generality of the Ising model.
We focus on the Ising model as the canonical example of a discrete Markov Random Field (MRF). However, the connection between correlation decay and weighted tree metrics is a general feature of graphical models on trees. For instance, in Gaussian Graphical Models on trees, the correlation decays exponentially with path length, inducing a similar geometry. We analyze the Ising case because its discrete nature presents the sharpest information-theoretic lower bounds (as formalized in Appendix D), but the geometric intuitions extend to general spin systems and latent tree models.
Definition 16 (Correlation dissimilarity).
Let (Xv)v∈V(X_{v})_{v\in V} denote the random spin configuration under ℙ\mathbb{P}. For u≠vu\neq v, define
dcorr​(u,v)≐−log⁡⟨Xu​Xv⟩G,d_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)\doteq-\log\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle_{G},
with the convention dcorr​(u,v)=+∞d_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=+\infty if ⟨Xu​Xv⟩G=0\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle_{G}=0.

Lemma 19 (McShane extension).
Let (𝒳,d𝒳)(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}}) be a metric space and A⊆𝒳A\subseteq\mathcal{X}. If gA:A→ℝg_{A}:A\to\mathbb{R} is LL-Lipschitz, then it extends to an LL-Lipschitz function g:𝒳→ℝg:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}.
Proof.
This is due to McShane (1934). The extension is given by
g⁡(x)≐infa∈A{gA​(a)+L​d𝒳​(x,a)}.g(x)\doteq\inf_{a\in A}\{g_{A}(a)+Ld_{\mathcal{X}}(x,a)\}.

Taking infimum over aa on the right: g⁡(x)≤g⁡(y)+L​d𝒳​(x,y)g(x)\leq g(y)+Ld_{\mathcal{X}}(x,y). By symmetry, |g⁡(x)−g⁡(y)|≤L​d𝒳​(x,y)\absolutevalue{g(x) - g(y)}\leq Ld_{\mathcal{X}}(x,y).
To obtain range [−1,1][-1,1], compose with the clipping map t↦min⁡(1,max⁡(−1,t))t\mapsto\min(1,\max(-1,t)), which is 1-Lipschitz.

Let T=(V,ET)T=(V,E_{T}) be a rooted tree, and let dT​(⋅,⋅){d_{T}}(\cdot,\cdot) denote the unweighted graph distance on TT (edge count along the unique path). A graph G=(V,EG)G=(V,E_{G}) is a KK-local extension of TT if ET⊆EGE_{T}\subseteq E_{G} and for every lateral edge (u,v)∈EG∖ET(u,v)\in E_{G}\setminus E_{T} we have dT​(u,v)≤K{d_{T}}(u,v)\leq K.
This covers sibling augmentation (K=2K=2), cousin connections (K=4K=4), and local grid-like structures attached to the hierarchy.
Lemma 36 (Quasi-isometry of tree-like graphs).
Let GG be a KK-local extension of TT. Then for all u,v∈Vu,v\in V:

Proof.
We shall again treat the upper and lower bound separately.
Upper bound.
Since ET⊆EGE_{T}\subseteq E_{G}, any path in TT is a valid path in GG. Thus dG≤dTd_{G}\leq{d_{T}}.
Lower bound.
Consider a shortest path PGP_{G} between uu and vv in GG. Replace every lateral edge e=(x,y)e=(x,y) in PGP_{G} with the unique path in TT between xx and yy. By definition, this tree-path has length dT​(x,y)≤K{d_{T}}(x,y)\leq K. The resulting path lies entirely in TT and has length at most K​length​(PG)=K​dG​(u,v)K\mathrm{length}(P_{G})=Kd_{G}(u,v).
Thus dT​(u,v)≤K​dG​(u,v){d_{T}}(u,v)\leq Kd_{G}(u,v).

Corollary 37 (Extension of results).
If the graph admits a tree backbone TT such that (i) correlations induce a metric quasi-isometric to dT{d_{T}}, and (ii) the supervised task family is defined w.r.t. TT, then the bounds carry through with constants depending on KK and the correlation-decay parameters:
1.
Euclidean representation lower bound. If dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} is quasi-isometric to the tree backbone distance dT{d_{T}}, then the Euclidean collision argument applies with constants distorted by the quasi-isometry parameters. In particular, a cut that is O⁡(1/R)O(1/R)-Lipschitz in dT{d_{T}} remains O⁡(1/R)O(1/R)-Lipschitz in dcorrd_{\mathrm{corr}} up to constants, and the resulting Euclidean Lipschitz lower bound remains exponential in R/kR/k (up to KK-dependent constants in the exponent).
2.
Rank lower bound. The wavelet/trace argument depends only on the leaf partition induced by the tree backbone and is unaffected by adding lateral edges on the same vertex set.
3.
Hyperbolic embedding. If dGd_{G} and dT{d_{T}} are bi-Lipschitz equivalent up to factor KK as above, then composing this equivalence with a DD-bi-Lipschitz embedding of (V,dT)(V,{d_{T}}) into ℍκk\mathbb{H}^{k}_{\kappa} yields an O⁡(K​D)O(KD)-distortion embedding of (V,dG)(V,d_{G}) into ℍκk\mathbb{H}^{k}_{\kappa}.
