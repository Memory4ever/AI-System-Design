L7@P0: Abstract
L8@P0: The evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) into autonomous agents necessitates the management of extensive, dynamic contexts. Current benchmarks, however, remain largely static, relying on
L9@P0: passive retrieval tasks that fail to simulate the complexities of agent-environment interaction, such as
L10@P0: non-linear reasoning and iterative feedback. To address this, we introduce AgentLongBench, which
L11@P0: evaluates agents through simulated environment rollouts based on Lateral Thinking Puzzles. This
L12@P0: framework generates rigorous interaction trajectories across knowledge-intensive and knowledgefree scenarios. Experiments with state-of-the-art models and memory systems (32K to 4M tokens)
L13@P0: expose a critical weakness: while adept at static retrieval, agents struggle with the dynamic information synthesis essential for workflows. Our analysis indicates that this degradation is driven by
L14@P0: the minimum number of tokens required to resolve a query. This factor explains why the high information density inherent in massive tool responses poses a significantly greater challenge than the
L15@P0: memory fragmentation typical of long-turn dialogues.

L19@P0: 1 Introduction
L20@P0: The rapid evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) from static chatbots to autonomous agents demands
L21@P0: capabilities that extend far beyond simple conversation. Agents must synthesize vast amounts of historical information to execute complex workflows; failure to do so leads to fragmented reasoning, hallucinations, and planning errors. Although the community has actively expanded context windows and developed retrieval-augmented architectures, evaluation frameworks remain disproportionately focused on passive reading comprehension.

L23@P0: solving involves dynamic tool usage and non-linear reasoning, creating a context that evolves based on the

L52@P1: We introduce AgentLongBench to evaluate agents through rigorous environment rollouts. True agentic capabilities emerge within evolving contexts that mirror the causality of real-world workflows, rather than in
L53@P1: static reading comprehension tasks. Consequently, our setting forces agents to parse high-density, machinegenerated tool logs instead of natural text distractors, requiring precise state tracking to manage dynamic
L54@P1: information updates. To guarantee validity, we rely on deterministic ground truth derived from the environment’s logic and incorporate a Knowledge-Free setting to eliminate parametric memory bias. Furthermore,
L55@P1: the framework emphasizes controllability and extensibility [72] to facilitate fine-grained diagnosis of failure
L56@P1: modes. The benchmark comprises 8 tasks in each setting. Figure 1 illustrates the representative task distribution, and we provide full statistics for all settings in Appendix B. Table 1 contrasts our approach with
L57@P1: prior benchmarks, highlighting how AgentLongBench uniquely targets the requirements of autonomous
L58@P1: operation. A comprehensive review of these related works is available in Appendix 5.

L72@P2-3: 32 AgentLongBench
L73@P3: AgentLongBench shifts the evaluation paradigm from static text processing to dynamic interaction through
L74@P3: simulated environment rollouts, with an overview shown in Figure 2. Instead of relying on concatenated
L75@P3: documents, this framework captures the continuous exchange between an agent and a reactive system. By
L76@P3: simulating the temporal evolution of context, we preserve the causal dependencies inherent in real-world
L77@P3: applications, which are often lost in static reading comprehension tasks.
L78@P3: The foundation of this framework is a Lateral Thinking Puzzle environment [2], selected for its rigorous
L79@P3: demands on iterative inquiry and logical deduction. As the agent hypothesizes and receives feedback, the
L80@P3: context expands procedurally based on the environment’s deterministic rules. This generation mechanism
L81@P3: ensures that the resulting long-context trajectories are verifiable and logically consistent, avoiding the artifacts common in benchmarks constructed via arbitrary document insertion.
L82@P3: We structure the benchmark around two orthogonal dimensions, creating four distinct experimental configurations. To decouple reasoning skills from parametric memory, the first dimension contrasts KnowledgeIntensive with Knowledge-Free scenarios. Simultaneously, we examine the trade-off between temporal span
L83@P3: and information density by varying the interaction format between Concise responses and Verbose responses.
L84@P3: This cross-dimensional design ensures that performance degradation can be traced directly to either memory
L85@P3: retention failures or information overload.
L86@P3: 2.1 Lateral Thinking Puzzle Environment
L87@P3: The core testbed utilizes Lateral Thinking Puzzles, where agents must reconstruct a hidden state by satisfying
L88@P3: a series of logical constraints. This iterative inquiry process contrasts sharply with static QA, serving as a
L89@P3: rigorous proxy for the complex, investigative workflows typical of autonomous agents.
L90@P3: 2.1.1 Puzzle Formulation
L91@P3: We formulate the puzzle as a target identification process under a closed-world assumption. The environment initializes a finite set of items, each defined by a unique vector of attributes ranging from categorical
L92@P3: properties (e.g., Type, Abilities) to numerical statistics. One item is designated as the hidden target. In the
L93@P3: Knowledge-Intensive setting, we instantiate this item set using the Pokémon[1] dataset (pre-July 2025), ensuring that no two items share identical attribute profiles. The agent operates as an investigator, engaging in
L94@P3: a goal-oriented dialogue to bridge the information gap between its current belief state and the ground truth.
L95@P3: The puzzle unfolds as the agent continuously queries the environment to isolate the target.
L96@P3: 2.1.2 Environment Response
L97@P3: The environment functions as a deterministic oracle that holds the ground truth. To simulate rigorous agentic constraints, it parses natural language inquiries and returns precise feedback rather than open-ended
L98@P3: narrative descriptions. Responses are typically limited to binary validation (Yes/No) or relational operators for numeric values. Crucially, upon an incorrect guess, the environment generates a comprehensive
L99@P3: attribute-wise evaluation. It returns the full profile of the hypothesized item, explicitly annotating the relationship between each attribute and the ground truth—confirming categorical matches while providing
L100@P3: directional constraints for numerical discrepancies. This feedback mechanism enforces strict state tracking,
L101@P3: as the agent must update its history of valid constraints to iteratively narrow the search space.
L102@P3: 2.1.3 Tool Response
L103@P3: Beyond direct interaction with the environment, the agent is equipped with auxiliary tools to aid in state
L104@P3: refinement. The Tool Response represents the output from these external function calls. We provide two
L105@P3: search tools corresponding to the two formatting strategies described in Section 2.3.2. The context generated
L106@P3: here differs from the Environment Response; it often contains structured, noisy, or verbose text that the agent
L107@P3: must parse, filter, and integrate into its working memory.
L108@P3-4: 42.2 Data Construction
L109@P4: The dataset is constructed through automated environment rollouts governed by a rule-based simulation.
L110@P4: The generation process begins with the random selection of an initial item to trigger the feedback loop. For
L111@P4: subsequent rounds, we generate a sequence of [Tool use, Tool Response, Guess, Environment Response] for
L112@P4: each interaction step. To achieve scalable context lengths, we parametrically adjust the granularity of puzzle
L113@P4: constraints or sequentially chain related interaction sessions. This methodology ensures that the expanded
L114@P4: context remains causally linked and logically coherent, avoiding the disjointed nature of random document
L115@P4: concatenation.
L116@P4: 2.3 Task Description
L117@P4: We decompose the evaluation into specific cognitive dimensions to pinpoint the mechanisms of agentic failure.
L118@P4: 2.3.1 Two Settings
L119@P4: We differentiate the evaluation based on the semantic nature of the information processed. The KnowledgeIntensive setting constructs trajectories using real-world entities, instantiated here with the Pokémon dataset.
L120@P4: Although the evaluation answers are theoretically derivable solely from the context history, the presence of
L121@P4: familiar entities triggers the model’s parametric memory. This design mimics realistic domain-specific tasks
L122@P4: where models may hallucinate based on prior knowledge—such as predicting a type based on a name rather
L123@P4: than tool output—or conversely, benefit from domain familiarity.
L124@P4: To rigorously evaluate pure in-context reasoning, we employ a Knowledge-Free setting via fully symbolic
L125@P4: masking. Unlike previous benchmarks that merely perform entity substitution (e.g., changing ”Newton” to
L126@P4: ”John”) while retaining semantic structures, we map all entities and attributes to abstract tokens. Specifically,
L127@P4: item names are mapped to IDs (e.g., Item_84), and attributes are mapped to abstract codes (e.g., Attr_1 with
L128@P4: value A1V1). This approach eliminates semantic cues, forcing the model to rely exclusively on the logical
L129@P4: constraints defined within the interaction history. This provides an unbiased measurement of state tracking
L130@P4: and memory retention, free from the interference of pre-trained knowledge.
L131@P4: 2.3.2 Two Formats
L132@P4: To investigate how information distribution impacts performance, we apply two distinct interaction formats.
L133@P4: While both are controlled to maintain comparable total context lengths, they present fundamentally different
L134@P4: challenges regarding information density.
L135@P4: The Concise-Response format prioritizes turns extension. Here, the tool returns only the pre-calculated
L136@P4: intersection of candidate items that satisfy the queried attributes.To accumulate a total context length comparable to the Verbose format, this setting involves a massive number of interaction turns (e.g., hundreds
L137@P4: of rounds). This design evaluates whether agents can maintain consistent state tracking over hundreds of
L138@P4: rounds without losing early constraints.
L139@P4: Conversely, the Verbose-Response format tests the capacity to handle information overload. In this setting,
L140@P4: tools return full, unfiltered candidate lists for each queried attribute. A query for three attributes results in
L141@P4: three extensive lists, creating a trajectory with fewer turns but high-density blocks of structured text. The
L142@P4: agent is thus forced to perform logical intersections internally and extract key information from massive,
L143@P4: noisy inputs within a single step.
L144@P4: 2.3.3 Task Taxonomy
L145@P4: To pinpoint the specific cognitive mechanisms behind agentic failures, we classify the eight tasks into three
L146@P4: dimensions (visualized in Figure 2; see Appendix D for detailed definitions).
L147@P4-5: 5Figure 3 Main Results on Knowledge-Intensive & Concise-Response Setting. The heatmap visualizes model performance
L148@P5: across varying context lengths (32K to 2M). Green indicates higher accuracy.
L149@P5: QA in Tool Response evaluates the robustness of parsing machine-generated logs. Tasks such as Find Duplicates measure the ability to extract precise details from structured noise, explicitly testing resilience against
L150@P5: information overload.
L151@P5: QA in Environment Response targets the capacity to track evolving states and interpret historical feedback
L152@P5: constraints, exemplified by the Weighted Summation task. This dimension assesses how well an agent maintains a coherent belief state across long, iterative interactions, isolating the impact of memory fragmentation.
L153@P5: Final Guess (Intersection) serves as the ultimate test of global understanding. It requires the agent to perform logical set operations across the entire context trajectory to deduce the target, thereby integrating retrieval with deductive reasoning.
L154@P5: This dimensional breakdown allows us to distinguish whether performance degradation stems from local
L155@P5: retrieval errors, calculation deficits, or a fundamental breakdown in logical consistency.

L193@P8: 4 Analysis
L194@P8: This section analyzes the experimental results with an eye toward concrete failure modes. We organize the
L195@P8: discussion around the three research questions in Section 2.3.
L196@P8: 4.1 Knowledge-Intensive vs. Knowledge-Free
L197@P8: We first contrast the Knowledge-Intensive and Knowledge-Free settings (Figure 3 and Figure 4). A clear example
L198@P8: is the Intersection task. In the Knowledge-Intensive setting, models achieve non-trivial accuracy at shorter
L199@P8: contexts (e.g., GPT-4.1 reaches roughly 30–40%). In the Knowledge-Free setting, the same task drops to nearzero performance for nearly all models, including the strongest ones.
L200@P8: This gap suggests that the Knowledge-Intensive setting allows models to lean on parametric associations
L201@P8: between familiar entities and attributes, which can partially substitute for the explicit set operations required by the context. When those semantic cues are removed, models must rely almost entirely on symbolic state tracking over the interaction history, where performance deteriorates sharply. In other words,
L202@P8: the Knowledge-Free setting exposes a weakness in maintaining and updating discrete logical states under

L209@P9: 4.2 Concise-Response vs. Verbose-Response
L210@P9: We analyze how the response format reshapes difficulty by comparing the Concise-Response setting (Figure 3)
L211@P9: with the Verbose-Response setting (Figure 5). The key difference is not simply whether responses are “more
L212@P9: informative,” but how information that is irrelevant to the queried target is distributed across an episode.
L213@P9: For tasks that query Environment Response (e.g., Count Frequency (Env)), the Verbose format often yields
L214@P9: higher accuracy. This improvement does not come from richer tool outputs—the tool content is largely incidental for these queries. Instead, verbose responses reduce the number of interaction turns. Under the
L215@P9: concise format, episodes become much longer, and the model must maintain the evolving state across many
L216@P9: steps even though most tool-return tokens are not needed for answering. This makes long-horizon state
L217@P9: tracking the dominant bottleneck, and performance degrades accordingly.
L218@P9: The trend reverses for tasks that query Tool Response(e.g., Find Duplicates). Here the tool-return content is
L219@P9: the evidence. The Verbose format concentrates a large amount of machine-generated text into fewer turns,
L220@P9: which increases the density of distractors and the cost of locating the relevant fields within a single response.
L221@P9: In contrast, the Concise format spreads tool outputs across more turns, but each turn is smaller and easier
L222@P9: to scan, which can make evidence localization and comparison more manageable for these tasks.
L223@P9: Overall, the two formats trade off where the difficulty lies: Concise increases episode length and long-range
L224@P9: state maintenance, while Verbose increases within-turn density when the answer must be extracted from
L225@P9: tool logs.
L226@P9: 4.3 Environment Response vs. Tool Response and Adequate Context Length
L227@P9: Across all four settings, we observe a consistent gap between tasks that query Tool Response and those
L228@P9: that query Environment Response. In Figure 3, tool-response tasks (top row) are uniformly harder than
L229@P9: environment-response tasks (bottom row), even when the total context length is matched.
L230@P9: To account for this gap, we introduce Adequate Context Length (ACL). ACL is a property of the input
L231@P9: trajectory: it measures how many tokens a model must traverse to locate and assemble the evidence needed
L232@P9: for a single query. Importantly, ACL is computed from the episode text alone and does not depend on model
L233@P9: outputs or prediction correctness.

L235@P10: This advantage stems from the experimental design rather than the content itself: Verbose trajectories inherently comprise significantly fewer interaction steps than their Concise counterparts. Crucially, since the
L236@P10: answers to these tasks are derived solely from environmental feedback, the voluminous tool outputs in the
L237@P10: Verbose format serve merely as distractors which do not aid reasoning. The results suggest that models find
L238@P10: it easier to bypass this dense, irrelevant noise within a single turn than to maintain a coherent state across
L239@P10: the hundreds of fragmented turns characteristic of the Concise format.
L240@P10: The pattern reverses for tasks that query tool logs, such as QA in Tool Response (e.g., Find Duplicates). Here,
L241@P10: Verbose responses often hurt. Consolidating tool outputs into a single turn yields long, dense, machinegenerated text where relevant fields are mixed with large amounts of irrelevant structure. This creates information overload: the model must extract and align specific values from the verbose block.

L254@P10: 5 Related works
L255@P10: Benchmarks for Long Contexts. The evaluation of long-context LLMs has evolved from adapting traditional
L256@P10: NLP tasks to designing complex, synthetic reasoning scenarios. However, the majority of these efforts focus
L257@P10: on the static context with no close relation with one target, showing less reality in agent scenerios..
L258@P10: General Understanding and Retrieval. Initial benchmarks, such as [53, 54] and [6], established the foundation by
L259@P10: aggregating datasets like NarrativeQA [33] and GovReport [27]. These were further standardized by suites
L260@P10: like L-Eval [4] and LongBenchV2 [7] to assess general capabilities across varying lengths. A major focus
L261@P10: has been strictly testing retrieval limits, popularized by the ”Needle-In-A-Haystack” paradigm [29]. This
L262@P10: approach has expanded into comprehensive suites like RULER [25] and NeedleBench [37], covering multineedle retrieval [49, 52], citation evaluation [40, 74], and instruction following stability [65]. Recent studies
L263@P10: have also utilized these setups to investigate phenomena like ”Lost-In-the-Middle” [19, 44] and the impact
L264@P10: of demonstration quality in long in-context learning [3, 8, 28, 38, 61].
L265@P10: Reasoning, Logic, and Domain Specifics. To evaluate deeper cognitive functions beyond passive retrieval, recent works have introduced tasks requiring logic, mathematics, and code understanding. ∞-Bench [78]
L266@P10: and BAMBOO [15] target complex reasoning across diverse domains, while others focus on repository-level
L267@P10: code [43] and mathematical noise filtering [62]. A critical direction involves testing long-range dependencies
L268@P10: and robustness. Benchmarks like LooGLE [23, 36] and BABILong [34] challenge models to trace multi-hop evidence or comprehend native long texts such as novels [60, 69, 73]. Concurrently, works like LV-Eval [77] and
L269@P10: NoCha [31] probe model robustness against confounding facts. Other efforts emphasize data synthesis and
L270@P10: controllability: Michelangelo [58] and HoloBench [46] test structure discernment, while Ada-LEval [59] and
L271@P10: LongBioBench [72] utilize generated content to decouple reasoning from parametric memory. Loong [63]
L272@P10: further pushes density by ensuring every document is crucial.
