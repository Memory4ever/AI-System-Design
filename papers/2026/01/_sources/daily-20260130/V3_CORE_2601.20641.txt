Abstract
We investigate whether LLM-based agents can develop task-oriented communication protocols that differ from standard natural language in collaborative reasoning tasks.
Our focus is on two core properties such task-oriented protocols may exhibit:
Efficiency—conveying task-relevant information more concisely than natural language, and
Covertness---becoming difficult for external observers to interpret, raising concerns about transparency and control.
To investigate these aspects, we use a referential-game framework in which vision-language model (VLM) agents communicate, providing a controlled, measurable setting for evaluating language variants.
Experiments show that VLMs can develop effective, task-adapted communication patterns. At the same time, they can develop covert protocols that are difficult for humans and external agents to interpret. We also observe spontaneous coordination between similar models without explicitly shared protocols.
These findings highlight both the potential and the risks of task-oriented communication, and position referential games as a valuable testbed for future work in this area.11

1 Introduction
“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”
— Ludwig Wittgenstein (1921)
Language has long served as the foundation of human communication and intelligence. But as large language models (LLMs) grow more capable, a fundamental question arises: Is natural language the best medium for communication between artificial agents?
Recent advances in LLMs and vision-language models (VLMs) have enabled agents to engage in grounded reasoning and collaborate using natural language (Kuckreja et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Yet as their capabilities begin to surpass humans in various domains, we must ask: Could natural language, shaped by the affordances of human cognition, limit AI agents in certain tasks?
Motivated by this view, we ask whether AI agents might benefit from developing task-oriented language variants that are better aligned with their internal representations and reasoning mechanisms.
In this paper, we investigate whether such task-oriented language variants can emerge, and examine how they compare to natural language along two task-specific dimensions: Efficiency—transmitting task-relevant information more concisely; and Covertness—remaining interpretable to communicating agents but opaque to external observers.
Figure 1:
Illustration of a referential game using the flags dataset. For clarity, the shared visual world, comprising 4 flag images, is shown on both sides.
In step (1), the sender is prompted to develop a lexicon for describing the images. In step (2), the receiver independently develops its own lexicon based on the same visual input. Alternatively, in step (2*), the sender shares its lexicon with the receiver.
In step (3), one image is randomly selected as the target, and the sender produces a description using its developed lexicon and communicates it to the receiver.
Finally, in step (4), the receiver attempts to match the sender’s description to one of the candidate images using its own visual and language representation.
Related questions have been considered in the literature on emergent communication, which studies
whether new languages can arise naturally as agents optimize for shared tasks (see Boldt and Mortensen 2024 for a recent survey). This possibility presents both opportunities and risks: such protocols may enhance cooperation and efficiency, yet may remain opaque to humans, raising concerns about transparency and alignment.
However, most of the work in this area assumes simple agents that learn to communicate from scratch.
In contrast,
LLMs are trained primarily on natural language, so prompting them to create entirely new communication protocols presents a significant transfer challenge. It requires models to repurpose linguistic knowledge in novel contexts and generalize in a zero-shot setting. Can they develop protocols that, while differing from natural language, offer practical advantages in constrained tasks like referential games (Lazaridou and Baroni, 2020)?22
2
Our focus is on such goal-directed tasks that probe the functional aspects of communication. In contrast, human language serves broader social, cultural, and emotional roles (Tomasello, 2010), which lie beyond the scope of this study.
Studying such language variants poses a methodological challenge: How can we evaluate communication that we cannot interpret? To address this, we adopt the referential game framework of Lazaridou and Baroni (2020), which enables objective evaluation based solely on communicative success.
In a referential game (Lewis, 1969), illustrated in Figure 1, a sender observes a target image and a set of distractor images, and generates a description. A Receiver, given the same set of images and the description, must identify the correct one. This setup allows us to measure communication success without requiring human interpretability, making it ideal for studying opaque, emergent protocols.
Importantly, our sender and receiver agents are prompted VLMs, with their existing knowledge and linguistic capability.
Contributions and key findings.
Through a series of experiments, we reveal four key findings:
1.
Models may spontaneously generate new words and symbols when operating under tight communicative constraints;
2.
When explicitly prompted, they develop language variants that may surpass natural language in efficiency;
3.
Agents with similar architectures and training procedures can independently develop covert protocols that remain difficult for external observers to interpret; and
4.
These protocols are often better understood by the models themselves than by humans.
2 Related Work
Referential games and emergent Communication. Referential games have long served as a testbed for studying how communication systems emerge, beginning with classical formulations of symbolic reference (Lewis, 1969; Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986). Modern work extends these ideas to neural agents trained from scratch, examining how communication protocols arise under cooperative objectives (Lazaridou et al., 2017; Lazaridou et al., 2018; Batali, 1998; Choi et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2018; Jaques et al., 2019; Das et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2020; Gratch et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2022). Variants include discrete vs. continuous message spaces (Havrylov and Titov, 2017), visually grounded settings (Denamganaï and Walker, 2020), interpretable symbol systems (Mu and Goodman, 2021; Dessì et al., 2021), and compositional generalization (Carmeli et al., 2025).
Referential games with pretrained VLMs.
Recent work has begun to explore referential games with pretrained models. Kouwenhoven et al. (2024) investigate structured communication by fine-tuning LLMs on artificial symbol spaces. Their follow-up study (Kouwenhoven et al., 2025) examines how communication protocols adapt to the inductive biases of humans and LLMs, showing that grounded vocabularies emerge across unified (Human–Human, LLM–LLM) and mixed settings, with mixed interactions yielding more human-aligned conventions. Similarly, Vaduguru et al. (2025) show that repeated referential-game interactions can elicit efficient communicative conventions in multimodal models, provided that both communicative success and message cost are jointly optimized. Along a theoretical dimension, Taniguchi et al. (2024) connect emergent communication, world models, and LLMs through collective predictive coding, offering an account of why structured communication may arise in pretrained models. Our work complements these efforts by eliciting task-oriented language variants through zero-shot prompting and by evaluating not only their efficiency but also the extent to which such communication becomes difficult for external observers to interpret.
Constructed and human-invented languages.
While emergent communication research typically focuses on systems developed from scratch, less work examines communication variants built on top of natural language. Human-designed constructed languages (“conlangs”) demonstrate how explicit design choices shape linguistic structure (Schreyer, 2021; Gonzalez, 2024). In contrast, our interest lies in language variants produced by VLMs during cooperative tasks.
Relatedly, Diamond (2023) analyze whether LLM-generated lexicons exhibit statistical properties found in natural languages, such as Zipf’s law.
Efficient communication and compression.
Efficiency, i.e., communicating the same information with fewer symbols, has been explored in natural-language-based image compression (Li et al., 2024; Lei et al., 2023; Careil et al., 2023) and LLM-driven textual transform coding (Weissman, 2023). Our work studies a complementary setting: task-adapted communication in referential games, where models may develop concise descriptions grounded in shared visual or latent representations.
Covert or hard-to-interpret communication.
Several works consider communication that remains opaque to external observers. Yu et al. (2022) introduce an adversarial referential game in which agents aim to avoid overseer leakage. More recently, Mathew et al. (2024) demonstrate steganographic signaling emerging as a byproduct of optimization pressure in LLMs. Our approach differs by prompting VLM-based agents to generate communication variants under objectives that include both efficiency and reduced interpretability, and by empirically evaluating these properties in controlled referential-game settings.
3 Methods
Figure 2: Examples from three datasets (Flags, COCO, CLEVR), each showing two images with three descriptions: Natural (top row), Efficient (middle row), and Covert (bottom row).
We adopt a referential game framework to evaluate whether VLMs can generate and use novel communication protocols. Unlike traditional emergent communication setups that train agents from scratch (Lazaridou and Baroni, 2020), we assume agents are already proficient in natural language and test whether they can develop more efficient or covert protocols. In each game (Figure 1), a visual world 𝕎{\mathbb{W}} consisting of up to 10 images is shared between a Sender SS and a Receiver RR, presented in different orders. One image is sampled as the target tt, which the sender describes, and the receiver must identify.
VLMs act as sender, receiver, and overseer agents, all prompted with the shared visual context and natural language instructions.
The overseer mirrors the receiver’s task but lacks access to sender’s task-adapted protocol, interpreting the target description as if natural language were used.
3.1 Zero-Shot Prompting
Zero-shot prompting allows LLMs to perform novel tasks from instructions alone (Ouyang et al., 2022). We use this capability to guide VLMs in producing and applying task-oriented language variants in referential games. Prompts encourage compression, opacity, or optimization of communication without fine-tuning, enabling emergent behaviors purely through prompt design.
Full prompts are given in §E.
3.2 Language Variants
We evaluate three language conditions:
Natural (baseline).
The sender freely describes the target image using natural language. To introduce mild optimization pressure, we optionally constrain the description length.
Efficient (efficiency-focused).
The sender is instructed to induce a concise communication protocol for the image set and describe the target using this protocol. Efficiency is encouraged by prompting the sender to invent a concise task-specific language and imposing limits on description length.
Covert (covertness-focused).
The sender is instructed to develop a protocol that allows successful communication with the receiver while remaining opaque to an uninformed overseer. Here, too, description length is constrained for comparability.
For the Efficient and Covert language variants, we evaluate two receiver setups:
(a) Shared-language: The receiver is provided with the sender’s induced communication schema and uses it to interpret the description.
(b) Locally-adapted-language: The receiver develops its own communication scheme based on the same image set seen by the sender.
This design allows us to compare standard language use with task-adapted communication developed independently or in coordination.
3.3 Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the quality and utility of the adapted communication protocols, we consider the following metrics:
Game accuracy: The proportion of turns in which the receiver correctly identifies the target image out of the candidate set.
Description length: Measured in two ways: word count (space-separated), and character count, to assess communicative efficiency.
New word rate (NWR): To determine whether a word is genuinely novel, we use a word classification module that integrates lexical and statistical tools. We first check for existing entries in the WordNet lexical database (Miller, 1995). If no synset is found, we rely on spaCy (Honnibal et al., 2020) to verify whether the word exists in a broad language model vocabulary, based on the presence of a word vector, sufficient unigram probability, or inclusion in a curated list of common words.

4.1 Vision-Language Models
In this work, we focus on four representative models: Gpt-4o (Hurst et al., 2024), Qwen2-VL-72B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), Pixtral-12b-2409 (Agrawal et al., 2024), and Llama-4-Maverick-17B-128E-Instruct-FP8 (Meta, 2025). Detailed descriptions of these models and the specific versions used are provided in § F.1.
4.2 Datasets
We conduct our experiments across three distinct visual datasets that capture complementary aspects of visual-linguistic reasoning: MS-COCO (Lin et al., 2014), CLEVR (Johnson et al., 2017), and our newly introduced Flags dataset.33
3
Based on github.com/hampusborgos/country-flags
MS-COCO provides diverse, natural photographic scenes rich in real-world semantics and object co-occurrence patterns, while CLEVR consists of procedurally generated images designed to emphasize compositional reasoning under controlled geometric configurations.
In contrast, the Flags dataset, introduced in this work, features structured, symbolic designs composed of geometric and abstract shapes with a wide color palette.
Figure 2 presents two example images from each dataset, accompanied by three corresponding descriptions per image, one for each language variant: Natural (top row), Efficient (middle row), and Covert (bottom row).
The Flags Dataset.
Many of the visual primitives in the Flags dataset are rare in natural imagery, leading to limited lexical coverage in natural language and posing a unique challenge for referential communication tasks.
We construct two variants of this dataset. The Real variant contains actual national flags, likely familiar to the models from pretraining. In contrast, the Synthetic variant consists of synthetic flags generated using the mixtral-8x22B-instruct model (Mistral, 2023); see §F.4 for details.
These synthetic flags are novel and compositional in nature, enabling a systematic assessment of models’ ability to describe and interpret unseen visual structures. This dual dataset variants allows us to probe the models’ prior visual and linguistic knowledge: while real flags may be identified through memorized associations (e.g., “France”), synthetic ones require relying solely on visual features, testing the capacity to generate novel, compositional descriptions.
Together, these datasets provide a unified testbed for studying grounded communication across naturalistic (MS-COCO), synthetic compositional (CLEVR), and symbolic abstract (Flags) domains, enabling a multifaceted analysis of multimodal language emergence.
4.3 Experiments
We evaluated the three language induction setups described in §3.2 using the datasets and vision–language models introduced above. Results are reported in §5.
In all setups, each game involves 1010 candidate images. Each reported data point averages the outcomes of 300300 rounds, with each round using a distinct set of targets and distractors. The standard error of the mean (SEM) for these setups reaches a maximum of 0.25/300≈0.029\sqrt{0.25/300}\approx 0.029.
Informed Sender:
Lazaridou et al. (2017) introduced the term informed sender to describe a setup in which the sender has access to all candidate images when generating the target description. We evaluated this configuration in comparison with an uninformed sender, with detailed results provided in Appendix I. Based on these findings, we adopted the informed-sender setting for our experiments.
5 Results
5.1 Natural Language Performance
Table 1 reports the performance of the four evaluated VLMs across the three datasets in the referential game using natural language.
As shown, substantial performance differences emerge both between models and across datasets, supporting the validity of our experimental setup.
GPT achieves the highest performance on all datasets, whereas Pixtral shows the lowest.
Most models struggle on the CLEVR dataset and attain their best average performance on MS-COCO.
Furthermore, model performance consistently improves with longer description lengths.
Generating new words using Natural language.
Table 2 reports the rate at which senders introduce novel words when describing targets in the Natural setting under varying length constraints. Stronger length limits consistently lead to higher rates of word invention, with most models producing substantial numbers of new words only under the single-word condition. GPT generates novel words across all datasets in this setting, reaching a peak rate of 0.640.64 on CLEVR, whereas Qwen does not introduce new words at all. For example, GPT invents words such as blorple and ragolay in Flags, Laptopcat and Surfdog in MS-COCO, and Varok and Zylo in CLEVR. These findings highlight our first main result: models can spontaneously invent novel lexical items when optimizing communication under strict constraints.

5.2 Developing an Efficient Language Variant
Figure 3 presents the results of experiments in which GPT agents were explicitly instructed to develop an Efficient language.
The orange lines indicate accuracy across the three datasets, the blue lines represent the baseline performance of agents using Natural language, and the green lines show receiver performance when the receiver is unaware that the sender is communicating in an Efficient language.
Graph labels further indicate the proportion of new words invented and used by the sender to describe the target.
As shown, agents communicating in an Efficient language achieve consistently higher accuracy than those using Natural language in both the Flags and MS-COCO datasets across all length constraints achieving 0.89 accuracy with descriptions of 10 characters long compared to 0.79 for Natural language in the Flags dataset.
In all datasets, the performance of an unaware receiver is markedly lower than that of an aware one.
A drop of approximately 0.10–0.15 in accuracy on average is observed for the Flags dataset and 0.30–0.40 for CLEVR.
Interestingly, the rate of new word invention increases with shorter description lengths, rising from 0.14 to 0.99 and from 0.46 to 0.89 for a single-word description on the Flags and MS-COCO datasets respectively.
When using Natural language, the GPT model generates new words only when constrained to single-word descriptions, whereas in the Efficient language condition, new words emerge under all length constraints.
These high rates of novel word generation limit the receiver’s ability to interpret the sender’s messages, as reflected by the green curves in Figure 3.
Collectively, these results underscore our second key insight: when explicitly instructed, agents can develop communication protocols that surpass natural language in efficiency.

Table 3 presents the results of experiments in which agents were instructed to develop a Covert language.
We tested two receiver configurations: Shared, where the sender’s language variant is shared with the receiver, and Local, where the receiver independently develops its own language variant.
An overseer model was included in all experiments to assess the interpretability of the communication without knowing that a Covert language was being used.
In all datasets, sender-receiver pairs with similar architectures consistently outperform mixed-model pairs when the language is not shared (compare top cells to the rest in middle columns).
Performance gaps are most pronounced for the GPT sender-receiver pair: It achieves up to 0.710.71 on Flags and 0.600.60 on MS-COCO, outperforming the best mixed-model pairs by about 0.300.30 points on average.
When the language is shared (left columns), performance differences between model types narrow, and the intrinsic quality of the individual models becomes more evident.
For instance, a Pixtral sender paired with a Qwen receiver achieves 0.810.81 accuracy on MS-COCO and 0.500.50 on CLEVR, surpassing the 0.720.72 and 0.470.47 achieved by the Pixtral sender-receiver pair on the same datasets.
Language sharing benefits cross-model pairs more strongly than same-model pairs, as evidenced by the marked increase in mixed-architecture performance (compare the top cells in the left columns with the bottom two cells in these columns).
This pattern suggests that agents with similar architectures can partially infer each other’s communication protocol even when the language is not shared, and thus benefit less from sharing it.
Finally, the performance gap between receivers and overseers of the same model type (top cells, right two columns) compared to those of mixed architectures (middle and bottom cells in these columns) further supports this observation.
For instance, when using GPT agents on the MS-COCO dataset, the gap between the receiver without language sharing and the overseer is 1212 points, whereas the corresponding gaps for Qwen and Pixtral are only 22 and 44 points, respectively.
Collectively, these findings highlight our third key insight:
agents with similar architectures can independently develop covert protocols that remain unintelligible to external observers.
5.4 Human Evaluation
Table 4 presents the results of a human evaluation in which 1515 participants acted as receivers in a referential game.44
4
https://eval-lang-v0.streamlit.app/
In all conditions, participants were not familiar with the artificial language developed by the sender and relied solely on their natural language understanding.
Each participant was shown a description produced by the sender and a set of 10 candidate synthetic-flag images. They had to select the image that best matched the given description.
We evaluated three representative experimental conditions, corresponding to the Natural, Efficient, and Covert setups, using GPT as the sender in all cases, as it consistently outperformed the other models.
The descriptions provided by the sender were constrained to five words in both the Natural and Covert setups, and to a single word in the Efficient setup.
As the table shows, on average, human participants performed comparably to the mean performance of the four model agents.
Interestingly, the GPT receiver consistently outperformed humans across all three experiments.
This highlights our fourth key insight: models can develop language variants that are more interpretable to themselves than to humans. This advantage may reflect their superior task proficiency or their enhanced ability to decode messages from senders with similar underlying architectures.

5.5 Language Analysis
We provide here a short analysis of the Efficient and Covert language variants generated by the sender during the target-description task. Appendix K contains a more detailed analysis.
In both setups, the models first define the building blocks of the language and then use these elements to describe the target image. We observe several key differences between the two languages.
Efficient languages use compact dictionaries, with 10.610.6, 9.99.9, 13.113.1, and 8.68.6 elements for GPT, Qwen, Pixtral, and Maverick, respectively, closely matching the 1010 images in the context window.
Many of these words are portmanteaus, such as Oranbath and Snowswoop (MS-COCO, GPT) or starbord and tribar (Flags, Maverick). While these lexicons generally enable models to produce efficient one-word descriptions for MS-COCO and Flags images, they are often insufficient for fully encoding the multi-object scenes in CLEVR. To compensate, some models (e.g., Pixtral) construct larger vocabularies, whereas others (e.g., GPT) occasionally display internal compositionality, as in its definition of snarp as “a combination of a zorp and a plok in close proximity”, where zorp is defined as “A cylinder with a matte finish” and plok is defined as “A small cube, often matte and solid-colored”. When models are permitted to generate multi-word descriptions, they frequently blend dictionary entries with natural language terms, such as Florvase on dining table.
Covert languages, in contrast, rely on structured obfuscation. Models construct these codes by identifying recurring visual elements, assigning symbolic tokens with weak structural cues, and defining rules for composing them into full descriptions. Covert lexicons are typically larger than their Efficient counterparts, comprising 16.816.8, 23.023.0, 17.717.7, and 14.614.6 elements for GPT, Qwen, Pixtral, and Maverick, respectively. Models then apply these lexicons and compositional rules to generate multi-token descriptions. For example, GPT produces the sequence Plo Rond Cen to encode a flag with a light-blue background and central yellow ovals, while Maverick generates Hum-Neb-Act-Hit-Out-Vel-Kri to describe a human (Hum) hitting a baseball (Act-Hit) outdoors (Out) in green (Neb), black (Vel), and white (Kri) colors from the MS-COCO dataset.
New word rate (NWR):
We measure the rate at which each model introduces new words across the three language settings.
In the Efficient language condition, all models produce a substantial number of new words, with average NWRs of 0.910.91, 0.440.44, 0.390.39 and 0.580.58 for GPT, Qwen, Pixtral and Maverick, respectively. See Appendix C for example descriptions.
In the Covert condition, NWR reflects each model’s ability to develop obfuscated protocols. For instance, GPT produces utterances like r2c2 k8c4 and Pixtral uses phrases such as zor dok un mor, for the Flags dataset. Similarly, Qwen produces tokens such as B G T 2 C and SBLs|SYsR for the CLEVR datasets, demonstrating the models’ ability to obscure meaning through novel surface forms.
The average NWRs for the three datasets are 0.680.68, 0.320.32, 0.160.16, and 0.350.35 for GPT, Qwen, Pixtral, and Maverick, respectively. See Appendix D for example descriptions.

6 Conclusions
In this work, we investigated how VLMs can develop task-oriented language variants to describe visual inputs. We showed that models such as Gpt-4o can create new lexical items or repurpose existing ones to communicate image content more efficiently, often with fewer words than when relying solely on natural language vocabulary.
Beyond efficiency, these models can develop internally consistent communication patterns that difficult for humans and external agents to interpret. Notably, models with the same architecture were able to interpret each other’s covert descriptions without having explicitly shared the constructed language variant, highlighting their reliance on a shared internal representation.
While our results highlight the potential for constructing more efficient and covert protocols, they also raise broader questions about which traits make a language truly useful to agents. Natural language, for example, is remarkably robust to ambiguity, noise, and variation, properties that contribute to its resilience. Investigating whether ask-oriented language variants can similarly achieve or trade off such properties remains an important direction for future work.
7 Limitations
While our study demonstrates that pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) can induce and utilize task-adapted communication protocols under zero-shot prompting, several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, our experiments are restricted to a small number of candidate images per referential game (up to 10), which may not reflect more complex or realistic visual environments. Scaling to larger sets of images could impact both protocol efficiency and model performance.
Second, we rely exclusively on pre-trained VLMs without fine-tuning. While this isolates zero-shot capabilities, it may limit the emergence of more sophisticated or consistent language variants that could appear under training or few-shot adaptation.
Third, our evaluation focuses on synthetic efficiency and covertness metrics, including novel word rate and game accuracy. These metrics may not fully capture semantic fidelity, interpretability, or generalization to out-of-distribution images.
Fourth, the overseer agent in our setup is assumed to have access to the same architecture as the receiver, potentially underestimating the challenge of detecting covert protocols in more diverse or realistic observers.
Finally, we limit our study to English-based communication and pre-defined datasets (Flags, COCO, CLEVR). Different languages, visual domains, or multi-agent scenarios may yield different emergent behaviors and require further investigation.
Overall, these limitations suggest that while our findings reveal important insights about zero-shot protocol emergence in VLMs, caution is warranted when extrapolating to more complex settings.

Appendix F Experimental Setup
In this section we provide more details on the experimental setup being used.
F.1 Vision Language Models
We use various vision language models in our experiments.
Specifically, we use the following four models:
GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024):
GPT-4o ("o" for omni) is a multimodal large language model developed by OpenAI that can natively process text, images, and audio.
Unlike earlier models that combined separate vision and language components, GPT-4o uses a unified architecture trained end-to-end across modalities.
It achieves strong performance on vision-language benchmarks such as RefCOCO and VQAv2, while also enabling efficient real-time interactions across modalities.
Qwen2-VL-72B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025):
Qwen2-VL-72B-Instruct is a large-scale, instruction-tuned vision-language model developed by Alibaba as part of the Qwen2 family.
It extends the Qwen2-72B base model by integrating a vision encoder and instruction-following capabilities across image-text tasks.
The model is optimized for high accuracy in grounded multimodal reasoning and visual instruction following, and supports multilingual understanding.
Pixtral-12b-2409 (Agrawal et al., 2024):
We use the mistralai/pixtral-12b-2409 model, a multimodal instruction-tuned model released by Mistral AI. Building on the architecture of their highly efficient language-only models, Pixtral combines a vision encoder with a LLaMA-style language decoder in a modular fashion. It is instruction-tuned for a variety of multimodal tasks, including image understanding, captioning, and visual question answering.
Llama-4-Maverick-17B-128E-Instruct-FP8 (Meta, 2025):
Llama-4-Maverick is part of Meta’s open foundation models. The 17B-128E-Instruct-FP8 variant adopts a mixed-precision FP8 training and inference scheme. It combines multimodal capabilities with enhanced reasoning and alignment performance.
F.2 Models, Compute, and Infrastructure.
We conduct all experiments using publicly available and internal remote deployments of the used VLMs. Specifically, GPT model is accessed via the publicly available Azure deployment at https://eteopenai.azure-api.net/, while Qwen, Pixtral and Maverick models are accessed through internal remote deployments. All experiments are orchestrated from a local desktop machine and interact with the models exclusively through the OpenAI chat completion API (https://platform.openai.com/docs/api-reference/chat). To improve throughput, we use asynchronous execution with up to 20 concurrent API calls. A single experiment, consisting of 300 referential game turns, requires approximately 10 minutes to complete. Since the models are accessed as remote services, exact parameter counts and hardware specifications are not exposed; however, the computational cost is dominated by API inference calls rather than local computation.
F.3 Experimental Setup and Randomization.
All experiments are conducted using a fixed temperature of 0, ensuring deterministic model behavior for a given input. Rather than varying the sampling temperature or random seeds, we achieve statistical robustness by repeating each experimental condition for at least 300 independent game rounds. In each round, a different target image and set of distractors are sampled, resulting in diverse prompts and model inputs. We argue that this form of input-level randomization provides statistical power comparable to varying model temperature or generation seeds. Accordingly, each reported data point reflects an average over 300 rounds. Under a Bernoulli assumption, the standard error of the mean (SEM) is upper-bounded by 0.25/300≈0.029\sqrt{0.25/300}\approx 0.029, indicating that observed differences are unlikely to be driven by sampling noise.
F.4 Generating Synthetic Flag Images
We use the SVG files of real country flags (W3C, 2011; Borgos, n.d.) as input for generating synthetic flags.
For each real flag, we prompt the mixtral-8x22B-instruct model to create a textually similar synthetic version. The resulting SVG files are then converted to PNG format, and we filter out any images that fail to render correctly during the conversion process. This process yielded 149 distinct synthetic images resembling national flags.

Appendix H Suboptimal Configurations
In this section, we describe several configurations and experimental setups that resulted in inferior performance or were found to be suboptimal for analysis and execution.
H.1 Developing Language Implicitly
We experimented with prompts that instructed the VLM to develop a new language and describe the target image in a single turn.
We tested two versions of this prompt, where in the more complex one, we additionally instructed the sender to “think step by step” and verify that the language variant could describe all candidate images as if each were a potential target.
We applied both versions to the Efficient and Covert prompting variants. While performance differences were not substantial, the Implicit prompt exhibited significant limitations. First, because the developed language remains implicit, the sender cannot share it directly with the receiver. Second, from an analysis perspective, it becomes more difficult to distinguish the developed language from the actual target description on the sender’s side.
For these reasons, we favor the Explicit prompt variant over the Implicit one for experiments that require agents to develop a new language variant.
H.2 Prompt Structure
We experimented with two variants of prompt structure related to the developed language and the target description.
Single-turn interactions:
In this variant, we use a single-turn setup for both interactions. That is, after obtaining the language variant from the sender, we concatenate it into the user prompt when instructing the sender to generate a description for a given target.
First Interaction (Single-Turn):
[System prompt] You are an agent participating in a referential game ...
[User prompt] Your task is to invent new language ....
[Assignment] ??
Second Interaction (Single-Turn):
[System prompt] You are an agent participating in a referential game ...
[User prompt] Here is the language you invented: {L}. your task is to describe the target using that language ...
[Assignment] ??
Multi-turn interaction:
In the second variant, we maintain a multi-turn structure and prompt the agent using four separate steps:
Interaction:
[System prompt] You are an agent participating in a referential game ...
[User prompt] Your task is to invent new language ....
[Assignment] Here is my invented language: {L}
[User prompt] Given your invented language describe the target ...
[Assignment] ??
Receiver’s prompt:
We experiment with the receiver’s prompt in a similar way. The receiver can be configured in two ways: either by developing its own language variant or by using a language variant shared by the sender.
In the case where a language is shared by the sender, we insert it into the receiver’s prompt as if it had been generated by the receiver itself:
Interaction:
[System prompt] You are an agent participating in a referential game ...
[User prompt] Your task is to invent a new language ...
[Assignment] Sender-invented-language
[User prompt] Given the invented language and the sender’s description target-description, guess the target ...
[Assignment] ??
We found that the multi-turn structure consistently produced better results, and therefore report our experiments using this setup throughout the paper.
H.3 Structure of the Task-Oriented Language Variant
We experimented with two formats for the developed language: JSON and plain text.
The JSON structure was encouraged by prompting the VLM to output entries in the format {‘concept’: ‘meaning’}.
Our hypothesis was that using a structured format like JSON would make it easier for the model to extend or merge the developed language across multiple turns.
However, we found that plain-text languages yielded better performance on the task. In particular, plain-text prompts led to richer and more flexible language descriptions, as they were not constrained by a rigid structure.
Moreover, despite the use of JSON formatting, we observed that models often struggled to successfully merge languages generated in different turns.
Based on these findings, we report results obtained using the plain-text version of the experiments.
H.4 Prompt Engineering
In-context learning has several limitations, chief among them being its strong sensitivity to the exact wording of the prompt, a challenge commonly referred to as prompt engineering (Liu et al., 2023; Reynolds and McDonell, 2021).
We experimented with various prompt formulations across different model interactions, including language development, target description, and target guessing.
Crafting effective prompts remains more of an art than a science. We ultimately settled on the prompt versions reported in Appendix E. While we cannot guarantee that these are optimal, they yielded the best results in our testing and were kept consistent across experiments of the same type.
H.5 Improving Language Across Turns
We explored whether language could be improved across interaction turns. While this may seem straightforward for a learning system, demonstrating such improvement in an in-context learning setup is far more nuanced (Kamoi et al., 2024).
Agents in our setting must engage in multi-step reasoning: first, they must develop an effective language based on the currently observable world; next, they must accurately describe a target using that language; and finally, their collaborator must correctly interpret the description.
Improving this process entails the possibility of intervening at any of these stages. For example, an agent might refine its developed language based on the previous one, revise its description based on a prior or newly revised language, or improve its target inference as a receiver. These refinements must rely solely on feedback from prior turns—specifically, whether the target prediction was correct—as this is the only new information the agents receive during the interaction.
A key challenge lies in the nature of the developed language variant, which is grounded in a specific set of ten candidate images. It is unclear how to refine this language using a different candidate set without risking a loss of semantic consistency for previously defined concepts. One alternative is to fix the candidate set and attempt to improve performance within that context. However, this approach has a major limitation: the developed language remains constrained to a narrow and potentially unrepresentative visual world.
We tested several such configurations. In the first setup, we attempted to improve the language by instructing the sender to merge its previously developed language with a newly generated one. While the merging process appeared successful, we did not observe any improvement in task performance across turns.
In a second setup, we fixed the visible world (i.e., the candidate images) and prompted the sender to refine its previously developed language before describing the target. Again, no measurable improvement was observed.
We conclude that improving language across turns likely requires explicit training beyond in-context learning, and we leave this direction for future work.

Table 12: Difference in performance between informed and uninformed sender settings.
Lazaridou et al. (2017) introduced the term informed sender, referring to a setup in which the sender has access to all candidate images when generating the target description.
Table 12 represents the differential performance between an uninformed sender compared to informed sender, presented in Table 11 (a duplicate of Table 1 in the main paper for convenience).
Across datasets and models, the impact of using an informed sender varies substantially. GPT is largely insensitive to the informed-sender setting. In contrast, Qwen and Pixtral generally perform worse with an informed sender, suggesting limited ability to exploit distractor information. Maverick benefits the most from differential descriptions, particularly for short outputs, showing large improvements in settings where images are visually similar (e.g., CLEVR and Flags). Overall, differential descriptions provide the greatest advantage when short messages are required and when scenes have high structural similarity.
More details and insights are provided below.
Model-Level Patterns.
GPT shows the smallest sensitivity to the informed-sender condition: across datasets and length limits, its differential performance remains near zero. This suggests that GPT naturally generates sufficiently discriminative messages even without distractor access.
In contrast, both Qwen and Pixtral exhibit consistently negative differential scores, indicating that they perform better in the uninformed setting. These models appear unable to leverage distractor information and may even be hindered by it, perhaps due to brittle or unstable short-description strategies. Pixtral in particular shows uniformly negative differentials, consistent with a systematic failure to benefit from distractor context; we speculate that the model’s low informed-sender performance may stem from an implementation issue.
Maverick stands out as the model that most effectively exploits differential information, showing large positive gains,especially for short descriptions, demonstrating that it can reliably adapt its messages when distractors are present.
Dataset-Level Effects.
The CLEVR dataset shows the greatest need for differential descriptions. Because images differ only in subtle compositional details, models that can exploit distractor information (GPT and Maverick) achieve substantial gains at short lengths.
For Flags, differential descriptions matter primarily at short lengths: Maverick shows a substantial boost at length 1, whereas uninformed senders often collapse to generic vocabulary (e.g., repeatedly using the token flag), leading to very low baseline accuracy.
For MS-COCO, the benefit of differential descriptions is modest. Because the images are visually diverse, distractor-aware conditioning adds little information, and most models exhibit only mild positive or negative differentials.
Effect of Description Length.
Differential information is most valuable when messages are short. At 11–55 tokens, positive gains, especially for Maverick and GPT in CLEVR, reflect the advantage of selectively encoding highly discriminative features. When long descriptions are permitted (length 100100), the uninformed sender often performs better for all models: with ample space, models can produce sufficiently detailed descriptions to uniquely identify the target without relying on contrastive compression.
Additional Observations.
Qwen’s consistently negative differentials suggest a structural limitation in its ability to encode contrastive information. Pixtral shows even stronger degradation under the informed condition, possibly indicating a model-specific failure mode or instability.
GPT’s near-flat differential profile implies that it relies on robust semantic representations that already encode strong discriminative cues.
Finally, Maverick demonstrates an adaptive strategy: it exploits differential information when necessary (short outputs, high-similarity domains) but defaults to nondifferential behavior when longer descriptions are available.
Summary.
Informed senders are most beneficial when (i) the visual domain exhibits high structural similarity (e.g., CLEVR, Flags), and (ii) message length is tightly constrained. When these conditions do not hold, uninformed senders perform as well as or better than informed senders. Among the models evaluated, Maverick benefits the most from contrastive conditioning, GPT is largely invariant to it, and neither Qwen nor Pixtral appears capable of effectively exploiting differential cues.
Based on these results, we used the informed-sender for the Efficient and Covert experiments, noting that it may degrade natural-language performance for the Pixtral model.
Appendix J Human Evaluation
We conducted three human evaluation experiments to assess participants’ ability to interpret the artificial language variants generated by the models.
Each experiment corresponded to one of the three language conditions, Natural, Efficient, and Covert, using descriptions produced by GPT on the synthetic-flag dataset. Descriptions in the Natural and Covert conditions consisted of five words, while those in the Efficient condition were limited to a single word.
Each experiment included a total of 50 trials. For each participant, 10 trials were randomly sampled.
In each trial, participants were shown a description and asked to select the target image it referred to from a set of 10 candidate images. A total of 15 participants took part in the evaluation. All held at least a bachelor’s degree and reported good to excellent proficiency in English.
Figure 5 shows the distribution of participants’ accuracy across the three experiments. The standard deviations were 0.100.10, 0.180.18, and 0.210.21 for the Natural, Efficient, and Covert conditions, respectively. The higher variance in the Efficient and Covert setups suggests that participants with better strategies or more experience might achieve higher performance under these more challenging conditions.

Appendix K Analysis of Task-Oriented Language Variants
In the following sections, we provide additional examples and insights into the Efficient and Covert Language variants developed by the four models across the three datasets under the informed-sender setup.
K.1 Efficient Language variants
Across all three datasets, models invent compact, task-oriented lexicons designed to encode distinctions with minimal descriptive overhead. In most cases, the number of words in a lexicon correlates with the number of images in the context window, likely reflecting an artifact of the training setup.
CLEVR dataset.
Models typically generate dictionaries of around ten invented words per game turn, with Maverick tending to produce fewer (around nine) and Pixtral slightly more (around eleven). Each word is accompanied by a short explanation; for example, Maverick defines snurfle as a metallic cube, groobly as a matte cylinder, and flumplen as a group of 3 or more objects of the same shape but different colors. These definitions often capture only the primary object in the visual scene, or a small subset of them. Some models (e.g., GPT) introduce explicit compositional structure into their dictionaries to handle multiple similar objects, such as defining plok as a combination of a glim and a blop in the same image.
Models like GPT and PIX sometimes evaluate these lexicons by re-describing images using only the invented vocabulary (e.g., PIX referring to a round, shiny object as a refl orb). They then compose these invented terms in target descriptions, occasionally generating combinatorial forms such as Qwen’s cubo_pequeno and cilindro_grande.
When prompted for single-word descriptions, models generally select only one invented term. When asked for five words, they tend to mix invented and natural terms, as in flumplen with purple cylinder and cyan cube (Maverick). In some cases, models produce much longer descriptions than requested; such outputs are filtered in our quantitative analysis.
MS-COCO dataset.
Most models again produce compact dictionaries of roughly ten invented terms, with Pixtral generating somewhat larger inventories of around twenty words. Descriptions of target images most often rely on a single invented word. Models such as GPT heavily employ portmanteau formations—e.g., Burgloaf, Cozycorner, Hugscape—allowing them to optimize expressivity under tight length constraints.
Other models prefer to invent new words with related meanings. Examples include Maverick’s jinkle: "a plate of food with a variety of items, such as vegetables and meat", wuggle: "a person riding a vehicle, such as a bicycle or motorcycle", and kabloo: "a large building or structure, such as a house or office building";
Qwen’s globo: "a large, round object with a reflective surface", zorba: "a dense, green forest with tall trees", and bomba: "a group of people gathered in a stadium, watching a game"; and
Pixtral’s Twinkle: "a small, bright point of light", Blob: "a large, irregular, amorphous shape", Sparkle: "a small, bright, sparkling object", and Zigzag: "a line that changes direction abruptly and repeatedly".
As in CLEVR, models generally adhere to their dictionaries for single-word descriptions. Multi-word outputs often combine invented terms with natural language, such as snurfle on a runway (Maverick) or raphael sitting on branch (Qwen).
Flags dataset.
Efficient vocabularies for the Flags dataset primarily reference geometric elements and their arrangement. GPT and Maverick rely heavily on portmanteau-style tokens, while Qwen and Pixtral mix invented, portmanteau, and natural words. Examples include GPT’s zor: "a red horizontal stripe", Bivisectra: "an image split vertically into two equal halves with different colors", and Tricolorbar: "an image split horizontally into three equal sections with different colors"; and Pixtral’s quiggle: "a line that curves and twists in an irregular pattern" and Stripe: "a long, narrow, and straight line of a single color".
Dictionary sizes vary substantially across models: Maverick uses 8 words, GPT 11, Pixtral 15, and Qwen up to 22 (occasionally exceeding twenty). Single-word descriptions typically use one invented term, whereas multi-word descriptions often combine several, as in Qwen’s Zob Zod. When longer outputs are allowed, models frequently mix invented terms with natural language, such as Quadtricolor with red triangle.

K.3 Adherence of Descriptions to Induced Lexicons
Table 14 reveals several notable trends in models’ ability to adhere to their own induced lexicons across datasets, requested lengths, and language settings.
Efficient (1).
When constrained to a single token, all models achieve high lexicon alignment across datasets, indicating that they can reliably invent and reuse a one-word-per-image lexicon. This setting appears to be the easiest for all models, as it allows them to collapse the image into a single symbolic identifier thus optimizing length constraint. GPT consistently achieves near-ceiling alignment, particularly on Flags and MS-COCO, while still maintaining strong performance on CLEVR. Maverick and Pixtral also perform well in this regime, though with slightly lower robustness across datasets, and Qwen exhibits a larger gap between simpler datasets and the more compositional CLEVR. Overall, these results suggest that inventing a single concept to uniquely characterize an image and reliably reusing that concept to distinguish it from others is well within the capabilities of contemporary models when output length is tightly constrained.
Efficient (5).
As the requested description length increases to five tokens, lexicon alignment decreases substantially for all models and datasets. This drop reflects a systematic tendency to introduce natural-language words beyond the induced lexicon. Importantly, this behavior is consistent with the Efficient prompt, which does not require exclusive use of invented symbols but instead asks models to produce the best possible description under a length constraint. In this setting, GPT appears to exploit the relaxed lexical constraint most effectively, strategically incorporating natural words to improve descriptive quality, which explains why lower alignment does not necessarily imply worse task performance (not shown). Other models, particularly Pixtral and Maverick, show a sharper decline without the same compensatory gains, suggesting more limited flexibility in balancing symbolic adherence and expressive adequacy. Dataset effects are also pronounced: alignment drops most severely on CLEVR, consistent with its higher compositional demands, while Flags remains comparatively easier.
Covert (5).
The Covert (5) setting poses a greater challenge, as models must invent a private lexicon while simultaneously remaining consistent in its use across multi-token descriptions. Here, alignment varies widely across both models and datasets. GPT generally performs best, but its performance drops markedly on Flags and CLEVR, indicating that maintaining covert symbolic consistency in highly compositional environments is particularly difficult even for the strongest model. Qwen shows moderate robustness, especially on MS-COCO, whereas Pixtral and Maverick struggle substantially, often failing to reuse invented symbols reliably. Across all models, CLEVR emerges as the most challenging dataset, suggesting that covert language induction and reuse becomes increasingly fragile as visual complexity and relational structure increase.
K.4 Covert Language Variants
In contrast to the compact lexicons observed in the Efficient setting, Covert language variants involve more elaborate and internally structured vocabularies. These variants aim to conceal the semantic content of the image from an external observer while remaining interpretable to the cooperating receiver.
CLEVR dataset.
Models typically construct their language variants in several steps: identifying recurring visual elements, assigning tokens to those elements, and formulating rules and compositional templates for describing images. For example, language variant developed by GPT may include:
Shapes – Cube: "Zog", Sphere: "Blip", Cylinder: "Flarp"; Colors – Red: "Rin", Green: "Grin", Blue: "Blu", Yellow: "Yel", Purple: "Pur", Brown: "Brun", Gold: "Glin", Teal: "Teel", Silver: "Sil"; Arrangement – Clustered: "Clum", Spread: "Spra"; Language Rules: 1. Shape–Color Pairing: Each object is described by combining its shape and color tokens (e.g., a red cube is "Zog-Rin"). 2. Arrangement Descriptor: The overall spatial organization is encoded using arrangement tokens. 3. Sequence: Describe the image by listing shape–color pairs followed by the arrangement descriptor. Example: "Zog-Grin Blip-Rin Flarp-Blu Clum".
As seen in the example, the GPT language variant defines three shape tokens, nine color tokens, and two arrangement tokens. Other models follow a similar structure.
The models then use their defined tokens and rules to describe target images, each employing a different vocabulary, as illustrated below:

Maverick: Y-p-L, S-r-s, S-w-s, S-w-s, C-c-s

MS-COCO dataset.
Although the overall strategy for constructing Covert language variants in MS-COCO resembles that of CLEVR, the resulting descriptions are typically more variable and noisier. Models often mix natural words, corrupted fragments, or image-specific invented terms. Some also produce long sequences whose meaning is conveyed implicitly through positional patterns or learned co-occurrence structures.
For example, GPT generates descriptions such as Zor Burg Solo and Flar Sizzle Brun; Maverick alternates between forms like Hum-Neb-Act-Hit-Out-Vel-Kri and more structured fragments such as M-People, K-Structure-Cen, R-Sign-R; and Pixtral uses low-level geometric tokens, as in Circle-Yellow-One-Center Square-White-Two-Right Rectangle-Brown-One-Top.
Flags dataset.
For the Flags dataset, models tend to develop Covert language variants organized around symbolic or morphological repetition. The high structural regularity of the dataset encourages systematic correspondences between visual elements (e.g., stripe orientation, color layout) and symbolic tokens. These vocabularies are internally consistent within each model but mostly opaque to humans.
Example descriptions include Zor Tri Kra Pax and Flar Wex Trig Blip Hor (GPT); Vst Zor Ast Hst Lys Sol and ro-ye.st-la.t ye.st (Maverick); red tri dia sm and SmaBlaHoriWitGra (Qwen); and G-Y-BL-OV-T (Pixtral).
K.5 Comparison and Conclusions
Efficient and Covert language variants diverge sharply in both purpose and structure.
Efficient language variants prioritize compression, yielding small, interpretable lexicons that often exhibit light compositionality and remain loosely aligned with natural semantics.
Covert language variants prioritize obfuscation, producing larger, more irregular, and internally structured codes designed to hide meaning from an external observer while preserving reliable coordination with a receiver.
Across datasets and models, these contrasting pressures lead to qualitatively different task-oriented language variants, ranging from compact symbolic dictionaries to full-fledged rule-governed artificially-developed communication systems, demonstrating the remarkable flexibility with which models deviate from natural language under different communicative constraints.
Appendix L Analyzing Language Variant Corpora
In this section, we present results from analyzing nine corpora, each corresponding to one of the nine language–model combinations used in our experiments.
L.1 Data Generation
We generated the corpora by instructing a sender from each model type to produce a description using one of three language prompt variants, for each of the 256 real flags in our Real flags dataset.
L.2 Used Metrics
1. Cosine Similarity
Cosine similarity compares the normalized word frequency distributions of two languages. Each language is represented as a vector over a shared vocabulary, where the values are normalized word counts. 1.01.0 score indicates identical distributions, and .0.0 means orthogonal vectors.
Definition: Given two word frequency vectors v→1\vec{v}_{1} and v→2\vec{v}_{2}, the cosine similarity is defined as:
CosineSim​(v→1,v→2)=v→1⋅v→2‖v→1‖​‖v→2‖\text{CosineSim}(\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2})=\frac{\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2}}{\|\vec{v}_{1}\|\|\vec{v}_{2}\|}
Answers the question: To what extent do the word frequency distributions of the two languages align in direction?
2. Jensen-Shannon Similarity
Jensen-Shannon Similarity evaluates the divergence between the normalized word frequency distributions of two languages from an information-theoretic perspective.
It measures how one probability distribution diverges from a mixture of itself and another.
We report 1-JSD, so higher values represent greater similarity.
The measure is symmetric and bounded between 0 and 1.
Definition: Given normalized word distributions PP and QQ, and M=12​(P+Q)M=\frac{1}{2}(P+Q), the Jensen-Shannon divergence is:
JSD(P,Q)=12DKL(P∥M)+12DKL(Q∥M)\text{JSD}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}D_{\text{KL}}(P\|M)+\frac{1}{2}D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|M)
We define similarity as:
JSSim​(P,Q)=1−JSD​(P,Q)\text{JSSim}(P,Q)=1-\text{JSD}(P,Q)
Answers the question: How similar are the word probability distributions across two languages, from an information-theoretic perspective?
3. Target-Grounded Cosine Similarity
This metric uses bag-of-words vectors created for each target description. It reflects how similar the language usage is across models for the same referent.
We average cosine similarities over all targets that appear in both datasets.
Definition: For each common target tt, merge all descriptions into documents dt(1)d^{(1)}_{t}, dt(2)d^{(2)}_{t}, and compute:
AvgCosine=1|T|​∑t∈TCosineSim​(dt(1),dt(2))\text{AvgCosine}=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}\text{CosineSim}(d^{(1)}_{t},d^{(2)}_{t})
Answers the question: Are the descriptions for the same target across language variants similar in vocabulary usage?
4. Normalized Edit Similarity
This is a character-level string similarity metric. It’s calculated as 1-normalized Levenshtein distance where normalization is done by dividing the raw edit distance by the maximum string length. This metric captures surface-level resemblance between descriptions regardless of semantics or tokenization.
Definition: Given two strings d1d_{1}, d2d_{2}, compute Levenshtein distance LL and normalize:
EditSim​(d1,d2)=1−L⁡(d1,d2)max⁡(|d1|,|d2|)\text{EditSim}(d_{1},d_{2})=1-\frac{L(d_{1},d_{2})}{\max(|d_{1}|,|d_{2}|)}
Answers the question: How similar are the character-level surface forms of two target descriptions?
5. FastText Embedding Similarity
Words from each description are embedded using pre-trained FastText66
6
https://fasttext.cc/ vectors, and descriptions are averaged into a single vector per target. Cosine similarity is then computed between the averaged embeddings of matching targets. This metric captures semantic similarity based on subword-informed word representations.
Definition: For each description dd, average the FastText embeddings:
e→d=1|d|​∑w∈dFastText​(w)\vec{e}_{d}=\frac{1}{|d|}\sum_{w\in d}\text{FastText}(w)
Then compute:
EmbedSim​(d1,d2)=cos⁡(e→d1,e→d2)\text{EmbedSim}(d_{1},d_{2})=\cos(\vec{e}_{d_{1}},\vec{e}_{d_{2}})
Answers the question: Are the descriptions semantically similar even when phrased differently?
Corpus-level version: We represent each language variant as a single vector by averaging the FastText word embeddings across all words in its corpus.
6. ChrF Score
ChrF (Character n-gram F-score) is a BLEU-like metric tailored to compare two sequences at the character level. It computes F1-scores over overlapping character n-grams (3 to 5-grams in our case) between two strings.
Unlike BLEU, it handles morphological variations and non-standard tokenization better, which is useful in analyzing task-oriented language variants. We normalize the score to the [0,1] range.
Definition: ChrF computes the F1-score over character nn-gram overlap between hypothesis hh and reference rr:
ChrF​(h,r)=F1-score of char-n-gram matches\text{ChrF}(h,r)=\text{F1-score of char-$n$-gram matches}
Answers the question: To what degree do two descriptions overlap at the character nn-gram level?
Corpus-level version: We compute the ChrF score at the corpus level by first concatenating all target descriptions for each language variant into a single document. The ChrF score is then calculated between these aggregated documents, capturing the overall character-level similarity between the two language variants.

L.3 Similarity Analysis Results
Table 15 presents results comparing the languages across different variants and senders’ VLM using multiple similarity metrics.
Corpus-level similarity measures the overall distributional similarity between entire corpora, while target-level similarity evaluates the similarity of descriptions on a per-target basis and reports the average across all targets.
Natural languages exhibit the highest cross-model similarity.
The natural variants demonstrate the highest degree of alignment across models, both at the corpus level and target level. For instance, the comparison between Qwen,natural and Pix,natural yields a cosine similarity of 0.86 and Jensen-Shannon similarity of 0.60 at the corpus level, with similarly strong FastText-based similarity (0.74) at the target level. These results reflect the shared semantic and syntactic structure of natural language, even when generated by distinct VLMs.
Efficient and Covert language variants show minimal alignment across models.
The Efficient and Covert protocols produce markedly low similarity scores across all metrics. For example, Gpt,efficient and Pix,efficient show a corpus-level cosine similarity of only 0.07, with corresponding target-level similarities near zero. Similar trends are observed for Covert variants, with Gpt,covert and Pix,covert yielding a cosine similarity of 0.00 and edit similarity of 0.07. These results suggest that the developed protocols diverge significantly across models, lacking a shared structure. This finding aligns with our broader observation that models with similar architectures tend to understand each other more effectively, whereas models with divergent architectures struggle to interpret one another’s developed language variants.
Within-model comparisons reveal moderate structural consistency.
Comparisons across different language variants within the same model indicate moderate levels of similarity, particularly between the Natural and Efficient variants. For instance, Qwen,natural and Qwen,efficient achieve a FastText similarity of 0.31, substantially higher than between-model comparisons for Efficientlanguage variant. This highlights a key distinction between the Efficient and Covert variants. In the Efficient condition, the model was simply instructed to create a more efficient language, without explicitly deviating from natural language, whereas in the Covert variant, the model was encouraged to produce a language that actively differs from natural language.
Pixtral exhibits greater divergence across language variants.
Compared to GPT and Qwen, the Pixtral model appears to struggle with developing an efficient language variant that diverges meaningfully from its natural language baseline. For instance, Pix,natural and Pix,efficient exhibit a relatively high corpus-level similarity (cosine = 0.46), in contrast to much lower values for GPT (0.010.01) and Qwen (0.060.06) under comparable conditions. This pattern highlights a limitation of the Pixtral model in generating distinct efficient protocols.
Summary.
These results highlight several consistent trends: natural language variants cluster more closely across models, while Efficient and Covert language variants show high divergence—especially across models—yet may preserve semantic information in non-obvious ways.
Additionally, intra-model comparisons reveal partial structural alignment, particularly for Gpt and Qwen, whereas Pixtral appears to struggle with the language development task.

Appendix M Language and Thought
The relationship between language and thought has long been debated in cognitive science. Foundational works by Vygotsky and Cole (1978) and Whorf (1956) proposed that language not only reflects but also shapes thought—a view known as linguistic relativity. In contrast, others argue for the independence of cognition from linguistic expression (Pinker, 2003). This debate has also permeated artificial intelligence research, particularly in studies of reasoning in large language models (LLMs).
A prominent recent line of work in AI explores the use of language as an explicit tool for reasoning within a single agent. Notably, Wei et al. (2022) introduced Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, showing that prompting LLMs to reason step-by-step using natural language significantly improves their performance on complex reasoning tasks. Other approaches, such as latent CoT (Hao et al., 2024), challenge this hypothesis by proposing to learn reasoning trajectories in latent (non-linguistic) spaces, suggesting that language is not the sole vehicle for thought in artificial agents..
Some researchers have proposed leveraging Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for interpretability and safety, based on the assumption that what an LLM “thinks” can serve as a faithful proxy for what it is about to “say” (Liang et al., 2022; Burns et al., 2022). By monitoring this internal reasoning process, it may be possible to intervene before the model generates inappropriate or harmful outputs. However, follow-up studies (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Roger and Greenblatt, 2023) have highlighted important limitations of this approach, showing that LLMs can deliberately articulate misleading or unfaithful thoughts or even conceal them. These works conceptualize language as an introspective trace of an agent’s internal cognitive process.
In contrast, our work focuses not on internal reasoning but on communication between agents. While CoT studies emphasize language as a medium for intra-agent cognition, we study language as a social tool: a shared protocol that must emerge between independent agents to coordinate action. These two uses of language—internal vs. external—are deeply connected. Just as improved linguistic articulation has been shown to enhance internal reasoning, we hypothesize that more effective external communication protocols (task-oriented languages) may foster more robust coordination, abstraction, and shared understanding in multi-agent systems. Our findings highlight the potential for such task-oriented languages to go beyond natural language in both efficiency and expressivity, offering a new lens on the co-evolution of communication and intelligence in artificial systems.
We close this discussion by returning to a perspective that has long inspired inquiry into the connection between language and cognition. As Ludwig Wittgenstein famously wrote:
“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”   — Ludwig Wittgenstein (1921)
This view resonates deeply with our investigation: in both human and artificial systems, the expressive power of language shapes the boundaries of what can be represented, reasoned about, and shared. Our work extends this intuition to emergent communication between agents, where language is not only a tool for thought, but a foundation for building shared worlds.
