Visualization Results of Token Distribution. Figure 4 illustrates an example from the forget set, including the image, the associated question, and the answer. We visualize the predicted token distributions after unlearning with different methods, using the normal token ‘_person’ (gray bars) and the key token ‘_civil’ (red bars) as representative cases. Ablation Studies. Table 3 reports the ablation studies. ‘W/o Reg’ denotes removing the second term in Eq (4), i.e., the regularization term. ‘W/o GA’ indicates removing the first term, i.e., the positive log-likelihood term. ‘W/o Vis’ refers to removing the visual guidance and applying regularization solely based on the image’s initial token distribution, i.e., using pfull​(v|I,x,i)p_{\rm full}(v|I,x,i) as ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} in Eq (4).
It can be observed that ‘w/o Reg’ degenerates to GA, leading to pronounced forgetting as well as a noticeable degradation in response coherence. This is evidenced by the lower ROUGE and BLEU scores on the ‘Retain’ and ‘Real’ sets, together with a reduced GIB score. In contrast, ‘w/o GA’ weakens the unlearning capability, resulting in an unexpected increase in ACC on the ‘Forget’ and ‘Generalization’ sets. These results indicate that both terms in Eq (4) are indispensable, validating the design of the token-level regularization. Although ‘w/o Vis’ relatively preserves response coherence, it still exhibits a significant drop in retaining performance, suggesting that our visual-guided token distribution estimation is likewise necessary. Please refer to Appendix C.3 for more results.

Hyperparameter Analysis. Figure 6 shows the effects of the regularization strength λ\lambda and the number of irrelevant visual inputs kk used to estimate ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i}. The first row reports GIB, measuring the coherence of MLLM responses after unlearning, while the second row shows unlearning and retaining performance, evaluated by ACC and ROUGE, respectively.
As the regularization strength increases, retaining performance improves, but unlearning effectiveness degrades. When λ\lambda is relatively large, the GIB score decreases and becomes unstable, with larger variance. We set λ=0.5\lambda=0.5 for the 15% tasks as a balanced choice. In contrast, the number of irrelevant visual inputs kk has a smaller impact on the results. With fewer reference images, the performance is unstable and shows higher variance. When k≥5k\geq 5, the metrics become stable. We adopt k=5k=5 in our experiments. Detailed results are included in Appendix C.4.
Results of Different Regularization. ViKeR employs KL divergence to regularize the distance between ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} and 𝒬^iy\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{y}_{i}, while other similarity measures can also be used as regularizers. Figure 6 presents the results using cosine similarity (CoS) (22) and Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) (32).
Compared to the setting without regularization, CoS substantially improves language coherence, but shows limited gains in unlearning and retaining performance. The results of JSD are nearly identical to those of KL divergence. However, from a computational perspective, JSD is approximately twice as expensive as KL divergence. Therefore, adopting KL divergence as the regularization term is a more reasonable choice. Detailed results are included in Appendix C.5.

Results of Different Visual References.
To study the impact of reference image set ℐ′\mathcal{I}^{\prime}, we further experiment with images from the forget set (‘forget’), the retain set (‘retain’), and other categories, including cats or dogs (‘pets’), real-world scenes (‘scene’), and different textures (‘pattern’), aside from the irrelevant celebrities (‘peoples’) we use in ViKeR. The results are in Figure 7 and Appendix C.6.
Types of references have little effect on GIB, indicating that language coherence is preserved regardless of ℐ′\mathcal{I}^{\prime}. However, unlearning and retaining performance vary. Using ‘pattern’ and ‘retain’ images yields the best unlearning performance, likely due to their relatively consistent internal distributions and large discrepancy from the forget set. In contrast, ‘forget’ and ‘scene’ images are the worst, as ‘forget’ images contain privacy information to unlearn, and ‘scene’ images exhibit high diversity, both of which hinder accurate ideal distribution estimation.
For retaining performance, ‘retain’ and ‘pet’ images lead to noticeable degradation, possibly because their estimated distributions contain task-related or human-like information that is also suppressed during unlearning. In contrast, using ‘people’ images achieves a better balance between unlearning and retaining.


4 Visual-Guided Key-Token Regularization
Method Overview.
The pipeline of ViKeR, illustrated in Figure 3, consists of two components: visual-guided token distribution estimation and token-level regularization.
The first part leverages irrelevant visual inputs (i.e., images of individuals not included in 𝒟f\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}) fed into the pre-unlearning full model to predict the ideal post-unlearning token distribution (as observed in Figure 2(c)) in a gradient-free manner.
The second component uses the predicted ideal distribution to regularize the GA unlearning process.
Visual-Guided Token Distribution Estimation.
Shown in Figure 2(c), a set of unlabeled images of irrelevant individuals can be used to estimate the ideal post-unlearning token distribution for each generated answer token.
Assuming that kk images of irrelevant individuals from a reference set ℐ′={I1′,I2′,…,Ik′}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\{I^{\prime}_{1},I^{\prime}_{2},...,I^{\prime}_{k}\} are used, the ideal distribution of the ii-th token in an answer yy is estimated as:
ℛ^iy​(v)=p¯θfull​(v|ℐ′,x,i)≜1k​∑j=1kpθfull​(v|Ij′,x,i),\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i}(v)=\bar{p}_{\theta_{\rm full}}(v|\mathcal{I}^{\prime},x,i)\triangleq\frac{1}{k}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{\rm full}}(v|I_{j}^{\prime},x,i),
(4)
where ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} is the distribution over tokens v∈𝒱v\in\mathcal{V}, with 𝒱\mathcal{V} being the complete token set, and pθfull(v|⋅)p_{\theta_{\rm full}}(v|\cdot) is the probability of token vv as defined in Eq. (1), given the full model θfull\theta_{\rm full}, a visual–question–answer triple (Ij′,x,y)(I^{\prime}_{j},x,y) and index ii.
It can be noticed that computing ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} only requires forward passes through θfull\theta_{\rm full}, being a gradient-free inference process. Therefore, it incurs minimal computational overhead. The hyperparameter kk is discussed in Section 6.
Token-Level Regularization.
Given an MLLM model θ\theta, a visual–question–answer triple (I,x,y)∈Df(I,x,y)\in D_{\rm f} and the token position ii, the predicted distribution of the ii-th token in yy is
𝒬^iy​(v)=pθ​(v|I,x,i),\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{y}_{i}(v)=p_{\theta}(v|I,x,i),
as defined in Eq. (1). 𝒬^iy\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{y}_{i} is the distribution over v∈𝒱v\in\mathcal{V}.
After ideal unlearning, we would like 𝒬^iy\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{y}_{i} to approximate ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} for all (I,x,y)∈𝒟f(I,x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm f} and positions ii.
The intuition is that unlearning is achieved when, for any (I,x,y)∈𝒟f(I,x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm f} fed into the MLLM, the resulting token
distributions resemble the expected distributions obtained by inputting the same question xx and identity-agnostic individuals.
It is supported by the observation in
Figure 2(c): across reference images from
ℐ′\mathcal{I}^{\prime}, the averaged distributions
ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} do not exhibit individual-specific peaks for
key tokens associated with private attributes, while remaining
sharp for normal tokens.
Therefore, the discrepancy between 𝒬^iy\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{y}_{i} and ℛ^iy\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i} can be used to regularize the GA unlearning process. ViKeR employs the widely-used Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence (17) for regularization, so the unlearning loss can be formulated as:
ℒViKeR(𝒟f;θ)=−ℒNLL(𝒟f;θ)+λ|𝒟f|⋅∑(I,x,y)∈𝒟f∑i=1|y|KL(ℛ^iy||𝒬iy^),\mathcal{L}_{\rm ViKeR}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta)=-\mathcal{L}_{\rm NLL}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta)+\frac{\lambda}{|\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}|}\cdot\sum\nolimits_{(I,x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{|y|}\text{KL}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{y}_{i}||\hat{\mathcal{Q}^{y}_{i}}),
where KL(⋅||⋅)\text{KL}(\cdot||\cdot) is the KL divergence, with a balance hyperparameter λ∈[0,1]\lambda\in[0,1], which is further discussed in Section 6.
