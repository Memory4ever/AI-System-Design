3 Mapping pass@kk to coverage@cost
3.1 pass@kk and its asymptotic power-law behavior
We follow similar notation to (Levi, 2025; Schaeffer et al., 2025; Kazdan et al., 2025). Throughout this work we will assume a perfect verifier, we discuss relaxation of this assumption in the outlook (Sec. 7). We denote the probability of success at a single attempt on the ii-th question as pip_{i} and its cumulative distribution function (CDF) as passi​@​k=1−(1−pi)k\text{pass}_{i}\text{@}k=1-(1-p_{i})^{k}. Each question has a different pip_{i} (“difficulty”) drawn from some distribution 𝒫⁡(p)\mathcal{P}(p). Then, averaging over the ensemble, pass@​k\text{pass@}k is defined as the probability of at least one success in kk trials,
pass@​k≔𝔼⁡[passi​@​k]==1−𝔼⁡[(1−pi)k]=1−∫01(1−p)k​𝒫​(p)​𝑑p.\begin{split}&\text{pass@}k\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\text{pass}_{i}\text{@}k\right]=\\
&=1-\mathbb{E}\left[(1-p_{i})^{k}\right]=1-\int_{0}^{1}(1-p)^{k}\mathcal{P}(p)\mathrm{d}p.\end{split}
(1)
Eq. 1 is general and leads, under mild assumptions, to an asymptotic power-law behavior of pass@kk. To see this, we consider difficulty distributions that for p≪1p\ll 1 have the form 𝒫⁡(p)≃c​pα−1\mathcal{P}(p)\simeq cp^{\alpha-1}, where cc and α\alpha are positive constants. We then observe that 1−pass@​k=1k​∫0k(1−z/k)k​𝒫​(z/k)​𝑑z1-\text{pass@}k=\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{k}(1-z/k)^{k}\mathcal{P}(z/k)\mathrm{d}z, by a simple change of variable z=p​kz=pk. Taking the large kk limit then yields
1−pass@​k​≈k≫1​ckα​∫0∞e−z​zα−1​dz=c​Γ​(α)​k−α,\begin{split}1-\text{pass@}k\underset{k\gg 1}{\approx}\frac{c}{k^{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z}z^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}z=c\Gamma(\alpha)k^{-\alpha},\end{split}
(2)
where we have used the definition of the Gamma function, Γ⁡(⋅)\Gamma(\cdot).
From Eq. 2 it is clear that any distribution of “difficulties” 𝒫⁡(p)\mathcal{P}(p) with power-law behavior at small pp, results in a power-law scaling for pass@​k\text{pass@}k, as also obtained by (Schaeffer et al., 2025).

Definition 3.1.

(3)
where, 𝕀\mathbb{I} is the indicator function and JnJ_{n} is the total number of attempts to answer questions i=1,…,ni=1,...,n,

3.3 Growth of the coverage@cost for a large dataset
Recalling the definition of the Z-transform,
𝒵​{coverage@cost}​(z)=∑t=0∞zt​coverage@cost​(t),\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}\{\text{coverage@cost}\}(z)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}z^{t}\text{coverage@cost}(t),
where z∈[0,1)z\in[0,1). We apply it to Eq. 6, and expand around z=1z=1, to get the behavior of coverage@cost at times that are larger than the typical time to answer a question in the dataset. As shown in Sec. A.2, depending on the inference power-law exponent α\alpha, two behaviors can be observed
{coverage@cost​(t)∝tα0<α<1,coverage@cost​(t)≃t𝔼⁡[T]α>1,\begin{cases}\text{coverage@cost}(t)\propto t^{\alpha}&0<\alpha<1,\\
\text{coverage@cost}(t)\simeq\frac{t}{\mathbb{E}[T]}&\alpha>1,\end{cases}
(7)
Namely, in the case where 0<α<10<\alpha<1, 𝔼⁡[T]\mathbb{E}[T] diverges, and then coverage@cost will scale sub-linearly in tt. However, if 𝔼⁡[T]\mathbb{E}[T] is finite, coverage@cost will scale linearly in tt.
As we will show in the next section, ReD is always able to improve coverage@cost for all tt.
Importantly, for the case where 𝔼⁡[T]\mathbb{E}[T] diverges, ReD will make it finite, making a dramatic, qualitative change in coverage@cost, moving from sublinear to linear growth. Analysis done in (Levi, 2025) on several models and datasets shows that the more common case is α<1\alpha<1 which means 𝔼⁡[T]\mathbb{E}[T] diverges, underscoring the strength of the ReD methodology. If 𝔼⁡[T]\mathbb{E}[T] is finite, ReD will reduce the mean number of attempts to answer a randomly sampled question, and therefore will increase the slope of the linear growth of coverage@cost as a function of tt.

4.2 Resetting always improves coverage@cost and resetting every attempt is the optimal strategy
The mean number of attempts to answer a randomly-chosen question correctly under resetting at interval τ\tau, 𝔼⁡[Tτ]\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}], can be found by evaluating the sum 𝔼⁡[Tτ]=∑k=0∞(1−Fτ​(k))\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-F_{\tau}(k)). Plugging Eq. 10 we get (Eliazar and Reuveni, 2020; Bonomo and Pal, 2021; Meir et al., 2025),
𝔼⁡[Tτ]=G⁡(τ)F⁡(τ),G⁡(τ)=∑k=0τ−1(1−F⁡(k)).\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}]=\frac{G(\tau)}{F(\tau)},\quad G(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}(1-F(k)).
(11)
Importantly, we get that both G⁡(τ)G(\tau) and 1/F⁡(τ)1/F(\tau) are finite for finite τ≥1\tau\geq 1, since 0<F⁡(t)≤10<F(t)\leq 1 for any t≥1t\geq 1. It means that 𝔼⁡[Tτ]{\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}]} is also finite. As a result, only the bottom row of Eq. 7 is relevant. Inserting Eq. 11 results in linear growth of coverage@costτ​(t)\text{coverage@cost}_{\tau}(t),
coverage@costτ​(t)≃t𝔼⁡[Tτ]=t​F​(τ)G⁡(τ).\text{coverage@cost}_{\tau}(t)\simeq\frac{t}{\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}]}=\frac{tF(\tau)}{G(\tau)}.

Comparing this equation with Eq. 7, one can immediately see that using ReD for the case where α<1\alpha<1, will increase dramatically the coverage@cost. Without ReD, one will get that coverage@cost will scale sub-linearly in time, and by simply applying resetting it will scale linearly, regardless the τ\tau used, or the specific value of α<1\alpha<1.
For the case where α>1\alpha>1, where both solve-to-completion protocol and ReD exhibit linear growth, we show in Cor B.1 at App. B, that no matter what the underlying distribution of question difficulties 𝒫⁡(p)\mathcal{P}(p) is, ReD is always beneficial.
Finally, we prove in App. B that the optimal resetting time is τ=1\tau=1 regardless of the difficulty distribution 𝒫⁡(p)\mathcal{P}(p), which is summarized in the following theorem.
Theorem 4.1 (Optimality of ReD).
For any 𝒫⁡(p)\mathcal{P}(p) and ∀τ∈ℕ∖{0}\forall\tau\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}, 𝔼⁡[Tτ]≤𝔼⁡[Tτ+1]\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}[T_{\tau+1}], i.e., the optimal resetting time is τ=1\tau=1 (proof in App. B).
So far, we considered only deterministic resetting intervals, but, in general, a stochastic resetting time can be sampled from some distribution. However, it is known that a deterministic τ\tau minimizes the mean time for completion of a task under resetting (Pal and Reuveni, 2017). Therefore, Thm 4.1 also shows that resetting deterministically after every attempt is the optimal resetting strategy.

5 Inference of pass@kk power-law exponent from coverage@cost with ReD
5.1 ReD Changes the Difficulty Distribution
In Sec. 4, we created a framework that predicts coverage@cost given that pass@kk is already known. Here, we focus on the scenario where pass@kk is unknown and show that the ReD strategy enables inference of the pass@kk scaling law exponent, without full evaluation of pass@kk.
We first consider the distribution of question difficulties 𝒫⁡(p)\mathcal{P}(p). In the ReD protocol, since we discard questions that were answered correctly in previous rounds, we effectively change the difficulty distribution at every round. We denote the distribution at the nn-th round as 𝒫n​(p)\mathcal{P}_{n}(p). We will now show that 𝒫n​(p)\mathcal{P}_{n}(p) is guaranteed to approach a beta distribution after several rounds as long as, for p≪1p\ll 1, the initial distribution 𝒫0​(p)≃c​pα−1\mathcal{P}_{0}(p)\simeq cp^{\alpha-1}. We then derive results for coverage@costτ=1​(t)\text{coverage@cost}_{\tau=1}(t) assuming 𝒫0​(p)∼Beta​(p,α,β)\mathcal{P}_{0}(p)\sim\text{Beta}(p;\alpha,\beta), neglecting the initial relaxation phase.
5.2 Asymptotic Convergence of Difficulty to the Beta Distribution
The distribution 𝒫n​(p)\mathcal{P}_{n}(p) represents all the questions that were left unanswered after nn rounds. This is given by
𝒫n​(p)=𝒫0​(p)​(1−p)n∫01(1−p)n​𝒫0​(p)​𝑑p,\mathcal{P}_{n}(p)=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{0}(p)(1-p)^{n}}{\int_{0}^{1}(1-p)^{n}\mathcal{P}_{0}(p)\mathrm{d}p},
(18)
where the new weight of questions with difficulty pp is given as a product of their initial weight 𝒫0​(p)\mathcal{P}_{0}(p) and the probability, (1−p)n(1-p)^{n}, to survive nn rounds.
Note that for large values of nn the term (1−p)n(1-p)^{n} suppresses contributions from high values of pp. In this limit, one can safely approximate 𝒫0​(p)≃c​pα−1\mathcal{P}_{0}(p)\simeq cp^{\alpha-1}, to obtain
𝒫n​(p)​≈n≫1​pα−1​(1−p)n∫01pα−1​(1−p)n​𝑑p∼Beta​(p,α,n+1).\mathcal{P}_{n}(p)\underset{n\gg 1}{\approx}\frac{p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n}}{\int_{0}^{1}p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n}\mathrm{d}p}\sim\text{Beta}(p;\alpha,n+1).
(19)
5.3 Inferring the Power-law exponent from ReD
Previous works (Brown et al., 2024; Schaeffer et al., 2025; Kazdan et al., 2025) have introduced several techniques to obtain the scaling exponent α\alpha of 1−pass@​k1-\text{pass@}k. Here, we derive an estimation method that is applicable to the ReD process.
We start by obtaining an expression for 𝒫n​(p)\mathcal{P}_{n}(p) for all values of nn.
Neglecting the relaxation towards the Beta distribution and repeating the derivation in Sec. 5.2 using 𝒫0​(p)=Beta​(p,α,β)\mathcal{P}_{0}(p)=\text{Beta}(p;\alpha,\beta) in Eq. 18, we obtain,
𝒫n​(p)=Beta​(p,α,β+n).\mathcal{P}_{n}(p)=\text{Beta}(p;\alpha,\beta+n).
(20)
The meaning of Eq. 20 is that γn​Rn\gamma_{n}R_{n} questions will be answered correctly, on average, at the nn-th round, where γn≔α/(α+β+n)\gamma_{n}\coloneqq\alpha/(\alpha+\beta+n) is the mean of Beta​(p,α,β+n)\text{Beta}(p;\alpha,\beta+n). As a result, the dynamics of this process can be written as,
⟨Rn+1⟩=⟨Rn⟩−γn​⟨Rn⟩.\langle R_{n+1}\rangle=\langle R_{n}\rangle-\gamma_{n}\langle R_{n}\rangle.
(21)
By rearranging Eq. 21, and using the definition of γn\gamma_{n}, we obtain a linear equation with respect to the number of rounds,
−⟨Rn⟩⟨Rn+1−Rn⟩=1α​n+α+βα.-\frac{\langle R_{n}\rangle}{\langle R_{n+1}-R_{n}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\alpha}n+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha}.
(22)
Thus, plotting the ratio ⟨Rn⟩/⟨Rn+1−Rn⟩\langle R_{n}\rangle/\langle R_{n+1}-R_{n}\rangle versus nn yields a straight line whose slope is 1/α1/\alpha.
After a few rounds, it is possible to extract the scaling exponent α\alpha from a linear fit.
6 Experiments on Large Language Models
To demonstrate the efficiency of ReD on LLMs, we ran experiments on the HumanEval dataset (Chen et al., 2021).
We used two query protocols: The first is the solve-to-completion method, in which we ask a question repeatedly until it is answered correctly before moving to the next question. The second is the ReD approach, resetting every attempt, which amounts to asking questions in rounds: we ask all the questions once and discard the ones that were answered correctly from the next round.
In practice, we performed these experiments by evaluating pass@kk up to k=100k=100 once for each model and saving, for each question and every attempt, whether it was answered correctly in a results matrix of dimension questions×\timesattempts. We also recorded how many input and output tokens were used.
To generate many random realizations of the solve-to-completion evaluation procedure, we shuffle rows and columns of the results matrix and analyze them as if we used the solve-to-completion protocol. From the same shuffled data, we also generate all the realizations for the ReD protocol, by analyzing the results matrix in rounds, going column-by-column, and discarding rows that were answered in previous rounds.
We performed the experiments using the Groq API, and three different models: llama-3.1-8b-instant, llama-3.3-70b-versatile and openai/gpt-oss-20b (OpenAI, 2025). All three models were given the same prompts. We set a strict system prompt to reduce conversational filler and enforce a consistent output format:
“You are an intelligent coding assistant. You will be provided with a function signature and docstring. You must complete the function body. Return ONLY the python code for the function implementation. Do not add explanations or tests. Wrap the code in a python markdown block."
Then, we used the standard HumanEval prompt, including the function signature and docstring. Regex is used to extract the output code. We used a temperature of 0.8 in all experiments. All the code used to generate the results of this paper will be posted on GitHub.

Another interesting question that arises is: given a dataset, which is the better strategy? To use a small model with ReD? or to train a much larger model? We answer this question in Fig. 2 by comparing the coverage@cost for the smaller llama-3.1-8b-instant model using the solve-to-completion protocol and ReD, and comparing it with the solve-to-completion protocol using the larger models. We employ three different measures for the cost: the total number of attempts to answer questions, the cumulative number of tokens used, and the budget in USD. The conversion from tokens to USD was done using the prices in Table 1. It is important to look at all three measures of the cost to understand the full efficiency of ReD in comparison to the standard policy.
In terms of the total number of attempts, performing ReD on the smaller model is more efficient than the larger llama model until a coverage of ∼90%\sim 90\% while ReD performs better or similar to the gpt-oss-20b model up to ∼80%\sim 80\% coverage. Clearly, the two larger models also end up answering questions correctly that the smaller model can not, but they require substantial training efforts. ReD can be used to effectively sort the questions by hardness using the small model, leaving just the hardest questions for the larger models.
In terms of tokens, the verbosity of the models plays a significant role. In this case, ReD on the smaller llama model performs better that gpt-oss-20b for nearly all accuracies. The reason is that the gpt-oss-20b model is very verbose in its output, which uses a lot of tokens. It seems plausible to be able to minimize this added token cost through prompt engineering, but we maintained the same prompt for all models to make a fair comparison. The larger llama model is much less verbose than the gpt-oss-20b model and therefore performs better in terms of coverage against the number of tokens. However, even in this case, ReD is the preferred strategy up to ∼80%\sim 80\% coverage.
In terms of USD, ReD is clearly the better strategy in all cases, since the larger models are either very token-hungry or very expensive (see Table 1). The preferred strategy would be to use ReD to first answer all the questions it can answer correctly, then switching to the larger models just for those questions it did not answer at all.
