2.3 Look-ahead mixed-precision evaluation
The two refinement approaches of Subsection 2.2 lead to very similar rounding error bounds for the composition.
The accuracy of the inner computation manifests itself in the second term, which we aim to reduce as follows: select a binary vector 𝐪∈{0,1}n\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n} so that
‖|diag​(f⁡(𝐲^))−1​𝐉f​(𝐲^)|​(𝟏−𝐪)‖∞≤τ\Big\|\big|\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\big|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\Big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau
(3)
or
‖|diag​(f⁡(𝐲^))−1​𝐉f​(𝐲^)​diag​(𝐲^)|​(𝟏−𝐪)‖∞≤τ\Big\|\big|\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\big|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\Big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau
(4)
for a given threshold τ≥0\tau\geq 0.
Let us discuss these objectives:
•
Objectives (3) and (4) improve the bound (2) for suitable τ\tau. Specifically, the unweighted (3) replaces the second term in (2) with 𝟏​τ​‖𝐲^‖∞​‖𝐜g‖∞​ug\bm{\mathrm{1}}\tau\|\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\|\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\mathrm{u}_{g}, and the weighted (4) with 𝟏​τ​‖𝐜g‖∞​ug\bm{\mathrm{1}}\tau\|\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{g}\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\mathrm{u}_{g}.
Which of the two objectives to choose is a problem-specific question.
•
Both (3) and (4) can always be attained with 𝐪=𝟏\bm{\mathrm{q}}=\bm{\mathrm{1}}, i.e., when every component of the inner function gg is computed more accurately.
•
When 𝐪=𝟎\bm{\mathrm{q}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}, the norms in (3) and (4) are proportional to the mixed and componentwise condition numbers of ff at 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}, respectively (Gohberg & Koltracht, 1993).
•
The exact f⁡(𝐲^)f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}) and 𝐉f​(𝐲^)\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}) are not available in practice, so their FP computed values will be used.
If the baseline 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}} with 𝐪=𝟎\bm{\mathrm{q}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}} satisfies (3) or (4), we deem the computation complete.
Otherwise, we try a different 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}}, which entails the recomputation of 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}.
Such recomputations can be costly, so we assume that the Jacobian is stable with respect to small variations in 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}} and fix the matrix 𝐊∈{𝐊u,𝐊w}\bm{\mathrm{K}}\in\{\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u},\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}\} corresponding to the baseline 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}, where44
4
If the Jacobian changes rapidly, the Hessian would need to be included in the analysis.
𝐊u=diag​(f⁡(𝐲^))−1​𝐉f​(𝐲^),\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}=\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}),
𝐊w=diag​(f⁡(𝐲^))−1​𝐉f​(𝐲^)​diag​(𝐲^).\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}=\mathrm{diag}(f(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}))^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{f}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}).
Having found a suitable 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}} that satisfies the bound
‖|𝐊|​(𝟏−𝐪)‖∞=‖𝐊⁡(𝐈−diag⁡(𝐪))‖∞,∞≤τ,\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\big\|\bm{\mathrm{K}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big)\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty,\infty}}\leq\tau,
we will recompute more accurately those components of 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}} that are indexed by the nonzeros of the binary vector 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}}.
To minimize the number of recomputations, we require 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}} to be sparse.
This leads to our look-ahead mixed-precision (LAMP) problem: seek 𝐪∈{0,1}n\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n} such that
‖𝐪‖0→mins.t.‖|𝐊|​(𝟏−𝐪)‖∞≤τ.\|\bm{\mathrm{q}}\|_{\mathrm{0}}\to\min\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\big\||\bm{\mathrm{K}}|(\bm{\mathrm{1}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}})\big\|_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq\tau.
(5)
The whole procedure of computing the inner function gg is summarized in Algorithm 1.
We shall call 𝐊u\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u} and 𝐊w\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w} the unweighted and weighted LAMP matrices, respectively.
Algorithm 1 LAMP evaluation of a composition
Input: functions ff and gg, variable 𝐱\bm{\mathrm{x}}, threshold τ≥0\tau\geq 0, weighted or unweighted objective
Output: adaptively computed value 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}} of g⁡(𝐱)g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
Compute 𝐲^≈g⁡(𝐱)\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\approx g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}) in FP arithmetic.
Compute 𝐊≈𝐊u\bm{\mathrm{K}}\approx\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u} or 𝐊≈𝐊w\bm{\mathrm{K}}\approx\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w} in FP arithmetic.
Find a solution 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}} of the LAMP problem (5).
Recompute components of 𝐲^\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}} indexed by nonzeros of 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}} more accurately.
3 Application to transformer inference
Algorithm 1 relies on the possibility to compute individual components of gg separately.
Yet this is not always possible.
For instance, consider the softmax function
softmax⁡(𝐱)=[exp⁡(x1)∑i=1kexp⁡(xi)⋯exp⁡(xk)∑i=1kexp⁡(xi)]⊺.\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\exp(x_{1})}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(x_{i})}&\cdots&\frac{\exp(x_{k})}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp(x_{i})}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.
(6)
If at least one component needs to be computed more accurately, every component has to be as well.
Meanwhile, the required property holds for matrix-vector products: it suffices to divide the matrix into two blocks and use different multiplication algorithms for each (see Example 2.1).55
5
When a bias term is present, it can be added to the accumulator in the required precision as well.
Matrix products are used heavily in all DNN architectures.
We thus apply LAMP evaluation to the following question:
How to adapt matrix multiplication to the ensuing nonlinearity during DNN inference?
What is the “ensuing nonlinearity” in question?
To target the accuracy of the end result of inference, an obvious choice is to set ff to be the remaining tail of the DNN.
However, the computation of its LAMP matrix becomes highly demanding.
Additionally, the sparse-optimization LAMP problem (5) is in general NP-hard (Natarajan, 1995).
Instead, let ff be one of the elementary DNN nonlinearities; for transformers, these are the activation function, layer normalization, and softmax.
We prove for these three functions that almost-the-sparsest solutions of the LAMP problem (5) can be obtained with a greedy algorithm of complexity 𝒪⁡(n​log⁡n)\mathcal{O}(n\log n)—the cost of a sorting algorithm.
3.1 Activation functions
Consider an activation function φ:ℝ→ℝ\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}.
Within DNNs, activation functions are applied componentwise to vectors:
f:ℝn→ℝn,f⁡(𝐲)=[φ⁡(y1)⋯φ⁡(yn)]⊺.f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad f(\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi(y_{1})&\cdots&\varphi(y_{n})\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.
As a consequence, the LAMP matrix is diagonal,
𝐊u=diag⁡(φ′​(y1)φ⁡(y1),…,φ′​(yn)φ⁡(yn)),\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{u}=\mathrm{diag}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{1})}{\varphi(y_{1})},\ldots,\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{n})}{\varphi(y_{n})}\bigg),
𝐊w=diag⁡(φ′​(y1)φ⁡(y1)​y1,…,φ′​(yn)φ⁡(yn)​yn),\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{w}=\mathrm{diag}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{1})}{\varphi(y_{1})}y_{1},\ldots,\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(y_{n})}{\varphi(y_{n})}y_{n}\bigg),
and the solution 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}} of the LAMP problem (5) can be written in closed form: an entry of 𝐪\bm{\mathrm{q}} is nonzero if and only if the corresponding diagonal entry of the LAMP matrix exceeds τ\tau in absolute value.
The diagonal structure of the Jacobian makes it possible to solve (5) immediately.
For activation functions, LAMP evaluation essentially repeats the mixed-precision accumulation idea of (El Arar et al., 2025).
The novelty of the proposed LAMP problem (5) is that it applies to arbitrary compositions, and we prove below that transformer-specific compositions are particularly suitable for LAMP evaluation.
3.2 Layer normalization
Layer normalization aims to stabilize the training of DNNs (Ba et al., 2016) by ‘‘standardizing’’ its input via66
6
We focus on layer normalization without scale and bias, as they can be seen as an affine function applied after the nonlinearity.

Its placement within a transformer can vary across specific architectures (Xiong et al., 2020), the two conventional choices being postnormalization (Vaswani et al., 2017; Devlin et al., 2019) and prenormalization (Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; Chowdhery et al., 2023).
Yet neither places layer normalization right after a matrix product.
Another possible placement is between a (feedforward or attention) sublayer and a skip connection (Liu et al., 2022; OLMo et al., 2025), then layer normalization directly follows a matrix product and we can apply Algorithm 1.
Note that the shifting step in layer normalization is itself a matrix-vector product, and we can “attach” this matrix to the preceding matrix multiplication.
The remaining function

4.1 Custom floating-point format
To focus on the question of precision and put aside the issue of overflows, we simulate low-precision matrix multiplications using a custom partial single FP format.
For every μ∈{1,…,23}\mu\in\{1,\ldots,23\}, we define the format PS⁡(μ)\mathrm{PS}(\mu) to have μ\mu mantissa bits, 8 exponent bits, and one sign bit.
This format is equivalent to FP32 when μ=23\mu=23, to TF32 when μ=10\mu=10, and to BF16 when μ=7\mu=7.
In code, we implement PS⁡(μ)\mathrm{PS}(\mu) numbers via FP32 numbers rounded to μ\mu mantissa bits according to the round-to-nearest-ties-to-even mode.
To perform matrix multiplication with input-output formats PS⁡(μA)×PS⁡(μB)→PS⁡(μC)\mathrm{PS}(\mu_{A})\times\mathrm{PS}(\mu_{B})\to\mathrm{PS}(\mu_{C}), we accumulate inner products as round⁡(c+a⋅b)\mathrm{round}(c+a\cdot b), where the scalar multiplication and addition are in FP32.
4.2 Experimental setting
To assess the impact of LAMP evaluation on inference, we compute the mean Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the probability distributions output by a reference model and a test model over 200 sequences of 1024 tokens each.
We also look at the flip rate, i.e., how often the most probable predictions of the reference and test models differ.
Our reference model uses FP32 inference uniformly for all FP operations.
The test models perform the key-query products in PS⁡(μ)\mathrm{PS}(\mu) and recompute some of them, as selected by the LAMP problem (5), in FP32.
We keep track of how many inner products are recomputed; to get the recomputation rate, we divide by the number of key-query inner products in the “causal mask.”
4.3 Numerical results
In Figure 2, we fix the threshold τ\tau of LAMP and vary the number of mantissa bits μ\mu.
The plots demonstrate clearly that our method works in practice, that is, as the threshold τ\tau decreases, it improves the accuracy of inference and increases the number of recomputations required.
Specifically, consistently for smaller μ\mu, our method reduces the KL divergence tenfold with 3.4% of recomputations (τ=1.4\tau=1.4), hundredfold with 15% of recomputations (τ=1.1\tau=1.1), and thousandfold with 34.3% of recomputations (τ=1.02\tau=1.02).
Similarly consistent improvements hold for the flip rate as well, and we observe exponential-like decay of the two metrics as the number of bits μ\mu is increased.
An interesting observation is that the recomputation rate hardly depends on μ\mu, yet we stress again that the choice of the components to be recomputed has a crucial impact on the performance of mixed-precision inference (cf. Figure 1 and Appendix B).
Figure 3 provides a different angle on the numerical results, fixing μ\mu and varying τ\tau.
The plots show the KL divergence is improved at the same rate, regardless of the (small) number of bits μ\mu, as the threshold τ\tau decreases.
The same observation holds for the flip rate, and both metrics decay faster as τ\tau tends to one, at which point everything is selected for recomputation.
We can also see that the recomputation rate is slightly higher for extremely low-precision accumulation (μ=2\mu=2) than for the rest.
The empirical results presented in Figures 2 and 3 convincingly demonstrate a strong performance of the proposed LAMP method, as it reaches high inference accuracies relative to the ground truth with the vast majority of key-query inner products performed in low precision.
The additional experiments in Appendix B strengthen this conclusion.
While not studied numerically in this paper, LAMP evaluation can be applied to all transformer nonlinearities simultaneously (Section 3), which could increase the computational throughput of transformer inference even further.
Together with a hardware-specific, efficient implementation of LAMP inference, this is a promising avenue for future research.
