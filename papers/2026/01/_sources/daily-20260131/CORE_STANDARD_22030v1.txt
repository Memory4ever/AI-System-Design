4 Method
The framework of PerTA and its difference from vanilla TV are shown in Algorithm 1 (violet) and Figure 3. PerTA flexibly scales TV via per-parameter multiplication between WW (in Eq.(3)) and TV. Each entry of WW quantifies the relative importance of its corresponding parameter for the forget set versus the retain set.
To this end, we compute parameter gradients with respect to both the forget and retain sets (once each, with minimal overhead) and use them to construct WW (detailed in Section 4.1). Moreover, we analyze the effectiveness of PerTA, extend it to a general form, and discuss its validity in Section 4.2.

4.1 Per-parameter Importance Estimation
Let W=[w1,w2,…,wn]W=[w_{1},w_{2},...,w_{n}] be the scaling weights corresponding to the model parameters θ=[q1,q2,…,qn]\theta=[q_{1},q_{2},...,q_{n}] with nn parameters. Each weight satisfies wi∈[0,1],1≤i≤nw_{i}\in[0,1],1\leq i\leq n. Values wiw_{i} closer to 1 indicate that TV at qiq_{i} should be kept, while values approaching 0 downweight TV at qiq_{i}.
Using Absolute Gradient (PerTA-grad).
Since the importance is independent of gradient direction, the absolute magnitude of the parameter gradients (54, 7) provides a natural measure of importance. While gradient estimation using either θ0\theta_{0} or θfull\theta_{\rm full} is justifiable, we adopt θ0\theta_{0} here because θ0\theta_{0} is a more neutral initialization model that does not contain training data from the forget or the retain set. However, in practice, estimating gradients on θ0\theta_{0} or on θfull\theta_{\rm full} makes a negligible difference (see Appendix C.3 for a detailed discussion). Let ∇ℒ​(𝒟f,θ0),∇ℒ​(𝒟r,θ0)\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta_{0}),\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm r};\theta_{0}) be the gradients of forget and retain sets. The weight for each parameter can be computed as the relative contribution of the forget set gradient to the total gradient magnitude, where WW can be formulated as:
Wgrad=|∇ℒ​(𝒟f,θ0)|+ϵ|∇ℒ​(𝒟r,θ0)|+|∇ℒ​(𝒟f,θ0)|+2​ϵ,W_{\rm grad}=\frac{|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta_{0})|+\epsilon}{|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm r};\theta_{0})|+|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta_{0})|+2\epsilon},
(4)
where ϵ\epsilon is a small constant to avoid division by zero.
Substituting Eq.(4) into Eq.(3) yields the final unlearned model. WgradW_{\rm grad} treats all deviations linearly. Next, we also propose a non-linear alternative.
Using Diagonal Fisher Information Approximation (PerTA-fisher).
The diagonal of the Fisher Information Matrix (30, 1) is widely used to reflect the sensitivity of parameters to the data. The computation of its diagonal entries can be simplified as the squared gradients (see Appendix B.1 for a detailed proof). Accordingly, WW can also be expressed as:
Wfisher=∇ℒ2​(𝒟f,θ0)+ϵ∇ℒ2​(𝒟r,θ0)+∇ℒ2​(𝒟f,θ0)+2​ϵ.W_{\rm fisher}=\frac{\nabla\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta_{0})+\epsilon}{\nabla\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm r};\theta_{0})+\nabla\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta_{0})+2\epsilon}.
(5)
Similar to WgradW_{\rm grad}, substituting Eq.(5) into Eq.(3) yields the final unlearned model, as detailed in Algorithm 1. Both WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher} essentially estimate the per-parameter importance of the forget set by computing the relative magnitude of gradients on 𝒟f\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}. However, the latter employs a square operation, which amplifies the gradient differences and thus drives wiw_{i} closer to 0 or 1. The detailed discussion is in the next subsection.
4.2 Discussion and A General Form
Discussion about PerTA. Denoting gf≜∇ℒ​(𝒟f,θ0)g_{\rm f}\triangleq\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm f};\theta_{0}), gr≜∇ℒ​(𝒟r,θ0)g_{\rm r}\triangleq\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\rm r};\theta_{0}), we further explore the effectiveness of WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher}, and their difference.
Definition 1.
(Retain-forget ratio).
For a parameter qiq_{i} in an LLM, the retain-forget ratio reflects its relative importance for retention versus forgetting.
Denoting [gr]i[g_{\rm r}]_{i} and [gf]i[g_{\rm f}]_{i} to be the gradients of qiq_{i} on the retain and forget sets, the retain-forget ratio can be represented as
ri=(|[gr]i|+ϵ)/(|[gf]i|+ϵ).r_{i}=({|[g_{\rm r}]_{i}|+\epsilon})/({|[g_{\rm f}]_{i}|+\epsilon}).
When ri≥1r_{i}\geq 1, the retain set dominates for qiq_{i}, and the forget set dominates when ri<1r_{i}<1.
Hence, we obtain the following proposition.
Proposition 1.
For parameter pip_{i}, we denote its corresponding weights calculated with PerTA-grad, PerTA-fisher to be ωigrad\omega_{i}^{\rm grad} and ωifisher\omega_{i}^{\rm fisher} respectively. Then we have:
12≥ωigrad≥ωifisher≥0, when ​ri≥1​; ​12<ωigrad<ωifisher<1, when ​ri<1,\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\geq\omega_{i}^{\rm grad}\geq\omega_{i}^{\rm fisher}\geq 0,\text{ when }r_{i}\geq 1\text{; }\frac{1}{2}<\omega_{i}^{\rm grad}<\omega_{i}^{\rm fisher}<1,\text{ when }r_{i}<1,
which is proved in Appendix B.2.
This implies that when pip_{i} has a larger influence on the retain set, PerTA applies a smaller reweighting to TV in order to reduce forgetting, and vice versa. Notably, compared to PerTA-grad, PerTA-fisher yields weights that are closer to 0 or 1, thereby creating a cleaner separation between parameters to be edited and to be preserved.
A General Form. Besides, the determination of WW is not limited to the aforementioned approaches. Here, we express WW in a more general form–as a function of gfg_{\rm f} and grg_{\rm r}:
Wgeneral=foprt​(gf,gr),W_{\rm general}=f_{\rm oprt}(g_{\rm f},g_{\rm r}),
where foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot) is a custom operation. Then both WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher} can be represented with foprt​(A,B)=|A|∘τ/(|A|∘τ+|B|∘τ)f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=|A|^{\circ\tau}/(|A|^{\circ\tau}+|B|^{\circ\tau}), where ∘τ\circ\tau is the per-parameter τ\tau-th power and the case τ=1\tau=1 and τ=2\tau=2 correspond to WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher}, respectively.
Discussion about foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot). In addition to the absolute gradient and diagonal Fisher Information approximation we applied, other operations—such as the SoftMax-based formulation foprt​(A,B)=exp⁡(|A|)/(exp⁡(|A|)+exp⁡(|B|))f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=\exp(|A|)/(\exp(|A|)+\exp(|B|))—can also be employed (see Section 1 for detailed results and discussions). Moreover, WgeneralW_{\rm general} subsumes more general cases: when foprt​(A,B)=1f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=1, it degenerates to vanilla TV, whereas when foprt​(A,B)=wf_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=w, PerTA employs the uniform constant ww to balance forgetting and retaining. Denoting weight wiw_{i} for parameter qiq_{i} as wi=[foprt​(gf,gr)]iw_{i}=[f_{\rm oprt}(g_{\rm f},g_{\rm r})]_{i} and the corresponding gradients are [gf]i[g_{\rm f}]_{i} and [gr]i[g_{\rm r}]_{i}, in the following, we discuss the design of foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot):
•
Intuitively, it should satisfy [foprt​(gf,gr)]i→1[f_{\rm oprt}(g_{\rm f},g_{\rm r})]_{i}\rightarrow 1 when |[gf]i|≫|[gr]i||[g_{\rm f}]_{i}|\gg|[g_{\rm r}]_{i}|, and [foprt​(gf,gr)]i→0[f_{\rm oprt}(g_{\rm f},g_{\rm r})]_{i}\rightarrow 0 when |[gr]i|≫|[gf]i||[g_{\rm r}]_{i}|\gg|[g_{\rm f}]_{i}|. This is because TV is the vector for forget set 𝒟f\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}: when |[gf]i||[g_{\rm f}]_{i}| is large, the parameter qiq_{i} is crucial for unlearning, and thus the rescaled TV should preserve its value; conversely, when |[gr]i||[g_{\rm r}]_{i}| is large, the parameter is critical for retention, and the TV should therefore be scaled down. WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher} are consistent with this intuition (see Appendix B.3).
•
Empirically, we explored several straightforward ways of designing foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot) and found that WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher} in this paper perform best among them, as detailed in the ablation studies from Section 1. Naturally, the choice of foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot) is not unique, and we hope our work will inspire further exploration and discussion.



5 Experiments
Baselines and Benchmarks.
Experiments are conducted on the widely used unlearning benchmark TOFU (29) (covering three tasks with 1%, 5%, and 10% of the data unlearned) and on MUSE News (35). On TOFU, following 8, we employ Llama-3.2 1B and 3B Instruct models (38) and evaluate them using five metrics: (1) Forget Quality (FQ) (29), which measures the effectiveness of unlearning (higher is better, we use log transformation in this paper); (2) Model Utility (MU) (29), which quantifies the model’s usefulness in retaining original knowledge (higher is better); (3) Extraction Strength (ES) (4) of the forget set, defined as the proportion of repeated content start positions in the forget set (lower is better); (4) ES of the retain set, defined analogously on the retain set (higher is better); (5) Gibberish (Gib), which represents the probability—determined by a binary classifier (17)—that answers to forget-set queries are non-gibberish (higher is better) and (6) ROUGE-L (ROUGE) (22), the proportion of the longest common sub-sequence between the ground truth and the answers. Additional dataset-related information is provided in Appendix 2, while detailed definitions of the metrics are given in Appendix A.2.
As for the baselines, for training-based methods we evaluate the mainstream approaches GA (49), GD (23), NPO (53), and NPO+ (NPO combined with GD). For task-arithmetic methods, we test vanilla TV (13) and our proposed method. In addition, we report the metrics of the full model before unlearning, alongside those of a ground-truth model trained solely on the retain set (29), as references. Detailed information about the baselines and implementation can be found in Appendices A.3 and A.4, respectively.
Performance Comparison.
The results of FQ and MU on the three TOFU tasks with 1%, 5%, and 10% samples to be unlearned are shown in Figure 4 (see more metrics in Appendix C.1). The ground-truth results are shown as black pentagram markers. The FQ metric measures the p-value of distributional differences from the ground truth; we perform the logarithmic transformation to better highlight variations. Dark-blue and purple circles denote methods PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher, respectively. On simpler tasks (e.g., unlearning 1% of the data), most training-based methods maintain model utility, while the task-arithmetic method TV achieves higher FQ but at the cost of MU. Our PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher improve MU relative to TV and yield results closer to the ground truth. On more challenging tasks (e.g., unlearning 5% or 10%), training-based methods degrade: MU for GA and NPO drops to nearly zero, and their FQ becomes both lower and unstable (with larger variance). In contrast, our PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher consistently outperform both training-based and task-arithmetic baselines in FQ and MU, confirming the effectiveness of PerTA in achieving unlearning while preserving model capability.
To examine why PerTA outperforms TV among task arithmetic methods, we evaluate four dimensions: forget, retain, real, and facts (29), corresponding to the forget set, retain set, original authors, and world facts. The first two measure forgetting and retention of post-training knowledge, while the latter two assess preservation of pretrained knowledge. ROUGE is used to measure similarity to reference answers. As shown in Figure 5 (see Appendix C.1 for more results), TV performs well on real authors and world facts, and PerTA preserves this capability. However, for post-training knowledge, TV suffers from over-forgetting, whereas PerTA significantly narrows the gap to the ground truth. In more challenging settings (e.g., unlearning 5% and 10%), TV falls far below the ground truth, while PerTA nearly doubles TV’s performance, being much closer to the reference.

More Backbones and Benchmarks. Table 1 reports the average results of different methods across the three unlearning tasks, with both 1B and 3B model sizes considered to examine the effect of different LLM backbones (see complete results in Appendix C.2). The results show that the baseline TV, compared with training-based methods, suffers from excessive forgetting on the retain set (low ES (𝒟r\mathcal{D}_{\rm r})), while our PerTA substantially improves ES (𝒟r\mathcal{D}_{\rm r}) without significantly reducing ES (𝒟f\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}). Moreover, PerTA delivers notable gains in FQ (e.g., with the ground truth being 00, TV achieves −6.174-6.174 and −5.284-5.284, whereas PerTA-grad reaches about −0.67-0.67 and PerTA-fisher about −1-1) and MU (e.g., on the 1B model, PerTA raises MU from 0.4950.495 to 0.5560.556 by PerTA-grad or 0.5620.562 by PerTA-fisher, narrowing the gap to the ground truth to within 0.040.04). On larger backbones such as 3B, PerTA maintains improvements in both FQ and MU while further increasing ES (𝒟r\mathcal{D}_{\rm r}) without compromising ES (𝒟f\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}). These results demonstrate the effectiveness of PerTA in achieving unlearning while preserving utility across different model scales. Additionally, results in Appendix C.2 show that PerTA is also effective on MUSE.
Ablation (General Form) Studies.
Figure 6 shows the curves of FQ when different foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot) are selected under varying hyperparameters. In addition to PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher proposed in Eq.(4) and Eq.(5), we consider several straightforward designs: (1) ‘Pruning’: removing (i.e., foprt​(A,B)=0f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=0) the λ%\lambda\% smallest weights in TV to mitigate over-forgetting and maintain others (i.e., foprt​(A,B)=1f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=1), where λ=0\lambda=0 reduces to vanilla TV; (2) ‘Random’: setting weights in WW to random values uniformly sampled between 0 and 1 with foprt​(A,B)=rand([0, 1])f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=\text{rand([0, 1])}; (3) ‘Weighted’: using a constant ω\omega to rescale TV with foprt​(A,B)=ωf_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=\omega, where ω=1\omega=1 reduces to vanilla TV; and (4) ‘SoftMax’: determining foprt​(A,B)=exp⁡(|A|)/(exp⁡(|A|)+exp⁡(|B|))f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=\exp(|A|)/(\exp(|A|)+\exp(|B|)) in the SoftMax form.
Among these, ‘Pruning’ and ‘Weighted’ methods vary with λ\lambda or ω\omega, as shown in Figure 6. We observe that ‘Pruning’ performs poorly on more challenging tasks (e.g., unlearning 10%), ‘Random’ exhibits very high variance, and ‘Weighted’ can achieve reasonable results when the optimal constant ω\omega is chosen but is highly sensitive to the hyperparameter. The ‘SoftMax’ method represents a successful design of foprt​(⋅,⋅)f_{\rm oprt}(\cdot,\cdot), yet our PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher still outperform other possible designs.

Time Efficiency Discussion.
Figure 7 shows the runtime comparison between the best-performing training-based method, NPO+, and our PerTA.
Unlike training-based approaches that require repeated iterations, the runtime of PerTA can be decomposed into: the time to obtain θfgt\theta_{\rm fgt}, the time to compute WW, and the time for task arithmetic, where the latter is negligible. It can be observed that PerTA inherits the advantage of task arithmetic—significantly reducing runtime—and this advantage becomes more pronounced as task complexity increases (i.e., when unlearning larger proportions). Moreover, as shown previously, estimating gradients with only 20% of the data already yields competitive results, suggesting that runtime can be further reduced. Together, these findings highlight the time efficiency of PerTA. More results are in Appendix C.2.

Alternative Variants Analysis.
When retaining the form of foprt​(A,B)=|A|∘τ/(|A|∘τ+|B|∘τ)f_{\rm oprt}(A,B)=|A|^{\circ\tau}/(|A|^{\circ\tau}+|B|^{\circ\tau}) but not using PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher, different τ\taus can be applied. The cases of τ=1,2\tau=1,2 correspond to our PerTA-grad and PerTA-fisher, respectively. Our methods strike a balance between forgetting and retaining: among the different τ\tau-based variants, they achieve relatively strong FQ and ES (𝒟f\mathcal{D}_{\rm f}) while keeping MU and ES (𝒟r\mathcal{D}_{\rm r}) at a reasonable level.
Sample Efficiency Discussion.
In this experiment, we estimate WgradW_{\rm grad} and WfisherW_{\rm fisher} using 0%, 20%, 40%, and 80% of the total samples, where 0% corresponds to vanilla TV and the other three represent PerTA with reduced sample sizes. The differences in metrics compared to using the full dataset are shown in Figure 9 and Appendix C.2. It is observed that using only one-fifth of the samples already yields results comparable to those obtained with the full dataset, and better than vanilla TV. This demonstrates that PerTA is also sample-efficient, which can be beneficial in further reducing the unlearning time.
