Abstract
Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) empowers large language models (LLMs) to tackle complex tasks by interleaving reasoning steps with external tool interactions.
However, existing reinforcement learning methods typically rely on outcome- or trajectory-level rewards, assigning uniform advantages to all steps within a trajectory.
This coarse-grained credit assignment fails to distinguish effective tool calls from redundant or erroneous ones, particularly in long-horizon multi-turn scenarios.
To address this, we propose MatchTIR, a framework that introduces fine-grained supervision via bipartite matching-based turn-level reward assignment and dual-level advantage estimation.
Specifically, we formulate credit assignment as a bipartite matching problem between predicted and ground-truth traces, utilizing two assignment strategies to derive dense turn-level rewards.
Furthermore, to balance local step precision with global task success, we introduce a dual-level advantage estimation scheme that integrates turn-level and trajectory-level signals, assigning distinct advantage values to individual interaction turns.
Extensive experiments on three benchmarks demonstrate the superiority of MatchTIR.
Notably, our 4B model surpasses the majority of 8B competitors, particularly in long-horizon and multi-turn tasks.
Our codes are available at https://github.com/quchangle1/MatchTIR.
1 Introduction
Figure 1: Comparison of reward and advantage assignment strategies for multi-turn TIR. (a) and (b) show traditional methods where all reasoning steps share the same reward and advantage. (c) and (d) illustrate our MatchTIR framework using hard and soft assignment to derive turn-level rewards and distinct advantages.
Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) has established itself as a critical paradigm for enhancing the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by enabling them to interact with external tools during the reasoning process Gou et al. (2024).
By leveraging external tools, TIR allows LLMs to overcome limitations of static parametric knowledge Jin et al. (2025); Zheng et al. (2025c), perform precise computations Wang et al. (2024); Das et al. (2024), and engage dynamically with external environments Qin et al. (2024); Qu et al. (2024); Qu et al. (2025a).
Through multi-turn interactions, agents interleave reasoning, tool execution, and feedback acquisition, refining their trajectory toward solving complex real-world tasks.
To equip agents with such sophisticated tool-integrated reasoning capabilities, recent research has largely adopted reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) methods Chang et al. (2025); Jiang et al. (2025); Lin and Xu (2025), such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Shao et al. (2024).
While early RLVR formulations primarily relied on sparse outcome-based rewards Li et al. (2025), subsequent works have incorporated trajectory-level signals Qian et al. (2025); Zeng et al. (2025b) to provide denser supervision.
However, as shown in Figure 1, even these trajectory-level formulations typically assign a uniform advantage value to every turn within a trajectory.
This “one-size-fits-all” credit assignment fails to distinguish critical reasoning steps from redundant or erroneous tool calls, leading to inefficient optimization and consequently hindering the model from learning precise and efficient tool-use strategies.
Several studies have attempted to introduce fine-grained rewards via intrinsic signals or external reward models Wang et al. (2025a); Zhang et al. (2025c).
However, external reward models are inherently susceptible to bias and hallucination, while Monte Carlo estimation Dong et al. (2025) incurs prohibitive computational costs and high variance, especially in long-horizon tasks.
Moreover, many of these efforts are confined to restricted tools such as search engines, where intermediate steps are characterized by high semantic variance due to the fact that multiple different queries may be equally valid, making it difficult to establish a unique ground truth for reliable step-level supervision.
In contrast, general TIR scenarios naturally expose structured and verifiable signals, such as tool names, parameter names, and parameter contents, which enable explicit evaluation of the correctness of the tool use at each turn.
This motivates our approach to formulate turn-level reward assignment as a bipartite matching problem between predicted and ground-truth tool interactions.
In this paper, we propose MatchTIR, a framework designed to assign distinct, precise advantages to individual turns within a reasoning trajectory.
We formulate turn-level credit assignment as a bipartite matching problem, constructing a weighted bipartite graph based on similarity scores across tool names, parameter names, and parameter contents to align predicted calls with ground-truth references.
Based on this formulation, we introduce both hard and soft credit assignment strategies to derive dense turn-level rewards:
the hard variant enforces “one-to-one” matching, while the soft variant allows “one-to-many” alignment.
Both strategies produce dense and precise turn-level rewards, enabling effective supervision of intermediate tool interactions.
To ensure the model optimizes for both local accuracy and global success, MatchTIR synthesizes turn-level rewards with final outcome-based rewards.
Furthermore, we introduce a dual-level advantage estimation mechanism that harmonizes local turn-level rewards with global trajectory-level outcomes.
Specifically, the trajectory-level advantage aggregates all rewards across the entire path to evaluate global quality, while the turn-level advantage captures the tt-th turn’s specific contribution via discounted accumulation of all subsequent rewards.
The policy is finally optimized using the GRPO objective with our integrated dual-level advantages.
Extensive experiments on both in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of MatchTIR.
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
∙\bullet We identify the uniform credit assignment problem in existing TIR methods as a key bottleneck preventing efficient optimization.
∙\bullet We propose MatchTIR, which formulates turn-level credit assignment as a bipartite matching problem and introduces hard and soft matching strategies to provide dense, turn-level supervision.
∙\bullet Extensive experiments on three benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of MatchTIR, with particularly strong gains on long-horizon scenarios, validating its robustness and generalizability.
2 Preliminaries
In this section, we first present the task formulation of TIR, and then introduce the reinforcement learning framework adopted in this work.
2.1 Task Formulation
Formally, given a user query qq and a tool set 𝒯={t1,t2,…,tn}\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}\} consisting of nn available tools, the goal of the agent is to generate an interaction trajectory τ={s1,s2,…,sT}\tau=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{T}\} to solve qq, where TT denotes the number of interaction turns.
Specifically, each interaction turn si=(ni,ci,oi)s_{i}=(n_{i},c_{i},o_{i}) is represented as a triplet, where nin_{i} is the natural language reasoning in ii-th turn, cic_{i} denotes the set of invoked tools from 𝒯\mathcal{T} along with their specific parameter names and parameter contents, and oio_{i} is the observation returned by the environment.
The trajectory terminates when the agent generates a final answer response (where cT=∅c_{T}=\emptyset and oT=∅o_{T}=\emptyset) or reaches the pre-defined maximum turn limit LL.
Goal. As discussed in §1, TIR is inherently a long-horizon, multi-turn process.
However, existing outcome- or trajectory-level reward formulations assign uniform credit across an entire trajectory, failing to distinguish between effective and unnecessary tool invocations.
This results in inefficient credit assignment and suboptimal tool-use behaviors.
Therefore, our goal is to assign precise turn-level rewards, enabling fine-grained credit assignment that incentivizes effective tool usage contributing to the final solution while penalizing redundant or erroneous actions at each turn.

(a) illustrates the process of multi-turn TIR, where the policy LLM interacts with external tools over multiple turns and receives an outcome reward. (b) shows turn-level reward modeling by matching predicted and golden tool calls using hard or soft assignment strategies. (c) depicts the dual-level advantage estimation that integrates trajectory-level and turn-level signals for policy optimization.
2.2 Agentic Reinforcement Learning
To optimize the agent policy πθ\pi_{\theta}, we adopt Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Shao et al. (2024), a specialized reinforcement learning algorithm.
Distinct from conventional methods, GRPO obviates the requirement for a learned value function by estimating advantages based on the relative performance within a group of GG rollouts {τi}i=1G\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G} generated from the same prompt.

&w_{i,j}\hat{A}_{i,j},\text{clip}(w_{i,j},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i,j})-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}|\pi_{\text{ref}})\bigg],\end{split}
where πo​l​d\pi_{old} is the old policy, wi,j=πθ​(τi,j|q,τi,<j)πθold​(τi,j|q,τi,<j)w_{i,j}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\tau_{i,j}|q,\tau_{i,<j})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\tau_{i,j}|q,\tau_{i,<j})} is the importance sampling ratio, A^i,j\hat{A}_{i,j} is the normalized advantage of the jj-th token in the ii-th rollout.

3 Our Approach: MatchTIR
In this section, we will provide a detailed introduction to our proposed MatchTIR.
The overall framework is illustrated in Figure 2.
3.1 Reward Modeling
As shown in Figure 2(a), TIR is inherently a multi-turn process, where an agent interleaves reasoning with multiple tool invocations before arriving at a final answer.
To optimize the agent’s policy, it is crucial to determine whether each predicted tool call contributes to the reasoning goal.
However, simply comparing individual calls is insufficient, as the agent may generate redundant tool invocations.
To address this, we reformulate turn-level credit assignment for TIR as a bipartite matching problem between two sets.
Specifically, given a rollout trajectory, we extract a set of mm predicted tool calls 𝒫={p1,…,pm}\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\} and identify nn ground-truth tool calls 𝒢={g1,…,gn}\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}.
Our goal is to derive precise rewards by finding an optimal alignment between 𝒫\mathcal{P} and 𝒢\mathcal{G}, thereby penalizing uninformative or repetitive actions while rewarding those that align with the expert reasoning path.
Matching Matrix Construction. We first construct a matching matrix 𝒮∈ℝm×n\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, where each entry Si​jS_{ij} represents the matching score between a predicted tool pip_{i} and a ground-truth tool gjg_{j}.
Inspired by the previous work Qian et al. (2025), the matching score Si​jS_{ij} includes three components:
∙\bullet Tool Name Matching. We first check the consistency of the tool name. Let pit​np_{i}^{tn} and gjt​ng_{j}^{tn} be the name of the predicted and golden tool, respectively:
St​n=𝕀⁡(pit​n=gjt​n)∈{0,1},S_{tn}=\mathbb{I}(p_{i}^{tn}=g_{j}^{tn})\in\{0,1\},
(1)
where 𝕀⁡(⋅)\mathbb{I}(\cdot) is an indicator function that returns 1 if the predicted tool name matches the ground-truth tool name, and 0 otherwise.
∙\bullet Parameter Name Matching. If the tool name matches, we calculate Jaccard similarity of parameter name sets NpiN_{p_{i}} and NgjN_{g_{j}} to measure the overlap:
Sp​n=|Npi∩Ngj||Npi∪Ngj|∈[0,1],S_{pn}=\frac{|N_{p_{i}}\cap N_{g_{j}}|}{|N_{p_{i}}\cup N_{g_{j}}|}\in[0,1],
(2)
∙\bullet Parameter Content Matching. We further assess the correctness of the values provided for each ground-truth parameter:
Sp​c=∑k∈Ngj𝕀⁡(pi​[k]=gj​[k])∈[0,|Ngj|],S_{pc}=\sum_{k\in N_{g_{j}}}\mathbb{I}(p_{i}[k]=g_{j}[k])\in[0,|N_{g_{j}}|],
(3)
where pi​[k]p_{i}[k] and gj​[k]g_{j}[k] denote the parameter content associated with the parameter name kk.
The final similarity score Si​jS_{ij} is normalized by the maximum possible score to ensure Si​j∈[0,1]S_{ij}\in[0,1]:
Si​j=St​n⋅St​n+Sp​n+Sp​c1+1+|Ngj|S_{ij}=S_{tn}\cdot\frac{S_{tn}+S_{pn}+S_{pc}}{1+1+|N_{g_{j}}|}
(4)
While the matrix 𝒮\mathcal{S} provides pairwise alignment scores, directly using these scores as rewards can lead to reward hacking, such as repeatedly invoking similar tools to accumulate high similarity scores.
To mitigate this, we introduce two assignment strategies to convert 𝒮\mathcal{S} into turn-level rewards.
Hard Credit Assignment.
The hard credit assignment strategy enforces a strict “one-to-one” alignment between predicted and ground-truth tool calls, preventing duplicated credit for the same ground-truth action.
We formulate this process as a maximum weight bipartite matching problem and solve it using the Hungarian (Kuhn–Munkres) algorithm Kuhn (1956).
Formally, let xi​j∈{0,1}x_{ij}\in\{0,1\} be a binary assignment variable that xi​j=1x_{ij}=1 implies that the predicted tool pip_{i} is matched with the ground-truth tool gjg_{j}.
The objective is to maximize the total matching score ∑i,jxi,j​Si,j\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}S_{i,j}, subject to the constraint that each predicted and ground-truth tool call is matched at most once.
Based on the resulting matching, the reward for each predicted tool call pip_{i} is defined as
rpi={Si​j,if ​∃j,xi​j=1−λ,otherwiser_{p_{i}}=\begin{cases}S_{ij},&\text{if }\exists j,x_{ij}=1\\
-\lambda,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
(5)
where λ\lambda is a fixed penalty (or zero) assigned to erroneous or redundant tool calls that fail to match any ground-truth tool call.
Soft Credit Assignment.
To facilitate a smoother optimization landscape and provide more nuanced feedback, we propose a soft relaxation of the assignment problem based on Optimal Transport (OT) Cuturi (2013).
Instead of enforcing a hard binary choice, this method treats the predicted and ground-truth tool sequences as discrete probability distributions and optimizes the cost of transforming one into the other.
The matching matrix 𝒮\mathcal{S} is transformed into a cost matrix 𝒞\mathcal{C} via a decreasing transformation (e.g., Ci​j=−Si​jC_{ij}=-S_{ij}), ensuring that high matching scores translate to minimal transportation costs.
We then compute an optimal transport plan 𝒵\mathcal{Z} that minimizes the total cost:
min⁡∑i,j𝒵⁡Zi​j​Ci​j,s.t. ​𝒵​𝟏n=𝐚,𝒵⊤​𝟏m=𝐛\min_{\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{i,j}Z_{ij}C_{ij},\;\;\;\text{s.t. }\mathcal{Z}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{a},\mathcal{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{m}=\mathbf{b}
(6)
where 𝐚∈ℝm\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{m} and 𝐛∈ℝn\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} are marginal distributions representing the importance weights of predicted and ground-truth tool calls, respectively.
The resulting transport plan 𝒵\mathcal{Z} effectively functions as a probabilistic mapping between predicted and ground-truth interactions.
Given the resulting transport plan, the reward rpir_{p_{i}} for each predicted tool call is then calculated as the weighted sum of its similarities across the ground-truth set:
rp​i=∑jZi​j⋅Si​j.r_{pi}=\sum_{j}Z_{ij}\cdot S_{ij}.
(7)
Unlike the hard version, soft assignment allows a single ground-truth tool call reward to be distributed across multiple predicted turns.
This mechanism ensures that the turn with the highest alignment receives the largest share of credit, while other relevant attempts still receive partial, dense feedback to guide the policy’s improvement.
Turn-Level Reward.
The assignment mechanisms described above yield a specific reward rpir_{p_{i}} for each individual predicted tool call.
However, within the multi-turn TIR framework, a single interaction turn may involve multiple tool calls, whereas policy optimization is typically performed at the turn level.
Therefore, it is necessary to aggregate tool call rewards into a unified turn-level signal.
To this end, we compute the turn-level reward by averaging the rewards of predicted tool calls within the same turn:
rt=1|Pt|​∑pi∈Ptrpi,r_{t}=\frac{1}{|P_{t}|}\sum_{p_{i}\in P_{t}}r_{p_{i}},
(8)
where |Pt||P_{t}| denotes the number of predicted tool calls issued at turn tt.
This averaging strategy normalizes the reward across turns and discourages excessive tool usage, ensuring fair comparison between turns with different numbers of tool calls.
Outcome-Level Reward.
To complement the fine-grained supervision provided by turn-level rewards, we incorporate an outcome-level reward ror^{o} to evaluate the global objective fulfillment of the entire reasoning trajectory.
Specifically, we use the F1 score between the predicted answer a^\hat{a} and the golden answer aa as the outcome-level reward:
ro=2​|a^∩a||a^|+|a|∈[0,1].r^{o}=\frac{2|\hat{a}\cap a|}{|\hat{a}|+|a|}\in[0,1].
(9)
This outcome-level reward serves as a global constraint that complements the turn-level supervision, encouraging the model to not only make correct tool calls at each turn but also remain aligned with successful end-task completion.
3.2 Dual-Level Advantage Estimation
In conventional outcome-supervised RL frameworks like GRPO, all tokens within a sampled rollout are typically assigned a uniform advantage value derived from the final outcome reward.
While this strategy is effective for single-turn or short-horizon tasks, it is fundamentally suboptimal for TIR, where a trajectory consists of multiple interaction turns with highly heterogeneous contributions.
Assigning identical advantages to all tokens fails to distinguish between informative actions that advance task completion and uninformative or redundant tool-use behaviors, resulting in coarse credit assignment and noisy policy optimization.
To address this limitation, we propose a dual-level advantage estimation framework that evaluates model actions from two complementary perspectives:
Trajectory-Level Advantage Estimation.
We first evaluate the overall quality of each rollout within a group of GG sampled trajectories.
For the ii-th rollout, we define the trajectory-level reward RiR_{i} as the sum of its turn- and outcome-level rewards:
Ri=∑t=1Trt(i),R_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}^{(i)},
(10)
where rt(i)r_{t}^{(i)} is the turn-level reward at turn tt of the ii-th rollout.
For the final turn TT, rT(i)r_{T}^{(i)} corresponds to the outcome-level reward rior_{i}^{o} .
Following the GRPO framework, the trajectory-level advantage AigA_{i}^{g} is computed by normalizing the rewards across the group:Aig=Ri−mean​({Rj}j=1G)std​({Rj}j=1G).A_{i}^{g}=\frac{R_{i}-\text{mean}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\text{std}(\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})}.
This advantage reflects the relative performance of the entire reasoning path compared to others generated for the same prompt, providing a global supervisory signal for policy optimization.
Turn-Level Advantage Estimation.
While trajectory-level advantage provides a global performance signal, it assigns identical supervision to all interaction turns within the trajectory.
To further differentiate the contributions of individual turns, we introduce local turn-level advantage estimation, which provides fine-grained supervision aligned with turn-level rewards.
Specifically, to capture the impact of current decisions on future turns, we first compute a discounted cumulative reward Ri,jR_{i,j} for each turn tt in the ii-th rollout:
Ri,t=∑k=tTγk−t​rk(i),R_{i,t}=\sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t}r_{k}^{(i)},
(11)
where γ\gamma is the discount factor.
This formulation encourages the model to account for the long-term consequences of its tool-use behaviors.
To provide fine-grained supervision, we then compute the local turn-level advantage Ai,tlA_{i,t}^{l} by comparing the cumulative reward against its group-level baseline: Ai,tl=Ri,t−mean​({Rj,t}j=1G)std​({Rj,t}j=1G).A_{i,t}^{l}=\frac{R_{i,t}-\text{mean}(\{R_{j,t}\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\text{std}(\{R_{j,t}\}_{j=1}^{G})}.
This term captures fine-grained differences across interaction turns.
This dual-view advantage estimation enables more precise credit assignment for TIR.

4.1 Experimental Setups
Training Dataset.
We train the model on the FTRL dataset Ye et al. (2025b), which comprises over 2,000 automatically constructed tool-use environments with verifiable feedback, covering single-hop, multi-hop, and parallel reasoning scenarios.
Evaluation Benchmarks.
We evaluate MatchTIR on three benchmarks:
FTRL is used for in-domain evaluation, while BFCL Patil et al. (2025) and ToolHop Ye et al. (2025a) are adopted for out-of-domain evaluation to assess generalization.
Details of these benchmarks are shown in Appendix A.
Baselines.
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed MatchTIR, we compare it against comprehensive baselines:
(1) Vanilla: We evaluate the vanilla Qwen3 Yang et al. (2025) models without reinforcement learning.
(2) GRPO Shao et al. (2024): The standard GRPO framework that optimizes the policy solely based on outcome rewards.
(3) ToolRL Qian et al. (2025): ToolRL assigns rewards based on the correctness of tool names, parameter names, and parameter content.
While originally designed for single-turn tool use, we extend it to the multi-turn interaction setting by applying its scoring mechanism at the trajectory level.
The single-turn and multi-turn variants are denoted as ToolRL-S and ToolRL-M, respectively.
(4) FTRL Ye et al. (2025b): FTRL computes rewards using the ratio of successful tool calls to total attempts.
Similar to ToolRL, we include both the single-turn baseline (FTRL-S) and its adaptation to the multi-turn setting (FTRL-M).
Implementation Details.
We adopt Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B as backbone models and optimize them using the GRPO objective implemented in the verl framework Sheng et al. (2025).
All experiments are conducted under the Zero-RL setting, with a batch size of 256 and 16 rollouts sampled per query.
The maximum number of turns is set to 10, the penalty scale λ\lambda is set to 0, and the discount factor γ\gamma is set to 0.9.
The reinforcement learning phase spans 3 epochs, conducted on 8 NVIDIA A800-80G GPUs.
Please refer to Appendix B for more details about hyperparameter configurations.

In contrast, KM provides a rigorous binary signal essential for exact tool usage by enforcing a strict one-to-one mapping.

5 Related Work
5.1 Tool-Integrated Reasoning
Recently, RLVR, such as GRPO-based methods Shao et al. (2024); Yu et al. (2025a); Zheng et al. (2025a), has been widely adopted to enhance TIR by directly optimizing models against task-level feedback Wang et al. (2025c); Xue et al. (2025); Qu et al. (2025b).
Early RL-based approaches rely on rewards derived solely from final outcomes, resulting in sparse and delayed supervision Li et al. (2025); Feng et al. (2025a).
Subsequent methods mitigate this issue by assigning rewards at the trajectory level, providing denser training signals Qian et al. (2025); Zhang et al. (2025a); Zeng et al. (2025b); Wei et al. (2025).
Despite these advances, most existing methods assign a single reward to an entire trajectory, causing all actions to share identical advantage signals regardless of their individual quality.
Such coarse-grained feedback prevents models from distinguishing effective from ineffective tool calls and obscures turn-level contributions in multi-turn scenarios Lightman et al. (2024).
In contrast, our work introduces a turn-level reward assignment and dual-level advantage estimation framework, which enables fine-grained supervision.
5.2 Fine-Grained Credit Assignment
Recent studies have explored turn-level or process-level rewards to perform fine-grained credit assignment Zeng et al. (2025a); Yu et al. (2025b); Yue et al. (2025); Zheng et al. (2025b); Zhang et al. (2025b), which can be broadly categorized into sampling-based and model-based methods.
Sampling-based approaches typically employ Monte Carlo rollouts to approximate turn-level credit Feng et al. (2025b); Tran et al. (2025).
While theoretically unbiased, these approaches suffer from high variance and substantial computational overhead, especially in multi-turn interaction settings.
Model-based methods instead leverage intrinsic reward functions or external reward models to estimate the utility of intermediate steps Wang et al. (2025a); Wang et al. (2025b).
However, the accuracy of these rewards is not guaranteed, as these signals are often model-dependent, difficult to calibrate, and prone to limited generalization across domains.
In this paper, we exploit the structured nature of TIR by formulating turn-level credit assignment as a bipartite matching problem, enabling fine-grained and precise turn-level rewards.
6 Conclusion
In this paper, we propose MatchTIR, a novel framework designed to provide fine-grained supervision for Tool-integrated Reasoning (TIR) via bipartite matching.
By introducing a turn-level reward design and dual-level advantage estimation, our method assigns distinct advantages to individual tokens, effectively distinguishing high-quality tool calls from redundant or uninformative ones.
Extensive experiments on both in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of MatchTIR across various model scales, with particularly pronounced improvements in long-horizon, multi-turn interaction scenarios.
Limitations
Despite the significant performance gains achieved on the Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B model series, our work has certain limitations.
First, due to constrained computational resources, we were unable to conduct experiments on larger-scale models.
We hope that the publication of this work will encourage further exploration of our framework on more massive architectures.
Second, our approach relies on the availability of ground-truth trajectories for turn-level supervision.
This poses a challenge in highly open-ended scenarios such as “deep research” tasks, where ground-truth parameters and reasoning steps are inherently difficult to determine or verify.
Consequently, adapting our method to such problems remains a promising but challenging direction for future research.

https://github.com/bytedance/FTRL The Feedback-Driven Tool-Use Reinforcement Learning (FTRL) Ye et al. (2025b) benchmark is designed for evaluating tool-integrated reasoning under automatically constructed local execution environments.
To mirror the complexity of real-world interactions, FTRL categorizes tool-use scenarios into four distinct levels of difficulty:
∙\bullet Single-Hop: Contains a single query that can be resolved with a one-time tool invocation.
∙\bullet Parallel Single-Hop: Involves multiple independent sub-queries, allowing the agent to invoke multiple tools in parallel to gather information.
∙\bullet Multi-Hop: Consists of a sequence of dependent sub-queries, where the execution of a subsequent tool depends on the output of previous steps.
∙\bullet Parallel Multi-Hop: A hybrid and complex structure that includes both independent and interdependent sub-queries, requiring advanced logical planning and orchestration.
To provide a multidimensional assessment of model performance, FTRL employs the following verifiable metrics:
∙\bullet Solve-P: Measures the precision of the tool invocations. It is defined as the ratio of valid tool calls to the total number of calls made:
Solve-P={qp,ifp>01,ifp=0\text{Solve-P}=\begin{cases}\frac{q}{p},&\text{if}\quad p>0\\
1,&\text{if}\quad p=0\end{cases}
(12)
where pp is the number of tools invoked, qq is the number of sub-questions successfully solved.
∙\bullet Solve-R: Evaluates the completeness of task execution by measuring the proportion of successfully resolved sub-tasks. The formula is:
Solve-R=qn\text{Solve-R}=\frac{q}{n}
(13)
where n is the number of sub-questions in total.

Solve-F1=2⋅Solve-P⋅Solve-RSolve-P+Solve-R\text{Solve-F1}=\frac{2\cdot\text{Solve-P}\cdot\text{Solve-R}}{\text{Solve-P}+\text{Solve-R}}
(14)

Its structured feedback and multi-turn interaction setting make it particularly suitable for studying fine-grained credit assignment and turn-level supervision in tool-integrated reasoning.

The Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) Patil et al. (2025) is a comprehensive and rigorous benchmark designed to evaluate the function-calling capabilities of LLMs across a wide range of scenarios.
To assess the performance of models in complex agentic settings, BFCL V3 and V4 introduce several critical task dimensions:
∙\bullet Multi-Turn:
This dimension evaluates the model’s ability to maintain state and handle dynamic decision-making over multiple conversational turns. It consists of four subsets: Base, which provides standard multi-turn interactions; Missing Parameters and Missing Functions, which test robustness when information is incomplete; and Long-Context, which requires extracting tool information from extensive distractor text.
∙\bullet Agentic:
This dimension focuses on the model’s capacity for autonomous reasoning and interaction within a stateful environment. it includes two subsets: Web Search, requiring real-time information retrieval, and Memory, testing the ability to recall and utilize information from previous interactions.
BFCL employs a multi-layered evaluation strategy to ensure execution rigor:
∙\bullet Multi-turn: Requires passing two per-turn checks for overall success: (1) State-based Evaluation, which verifies backend system state changes, and (2) Response-based Evaluation, which validates the execution path against minimal viable result paths for read-only requests.
∙\bullet Agentic: Employs an exact-match metric isolated to the “answer” field. This prevents false positives from incidental phrasing in unstructured text, ensuring the model commits to a precise and intended response.

In addition to the standard training setup, we provide further clarifications on the implementation of our reward and evaluation mechanisms.
Our turn-level advantage estimation is performed by comparing rollouts within the same group at each specific turn tt. Given that trajectories in a group may have varying lengths, we implement the following logic to ensure robust estimation:
For a given turn tt, the advantage is computed only among the subset of rollouts that actually reach or exceed tt turns.
In scenarios where only a single rollout within a group contains the tt-th turn, we provide a fallback by setting the mean to 0 and the variance to 1 for normalization purposes.

C.1 Analysis on Advantage Estimation
To further investigate the mechanism of turn-level credit assignment, we conduct supplementary experiments using the Qwen3-8B model on the FTRL benchmark.
We compare MatchTIR with an alternative intuition-based approach, referred to here as the Weighted Intra-trajectory Method.
Weighted Intra-trajectory Method.
To further explore the mechanisms of turn-level credit assignment, we investigate two alternative variants that estimate advantages within an individual trajectory (intra-trajectory) rather than across different rollouts (inter-group).
For both variants, we first compute a local turn-level advantage Ai,tl​o​cA_{i,t}^{loc} by treating each turn as an independent sample within the same rollout:
Ai,tl​o​c=Ri,t−mean​({Ri,j}j=1T)std​({R​i,j}j=1T).A_{i,t}^{loc}=\frac{R_{i,t}-\text{mean}(\{R_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{T})}{\text{std}(\{R{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{T})}.
Based on Ai,tl​o​cA_{i,t}^{loc}, we propose two weighting strategies to adjust the global trajectory advantage AigA_{i}^{g}:
∙\bullet Weighted Product: This method uses the local advantage as a scaling factor to modulate the magnitude of the global signal.
The final adjusted advantage A^i,t\hat{A}_{i,t} for each token in turn tt is defined as
A^i,t=(1+0.1⋅sgn​(Aig)⋅Ai,tl​o​c)​Aig\hat{A}_{i,t}=\left(1+0.1\cdot\text{sgn}(A_{i}^{g})\cdot A_{i,t}^{loc}\right)A_{i}^{g},
where sgn​(⋅)\text{sgn}(\cdot) ensures the weighting direction aligns with the trajectory performance.
∙\bullet Weighted Sum: This method directly incorporates the local turn-level signal by adding it to the global advantage:
A^i,t=Aig+Ai,tl​o​c\hat{A}_{i,t}=A_{i}^{g}+A_{i,t}^{loc}
Results and Discussion.
As shown in Table 6, the results indicate that this Weighted Intra-trajectory Method approach yields inferior performance compared to MatchTIR.
We attribute this to the fact that intra-trajectory normalization inherently overlooks the temporal interdependencies between turns.
In multi-turn TIR tasks, the success of a later turn is often contingent upon the precise execution of earlier tool calls.
By solely focusing on the internal variance of a single rollout, the weighted method fails to capture the relative quality of the reasoning path across different potential strategies, whereas our inter-group discounted approach provides a higher-resolution signal for mastering long-horizon interactions.

C.2 Analysis on Cost Matrix Construction
Since the cost matrix CC in our Optimal Transport (OT) framework is derived from the similarity matrix SS, the transformation function applied to SS significantly influences the final turn-level reward assignment.
To evaluate this impact, we compare three distinct construction methods:
∙\bullet Linear Inversion: C=−SC=-S. This maintains the original linear distribution of similarity scores.
∙\bullet Normalized Inversion: C=1−SnormC=1-S_{\text{norm}}, where Snorm=S−min⁡(S)max⁡(S)−min⁡(S)+ϵS_{\text{norm}}=\frac{S-\min(S)}{\max(S)-\min(S)+\epsilon}.
∙\bullet Exponential Transformation: C=−eSC=-e^{S}. This non-linear mapping amplifies the differences between high-similarity pairs and others.
Results and Discussion.
As shown in Table 7, the results indicate that the model performance is relatively insensitive to the choice between Linear Inversion and Normalized Inversion. This consistency suggests that the raw similarity scores generated by our matching module are already well-distributed, and linear re-scaling does not alter the relative matching order.
In contrast, we observe a performance drop with the Exponential Transformation.
We hypothesize that this is primarily due to numerical instability and noise amplification.
The exponential function excessively magnifies minor fluctuations in similarity scores, creating a cost matrix with values spanning several orders of magnitude.
This not only hinders the convergence of the Sinkhorn solver due to precision errors during iterative normalization but also leads to high-variance advantage that destabilize the policy gradient.

C.3 Multi-Turn vs. Expanded Single-Turn
While our main results demonstrate the superiority of multi-turn training, one could argue that this advantage stems from increased computational expenditure during inference and training.
To ensure a fair comparison, we conduct an experiment by decomposing multi-turn trajectories into expanded single-turn training sets.
Specifically, a trajectory consisting of TT turns is decomposed into T−1T-1 independent training samples.
For each sample at turn tt, the model is provided with the conversation history of the preceding tt turns and tasked with predicting the (t+1)(t+1)-th tool-calling action.
This data expansion ensures that the single-turn baseline is exposed to the same volume of state-transition information as the multi-turn approach.
Results and Discussion. As shown in Table 8, while increasing the data volume via decomposition improves performance on the FTRL benchmark, it still falls short of our end-to-end multi-turn optimization.
Interestingly, on the ToolRL benchmark, we observe that increasing data through single-turn decomposition actually leads to a performance decline.
We attribute this to the inherent limitations of the single-turn paradigm: Single-turn models rely heavily on the immediate reward design for each isolated step.
Without the context of the entire trajectory, they struggle to reconcile local actions with long-term goals.
The single-turn format restricts the potential for sophisticated reward and advantage estimation, which requires a holistic view of the interaction.
These findings suggest that end-to-end multi-turn optimization is fundamentally more effective, as it allows the model to capture inter-turn dependencies that simple data expansion in a single-turn setting cannot replicate.
