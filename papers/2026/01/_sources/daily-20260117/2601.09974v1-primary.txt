Abstract
Personalizing Large Language Models typically relies on static retrieval or one-time adaptation, assuming user preferences remain invariant over time.
However, real-world interactions are dynamic, where user interests continuously evolve, posing a challenge for models to adapt to preference drift without catastrophic forgetting.
Standard continual learning approaches often struggle in this context, as they indiscriminately update on noisy interaction streams, failing to distinguish genuine preference shifts from transient contexts.
To address this, we introduce SPRInG, a novel semi-parametric framework designed for effective continual personalization.
During training, SPRInG employs drift-driven selective adaptation, which utilizes a likelihood-based scoring function to identify high-novelty interactions.
This allows the model to selectively update the user-specific adapter on drift signals while preserving hard-to-learn residuals in a replay buffer.
During inference, we apply strict relevance gating and fuse parametric knowledge with retrieved history via logit interpolation.
Experiments on the long-form personalized generation benchmark demonstrate that SPRInG outperforms existing baselines, validating its robustness for real-world continual personalization.11

1 Introduction
Figure 1:
Comparison between static and continual personalization.
(Top) Static personalization assumes time-invariant preferences, using a frozen adapter trained on a fixed history snapshot.
(Bottom) Continual personalization models users as dynamic entities. The framework iteratively updates the adapter at sequential time steps (t=1,2,3,⋯t=1,2,3,\cdots) to capture preference drifts (shifting colors) while maintaining historical context.
Figure 2:
An overview of SPRInG.
Training stage: incoming interactions are scored by comparing likelihoods between the base and adapted models. High-scoring drift samples trigger a parametric update, while samples that remain poorly internalized are stored in the replay buffer through re-evaluation with respect to the updated model.
Inference stage: for a new query, the model computes parametric logits (pparp_{\texttt{par}}) from the adapter and non-parametric logits (pretp_{\texttt{ret}}) via gated retrieval. The final output is generated through logit interpolation, dynamically weighting the two distributions to balance internal knowledge with retrieved context.
While Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate remarkable performance across diverse domains, (Jaech et al., 2024; Comanici et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025), the need for LLM personalization, which adapts outputs to individual or group preferences, is rapidly increasing (Zhang et al., 2024).
Existing LLM personalization methods adopt either non-parametric strategies that leverage retrieved interaction histories or user profiles during prompting (Santurkar et al., 2023; Hwang et al., 2023), or parametric strategies that encode user preferences through parameter adaptation (e.g., training user-specific LoRA adapters (Tan et al., 2024; Bu et al., 2025)).
However, both paradigms assume a time-invariant view of the user, in that a fixed history is repeatedly used for retrieval and parametric models are generally frozen after initial training.
Yet, in reality, user preferences and traits constantly evolve over time, causing existing methods to fail to account for such preference drifts.
To be more specific, non-parametric methods struggle with expanding histories due to weak recency signals and rising costs of storage and retrieval, whereas parametric approaches have expensive updates and a risk of catastrophic forgetting.
This limitation necessitates the development of continual LLM personalization which takes into consideration the changing states of users over time.
Continual learning (CL) (Ke and Liu, 2022; Shi et al., 2024), which aims to update models sequentially with streaming data, offers a promising paradigm for handling evolving user preferences.
Prior CL research has primarily concentrated on mitigating catastrophic forgetting in task-incremental settings, typically relying on clear task boundaries or abrupt distribution shifts to determine when and how to update (Du et al., 2024; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017).
However, continual LLM personalization poses distinct challenges; user interaction streams are inherently continuous and noisy, where preferences drift gradually rather than exhibiting discrete shifts.
The central challenge, therefore, lies in distinguishing genuine preference evolution from transient noise.
In this context, standard CL approaches could be suboptimal due to this discrepancy; for example, transient noise could be considered informative from a CL perspective (e.g., due to high loss or uncertainty).
This necessitates a framework for selective drift detection, ensuring the model adapts to meaningful new traits while robustly filtering out noise.
To bridge this gap, we propose SPRInG (Selective Parametric adaptation and Retrieval-Interpolated Generation), a novel semi-parametric framework designed to continuously adapt to evolving user preferences while preserving important historical contexts.
Our key insight is to address continual personalization across both training and inference stages, jointly utilizing parametric and non-parametric approaches for distinct yet complementary roles.
Specifically, during training, we employ Drift-Driven Selective Adaptation: with a new scoring function to measure sample novelty relative to the current adapter, we fine-tune the model exclusively on interactions that indicate genuine preference drift.
Simultaneously, we construct a residual replay buffer by identifying hard samples that remain challenging even after parametric adjustments, preserving sparse but critical historical nuances that the adapter fails to internalize.
At inference, we apply Selective Retrieval-Interpolated Generation; instead of naive retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), we introduces a strict gating mechanism to filter out irrelevant context and dynamically fuse parametric adapter knowledge with non-parametric replay-buffer evidence through logit-level interpolation.
This hybrid design ensures SPRInG to be both stable over time and responsive to the user’s evolving preferences.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of SPRInG by evaluating personalized long-form text generation tasks (Abstract Generation and Review Writing) from the LongLaMP benchmark (Kumar et al., 2024).
Empirical results show that SPRInG consistently outperforms comprehensive baselines, surpassing the strongest CL method, CAMA (Kim et al., 2024), by a substantial margin of 15.85% in ROUGE-L and standard RAG by 18.12% in Review Writing.
Furthermore, our thorough analyses validate the effectiveness of our design choices, showing a 12.35% gain from drift-driven training, and an improvement up to 22.58% when interpolating parametric and non-parametric knowledge, compared to single-source methods.
Overall, these results validate SPRInG as a robust framework for continual personalization, effectively reconciling the challenges of adapting to evolving preferences while preserving historical stability.
2 Problem Formulation
We formulate the personalized text generation task in a CL setup.
Let a user uu interact with an LLM sequentially over time, producing an interaction history 𝒟u={(qi,ai)}\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{(q_{i},a_{i})\}, where qiq_{i} and aia_{i} denote the query and response for the ii-th interaction, respectively.
We employ a frozen base LLM ℳθ\mathcal{M}_{\theta} equipped with a lightweight user-specific adapter ϕu\phi_{u} to capture user preferences.
We assume that accumulated interactions arrive sequentially over discrete time steps (periods) tt, with the initial history given at t=0t=0.
At each period t∈ℕt\in\mathbb{N}, a new batch of interactions 𝒟u(t)\mathcal{D}_{u}^{(t)} becomes available.
The objective is then to update the user adapter from ϕu(t−1)\phi_{u}^{(t-1)} to ϕu(t)\phi_{u}^{(t)} to reflect the evolving user preferences encoded in 𝒟u(t)\mathcal{D}_{u}^{(t)}, while maintaining a non-parametric retrieval buffer ℬu(t)\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t)} to mitigate forgetting important past information.
We denote the user-adapted model at period tt as ℳu(t)\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)}, which is the frozen base model ℳθ\mathcal{M}_{\theta} equipped with the current user adapter ϕu(t)\phi_{u}^{(t)}.
With the user-adapted model, the generation of response aa for a given query qq is modeled by conditional probability P⁡(a|q;ℳu(t))P(a|q;\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)}).
Standard CL approaches generally update the adapter ϕu(t)\phi_{u}^{(t)} by minimizing the negative log-likelihood over the entire incoming stream 𝒟u(t)\mathcal{D}_{u}^{(t)}.
However, such an indiscriminate update strategy is inappropriate for capturing subtle preference drift.
Since user interactions are often noisy or context-dependent, treating all samples equally could obscure meaningful signals and cause the model to overfit to temporary contexts rather than genuine preference shifts.
To address this, we propose a method that selectively utilizes interactions indicative of true preference drift, thereby achieving effective personalization while filtering out noise.
3 Selective Parametric Adaptation and Retrieval-Interpolated Generation
In this section, we propose SPRInG (Selective Parametric adaptation and Retrieval-Interpolated Generation), which conducts a two-phase process for continual LLM personalization.
First, drift-driven selective adaptation (Sec. 3.1) selectively updates the adapter by prioritizing interactions with high drift scores, capturing genuine preference shifts while retaining critical interactions in a replay buffer.
Second, selective retrieval-interpolated generation (Sec. 3.2) dynamically fuses this parametric knowledge with historical evidence during inference.
This separation ensures that the adapter focuses on learning persistent preference patterns, leaving complex nuances to be handled by the retrieval memory.
See Figure 2 for an overview.
3.1 Training: Drift-driven selective adaptation and residual replay
The training phase of SPRInG is designed to efficiently capture user preferences from the incoming interaction stream through a three-step pipeline.
First, we perform drift selection by comparing likelihoods between the base LLM and the same model equipped with the current adapter to isolate high-novelty interactions.
Next, we execute a parametric update, fine-tuning the user adapter on these identified samples to internalize genuine preference shifts.
Finally, we construct a residual replay buffer by re-evaluating the samples post-update; interactions that remain challenging are stored in the buffer to mitigate forgetting for the inference phase.
The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.
Drift sample selection.
To identify interactions to use for an adapter update, we define a scoring function that quantifies sample novelty with respect to the current adapter, thereby distinguishing genuine preference drift from uninformative noise.
Let pbase=P⁡(a∣q;ℳθ)p_{\texttt{base}}=P(a\mid q;\mathcal{M}_{\theta}) denote the likelihood of the response aa under the base model, and padap(t−1)=P⁡(a∣q;ℳu(t−1))p_{\texttt{adap}}^{(t-1)}=P(a\mid q;\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t-1)}) denote the likelihood under model adapted with the current adapter.
Then, we define drift score 𝒮⁡(q,a)\mathcal{S}(q,a) as follows:
𝒮⁡(q,a)=(−log⁡padap(t−1)pbase⏟Novelty Term)+α​log⁡pbase⏟Quality Term\mathcal{S}(q,a)=(\underbrace{-\log\frac{p_{\texttt{adap}}^{(t-1)}}{p_{\texttt{base}}}}_{\text{Novelty Term}})+\underbrace{\alpha\log p_{\texttt{base}}}_{\text{Quality Term}}
(1)
where α\alpha is a balancing hyperparameter.
We prioritize samples that maximize this score, balancing two critical objectives.
∘\circ
The Novelty Term assigns higher priority to samples with unfamiliar preference patterns.
Thus, if the adapter already outperforms the base model (padap≫pbasep_{\texttt{adap}}\gg p_{\texttt{base}}), the novelty score decreases as the preference is considered already internalized.
∘\circ
The Quality Term filters for linguistic validity, using a high pbasep_{\texttt{base}} to identify well-formed and intelligible samples.
This term acts as a precautionary safeguard against pseudo-novelty, preventing noisy outliers or gibberish from being erroneously selected simply because they are difficult for the adapter to predict.
Finally, we construct the drift set 𝒟drift(t)\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{drift}}^{(t)} by retaining the top p%p\% of the samples:
𝒟drift(t)={(q,a)∈𝒟u(t)∣𝒮⁡(q,a)≥τp}\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{drift}}^{(t)}=\{(q,a)\in\mathcal{D}_{u}^{(t)}\mid\mathcal{S}(q,a)\geq\tau_{p}\}
(2)
where τp\tau_{p} is a score threshold at the pp-th percentile of the current batch 𝒟u(t)\mathcal{D}_{u}^{(t)}.
Parametric adapter update.
We employ Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) to efficiently evolve the user model, which has been demonstrated in the context of LLM personalization (Tan et al., 2024).
For the initial period (t=0t=0), we utilize a pre-trained adapter ϕu(0)\phi_{u}^{(0)} established on the complete interaction history.
For subsequent periods (t>0t>0), we initialize the current adapter with parameters from previous step (ϕu(t)←ϕu(t−1)\phi_{u}^{(t)}\leftarrow\phi_{u}^{(t-1)}) to inherit prior user preferences.
We then fine-tune ϕu(t)\phi_{u}^{(t)} exclusively on the selected drift set 𝒟drift(t)\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{drift}}^{(t)}.
By restricting training to high-novelty samples, we force the adapter to focus solely on acquiring new preference patterns.
This targeted approach not only minimizes computational overhead but also prevents the model from overfitting to well-internalized stationary data.
Residual replay buffer construction.
Since parametric updates favor generalizable patterns, weight-encoded knowledge often misses sparse but critical historical nuances.
To mitigate this, we maintain a non-parametric replay buffer ℬu\mathcal{B}_{u} as a complementary memory, preserving interactions that remain challenging for the updated adapter.
At period tt, we first merge the newly identified drift samples with the existing buffer to form a candidate pool:
𝒞u(t)=𝒟drift(t)∪ℬu(t−1).\mathcal{C}_{u}^{(t)}=\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{drift}}^{(t)}\cup\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t-1)}.
(3)
We then re-evaluate all samples in 𝒞u(t)\mathcal{C}_{u}^{(t)} using the updated adapter ϕu(t)\phi_{u}^{(t)}; we compute the post-update score
𝒮t′​(q,a):=𝒮⁡(q,a,ℳθ,ϕu(t))\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{t}(q,a):=\mathcal{S}(q,a;\mathcal{M}_{\theta},\phi_{u}^{(t)}),
which quantifies the residual difficulty after the parametric update.
This step is crucial because many drift samples that initially appear novel may become well-internalized after the adapter is updated; re-scoring allows to filter such redundant data and find samples that remain poorly predicted.
Finally, we update the replay buffer ℬu(t)\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t)} under a fixed budget NmaxN_{\max}.
When |𝒞u(t)|≤Nmax|\mathcal{C}_{u}^{(t)}|\leq N_{\max}, we directly set ℬu(t)=𝒞u(t)\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t)}=\mathcal{C}_{u}^{(t)} without any filtering.
Otherwise, we employ a Global Highest retention policy to satisfy the budget constraint; we rank the candidates by their post-update scores 𝒮t′\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\prime} in descending order and retain only the top-NmaxN_{\max} samples to form ℬu(t)\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t)} (Eq. 4).
We provide an ablation study comparing this policy with alternative strategies in Appendix A.2.

3.2 Inference: Gated replay-augmented generation and logit interpolation
In the inference stage, SPRInG employs a Token-level Probability Interpolation strategy, which dynamically balances the parametric knowledge of ℳu(t)\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)} with an explicit non-parametric context from the replay buffer ℬu(t)\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t)} at each decoding step.
This inference-time approach effectively addresses the limitation where parametric weights often fail to capture specific recent contexts or nuances that have not yet been fully internalized, whereas standard retrieval risks introducing irrelevant noise.
The overall procedure is presented in Algorithm 2.
Relevance gating mechanism.
Given a test query qq, we first retrieve a candidate set ℛq\mathcal{R}_{q} containing the top-kk relevant interactions from ℬu(t)\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(t)}.
To prevent the detrimental effects of irrelevant retrieved contexts, we introduce a relevance gating mechanism using a threshold σ\sigma.
Specifically, we define 𝐜ret\mathbf{c}_{\texttt{ret}} as the concatenation of gated interactions from ℛq\mathcal{R}_{q}:
𝐜ret=Concat​{h∈ℛq∣Sim​(q,h)>σ}\mathbf{c}_{\texttt{ret}}=\textsc{Concat}\{h\in\mathcal{R}_{q}\mid\textsc{Sim}(q,h)>\sigma\}
(5)
where Sim​(⋅)\textsc{Sim}(\cdot) is a retrieval function.
This mechanism ensures that the model is augmented only when strong historical evidence exists.
If no documents pass, 𝐜ret\mathbf{c}_{\texttt{ret}} remains empty, reverting to purely parametric generation.
Dual-path logit interpolation.
To dynamically balance internalized preferences with retrieved evidence during decoding steps, we employ a logit interpolation strategy between two different paths using the same user-adapted model ℳu(t)\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)}.
∘\circ
Parametric Path takes only the query qq as input of ℳu(t)\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)}, relying solely on encoded knowledge in adapter’s weight to guide generation.
∘\circ
Retrieval-augmented Path inputs the query qq augmented with the retrieved context 𝐜ret\mathbf{c}_{\texttt{ret}}, providing the model with explicit historical evidence.
Specifically, at each decoding step jj, we compute the next-token distributions given the prefix a<ja_{<j}:
ppar\displaystyle p_{\texttt{par}}
=P⁡(aj∣q,a<j;ℳu(t))\displaystyle=P(a_{j}\mid q,a_{<j};\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)})
(6)
pret\displaystyle p_{\texttt{ret}}
=P⁡(aj∣q,𝐜ret,a<j;ℳu(t))\displaystyle=P(a_{j}\mid q,\mathbf{c}_{\texttt{ret}},a_{<j};\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(t)})
(7)
We then fuse these distributions via linear interpolation with a mixing coefficient λ\lambda:
pfin=λ⋅pret+(1−λ)⋅pparp_{\texttt{fin}}=\lambda\cdot p_{\texttt{ret}}+(1-\lambda)\cdot p_{\texttt{par}}
(8)
Finally, the selected token a^j∼pfin\hat{a}_{j}\sim p_{\text{fin}} is appended to the prefix a<ja_{<j} for both paths, ensuring synchronization for the next decoding step.
4 Experiments
4.1 Setups
Datasets and metrics.
We evaluate the effectiveness of SPRInG on the LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024) benchmark, which is designed to assess long-form personalized text generation capabilities.
Specifically, Abstract Generation and Review Writing are selected for their accessible temporal metadata, unlike Topic Writing (no timestamps) and Email Generation (restricted access).
For the Abstract Generation task, we sort samples based on the publication year.
However, in the case of the Review Writing task, the LongLaMP dataset does not contain timestamps; hence, we retrieve the reviewTime by matching samples to the original Amazon Product Review dataset (Ni et al., 2019) and sort them accordingly.
Based on this temporal data, we filter users based on their data volume to ensure sufficient history for longitudinal analysis, resulting in a final set of 100 users.
These users are further categorized into Small and Large groups to evaluate performance across different interaction scales.
To simulate a realistic continual personalization scenario, we construct a temporal stream for each user by partitioning it into five sequential periods, denoted as Period 0–4.
In this setup, Period 0 serves as the initial data for user profiling, while Periods 1–4 constitute the stream where new data is sequentially observed.
Within each period, we implement a split where the earliest 90% of samples are utilized for the training, and the most recent 10% are reserved as the test set.
To evaluate performance, we employ ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L to measure content fidelity and style alignment with the ground truth.
We present the results for each individual period as well as the average performance (Period Avg) to show the overall trends.

Baselines.
We compare SPRInG against several baselines categorized as follows: (1) Base – generating responses using the vanilla LLM without any personalization or fine-tuning.
For standard personalization baselines (2-4), we evaluate two setups: each (using only current period data) and accum (using cumulative data from period 0 to current period).
These include:
(2) RAG (Hwang et al., 2023) – augmenting input with retrieved history from the respective corpus;
(3) PAG (Richardson et al., 2023) – utilizing summarized user profiles generated from the corresponding data scope;
and (4) OPPU (Tan et al., 2024) – fine-tuning user-specific LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) either locally on the current period or sequentially on the cumulative stream.
We also include continual learning baselines:
(5) CAMA (Kim et al., 2024) – a replay-based method that selectively buffers high-confidence samples exceeding the previous period’s average confidence and mixes them with historical data for training;
(6) SAR (Seo et al., 2025) – a method that prioritizes replay samples from under-represented clusters based on effective use-frequency to mitigate overfitting to dominant patterns;
(7) O-LoRA (Wang et al., 2023) – a method that projects new parameter updates onto a subspace orthogonal to previous adapters to reduce interference;
and (8) EWC-LoRA (Xiang et al., 2023) – a method that applies elastic weight consolidation with Fisher Information to penalize drastic changes in parameters critical for prior periods.
Implementation details.
In our experiments, we utilize Gemma-3-4B-IT (Team et al., 2025) as the backbone LLM.
For training, we employ the AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a weight decay of 0.01 and a learning rate of 1×10−41\times 10^{-4}, utilizing a linear decay scheduler with a warmup ratio of 0.1.
We use a per-device batch size of 4, train for 2 epochs per period, and truncate the maximum sequence length to 768 tokens.
For LoRA, we target the query and value projection layers with a rank r=8r=8, scaling factor α=8\alpha=8, dropout of 0.05, and no bias.
During inference, we ensure deterministic outputs using greedy decoding with a repetition penalty of 1.2, setting the maximum generated tokens to 600.
Regarding the hyperparameters for SPRInG, we set the balancing term α\alpha in the drift score function (Eq. 1) to 1.0 and construct the drift set by retaining the top 30% (p=30p=30) of samples.
For the residual replay buffer, we maintain a maximum budget of Nmax=50N_{\max}=50 samples per user.
During inference, we retrieve k=2k=2 documents using BM25 (Robertson et al., 2004) as Sim and apply a relevance gating threshold σ\sigma, defined as the task-specific third quartile (Q3) of the retrieval score distribution aggregated across all users and periods, to filter out less relevant contexts.
Finally, we set the mixing coefficient λ\lambda for logit interpolation (Eq. 8) to 0.5 to balance the parametric and non-parametric distributions equally.

Table 1 presents the evaluation results of SPRInG against baselines on Abstract Generation and Review Writing.
First, SPRInG consistently achieves the best performance across all evaluation periods for both tasks, with gains both in ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L metrics.
To be specific, in Abstract Generation, our method achieves relative improvements of 3.86% in ROUGE-1 and 15.85% in ROUGE-L over the strongest CL baseline.
Similarly, in the Review Writing task, SPRInG maintains a lead over existing competitive approaches, achieving improvement of 4.49% in ROUGE-1 and 13.60% in ROUGE-L.
These results indicate that our proposed approach effectively balances plasticity and stability, delivering substantial performance gains over the strongest existing methods.
Furthermore, we observe that standard personalization approaches (e.g., RAG, PAG) can be ineffective or even detrimental in certain contexts.
Notably, in the Review Writing task, both the Each and Accum variants of RAG and PAG fail to surpass the non-personalized Base model.
For instance, RAG each results in an 8.60% drop in ROUGE-1 compared to the Base model, indicating a vulnerability to noisy or irrelevant context.
However, SPRInG overcomes this limitation, demonstrating an 18.12% relative improvement over RAG each.
This suggests that SPRInG selectively leverages beneficial context while filtering irrelevant history.
Finally, CL baselines show greater stability than the naive accumulation strategy.
We observe a general trend where replay-based approaches (e.g., CAMA, SAR) tend to yield robust performance, slightly outperforming or matching regularization-based methods like O-LoRA.
However, even these strong baselines fall short of SPRInG.
This limitation likely stems from treating historical samples uniformly or relying on fixed heuristics, which are not designed to prioritize drift-relevant update.
In contrast, SPRInG achieves superior results by explicitly targeting and adapting to meaningful preference drifts, allowing for more efficient and effective personalization.

5 Related Works
LLM personalization.
LLM personalization aligns model behaviors with individual preferences (Zhang et al., 2024), broadly categorized into non-parametric and parametric approaches.
Non-parametric methods condition generation on user context via retrieval of historical data (Hwang et al., 2023) or summarized profiles (Richardson et al., 2023).
While interpretable and easy to deploy, their effectiveness relies on retrieval quality and context window constraints.
Conversely, parametric methods directly encode preferences by fine-tuning user-specific parameters, often employing efficient adapters like LoRA (Tan et al., 2024) or hierarchical structures (Zhang et al., 2025).
However, these approaches primarily focus on static optimization, overlooking the temporal evolution of user traits required for continual personalization.
Continual learning.
Continual learning (CL) addresses the challenge of sequentially updating models while mitigating catastrophic forgetting (Ke and Liu, 2022; Shi et al., 2024).
Research typically focuses on two complementary strategies: regularization and replay.
Regularization methods constrain the optimization trajectory to preserve prior knowledge, such as applying Fisher-weighted penalties (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) or enforcing orthogonality in subspace updates (Wang et al., 2023).
Conversely, replay-based methods maintain a buffer to interleave past examples with current streams.
To maximize efficiency, recent work prioritizes selective data retention over uniform sampling (Rolnick et al., 2019), utilizing metrics like interference (Aljundi et al., 2019), confidence scores (Kim et al., 2024), activation magnitudes (Du et al., 2024), or spaced repetition heuristics (M’hamdi and May, 2024) to identify the most informative samples.
6 Conclusion
In this paper, we propose SPRInG, a semi-parametric framework that accounts for the challenges arising from evolving user preferences in continual LLM personalization.
Our method addresses the limitations of existing CL and retrieval-based approaches by (1) implementing a drift-driven selective adaptation that isolates high-novelty interactions to update user-specific adapters, and (2) employing a gated retrieval-interpolated generation that dynamically balances parametric knowledge with historical evidence at the logit level.
Through extensive experiments, we show that SPRInG effectively captures genuine preference drifts while robustly filtering out transient noise.
Collectively, our findings underscore the importance of distinguishing genuine preference drift from transient contexts, and establish a scalable path for deploying responsive, long-term personalized LLMs in real-world scenarios.
Limitation and Future Work
While the proposed approach yields substantial gains, it comes with practical trade-offs that offer directions for future research.
First, the dual-path logit interpolation strategy introduces inference latency and cost overhead, as it requires two forward passes per decoding step.
However, this is partially offset by our selective training strategy, which updates parameters on only 30% of the data, reducing the overall training cost compared to exhaustive retraining.
To further optimize inference efficiency, future work could explore adaptive interpolation strategies, such as terminating the dual-path process mid-generation or reverting to a single parametric path once the divergence between the two distributions falls below a certain threshold.
Next, the reliability of the drift score may be challenged by transient noise or abrupt topic shifts (false drift) that do not reflect persistent preference evolution.
In our framework, we reduce the impact of such noise by restricting updates to a small, high-novelty subset and preserving hard-but-informative cases in a bounded residual buffer, while inference-time relevance gating additionally prevents noisy retrieved histories from interfering with generation.
Future research could enhance this by adopting more sophisticated retrieval and gating frameworks, such as dense vector-based retrieval or cross-encoder re-ranking, to more precisely isolate semantically relevant evidence from diverse interaction streams.
Lastly, scaling user-specific adapters poses an infrastructure challenge as the user base grows.
Nevertheless, the parameter-efficient nature of LoRA and modern multi-adapter serving techniques make this approach increasingly viable for large-scale deployment.
As a potential extension, exploring hierarchical or shared adapter architectures that can group users with similar preference trajectories could alleviate storage burdens while facilitating cross-user knowledge transfer (Tan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025).
Broader Impact and Ethical Implications
The deployment of SPRInG for continual personalization can improve long-term user experience and reduce wasteful computation by avoiding indiscriminate retraining or unfiltered retrieval, thereby enabling more resource-efficient adaptation.
However, strong personalization may reinforce biases and create echo chambers by repeatedly optimizing for a user’s current viewpoint; practical deployments should include mechanisms that promote diversity and provide transparent user controls (e.g., reset or edit preferences).
In addition, responsive adaptation raises safety risks, including preference poisoning attacks and potential drift toward harmful objectives.
Future systems should therefore integrate safety-aware filtering and anomaly detection to prevent malicious or unsafe signals from being incorporated into personalization updates.

A.2 Replay buffer retention policies
To effectively manage the limited memory of the replay buffer, we investigate four distinct retention policies.
For strategies requiring semantic awareness, we generate embeddings using Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) with the all-mpnet-base-v2, which provides a robust balance between encoding speed and semantic accuracy.
We then group samples into KK clusters using the KK-Means algorithm, where the optimal KK is determined dynamically by maximizing the Silhouette Score within the range [2,10][2,10].
∘\circ
Global Highest: Prioritizes absolute difficulty.
We sort all candidates in 𝒞u(t)\mathcal{C}_{u}^{(t)} by their scores 𝒮′\mathcal{S^{\prime}} and retain the top-NmaxN_{\max} samples.
∘\circ
Cluster-wise: Prioritizes "hard topics."
We calculate the average 𝒮′\mathcal{S^{\prime}} score for each cluster and fill the buffer by selecting entire clusters with the highest averages.
If the last selected cluster exceeds the remaining capacity, we select samples within it by highest score.
∘\circ
Cluster Round: Balances diversity with difficulty.
We iteratively select the single hardest sample (highest 𝒮′\mathcal{S^{\prime}}) from each cluster in a round-robin fashion until the capacity NmaxN_{\max} is reached.
This ensures a uniform representation of all semantic groups (hard and soft topics alike).
∘\circ
Random: A baseline strategy that uniformly samples NmaxN_{\max} interactions from the candidate pool without regard to difficulty or semantics.

Appendix B Datasets
We conduct our xperiments on LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024) benchmark.
In this section, we provide a detailed description of the datasets used in our experiments, the preprocessing transformations applied to align them with our experimental setup, and their corresponding descriptive statistics.
B.1 Dataset specification
Abstract Generation.
This task assesses the ability of a model to generate scientific abstracts given paper titles and keywords.
The user profile consists of the researcher’s previous publications, which the model must leverage to personalize the academic tone, logical structure, and domain-specific vocabulary unique to each author.
We follow the User Setting in LongLaMP, where each sample is structured such that both the profile and the target output belong to the same author.
To adapt this to our setup, we first sort each researcher’s publication history in reverse chronological order based on the publication year.
The history is then partitioned into five sequential periods.
Within each period, we designate the most recent 10% of publications as test queries (input: title, output: abstract) and the remaining 90% as the user’s historical profile.
To ensure a sufficiently long-term interaction history for drift analysis, we filter for users whose total profile size exceeds 500 publications.
Review Writing.
This task involves drafting a detailed product review from a product’s name and description, alongside a summary of the user’s review.
The profile includes a user’s past reviews and ratings, requiring the model to follow the user’s subjective perspective, rating tendencies, and unique writing styles when expressing opinions.
Consistent with the User Setting, all historical interactions are mapped to a specific reviewer ID.
We follow a similar temporal splitting strategy, dividing the history into five chronological periods.
For this task, we choose users with a total profile size exceeding 300 reviews to focus on those with rich historical data.
B.2 Dataset statistics
User categorization criteria.
We categorize users based on their total number of historical interactions.
To ensure a distinct contrast between the two groups, we utilize the 50th and 80th percentiles of the profile size distribution within each task as thresholds:
∘\circ
Small Interactions: Users within the bottom 50% of the interaction frequency, representing cold-start or low-engagement cases.
∘\circ
Large Interactions: Users within the top 20% of the interaction frequency, representing users with extensive behavioral data.
We randomly sampled 50 users from each category for our main experiments to maintain computational efficiency.
Descriptive statistics.
Table 5 presents the profile size statistics for each task and group.
In Abstract Generation, where only users with more than 500 interactions are included, the Large group exhibits a clearly higher mean profile size, testing the model’s ability to handle long-term shift.
In Review Writing, the Small group reflects data sparsity with a minimum of 300 reviews, while we need to deal with a challenge for noise management for the Large group.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) (Hwang et al., 2023) is a strategy that retrieves the top-kk relevant records from the user’s history as in-context examples. In our setup, we utilize the BM25Okapi implementation from the rank_bm25 library to retrieve the top-4 interactions. We consider two variations: RAG each, which restricts the retrieval pool to the history within the same period tt as the test query, and RAG accum, which performs retrieval from the cumulative history from periods 00 to tt.
⊞\boxplus
PAG (Profile-Augmented Generation) (Richardson et al., 2023) is a method that utilizes a generated user profile as context, following the profiling strategy of prior work (Tan et al., 2024). For each user, we construct a profile by randomly sampling k=10k=10 historical interactions and summarizing them using Gemma-3-4B-IT. Similar to RAG, we evaluate two settings: PAG each, where the 10 samples are drawn exclusively from the history within the current period tt, and PAG accum, where the 10 samples are selected uniformly from the cumulative history across periods 00 to tt.
⊞\boxplus
OPPU (One PEFT per User) (Tan et al., 2024) originally trains individual user LoRA adapters starting from a Task Adapted Model (TAM). In our implementation, we adapt this method by directly training individual LoRA adapters for each user from the base model, bypassing the intermediate TAM stage to focus on direct user-specific optimization. We evaluate two training settings: OPPU each, which fine-tunes the adapter only on the history of the current period tt, and OPPU accum, which utilizes the cumulative history from periods 00 to tt for training.
■\blacksquare
CAMA (Confidence-Aware Memory Augmentation) (Kim et al., 2024) is a replay-based CL strategy that filters new samples according to the model’s self-confidence. At the start of each new period, the model calculates its average confidence level using samples from the previous period. For new incoming data, only those samples that exceed this average confidence threshold are selected for fine-tuning. These high-confidence samples are integrated with a subset of past interactions from the replay buffer, ensuring that the adapter focuses on reliable information while maintaining previously established user patterns.
■\blacksquare
SAR (Similarity-Aware Replay) (Seo et al., 2025) optimizes the replay process by maintaining a balanced representation of the user’s behavioral space. This method clusters user interactions in the hidden representation space and tracks a use-frequency score for each sample. To prevent the model from overfitting to repetitive patterns, SAR assigns higher sampling probabilities to interactions that are from under-represented clusters or have been less frequently used in training. By accounting for similarity and usage history, it ensures that the replay buffer provides a diverse and comprehensive coverage of the user’s history.
■\blacksquare
O-LoRA (Orthogonal LoRA) (Wang et al., 2023) mitigates catastrophic forgetting by constraining learning updates for different periods to mutually orthogonal subspaces. When training on a new period, the model projects the weight updates onto the orthogonal complement of the space utilized in previous stages. This restriction prevents new knowledge from distorting the previously learned weights, allowing the model to accumulate information across multiple periods without degrading performance on earlier tasks.
■\blacksquare
EWC-LoRA (Elastic Weight Consolidation for LoRA) (Xiang et al., 2023) is a method that preserves past knowledge by penalizing changes to parameters that were critical for previous learning stages. After each period, the model estimates the importance of each LoRA parameter based on its contribution to the past data distribution. During the training of a new period, a regularization penalty term is added to the loss function, which restricts the movement of these important parameters. This elastic constraint forces the model to learn new information primarily through less critical parameters, thereby consolidating user-specific knowledge acquired in earlier stages.
