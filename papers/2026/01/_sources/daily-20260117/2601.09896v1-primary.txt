1. Introduction
Although artistic taste is deeply subjective and highly political (Bourdieu, 1984), AI researchers often attempt to quantify the aesthetic quality of media (Tjandra et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). While aesthetic quality assessment (AQA) has been a topic of research in the computer vision community for decades (Datta et al., 2006; Murray et al., 2012), this task is particularly important to examine in light of the rapid growth in visual generative AI. Not only are AQA models used to curate the training data for visual generative AI models (Chen et al., 2024), but they are also used to assess the quality of AI-generated images (Gao and Liu, 2024). As generative AI models are trained on datasets scraped from across the web, researchers rely on computational methods to filter out “low-quality” data. Prior work has called attention to the myriad gender and cultural biases in common dataset filtering techniques—such as blocklists (Dodge et al., 2021) and image-text similarity metrics (Hong et al., 2024)—but the consequences of aesthetic filtering has yet to be explored in detail.
In this work, we investigate an AQA model—the LAION-Aesthetics Predictor (LAP)—that has been widely influential in the visual generative AI research and commercial development. For example, LAP was used to curate the open-source LAION-Aesthetics Dataset from which Stability AI pre-trained the earliest versions of Stable Diffusion (Schuhmann, 2022). Additionally, LAP is often used to evaluate the quality of AI-generated images (Ding et al., 2024; Fong and See, 2024; Gao and Liu, 2024; He et al., 2023). Despite its far-reaching impact, the LAP itself has yet to be explored in detail.
Due to the subjectivity of aesthetic evaluation, we first explored (RQ1) what the LAP model determines to be a “high-quality” image using three datasets. First, we examined the impact of aesthetic filtering on the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset (\sim1.2B images), which was curated using LAP. We find that the LAP disproportionally filters in images with captions mentioning women, while filtering out images with captions mentioning men or LGBTQ+ people. Then, we used LAP to score \sim330k images across two art datasets, finding the model rates realistic images of landscapes, cityscapes, and portraits from western and Japanese artists most highly. In other words, the algorithmic gaze of LAP reproduces the male gaze within western art history (Berger, 2008). In order to understand where these biases may have originated, we performed a digital ethnography of public materials to examine (RQ2) how was LAP developed. We find that the development of LAP reflects the biases we found in our audits, such as the aesthetic scores used to train LAP primarily coming from English-speaking photographers and western AI-enthusiasts.
In light of our findings, we call on AI researchers to contend with aesthetics as a site of cultural representation. At the same time, we caution that greater “inclusion” in visual generative AI training data risks exacerbating the harms perpetuated by these technologies. In order to create more inclusive visual AI models, we call on developers to shift away from prescriptive, universalist measures of “aesthetics” toward more descriptive, pluralistic evaluations. Finally, we discuss how future FAccT researchers might also combine audits and ethnographies of algorithmic systems.
2. Background & Related Work
2.1. Background on LAION-Aesthetics
LAION (Large-Scale Artificial Intelligence Open Network) is a non-profit founded in 2021 by Christoph Schuhmann, a German high school teacher, with the goal of creating open-source datasets and machine learning models (Newman and Cantrill, 2023). Despite its humble origins, LAION has highly influenced generative image model development. Stability AI researchers used the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset to pre-train the earliest versions of Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022). Unfortunately, the extent to which contemporary generative image models rely on the LAION datasets can be challenging to asses because developers have become cautious of disclosing their training data. While Stability AI shared that early versions of Stable Diffusion were trained using LAION datasets, the model card for their 2024 model Stable Diffusion 3.5-Large vaguely says it was “trained on a wide variety of data, including synthetic data and filtered publicly available data.” 11
1
https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-diffusion-3.5-large This secrecy may be due to the increased scrutiny faced by LAION and generative AI developers (Reisner, 2023; Reisner and Gilbertson, 2024), which we will describe below.
LAION has faced substantial criticism from both AI researchers and artists. LAION researchers compiled the 5.86 billion image-text pairs of the LAION-5B dtaset by extracting image links and alternative text descriptions from the IMG HTML tags of websites archived by the Common Crawl, a US-based non-profit that has scraped publicly available websites since 2008 (Reisner, 2025; Baack, 2024b). Due to LAION researchers’ indiscriminate data collection practices, researchers found that LAION-5B contained over a thousand images of child sexual abuse material (CSAM) (Thiel, 2023). In addition to containing CSAM, LAION-5B contains millions of copyrighted images, leading to numerous lawsuits against LAION (Filikhina and Elsaß, 2025) and Stability AI (Tobin, 2025). Furthermore, Birhane et al. found that the image descriptions in LAION-400M and the English language subset of LAION-5B (LAION-2B-en) often contain hate speech (Birhane et al., 2023).
Although prior research has examined biases in LAION datasets (Birhane et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024), the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset and the LAION-Aesthetics Predictor (LAP) model used to curate this dataset are underexplored. Not only is the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset widely used to pre-train and fine-tune visual generative AI models, but the LAP model used to to curate this dataset has also been used to curate internal corporate datasets (Chen et al., 2024) and to evaluate the performance of visual generative AI models (Ding et al., 2024; Fong and See, 2024; Gao and Liu, 2024; He et al., 2023). Although some humanistic research has critiqued how LAP was trained (Itävuori, 2025; Vianna, 2025), researchers have not yet empirically investigated what this image quality assessment model measures in practice. In doing so, we contribute both to scholarship on art and AI as well as, more broadly, AI evaluation and alignment practices. In the following sections, we briefly describe each of these contexts in greater detail.
2.2. AI Evaluation & Alignment
AI researchers are increasingly critiquing the measurements used to train and evaluate AI models. For example, benchmarks often claim to measure concepts with no agreed upon definition, such as “intelligence” (Bean et al., 2025). Researchers have also criticized the notion of universal “gold standards” in data annotation (Wang et al., 2024). While researchers often treat annotator disagreement as noise to be filtered out, disagreements can stem from annotators’ differing lived experiences (Wang et al., 2024; Sap et al., 2022). In response, researchers have called for distinguishing between datasets intended to represent different beliefs or encode one specific belief (Röttger et al., 2022). AI alignment research focuses on normative evaluations where people can reasonably disagree about how a model should behave. For instance, LLMs tend to reflect social values more commonly held in western countries according to national value surveys (Tao et al., 2024). At the same time, methodology can flatten intra-cultural differences (AlKhamissi et al., 2025). Instead of choosing one set of values to reflect, researchers have increasingly called for designing AI systems more pluralistically (Sorensen et al., 2024a; Sorensen et al., 2024b). In this work, we contend with “aesthetics” as an underexamined axis of alignment.
Prior work has found that design of visual generative AI models often harms marginalized communities. Text-to-image (T2I) models often stereotype or poorly represent women (Bianchi et al., 2023), LGBTQ+ people (Ungless et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2025), people of color (Wolfe and Caliskan, 2022), and non-western cultures (Fu et al., 2024; Mim et al., 2024; Qadri et al., 2025; Yerukola et al., 2025). T2I models made by western tech companies tend to perform worse in languages other than English, such as Farsi (Qadri et al., 2025) or Bangla (Mim et al., 2024). This linguistic bias can also be seen in a dataset we examine in this study: the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset is restricted to images with English captions (Schuhmann, 2022). HCI researchers have found that artists often enjoy the glitches and strangeness of visual generative AI models (Chang et al., 2023), leading some to critique the stylistic bias toward realism in newer models (Taylor et al., 2025). In light of this concern, Taylor et al. have called for future research “examining measurements of aesthetic quality in popular datasets” (Taylor et al., 2025), which we take up in this work.
While the artistic consequences of aesthetic evaluation are underexamined, aesthetic quality assessment (AQA) has been a subject of research in the computer vision community for decades (Murray et al., 2012; Datta et al., 2006). In this work, researchers formulate AQA as a regression problem predicting a numerical aesthetic score. At the same time, this de-contextualized measurement of aesthetic quality has been critiqued (Goree et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). Goree et al. (2025) highlight value misalignments between various AQA models and human-preferences through the development of an aesthetic evaluation mobile camera application. Like ourselves, Li et al. (2025) recently advocated for investigating aesthetic biases as an underexamined avenue of representational harms in mulitmodal LLMS. However, the authors conceptualize aesthetic biases as a distributional alignment problem wherein aesthetic evaluation models may privilege the values of one demographic group over others, specially studying age, gender and education. We extend this work by examining the broader cultural consequences of AQA on data curation and model evaluation, grounding the task within art history.
3. Auditing LAION-Aesthetics Predictor Model
3.1. Audit Methodology
We audited LAP using three datasets. First, we examine the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset that LAP was designed to curate from LAION-5B. We then audited LAP using two art datasets with robust metadata on the artists, cultures, styles, and mediums associated with each image. The first dataset consists of 249,351 public-domain images from the Metropolitan Museum of Art (MET). 22
2
https://www.metmuseum.org/hubs/open-access Located in New York City, the MET is the fourth largest museum in the world and its collection spans over 5,000 years (Tapia, 2021). While the MET dataset contains images from a wide breadth of art across various cultures and time periods, few images are of modern or contemporary art (only 865 images). In response, we decided to evaluate LAP on the WikiArt dataset (Saleh and Elgammal, 2015) of 81,444 images of visual art by 129 primarily modern (mid-1800s to mid-1900s) artists. WikiArt is a community edited visual art encyclopedia commonly studied in digital humanities research (Mohamed et al., 2022; Fu and Razmjooy, 2025).
Across each datasets, our analyses focus on understanding differences between images rated above and below an aesthetics score threshold of 6.5. We chose this threshold because computer vision researchers often use images scored above 6.5 by LAP to represent high-quality images. The LAION-Aesthetics Dataset was released in multiple subsets based on different aesthetic thresholds, with images scored 4.5+ (\sim 1.2B images) being the lowest rated dataset and 6.5+ (\sim 625k images) being the highest rated. The LAD 6.5+ is widely used by computer vision researchers to fine-tune generative image models (Guo et al., 2024; Castells et al., 2024), evaluate new training methods (Luo et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2024), and create new datasets (Tudosiu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a). Additionally, Ding et al. measure the effectiveness of data poisoning attacks on text-to-image models by defining "generation aesthetics" as the "% of images with [LAP] aesthetics > 6.5" (Ding et al., 2024).
3.2. Findings: The LAION-Aesthetics Dataset
LAD consists of all images from LAION-5B with English captions that LAP rated at least 4.5. In LAD, images are not stored directly. Rather, the dataset contains URLs to images hosted on websites across the internet. Most relevant for our findings, the metadata for each image in LAD contains its LAP predicted aesthetic score and a caption derived from the scraped image’s alternative text. We find that LAP rates images from independent visual artists and photographers highly. Also, images with captions mentioning women, Hindus, and Christians are more likely to be included in LAP 6.5+, while images with captions mentioning men, Jews, or Muslims are more likely to be filtered out.

3.2.1. The LAION-Aesthetics Predictor highly rates images from websites used by independent visual artists and photographers
We find substantial differences between the URLs of images below and above the 6.5 aesthetic threshold. In LAD 4.5+, the top 25 most common domains account for about 36% of all the images in the dataset. These domains are primarily from e-commerce websites (e.g., Shopify), stock image sites (e.g., Getty Images), and web hosts (e.g., Wordpress). For LAD 6.5+, the top 25 image domains account for proximately 52% of the dataset, meaning a majority of the images in this dataset come from the handful of websites in Figure 1. Of these 25 domains, 13 were also in the top 25 domains for LAD 4.5+. However, the other domains are primarily from smaller websites where independent visual artists (e.g., Redbubble, DeviantArt) and photographers (e.g., SmugMug, Flikr, 500px) go to share or sell their work. Additionally, the 25th most common source of images in LAD 6.5 are from a small nature photography agency, robertharding.com. This indicates that LAP may rate photographic images more highly.
3.2.2. The LAION-Aesthetics Predictor is more likely to rate images with descriptions mentioning woman \geq 6.5, while those mentioning men or LGBTQ+ people are more likely to be filtered out.
To understand the content of images in LAD, we compared the frequencies of words in image captions above and below the 6.5 aesthetics score threshold. Similar to Dodge et al.’s prior audits of generative AI dataset filtering (Dodge et al., 2021), we computed the pointwise mutual information (PMI) (Church and Hanks, 1990) of a regular expression (regex) appearing in the 6.5+ subset of images in LAD. At a high level, PMI in this case measures the the probability of observing a search term in the description for an image rated 6.5+ relative to chance. Therefore, a PMI much greater than 0 suggests the search term appears more often than by chance and a PMI much less than 0 suggests the regex occurs less often than expected. Building on identity-related regexes used in prior audits (Dodge et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2024), we computed the PMI of the regexes in Figure 2 appearing in LAD 6.5+ image descriptions.
We find that images with descriptions mentioning women are more likely to be included in LAD 6.5+, while images mentioning men or LGBTQ+ communities are more likely to be excluded. This finding echoes scholarship on the heteronormative, male gaze in western art (Mulvey, 1975; Berger, 2008). According to Berger (2008) “Women [in western art] are depicted in a quite different way from men — not because the feminine is different from the masculine — but because the ideal spectator is always assumed to be male and the image of the woman is designed to flatter him.”
We also find that images with descriptions mentioning Hindu, Buddhist, or Christian communities are more likely to be included in LAD 6.5+, while images with descriptions mentioning Jews or Muslims are more likely to be filtered out. Additionally, images mentioning Asian or African communities are more likely to be included in LAD 6.5+ than those mentioning Latinx/e, Caucasian, or European communities. This may suggest that LAP more highly rates images of Asian and African communities than European communities. Alternatively, images containing Caucasians or Europeans may not mention this detail in their captions because whiteness is an unmarked racial category (Brekhus, 1998). Understanding the cultural implications of aesthetic evaluation requires considering more than the representation of identity groups (Taylor et al., 2025). 3.3. Findings: The Metropolitan Museum of Art Dataset
To examine how the LAP evaluates art across different mediums and cultures, we scored the 249,351 images from the MET dataset using LAP. We find LAP rates two-dimensional art by western or Japanese artists most highly.
3.3.1. The LAION-Aesthetics Predictor rates western art more highly than art from the Majority World
As seen in Table 1, the departments with images scored \geq 6.5 are primarily European Paintings, Photographs and Drawing & Prints as well as art from Europe, Asia, and America. On the other hand, African, Native American, Oceanian, Egyptian, Islamic, Ancient West Asian, and Greek & Roman Art. This may be partially because the LAP privileges figurative, two-dimensional art, which would also explain why more abstract art (Modern & Contemporary Art) and physical artifacts (European Sculpture & Decorative Arts) scored lower.
3.3.2. The LAION-Aesthetics Predictor rates paintings and photographs by western or Japanese artists most highly.
Across the 249,351 images corresponding to 37,878 unique mediums and 14,946 known artists in the MET dataset, only 177 images across 35 mediums and 99 known artists were rated as \geq 6.5 by LAP. After manually combining similar mediums (e.g., ‘watercolor’ and ‘watercolor on paper’ ), we found that the MET images rated \geq 6.5 come from only 5 mediums: 72 oil paintings, 60 Japanese woodblock prints, 26 watercolor paintings, 15 photographs, 2 drawings, 1 piece of stained glass, and 1 tempera painting (a predecessor to oil paint). A total of 14 artists had more than one piece rated \geq 6.5, accounting for 88 (50%) of the 177 top rated images.
A manual inspection indicates that images rated \geq 6.5 are typically two-dimensional realistic representations of landscapes (e.g., paintings of mountains) or portraits (e.g., photographs or paintings of people). This can also be seen in the artists with multiple images in the top 177. The top two artists with the most images rated \geq 6.5, Katsushika Hokusai (32) and Utagawa Hiroshige (23), are mid-19th century ukiyo-e woodblock print artists who focus on Japanese landscapes (Kafū et al., 2012). Likewise, the artists with the third and sixth most images rated \geq 6.5 are the landscape painters William Trost Richards (6) and Albert Bierstadt (3). The artists with the fourth (Southworth and Hawes, 4) and fifth (Suzuki Shin’ichi, 4) most images in the top 177 are portrait photographers. While the MET dataset contains a broad range of art across cultures and mediums, those that LAP rated \geq 6.5 were mostly paintings or photographs by western or Japanese artists. 3.4. Findings: The WikiArt Dataset
The WikiArt dataset is made of 81,444 pieces of visual art from 128 known artists (excluding unknown artists), primarily western artists from the mid-1800s to mid-1900s. Each piece is assigned one of 27 styles across 10 known genres (excluding unknown genres). Genres describe the content of a piece (e.g., landscape or portrait) meanwhile style describes the piece’s form (e.g., realism or impressionism).
3.4.1. The LAION-Aesthetics Predictor more highly rates realistic images of cityscapes, landscapes, and portraits, while less realistic art was rated lower.
As seen in Table 2, we find that LAP highly rates visual art of Cityscapes, Landscapes, and Portraits. These three genres account for 39% of all images in WikiArt, but these genres make up 73% of all images rated \geq 6.5. Likewise, we found that more realistic and representational styles, such as Realism, Romanticism, and Impressionism were highly rated. Here, both style and genre are linked as portraits, landscapes, and cityscape are common subjects in realist, romantic, and impressionistic art. On the other hand, LAP rates less representational art, such as cubism or abstract art, lower. The consequences of this aesthetic bias can be seen across artists. Nearly every piece by the french cityscape artists Edouard Cortès (208 of 214) and Antoine Blanchard (168 of 170) were rated \geq 6.5. In contrast, more influential but less realistic artists—Pablo Picasso, Andy Warhol, and Salvador Dalí— rated lower.

4. How the LAION-Aesthetics Predictor was Made
Seaver (2017) suggests that audits can demonstrate the existence of biases, but “what [audits] cannot do is explain conclusively how that disparate impact came about.” In this section, we explore the potential origins of the aesthetic biases described above by conducting a digital ethnography of how the LAP was made.
4.1. Ethnographic Methodology
Ethnography is a qualitative method from anthropology seeking to explain how groups of people interpret the world (Dourish, 2014). While ethnography began within physical field sites, the 1990s saw a proliferation of multi-sited ethnographic research (Marcus, 1995). Our approach aligns with what Marcus (1995) describes as “following the thing,” a tactic often used to study supply chains (Tsing, 2015). We also draw on the later development of trace ethnography (Geiger and Ribes, 2011), which follows digital documentation, blogs, and code repositories to understand collaboration. 4.2. Findings: How the LAION-Aesthetics Predictor was Created
4.2.1. Model development choices were made according to the individual taste of LAION’s founder
According to the aforementioned YouTube video, the founder of LAION (Christoph Schuhmann) created LAP on his own. LAP is a multi-layer perceptron model with linear layers trained to predict the aesthetic score of an image based on a CLIP embedding. 88
8
CLIP models are commonly used in computer vision to semantically represent both texts and images in the same vector space (Radford et al., 2021) Although he experimented with other models, Schuhmann chose this architecture because “for [his] use case the simple linear layer worked best just from visual inspection.” Schuhmann trained LAP using three distinct datasets, each of which measures the aesthetic quality of an image on a scale from 1 to 10: (1) Aesthetic Visual Analysis (AVA), (2) Simulacra Aesthetic Captions (SAC), and (3) LAION-Logos. Once again, Schuhmann explains that he chose to weight each dataset equally in the training process because “from [his] subjective taste it was best to just have every dataset once in the training data.” In other words, Schuhmann decided how to train LAP based largely on his individual taste.
Schuhmann seems to recognize various limitations of LAP. For example, he encourages others to “experiment” with different model architectures, newer clip embedding models, and larger aesthetic evaluation datasets. Additionally, Schuhmann asks for volunteers to reproduce the training data curation steps described in the video because “[he] had some ugly code somewhere but [he] can’t find it anymore.” He also asks for volunteers in the last comment of the explainer video: “This is all an ongoing area of research and it would be really cool to have someone working on this.” Schuhmann appears to recognize the slapdash way LAP was made and expects that it will be improved upon in the future. However, at the time of our writing in January 2026, LAION has not released a new aesthetic evaluation model.

4.2.2. Training datasets are inconsistently documented
AVA is an influential predecessor to the LAION-Aesthetics dataset within the computer vision community (Ramesh et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Khosla et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2014). The computer vision researchers Murray et al. collected AVA by scraping images and ratings from an online photography competition website (www.dpchallenge.com) in 2012 (Murray et al., 2012). SAC was released in July of 2022 by John David Pressman, a Stability AI researcher at the time (Pressman et al., 2022). Pressman collected this dataset using a Discord bot shared in AI-art communities that allowed volunteers to generate images and vote on their perceived aesthetic quality. Although we were able to find the content of each dataset, the quality of their documentation varies widely. AVA was the most well documented because it was published at a computer vision conference (Murray et al., 2012). While SAC was not formally published in an academic setting, its GitHub repository has an extensive README.md file that functions as a data card (Pushkarna et al., 2022). As we will describe next, LAION-Logos had little documentation .
We initially struggled to find LAION-Logos. While AVA and SAC are linked in the LAION-Aesthetics blog post, LAION-Logos is not. Searching ‘‘LAION-Logos’’ on Google yielded only websites and academic articles alluding to the original blog. While LAION-Logos is technically publicly available, our challenges finding the dateset suggests that it has been de facto private. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to document LAION-Logos.
4.2.3. Training datasets were created for different purposes across different time periods
LAION-Logos and SAC were created to improve generative image models. LAION-Logos was collected to “improve the [LAION aesthetic prediction] models abilities to evaluate images with more or less aesthetic texts in them” (Schuhmann, 2022) While the SAC documentation says the dateset can be used in multiple ways, like “Filtering Datasets” and “Alignment Research,” its documentation indicates that SAC was primarily collected for LAION-Aesthetics. This can be seen in the acknowledgment: “Special thanks to [name removed] for his early feedback and suggestions on the bot, as well as writing the initial public domain prompt and rating dataset that allowed us to make LAION-aesthetic as early as we did”
In contrast, AVA was collected a decade prior to popularization of generative AI research. To motivate the construction of AVA, Murray et al. (2012) suggest that “With the ever-expanding volume of visual content available, the ability to organize and navigate such content by aesthetic preference is becoming increasingly important.” The authors position AVA as a solution to the problem of information overload, a common refrain in recommendation research (Seaver, 2022). Perhaps because AVA was created within a personalization paradigm, AVA embodies a more pluralistic conception of aesthetics than the universalism of LAION-Logos and SAC. We describe these competing conceptions of aesthetics below in greater detail.
4.3. Limitations of the Images and Aesthetic Annotations Used to Train the LAION-Aesthetics Predictor
4.3.1. Images and aesthetic annotations were collected with mixed levels of consent
These datasets were collected with varying levels of consent (Table 4). For AVA, neither the images nor the ratings were collected with the consent of dpchallenge.com members. With LAION-Logos, annotators likely knew their ratings would be used to create an aesthetic evaluation dataset, but image creators did not consent to this use. For SAC, the AI enthusiasts generating and annotating images consented to their images and annotations being used to create an aesthetic evaluation dataset.
4.3.2. The images used to train LAION-Aesthetics Predictor are primarily photographs.
Of the 428,632 images used to train LAP, 255,530 (\sim60%) come from AVA, 146,372 (\sim34%) from SAC, and 26,730 (\sim6%) from LAION-Logos. Both the images and ratings from AVA are from photographers who participated in the English-language online art competition website dpchallenge.com between its founding in 2006 and its scraping in 2012. The images in SAC were generated and rated by a handful of AI enthusiasts in 2022 using an early version of Stable Diffusion, with the top 50 most active volunteers accounting for 87.4% of all ratings. Finally, the images in LAION-Logos from LAION-5B were scraped from the internet no later than ealry 2022 and rated by only 18 LAION volunteers. The images used to train LAP have a number of limitations. Temporally, most images are from before 2012, meaning newer content may be underrepresented. Also, the over-representation of photographs in LAP’s training data may explain why the model highly rates photorealism.
4.3.3. Annotators are primarily English-speaking photographers or western AI-enthusiasts.
When the AVA data was created (2006 - 2012), the World Bank estimate that less than 33% of the world’s population used the internet, most of whom were in western countries. 1111
11
https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/IT.NET.USER.ZS Therefore, the images and ratings posted on the English-language website dpchallenge.com at the time likely reflect the tastes of western photographers. The SAC README cautions: “Participants in [SAC] are largely WEIRD, or Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic. This means that the aesthetic preferences recorded are not universal among humanity. While we didn’t take a survey of the demographic makeup of SimulacraBot users, it can be assumed they largely reside in the United States and Europe.” The README also warns that participants were “mostly open source AI developers and enthusiasts,” so “their aesthetic feedback is going to lean STEM, fantasy, nerdy, esoteric, etc.” While the identity of LAION-Logos annotators is unclear, we expect they share similar biases to SAC. Moreover, a majority of the ratings in SAC and LAION-Logos were chosen by a single person (Table 4).
4.3.4. The LAION-Aesthetics Predictor was trained by conflating different measurements of aesthetics across the datasets.
Per the LAION-Aesthetics blog, LAP was trained to "predict the rating people gave when they were asked ’How much do you like this image on a scale from 1 to 10?’" (Schuhmann, 2022). While it is true that SAC and LAION-Logos measures aesthetics as an absolute image property, this is not what AVA measures. Votes on dpchallenge do not measure the perceived aesthetic quality of a de-contextualized image but rather the quality of an image relative to a topic, like skyscapes. As prizes are award to images based on the average final ranking of aesthetic votes for a challenge, votes are comparable within a topic but not between topics. Murray et al. (2012) find that images in challenges with negative affective valences, like fear, tend to receive lower absolute scores. This implies that a model built on absolute scores rather than relative scores within challenges may be confounded by the emotional valence of an image.
Murray et al. (2012) are highly critical of de-contextualized aesthetic scores: “The interpretation of these aesthetic judgments [in prior aesthetic datasets], expressed under the form of numeric scores, has always been taken for granted. Yet a deeper analysis of the context in which these judgments are given is essential. The result of this lack of context is that it is difficult to understand what the aesthetic classifiers really model when trained with such datasets.” This is why AVA represents aesthetic scores as a distribution of votes, allowing one to examine not only the mean aesthetic vote but also the variance. The authors found that images with high variance, or greater disagreement between voters in the image competition, tended to be “non-conventional,” such as “innovative application of photographic techniques.” This nuance, however, is lost because LAP is trained by simply averaging the aesthetic scores.
5. Discussion
5.1. The Algorithmic Gaze
Western art has historically been made to please white, western, heterosexual, men (Berger, 2008; Mulvey, 1975; Kaplan, 2012). Similarly, we found that LAP was designed to the taste of one particular white, western, man: the founder of LAION. Perhaps as a result, LAP is aesthetically biased toward realistic images of western and Japanese art. Also, images with captions mentioning women in the LAION-Aesthetics Dataset were more likely to be rated 6.5+ than those mentioning men or LGBTQ+ people. Below, we discuss LAP’s imperial, realist, and male algorithmic gaze.
5.1.1. The Imperial Gaze
In our analysis of art from the MET, all of the images rated 6.5+ were paintings, photographs and prints from western or Japanese artists. Not a single piece of African, Oceanian, Native American, Islamic, Egyptian, or West Asian art was rated 6.5+. Although the highly-rated Japanese art may seem like an outlier, western artists have been influenced by a racist fascination with “eastern” (Said, 1979) aesthetics since at least the 1600s (Porter, 1999). Overall, LAP embodies what the post-colonial scholar Kaplan (2012) refers to as the imperial gaze, wherein art is judged according white western tastes. Due to the imperial gaze of LAP, the widespread use of this model to curate training data or evaluate models may exacerbate existing multi-modal biases, like struggling to generate Native American (Taylor et al., 2025) or Persian (Qadri et al., 2025) art.
Similar to prior work on biases in data “quality” filtering (Dodge et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2024), our findings demonstrate that aesthetic evaluation can lead to representational harms by filtering out non-western art. We encourage future research into how other popular image evaluation methods, such as FID scores (Heusel et al., 2017) or CLIP similarity (Radford et al., 2021), asses non-western art. At the same time, we encourage researchers to explore aesthetic biases outside of western ontologies. Notably, none of the images in the MET department of “Africa, the Ancient Americas, and Oceania” scored 6.5+ (Table 1). However, the consolidation of art from different continents and time periods into a monolithic department is a vestige of the colonialist category of “primitive art” (Price, 2001). By using the MET to evaluate LAP, our analysis inherits this coloniality. In calling for a research agenda focused on aesthetic biases, we caution against reinscribing the imperial gaze embedded in western art history.
5.1.2. The Realist Gaze
We found that LAP highly rates realistic and figurative images, such as landscape paintings. For centuries, one of the primary criteria for evaluating western visual art was how well it imitates reality (Potolsky, 2006). However, the modern art diverged from this tradition by focusing on the ideas and emotions behind art (Fry, 1920), often drawing inspiration from less realistic non-western art (Harney and Phillips, 2018). LAP’s realism bias risks devaluing both modern and non-western artistic cultures and parallels troubling historical precedents. Famously, the Degenerate Art Exhibition under Nazi Germany encouraged visitors to mock modern art (Smee, 2025; Morrison, 2025). Echoing this sensibility, some of the styles and artists from this exhibition, like Picasso, were among the lowest rated in the WikiArt dataset.
Realist biases cam also restrain the creative possibilities of visual generative AI. A common throughline in HCI research on visual generative AI models is that artists most often enjoy the glitches or surrealism of text-to-image (T2I) models (Shelby et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2023). As a result, visual artists have critiqued the increasing realism of newer T2I models (Taylor et al., 2025). Therefore, aesthetic evaluation models that privilege realism may poorly align with the taste of some visual artists.
So far, we discussed the harms of being excluded by LAP’s algorithmic gaze. However, realism biases can also lead to harm through non-consensual inclusion. We found that the LAION-Aesthetics 6.5+ Dataset is disproportionally made of images from independent visual artists and photographers. Not only have these communities lost jobs due to visual generative AI (Merchant, 2025), but this suggests that their art is driving the development of these technologies. As increasing photorealism exacerbates the greatest harms of visual generative AI—like deepfakes (Mink et al., 2024), job replacement (Jiang et al., 2023), and sexual abuse imagery (Abdulla, 2025)—we encourage researchers to reconsider the value placed on realism in visual evaluation.
5.1.3. The Male Gaze
We found that images with captions mentioning women were more likely to included in the LAION-Aesthetics 6.5+ Dataset, while those mentioning men or LGBTQ+ people were more likely to be excluded. It follows that datasets curated and models evaluated with LAP will reflect these biases. In doing so, LAP may be reinforcing the heterosexual male gaze in western visual art history (Mulvey, 1975; Berger, 2008) at scale. Although AI fairness researchers often critique data exclusions, Hoffmann (2021) critiques including marginalized communities in oppressive technologies. Recently, women have been immensely harmed by the proliferation of visual generative AI models. The United Nations has warned that generative AI is a major contributor to violence against women (Carmo, 2025). This violence has been facilitated by a rise of commercial AI-based nudification apps, often specifically designed to create nude images of women (Gibson et al., 2025). However, this problem is not unique to dedicated nudification apps. At the time of our writing, thousands of men on X (formerly Twitter) have began using Grok to create and share NCII and CSAM of women and girls (McMahon and Cress, 2026). Aesthetic evaluation models that increase the likelihood of women being included in AI training data, like LAP, risk exacerbating these harms. We caution that greater inclusion in the training data for harmful AI systems can do more harm than good.
5.2. Reevaluating Evaluation
5.2.1. Pluralistic Aesthetic Evaluation
As discussed above, we found numerous aesthetic in the behavior and design of LAP. In raising these issues, we do not wish advocate for designing “better” universal aesthetic quality evaluation models and datasets. As assessing the visual aesthetic quality of an image is deeply subjective and value-laden (Bourdieu, 1984), any attempt to represent the aesthetic quality of an image with a single number will be deeply flawed. As an alternative to this normative problem formulation, we encourage researchers to make use of more descriptive evaluating metrics (Röttger et al., 2022). Although LAP is often used as a general measure of aesthetic quality (Ding et al., 2024; Fong and See, 2024; Gao and Liu, 2024; He et al., 2023), we found that it could be more accurately described as a measure of photorealism. As described above, there are a number of limitations to using realism to assess the quality of an image. However, a model that measures photorealism is not in-and-of-itself harmful. Rather, we caution researchers against treating photorealism as a prescriptive rather than descriptive property of images, conflating how images ought to be with merely how images can be.
To support more pluralistic AI alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024b), we encourage the use of descriptive measurements when evaluating visual generative AI model outputs and training data. Abandoning the flawed construct of universal “aesthetics” would allow researchers to more clearly articulate which aesthetic values their visual generative AI models prioritize, such as photorealism or particular visual cultures. For instance, Tencent’s Hunyuan-DiT T2I model is specifically designed to improve Chinese visual element understanding (Li et al., 2024b). In addition to improving transparency, more descriptive evaluations may help artists choose models that better align their tastes, perhaps addressing artists’ critique of increasing photorealistic T2I models (Shelby et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2025). The seeds of more descriptive measures of aesthetic quality can actually be seen in the 2012 AVA dataset itself (Murray et al., 2012). Recognizing the limits of de-contextualized aesthetic evaluation, Murray et al. grouped similar photography challenges to measure 14 different photographic styles, such as “motion blur” and “long exposure.”
The powerful companies developing T2I models may have little incentive to shift from prescriptive to descriptive aesthetic evaluations. For-profit companies go to great lengths arguing that their new models supplant their competitors. For instance, Meta researchers manipulated benchmarks to give an inflated impression of Llama 4’s performance (Cranz, 2025). Acknowledging that different T2I models may be better in different visual contexts undermines companies’ efforts to prove that their models are uniquely “state-of-the-art.” Much like corporate AI rhetoric, academic AI research is increasingly driven by benchmark competition (Gururaja et al., 2023; Orr and Kang, 2024), often without questioning the foundational assumptions embedded in said benchmarks (Raji et al., 2021). We encourage future researchers to examine what the universal aesthetics measurements used by companies and AI researchers actually measure.
5.2.2. Combining an Audit and Ethnography
In this work, we were able to examine upstream design decisions contributing to downstream biases by combining an audit and ethnography. Auditing is a core FAccT methodology, with the regex ‘‘audit*’’ appearing in nearly half of all FAccT publications.1212
12
https://dl.acm.org/action/doSearch?fillQuickSearch=false&target=advanced&ConceptID=122580&expand=all&AllField=audit* With some notable exceptions (Miceli et al., 2021; Marda and Narayan, 2020; Riley, 2024; Scheuerman, 2024; Passi and Barocas, 2019; Baack, 2024a), ethnography is far less common at FAccT. As Seaver (2017) argues, audits can help identify the existence of algorithmic biases while ethnographies can help explain where these biases may have originated by focusing on the people behind the algorithm. In addition to focusing on the harms of AI models themselves, responsible AI researchers are increasingly advocating for “studying up” the powerful people behind AI development and deployment (Barabas et al., 2020; Kawakami et al., 2024). In service of these goals, we advocate for combining audit and ethnography as a socio-technical method for evaluating algorithmic systems.
While one does not need permission from its creators to audit an algorithm, ethnographic methods often require negotiating access (Scheuerman, 2024). For example, prior ethnographic FAccT research has relied on access to field sites like major tech companies (Scheuerman, 2024; Passi and Barocas, 2019) or the Delhi Police Headquarters (Marda and Narayan, 2020). However, maintaining access can limit critical scholarship (Scheuerman, 2024). Even in the case of “open” models, development practices may be opaque (Widder et al., 2024). We were only able to understand out how LAP was made by finding an explainer video in an old Discord message. Herein lies a lesson for future ethnographic FAccT research beyond traditional field sites. Ethnographies of algorithms may require “scavenging” (Seaver, 2017) for diverse materials, including formal documentation, datasets, academic papers, and social media conversations. At the same time, sharing an unlisted YouTube video—even if posted in a public Discord—could raise ethical concerns. We have chosen to include this video in our analysis due to LAION’s advocacy for data scraping and refusal to remove artists’ work from their datasets (Filikhina and Elsaß, 2025). That said, the “public-ness” of data does not always make its analysis ethical (Zimmer, 2010).
6. Conclusion
Generative AI models and algorithmic systems are increasingly mediating our media, art, and culture. Therefore, it is imperative to understand for whose taste these systems are designed. In this work, we audited the LAION-Aesthetics Predictor (LAP) model, finding that LAP reflects imperial, realist, and male aesthetic sensibilities. Through an ethnographic analysis of public materials, we found that the development of LAP reflects similar biases. In light of these findings, we call for greater attention on aesthetic biases as a source of representational harm and advocate for more pluralistic revelations. Much like we examine the algorithmic gaze of LAP, we encourage future research into the cultural consequences of algorithmic “quality” assessment.
