 3 Method 

 

In this section, we first motivate the use of LoRA for efficient temporal adaptation and for enabling the Matryoshka property in text embedding models (TEMs), constituting a strong baseline.
We then introduce Temporal-aware Matryoshka Representation Learning (TMRL) , which leverages the Matryoshka structure to introduce an explicit temporal subspace, enhancing temporal representations across embedding dimensions.
The core concepts of TMRL are illustrated in Figure  2 . 
 



 3.1 LoRA-based temporal adaptation. 

 

Fine-tuning TEMs on temporal retrieval datasets often incurs a trade-off between semantic and temporal representations  ( Han et al., 2025 ) .
Prior approaches  Wu et al. (2024) ; Abdallah et al. (2025) address this via query routers that switch between fine-tuned and original models, but full fine-tuning is costly and routing increases training complexity and inference latency.
LoRA  ( Hu et al., 2021 ) mitigates this trade-off by freezing the semantic TEM and training a small set of additional parameters for temporal adaptation.
It offers stable training, lower hyperparameter sensitivity, and can be merged into the base model at zero inference overhead (Table  1 ).
In Section  5 , we empirically show that LoRA efficiently endows TEMs with the Matryoshka property while preserving semantic retrieval, establishing it as a strong baseline for TMRL.
For simplicity, we continue to denote LoRA-fine-tuned retrievers as f_{\theta} in the following sections. 
 
 



 3.2 Temporal-aware Matryoshka Representation Learning 




Matryoshka temporal subspace. 

 

Time-sensitive queries contain temporal expressions that specify temporal constraints for retrieval.
Most semantic TEMs encode temporal and non-temporal information jointly, but non-temporal semantics often dominate the representation, overshadowing temporal signals and leading to suboptimal temporal retrieval performance, as observed in zero-shot performance in Table  1 . 
 
 

We conceptually view a time-sensitive query or passage as containing two components:
a temporal component q_{\mathrm{T}} (or p_{\mathrm{T}} )
and a non-temporal semantic component q_{\mathrm{S}} (or p_{\mathrm{S}} ),
where the temporal component typically comprises fewer tokens.
Let m\in\mathcal{M} and t , with m\geq t , denote the target Matryoshka embedding dimension and the predefined temporal subspace dimension, respectively.
To preserve temporal cues across all Matryoshka truncation levels, we allocate the first t dimensions of the embedding to encode temporal information (Eq.  6 ).
As a result, we effectively structure the truncated embedding as the concatenation of a temporal subspace (first t dimensions) and a semantic subspace (the remaining m-t dimensions) (Eq.  7 ), ensuring that all Matryoshka embeddings retain the temporal subspace (Top of Figure  2 ). 
 

 

 
 \displaystyle f_{\theta}(q)_{1:t}\approx q_{\mathrm{T}}, 
 
 (6) 
 

 
 \displaystyle f_{\theta}(q)_{1:m}\approx f_{\theta}(q)_{1:t}\ \oplus\ f_{\theta}(q)_{(t+1):m}.\vskip-10.00002pt 
 
 (7) 
 

Let \mathcal{T}(q)\subseteq\{\texttt{[CLS]},1,\ldots,L\} denote the set of temporal token indices in a time-sensitive query representation f_{\theta}(q)=\{h_{\texttt{[CLS]}},h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\} . The temporal token representations are defined as: 
 

 

 
 f(q)_{\mathrm{T}}=\{\,h_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{T}(q)\,\}.\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (8) 
 

Regarding CLS-pooling TEMs, we include the [CLS] token in \mathcal{T}(q) as it aggregates global information and may encode temporal signals. 
 
 

To compress the temporal information into a t -dimensional subspace, we introduce a temporal projector 
 \mathcal{P}:\mathbb{R}^{d}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{t} ,
a two-layer feedforward network that follows the TEM’s activation and layer-normalization configuration.
 \mathcal{P} takes in only temporal tokens f(q)_{\mathrm{T}} to learn a compact and compatible t -dimensional temporal representation.
The final temporal representation for query q is obtained by averaging over the projected temporal tokens: 
 

 

 
 \bar{q}_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}(q)|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}(q)}\mathcal{P}(h_{i})\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{t}.\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (9) 
 
 
 



Temporal subspace contrastive learning. 

 

The final step is to incorporate the projected temporal representation \bar{q}_{\mathrm{T}} into the contrastive learning framework to obtain the desired embedding in Eq.  7 .
To this end, we introduce a temporal subspace contrastive learning objective that integrates \bar{q}_{\mathrm{T}} into the contrastive learning framework (Bottom of Figure  2 ).
Since temporal contrastive learning operates on positive and negative queries rather than passages  Wu et al. (2024) , we define the negative set as \mathcal{N}_{p}=\{q^{-}_{1},\ldots,q^{-}_{n}\} .
The InfoNCE loss for the t -dimensional temporal subspace is: 
 

 

 
 \begin{gathered}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{Temp}}(p,q^{+},\mathcal{N}_{p})=-\log\\
\frac{e^{\,\mathrm{sim}^{(t)}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\bar{p}_{\mathrm{T}}},\,{\color[rgb]{0.5,1,0}f_{\theta}(q^{+})})/\tau}}{e^{\,\mathrm{sim}^{(t)}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\bar{p}_{\mathrm{T}}},\,{\color[rgb]{0.5,1,0}f_{\theta}(q^{+})})/\tau}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\,\mathrm{sim}^{(t)}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\bar{p}_{\mathrm{T}}},\,{\color[rgb]{1,0.47,0.62}f_{\theta}(q^{-}_{i})})/\tau}},\\
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{Temp}}(p,q^{+},\mathcal{N}_{p})=-\log\\
\frac{e^{\,\mathrm{sim}^{(t)}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}f_{\theta}(p)},\,{\color[rgb]{0.5,1,0}\bar{q}^{+}_{\mathrm{T}}})/\tau}}{e^{\,\mathrm{sim}^{(t)}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}f_{\theta}(p)},\,{\color[rgb]{0.5,1,0}\bar{q}^{+}_{\mathrm{T}}}))/\tau}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\,\mathrm{sim}^{(t)}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}f_{\theta}(p)},\,{\color[rgb]{1,0.47,0.62}(\bar{q}^{-}_{i})_{\mathrm{T}}})/\tau}},\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Temp}}=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{Temp}}+\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{Temp}},\end{gathered}\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (10) 
 

where \bar{p}_{\mathrm{T}} , \bar{q}^{+}_{\mathrm{T}} , and (\bar{q}^{-}_{i})_{\mathrm{T}} denote the passage, positive, and negative queries counterpart of Eq.  8 .
This loss encourages the first t -dimensions to align with the projected \bar{p}_{\mathrm{T}} and \bar{q}^{+}_{\mathrm{T}} , while pull it apart from (\bar{q}^{-}_{i})_{\mathrm{T}} .
This way, we explicitly encode temporal information in the allocated t -dimensional temporal subspace (Eq.  6 ), resulting in our temporal-aware Matryoshka embeddings (Eq.  7 ). 
 
 



Local and structural self-distillation. 

 

Since lower-dimensional Matryoshka embeddings are trained to mimic higher-dimensional ones, similarities in the full embedding space naturally provide a supervisory signal  Yoon et al. (2024b) .
We thus view MRL as a form of self-distillation, where higher-dimensional embeddings act as teachers for their truncated counterparts.
Building on this perspective, we introduce auxiliary objectives to strengthen temporal-aware Matryoshka embeddings.
The first objective preserves local structure by aligning nearest-neighbor similarities in the teacher space, encouraging student embeddings to retain fine-grained semantic relationships from the full-dimensional representation.
Given a mini-batch of embeddings X^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d} and their corresponding m -dimensional Matryoshka embeddings X^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times m} , where d{>}m , we employ the top- k pairwise similarity loss to preserve local similarity: 
 

 

 
 \begin{gathered}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dist}}=\sum\limits_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{M},\\
m\neq d\end{subarray}}}\sum_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}\left\lVert\mathrm{sim}^{(d)}(x_{i},x_{j})-\mathrm{sim}^{(m)}(x_{i},x_{j})\right\rVert_{1}\\
\vskip-5.0pt\text{where}\quad\mathcal{N}=\operatorname{arg\,top}_{k}\,\mathrm{sim}^{(d)}(x_{i},x_{j}).\end{gathered}\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (11) 
 

Beyond local alignment, preserving the global structure of the embedding space is also important. While pairwise similarity loss enforces local consistency, it fails to capture the overall geometry.
Because full-dimensional embeddings encode richer relational structure, we adopt linear Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)  Kornblith et al. (2019) as a structural regularizer, encouraging lower-dimensional embeddings to remain geometrically aligned with the teacher space at the batch level. This complements the local pairwise loss by preserving global semantic geometry.
The linear CKA between two embedding sets can be expressed using covariance \mathrm{cov(\cdot,\cdot)} as: 
 

 

 
 \mathrm{CKA}(X,Y)=\frac{\|\mathrm{cov}(X,Y)\|_{F}^{2}}{\|\mathrm{cov}(X,X)\|_{F}\,\|\mathrm{cov}(Y,Y)\|_{F}}.\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (12) 
 
 
 

Let us denote the centered variants of the d and m -dimensional embeddings as: 
 

 

 
 \tilde{X}^{(d)}=X^{(d)}-\frac{1}{B}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}X^{(d)},\;\tilde{X}^{(m)}=X^{(m)}-\frac{1}{B}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}X^{(m)}.\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (13) 
 

The auxiliary Matryoshka CKA is defined as: 
 

 

 
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKA}}=\sum\limits_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathcal{M},\\
m\neq d\end{subarray}}}(1-\mathrm{CKA}(\tilde{X}^{(d)},\tilde{X}^{(m)}))\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (14) 
 
 
 



Optimization objective. 

 

Combining with the semantic MRL objective in Eq.  5 , the temporal projector \mathcal{P} and the LoRAs are jointly optimized with the following TMRL training objective: 
 

 

 
 \begin{gathered}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TMRL}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MRL}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Temp}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dist}}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKA}},\end{gathered}\vskip-5.0pt 
 
 (15) 
 

where \alpha,\beta,\gamma are hyperparameters (ablation in Section  5.3 ).

 4 Temporal Contrastive Learning Data Augmentation 

 

The temporal projector \mathcal{P} in TMRL requires clean temporal expressions from both queries and passages.
However, upon inspecting the Temporal Nobel Prize (TNP) dataset  Wu et al. (2024) , we observe that some training samples are ill-suited for temporal contrastive learning (e.g., Figure  3 , left).
In particular, passages may contain multiple temporal expressions (e.g., “In 1972” and “In 1966”), which can induce conflicting temporal gradient signals during contrastive training by forcing a query’s temporal expression to align with unrelated years.
This observation motivates a refinement of the original dataset.
As illustrated in Figure  3 (right), we
(1) split long passages into shorter passages such that each resulting passage contains a single temporal expression; and
(2) systematically generate additional positive and negative queries for each shorter passage based on the original queries, while remaining faithful to the source corpus. 
 



Passage splitting. 

 

We use SUTime  Chang and Manning (2012) to identify temporal expressions at the sentence level.
To preserve semantic context for retrieval, we iteratively merge adjacent sentences until the resulting passage would contain more than one temporal expression, at which point merging stops.
Sentences containing multiple temporal expressions are discarded.
This procedure yields passages with a single temporal expression while retaining as much local context as possible. 
 
 



Positive/Negative query augmentation. 

 

To systematically guide query augmentation, we characterize the temporal properties of queries along three dimensions.
First, we focus on L2 (time–event) and L3 (event–event) temporal understanding  Tan et al. (2023) , excluding L1 (time–time relations) as it primarily involves temporal arithmetic, which is uncommon in information retrieval.
Second, we adopt the query taxonomy of  Jia et al. (2021) , categorizing queries as explicit , implicit , or temporal answer , and exclude ordinal queries, which rely on ordinal cues (e.g., “first”, “second”) that could be missing from the corpus.
Finally, we employ Allen’s interval relations  Allen (1983) as a rubric to control the temporal alignment between each query and its corresponding passage. 
 
 

We design a structured schema and prompt template (Table  13 and  11 ) to instruct Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507  Yang et al. (2025) to generate augmented positive and negative queries from the original TNP dataset.
The LLM also extracts temporal spans from queries and passages, refined with preliminary SUTime outputs, without introducing new facts to preserve dataset fidelity.
Examples queries are shown on the right of Figure  3 .
The final dataset statistics are summarized in Appendix  A.3 . 
 
 
 



 5 Experiments 




 5.1 Experimental Setting 




Datasets and metrics. 

 

We evaluate our methods on Temporal Nobel Prize  Wu et al. (2024) and extend to TimeQA  Chen et al. (2021) .
Following the BEIR benchmark  Thakur et al. (2021) , we report nDCG@10 and Recall@100.
To assess general semantic retrieval performance, we also report nDCG@10 on the Natural Questions (NQ) dataset  Kwiatkowski et al. (2019) from BEIR using the original embedding dimension of each TEM.
Please refer to Appendix  A.3 for dataset statistics and further details. 
 
 



Baselines. 

 

For both datasets, we compare TMRL’s full-dimensional retrieval performance against:
BM25  Robertson and Zaragoza (2009) ,
zero-shot performance of the original TEMs,
Ts-Retriever  Wu et al. (2024) ,
and TempRetriever (element-wise interaction)  Abdallah et al. (2025) .
To evaluate the quality of Matryoshka embeddings , we include
Matryoshka Adaptor (M-Adaptor)  Yoon et al. (2024b) 
and vanilla LoRA-based MRL as baselines.
Each method is evaluated on six open-source TEMs with fewer than 600 M parameters: contriever  Izacard et al. (2022) ,
gte-base (gte)  Li et al. (2023) ,
nomic-embed-text-v1.5 (nomic)  Nussbaum et al. (2025) ,
gte-base-en-v1.5 (gte1.5)  Zhang et al. (2024) ,
bge-base-en-v1.5 (bge)  Xiao et al. (2024) ,
and bge-m3 (bgem3)  Chen et al. (2024) . 
 
 



Implementation details. 

 

All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU using the AdamW optimizer  Loshchilov and Hutter (2019) .
For Ts-Retriever and TempRetriever, we follow the training settings reported in their respective papers.
For M-Adaptor, LoRA-based MRL, and TMRL, we use a batch size of 256 with 4 hard negatives, a learning rate of 1\times 10^{-4} , and train for 5 epochs (1 epoch for nomic ).
We apply LoRA with rank r{=}4 to all linear layers of the TEM for LoRA-based methods.
TMRL hyperparameters are elaborated in Sections  5.3 .
Additional implementation details are provided in Appendix  A.2 .

Matryoshka embedding results. 

 

Figure  4 and  7 present Matryoshka embedding performance on TNP and TimeQA, respectively.
Across both datasets, M-Adaptor is largely constrained by the TEM’s zero-shot performance, highlighting a limitation of semantic-only Matryoshka adaptation.
For TNP, LoRA-based MRL consistently outperforms M-Adaptor across embedding dimensions and TEMs, overcoming the zero-shot performance cap.
Building on LoRA-based MRL, TMRL further improves retrieval, particularly for lower-dimensional embeddings, with notable gains in nDCG@10 across most TEMs, indicating that the dedicated temporal subspace effectively enhances temporal encoding.
Furthermore, TMRL preserves general semantic retrieval performance, unlike Ts-Retriever.
We also observe that TMRL exhibits slightly lower Recall@10 at higher dimensions for gte and bge .
However, since retrieving more documents does not necessarily lead to better RAG performance  Jin et al. (2025a) , we argue that temporally relevant documents ranked at the top of the retrieval list are more valuable.
As a result, improvements in nDCG@10 are more impactful for downstream temporal RAG.
Due to limited space, we place the results of TimeQA in Appendix  A.1 .
On TimeQA, TMRL’s gains emerge at higher embedding dimensions (i.e., \geq 256), indicating that stronger semantic capacity is more important for this benchmark.
From 256 dimensions onward, TMRL matches the Recall@100 of the LoRA-based MRL baseline while consistently improving nDCG@10, with semantic retrieval performance on NQ remaining comparable to other methods.
Our augmentation pipeline continues to improve nDCG@10 across most TEMs, with modest Recall@100 trade-offs at smaller embedding sizes.
In conclusion, TMRL equips TEMs with temporal-aware Matryoshka embeddings that improve temporal retrieval while enabling flexible accuracy–efficiency trade-offs under different computational constraints.

 5.3 Discussions 




Effect of temporal subspace. 

 

Figure  5 analyzes
the temporal subspace dimension t and the weighting coefficient \alpha in Eq.  15 .
Auxiliary losses are disabled in this ablation, and the corresponding NQ results are reported in Table  2 .
For \alpha , values above 0.5 cause substantial degradation for bge , whereas contriever benefits from increasing \alpha up to 0.5, improving temporal retrieval at the cost of semantic retrieval.
With respect to t , bge requires at least 64 temporal dimensions to improve temporal retrieval while preserving semantic performance.
A similar trend holds for contriever , where larger t consistently improves temporal retrieval with minimal semantic impact.
Based on these results, we set \alpha\in[0.1,0.25] and t\in[64,128] ; final configurations for each TEM are provided in Appendix  A.2 . 
 
 



Auxiliary losses ablation. 

 

Tables  3 and  4 analyze the effect of the local ( \beta ) and structural ( \gamma ) self-distillation auxiliary losses in Eq.  15 .
Setting both \beta and \gamma to a small value (e.g., 0.1) improves alignment at both local and global levels compared to using no auxiliary loss.
This setting is robust across contriever and bge : the former trades semantic for temporal retrieval, while the latter shows only minor changes.
However, larger values introduce competition with the contrastive objective.
For simplicity, we set \beta=\gamma=0.1 in all experiments. 
 
 



Temporal RAG results. 

 

Figures  1 and  6 report temporal RAG F1 performance on TNP and TimeQA using Qwen3-8B  Yang et al. (2025) with different contriever variants trained on our augmented data.
RAG performance largely mirrors the corresponding retrieval trends in Figures  4 and  7 , supporting the hypothesis that retriever quality is a main bottleneck in temporal RAG.
On TNP, TMRL achieves the best Pareto frontier among Matryoshka baselines.
Its temporal subspace improves full-dimensional embeddings over full fine-tuning and the LoRA-based MRL baselines.
Consistent with retrieval results, for TimeQA, TMRL attains the highest F1 score from 256 dimensions onward, enabling up to 3{\times} storage reduction while maintaining competitive performance.
