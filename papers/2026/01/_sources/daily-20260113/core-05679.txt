 3 Methodology 

 

This section specifies (i) an operational definition of genuine reasoning features in SAEs, (ii) a contrastive approach for selecting candidate reasoning features, and (iii) two falsification tests designed to distinguish genuine reasoning features from superficial correlates. 
 



 3.1 Defining Reasoning Features in SAEs 

 

In this section, we define what we term a genuine reasoning feature in SAEs. Intuitively, such a feature should reflect the presence of reasoning processes rather than surface-level correlates of reasoning-style text. These processes include, but are not limited to, logical deduction, counterfactual reasoning, and deliberative planning. Crucially, they should be defined at the level of semantic computation rather than lexical form. 
 
 

Formally, let \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}} denote a residual stream activation at layer \ell of a transformer language model, and let \mathbf{f}=\mathrm{SAE}_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{SAE}}} be the output of an SAE encoder. We refer to f_{i} as feature i . We say that f_{i} is a genuine reasoning feature iff it satisfies all of the following criteria: 
 



Reasoning specificity. 

 

The feature activates reliably on text that involves reasoning behavior across diverse domains and contexts, while exhibiting substantially lower activation on non-reasoning text. 
 
 



Non-spurious correlation. 

 

The feature does not activate on non-reasoning text that merely contains phrases that are associated with reasoning corpora. Examples include discourse markers such as “therefore” or “let us consider,” as well as other surface-level lexical artifacts frequently found in CoT text. 
 
 



Semantic invariance. 

 

The feature remains stable under paraphrases and stylistic transformations that preserve the underlying reasoning structure. That is, changing tone, vocabulary, or presentation style without altering the logical content should not substantially alter the feature’s activation. 
 
 



 

 

This definition is intentionally strict to exclude features that correlate strongly with reasoning datasets but whose activation is attributable to shallow lexical terms or narrow stylistic conventions, rather than to an underlying reasoning process. 
 
 

This distinction is essential for mechanistic interpretability. Recent results show that simple token-level interventions can improve performance on reasoning benchmarks without engaging any deeper reasoning mechanism. For instance, Muennighoff et al. (2025) demonstrate that replacing end-of-thinking tokens with a filler word such as “wait” substantially increases accuracy on AIME-style problems for large models. Such effects highlight the risk of conflating performance gains driven by the amplification of spurious token-level correlates with genuine reasoning processes. Accordingly, our analysis focuses on identifying features that encode reasoning processes , not merely reasoning vocabulary . More discussion for the rationale of this definition is provided in Appendix   A . 
 
 
 



 3.2 Identifying Candidate Reasoning Features 

 

Following prior work ( Venhoff et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025b ) , we start by identifying candidate features that satisfy Reasoning specificity using contrastive, activation-based methods applied to reasoning and non-reasoning text. We then consider a range of metrics to select top-ranked features as reasoning-related candidates. 
 



 3.2.1 Contrastive Activation Analysis 

 

Let \mathcal{D}_{\text{R}} and \mathcal{D}_{\text{NR}} denote corpora of reasoning and non-reasoning text, respectively. For a fixed model, layer, and SAE, each input produces a sequence of feature activations across token positions. We then aggregate each feature’s activation over a sequence by taking the maximum across tokens: 
 

 

 
 a_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\max_{t}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t), 
 
 

where f_{i}(\mathbf{x},t) denotes the activation of feature i at token position t for input \mathbf{x} . 
 
 

Applying this aggregation to all samples yields two empirical activation distributions, \{a_{i,j}^{\text{R}}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{R}}} and \{a_{i,k}^{\text{NR}}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\text{NR}}} , corresponding to reasoning and non-reasoning corpora. Candidate reasoning features are those whose activation distributions differ substantially between these two sets. We next describe a range of metrics used to quantify these differences and select top-ranked features. 
 
 



 3.2.2 Contrastive Feature Selection Metrics 

 

Our primary metric for selecting candidate features is Cohen’s d , which measures the standardized mean difference between two distributions ( Cohen, 2013 ) . Specifically, for each feature i , we compute 
 

 

 
 d_{i}=\frac{\bar{a}_{i}^{\text{R}}-\bar{a}_{i}^{\text{NR}}}{s_{\text{pooled}}}, 
 
 

where s_{\text{pooled}}=\sqrt{\frac{(n_{\text{R}}-1)(s_{i}^{\text{R}})^{2}+(n_{\text{NR}}-1)(s_{i}^{\text{NR}})^{2}}{n_{\text{R}}+n_{\text{NR}}-2}} , and \bar{a}_{i}^{\text{R}} and \bar{a}_{i}^{\text{NR}} denote the sample means of \{a_{i,j}^{\text{R}}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{R}}} and \{a_{i,k}^{\text{NR}}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\text{NR}}} , respectively, with s_{i}^{\text{R}} and s_{i}^{\text{NR}} denoting the corresponding sample standard deviations. 
 
 

Cohen’s d provides a scale-invariant measure of effect size, reflecting the practical significance of activation differences. We rank all SAE features by d_{i} and select the top k features as candidate reasoning features, with k=100 in all experiments. In addition to Cohen’s d , we assess robustness using two additional metrics: (i) significance under a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test comparing \{a_{i,j}^{\text{R}}\} and \{a_{i,k}^{\text{NR}}\} ; and (ii) ROC-AUC when using a_{i}(\mathbf{x}) as a score to discriminate between reasoning and non-reasoning samples. We observe consistent results across metrics, and provide detailed definitions and analyses for these additional metrics in Appendix   C . 
 
 
 



 3.3 Causal Token Injection Framework 

 

In this section, we test whether the candidate features identified in Section   3.2 satisfy the Non-spurious correlation criterion through causal token injection experiments. Our central hypothesis is straightforward: if a feature encodes genuine reasoning, then inserting a small number of its most activating tokens into non-reasoning text should not substantially increase its activation. 
 



 3.3.1 Identifying Top-Activating Tokens 

 

For each candidate feature f_{i} , we start by identifying the tokens and short token sequences that most strongly activate it on the reasoning corpus. 
 



Unigram ranking. 

 

For each unique token t , we compute its mean activation 
 

 

 
 \bar{f}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t}|}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},j)\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},j), 
 
 

where \mathcal{I}_{t}=\{(\mathbf{x},j)\colon\mathrm{token}_{\mathbf{x},j}=t\} denotes the set of all token positions at which token t occurs in the reasoning corpus. Tokens are then ranked by \bar{f}_{i,t} in descending order. 
 
 



Bigram and trigram ranking. 

 

We extend the same procedure to contiguous bigrams and trigrams. To control variance from rare sequences, we restrict attention to n -grams that appear at least three times for bigrams and at least two times for trigrams. Mean activations are computed analogously and ranked within each n -gram category. 
 
 
 



 3.3.2 Token Injection Experiments 

 

This section evaluates whether the lexical patterns identified above are sufficient to drive feature activation outside reasoning contexts. For each feature f_{i} , we construct two sets of samples: Baseline , consisting of non-reasoning inputs drawn from \mathcal{D}_{\text{NR}} , and Injected , consisting of the same non-reasoning inputs with top-activating tokens inserted. 
 



Injection strategies. 

 

To probe different forms of lexical and contextual sensitivity, we consider various injection strategies. First, simple token injection inserts three top-ranked tokens either by prepending them to the text, uniformly interspersing them throughout the text, or replacing randomly selected words. Second, n -gram injection inserts either two top-ranked bigrams or one top-ranked trigram at uniformly sampled positions within the text. Third, contextual injection preserves local co-occurrence structure by inserting tokens together with their frequent preceding tokens. 
 
 



Feature Classification. 

 

For each feature i and injection strategy, we quantify the activation shift induced by injection using Cohen’s d ( Cohen, 2013 ) : 
 

 

 
 d_{i}^{\text{injected}}=\frac{\bar{a}_{i}^{\text{injected}}-\bar{a}_{i}^{\text{baseline}}}{s_{\text{pooled}}}, 
 
 

where \bar{a}_{i}^{\text{injected}} and \bar{a}_{i}^{\text{baseline}} denote the mean activations on injected and baseline samples, respectively, and s_{\text{pooled}} is the pooled standard deviation. 
 
 

We assess statistical significance using independent two-sample t -tests comparing injected and baseline activations. Following standard conventions for effect size interpretation ( Cohen, 2013 ) , each feature is classified based on the strongest effect observed across all injection strategies:

 Token-driven : d\geq 0.8 with p

 Partially token-driven : 0.5\leq d

 Weakly token-driven : 0.2\leq d

 3.4 LLM-Guided Falsification 

 

The token injection experiments described above eliminate a large class of lexical and short-range contextual confounds. Features classified as context dependent, however, are not well explained by token-level triggers alone and may instead respond to higher-level semantic patterns that are difficult to probe through predefined token manipulations. We therefore apply LLM-guided falsification to test whether these features satisfy the Semantic invariance criterion by constructing targeted counterexamples that attempt to falsify proposed explanations. These counterexamples include both non-reasoning text designed to elicit spurious activation and reasoning text that avoids the hypothesized pattern. We detail this procedure below. 
 



Hypothesis generation. 

 

At the start of each iteration, the LLM is provided with three sources of information: the top-activating tokens and n -grams identified in Section   3.3.1 , a small set of reasoning samples that strongly activate the feature, and token-level activation traces within those samples. Using this information, the LLM proposes an explicit hypothesis describing what linguistic or structural pattern the feature may be detecting. 
 
 



Counterexample construction. 

 

Given a proposed hypothesis, the LLM generates two complementary types of test cases. First, it produces non-reasoning text that instantiates the hypothesized pattern, serving as candidate false positives (FP). Second, it generates reasoning text that intentionally avoids the hypothesized pattern by varying vocabulary, syntax, or presentation style, serving as candidate false negatives (FN). 
 
 

Each candidate is then evaluated by running it through the target model and measuring the maximum activation of the feature. A non-reasoning candidate is considered a valid FP if its activation exceeds a threshold \tau=0.5\times\max_{j}a_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\mathrm{R}}) , while a reasoning candidate is considered a valid FN if its activation remains below 0.1\tau , where the maximum is taken over reasoning samples. The generation and evaluation steps are repeated for up to T=10 iterations, with early termination if at least three valid FP and three valid FN are obtained. 
 
 



Final classification. 

 

After the iterative analysis, the LLM produces a consolidated interpretation of the feature, including the linguistic or structural pattern it appears to detect and the conditions under which it activates or fails to activate. This interpretation is informed by all evidence accumulated during the procedure, including the success or failure of generated counterexamples, activation measurements on those candidates, and consistency across iterations. To ensure reliability, we report all LLM-generated interpretations and counterexamples for a representative experiment in Appendix   F . 
 
 
 



 3.5 Steering Experiments 

 

As a supplementary analysis, we conduct steering experiment to probe whether amplifying candidate features identified in Section   3.2 has a measurable effect on downstream reasoning task performance. 
 



Feature Steering. 

 

For a feature f_{i} with decoder direction \mathbf{W}_{\text{dec},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}} , we intervene on the residual stream at layer \ell according to 
 

 

 
 \mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}+\gamma f_{i}^{\max}\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec},i}, 
 
 

where \gamma\in\mathbb{R} is the steering strength and f_{i}^{\max} is the max activation of f_{i} on the reasoning corpus. 
 
 
 
 



 4 Experiments 




 4.1 Experimental Setup 

 

We conduct experiments on five open-weight transformer language models. Our primary models are Gemma-3-12B-Instruct and Gemma-3-4B-Instruct ( Team et al., 2025 ) . For both, we analyze a subset of middle-to-late layers and use sparse autoencoders from the GemmaScope-2 release ( McDougall et al., 2025 ) , each with 16,384 features trained on residual stream activations. We additionally study DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B ( Guo et al., 2025 ) , analyzing a representative middle layer using an SAE trained on reasoning-oriented corpora, specifically LMSys-Chat-1M ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) and OpenThoughts-114k ( Guha et al., 2025 ) , with 65,536 features ( Galichin et al., 2025 ) . Results for Gemma-2-2B and Gemma-2-9B ( Team et al., 2024 ) are reported in Appendix   B . 
 
 

For reasoning corpora, we use s1K-1.1, which consists of 1,000 challenging mathematics problems with detailed CoT traces ( Muennighoff et al., 2025 ) , and the General Inquiry Thinking Chain-of-Thought dataset, which contains 6,000 question-answer pairs spanning diverse domains with explicit reasoning annotations ( Wensey, 2025 ) . As a non-reasoning corpus, we use an uncopyrighted subset of the Pile ( Gao et al., 2020 ) , a large-scale collection of general web text. From each corpus, we uniformly sample 1,000 texts and truncate inputs to 64 tokens. 
 
 

We focus on middle-to-late layers, motivated by both prior work and empirical analysis. Early layers are typically dominated by lexical processing, while late layers are increasingly specialized toward output token prediction ( Ma et al., 2025 ) . To further support this choice, we analyze how concentrated SAE feature activations are on individual tokens. Specifically, we compute a token concentration ratio, defined as the fraction of a feature’s activation mass attributable to its 30 most activating tokens, along with the normalized entropy of the activation distribution across tokens. 
 
 

 Figure   2 shows diagnostics for Gemma-3-12B-Instruct on the s1K dataset. Middle layers consistently exhibit lower concentration ratios and higher entropy than early or late layers. Similar trends are observed across models. If genuine reasoning exists at the level of individual features, these layers constitute the most plausible candidates. 
 

 
 Figure 2: Token concentration ratio and normalized activation entropy for SAE features across all layers of Gemma-3-12B-Instruct on the s1K dataset. Middle layers exhibit lower concentration and higher entropy, indicating reduced reliance on specific tokens. 
 
 

We conduct all experiments on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80 GB of memory. Feature detection and token injection experiments require approximately 1 hour per configuration, while LLM-guided falsification requires approximately 2 hours.

Across all 196 context-dependent features analyzed, none are classified as genuine reasoning features. Every feature admits systematic false positives on non-reasoning text and false negatives on reasoning text under the LLM-guided protocol. Moreover, the majority of features are classified as confounds with high confidence, reflecting repeated success in generating counterexamples that satisfy the falsification criteria. This provides strong evidence that their activations are better explained by linguistic or semantic correlates than by reasoning processes. 
 
 

Qualitative inspection of the LLM-generated interpretations reveals recurring categories of confounds. Many features respond to instructional or planning-oriented discourse openers, such as “Let’s break down” , “I need to figure out” , and “Alright, so” . Others are triggered by formal academic framing common in explanatory writing, including phrases like “We are given” , “First, recall” , and “The problem asks” . In each case, the feature activates reliably on non-reasoning text that matches the identified pattern and fails to activate on reasoning text that avoids it. All interpretations, counterexamples, and activation traces for a representative experiment are reported in Appendix   F . 
 
 

Taken together, these results indicate that even features which resist simple token injection do not satisfy our criteria for genuine reasoning features. Instead, they reflect increasingly subtle semantic confounds that correlate with reasoning corpora but do not capture the underlying reasoning process itself.

 5 Summary 

 

We conducted a systematic evaluation of whether sparse autoencoders identify genuine reasoning features in large language models. Building on features selected by standard contrastive activation methods, we applied causal token injection and LLM-guided falsification to test whether feature activations reflect underlying reasoning processes or instead arise from reasoning-associated linguistic patterns. Across a wide range of models, layers, and datasets, we find that the majority of candidate features are strongly driven by token-level interventions, while the remaining features admit reliable counterexamples that decouple activation from reasoning behavior. Under our evaluation criteria, we do not identify any feature that robustly captures reasoning itself. These findings indicate that contrastively identified SAE features predominantly reflect linguistic correlates rather than reasoning computations, underscoring the importance of falsification-based evaluation when interpreting high-level behaviors in language models. 
 
 



 6 Limitations 

 

Our study has several limitations. First, while we evaluate a diverse set of open-weight language models and sparse autoencoders, our conclusions are restricted to the architectures, training procedures, and SAE variants considered. It remains possible that alternative representation learning methods or future SAE designs may yield features that better isolate reasoning processes. 
 
 

Second, our definition of genuine reasoning features is necessarily operational and focuses on robustness to lexical, stylistic, and contextual confounds. This definition prioritizes falsifiability and causal testing, but it may exclude representations that participate in reasoning in more distributed or indirect ways. In particular, reasoning may emerge from interactions among many features rather than being localized within individual SAE directions. 
 
 

Third, the LLM-guided interpretation procedure relies on the capabilities and limitations of the language model used for counterexample generation. Although all generated counterexamples are empirically validated against the target models and manually reviewed, the search over linguistic confounds is not exhaustive. 
 
 

Finally, our steering experiments are intentionally limited in scope and are not designed to provide definitive conclusions about the causal role of individual features in reasoning. These experiments serve only as supplementary evidence and do not rule out more subtle or distributed causal effects.
