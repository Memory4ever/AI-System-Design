L90: ### 2.1 Task Description
L91: 
L92: In MICL for ASR, a model learns to predict a textual output by conditioning on a sequence of paired examples from different modalities, which is audio and text. Formally, we define a context set
L93: 
L94:  | $$\mathcal{C}=\{(a_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},$$  |  | (1)
L95: 
L96: where $a_{i}$ represents the $i$-th audio input and $t_{i}$ is its corresponding text transcription. Given this context, the model receives a target audio $a^{*}$ and is tasked with predicting its target sentence $t^{*}$.
L97: The multimodal model $f_{\theta}$, parameterized by $\theta$, defines a conditional probability distribution over the target transcription given the context and the target audio:
L98: 
L99:  | $$p_{\theta}(t^{*}\mid a^{*},\mathcal{C})=\prod_{j=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(w_{j}^{*}\mid w_{<j}^{*},a^{*},\mathcal{C}),$$  |  | (2)
L100: where $w_{<j}^{*}=(w_{1}^{*},\ldots,w_{j-1}^{*})$ denotes the previously generated tokens and $T$ is the length of the target sequence. MICL allows the model to condition on paired audio and text demonstrations, which provides additional cues about the mapping from audio to text, and helps the model predict the transcription for unseen audio.
L101: ### 2.2 Prompt Modality Design
L102: 
L103: To isolate the contribution of each modality in MICL, we design three prompt types that differ only in the information provided in the context^{2}^{2} 2 One example is in Appendix cite37†I . For reference, we also include a standard ASR prompt that represents the task formulation commonly used to train speech LLMs.
L104: T-ICL. In this setting, we provide only the transcriptions of the in-context samples and remove their corresponding audio. This measures how much performance gain can be attributed solely to textual information, such as lexical patterns or orthography, without any acoustic evidence from the demonstrations cite58†Hsu and yi Lee (2025) ; cite54†Li and Niehues (2025b) .
L105: ICL. Here, we provide the transcriptions of the in-context samples together with the target audio. This evaluates whether the model can leverage text-only demonstrations as guidance when processing an audio input. Comparing ICL with T-ICL allows us to assess whether LLMs not only understand the task from text but also effectively utilize acoustic information from the query audio.
L106: MICL. In this setting, we provide paired audio and text for the in-context samples, along with the target audio. This design assesses whether adding in-context audio yields additional benefits beyond text alone, indicating that the model can effectively utilize both modalities instead of relying only on textual cues.
L107: ASR. This setting follows the standard task description of directly transcribing the input audio, and serves as a baseline for comparing in-context learning approaches against conventional speech LLM inference.
L108: ### 2.3 Sample Selection Strategy
L109: Selecting relevant demonstrations is important for effective text-based ICL, especially when the target language is low-resource or unseen cite67†Cahyawijaya et al. (2024) ; cite54†Li and Niehues (2025b) . We therefore include a retrieval-based sample selection module in this MICL work. Referring to cite54†Li and Niehues (2025b) , we use SONAR cite68†Duquenne et al. (2023) , a multilingual and multimodal embedding model, to embed the candidate samples and the target sample.
L110: We rank candidates samples by calculating cosine similarity to the target in the SONAR embedding space and select the top-$N$ samples as the in-context demonstrations. More details are provided in Appendix cite32†D .
L111: ### 2.4 Cross-lingual Instruction Fine-tuning
L112: We explore cross-lingual instruction fine-tuning to investigate whether supervised adaptation on MICL tasks can improve cross-lingual generalization to unseen languages and enhance the model’s ability to follow MICL prompt formats. During training, we compute loss only on the target transcription tokens, while treating the in-context pairs as conditioning inputs rather than prediction targets. Specifically, we minimize the negative log-likelihood of the target transcription:
L113:  | $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(w_{t}^{*}\mid w_{<t}^{*},a^{*},\mathcal{C}),$$  |  | (3)
L114: 
L115: where $w_{t}^{*}$ denotes the $t$-th token of the target transcription. This objective encourages accurate transcription of the target audio while allowing the model to learn how to utilize the multimodal context provided by the MICL prompt cite60†Abouelenin et al. (2025) .
L116: ### 2.5 ASR Hypothesis Selection System
L117: 
L118: We further develop an ASR hypothesis selection system that leverages MICL to re-rank hypotheses generated by an external acoustic-based ASR model, which is typically more robust than speech LLMs in low-resource settings.
L119: Given an input utterance $a_{1:S}$, an external ASR system first produces an $N$-best list of candidate transcriptions $\mathcal{H}=\{h^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N}$. We then score each hypothesis using two signals: (1) the acoustic score from the external ASR model, and (2) the LM score from a language model, which measures the contextual understanding. The final output is selected by re-ranking the $N$ candidates with a combined score:
L120:  | $\displaystyle\hat{h}=\arg\max_{h^{(k)}\in\mathcal{H}}\Big[\mathrm{Acoustic\_score}(h^{(k)};a_{1:S})$  |  | (4)
L121:  | $\displaystyle+\mathrm{LM\_score}_{\mathrm{MICL}}(h^{(k)};a_{1:S},\mathcal{C})\Big].$  |  | (5)
L122: We compute $\mathrm{LM\_score}$ as the log-likelihood of the hypothesis tokens under the language model. In preliminary experiments, using the LM score alone performs worse than the combined approach; so we use the combined scoring through experiments. Implementation details are referred to in the scripts.

L133: ### 3.2 Models
L134: 
L135: We experiment with two state-of-the-art open-source multimodal LLMs, Phi-4 and Qwen3-Omni, which are trained on diverse instruction-following datasets covering multiple modalities. This makes them particularly well suited for MICL, where models must understand the task from provided examples and generalize to unseen inputs. Details regarding model versions and inference settings are provided in Appendix cite29†A .
L136: ### 3.3 Fine-tuning
L137: To enhance MICL learning, we fine-tune the model on a customized MICL dataset derived from ML-SUPERB 2.0 cite73†Shi et al. (2024) , where each training instance is constructed to align with our prompt designs. Based on our preliminary results on different fine-tuning strategies (Appendix cite33†E ), we add LoRA layers to the decoders for fine-tuning while keeping the remaining parameters frozen.
L138: Under computational constraints and informed by our preliminary analysis on the number of samples (Appendix cite34†F ), all fine-tuning experiments use training instances with an in-context sample number randomly selected from 1 to 10. Detailed fine-tuning settings are provided in Appendix cite29†A .
L139: ### 3.4 Hypotheses Generation
L140: 
L141: We use the MMS model cite74†Pratap et al. (2024) to generate recognition hypotheses, as it demonstrates strong performance in low-resource speech recognition due to its self-supervised learning architecture and broad language coverage. Specifically, we fine-tune the model on each target language and decode with beam search to obtain 10 hypotheses.
L142: ### 3.5 Evaluation Metrics
L143: 
L144: We evaluate ICL performance with perplexity. For each test instance, we construct the prompt input, append the gold transcription, and run the model in a teacher-forcing manner. Perplexity is computed only over the gold transcription tokens, with the prompt treated as conditioning context. Lower perplexity indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the correct transcription and thus exhibits greater confidence under the given prompt.
L145: To evaluate ASR performance, we report word error rate (WER), where lower WER indicates better recognition accuracy.

L206: ### 4.3 Interpretable MICL
L207: We analyze how speech LLMs utilize multimodal demonstrations in MICL by examining attention weights. Our analysis focuses on Phi-4 as a representative speech LLM, in which a decoder attends over interleaved text and speech representations produced by modality-specific encoders. We note that attention is not a perfect explanation of model behavior. Speech LLMs can exhibit attention artifacts, such as attention sinks, and strong head-level specialization.
L208: Therefore, we treat attention statistics as a diagnostic signal rather than causal evidence cite77†Chefer et al. (2021) ; cite78†Kang et al. (2025a) ; cite79†Kang et al. (2025b) . Implementation details are provided in Appendix cite30†B .
L209: Imbalanced attention on modalities: We first quantify how much the model attends to the audio part versus the text part among all attention goes into the in-context demonstrations. Here, we average the attention mass across heads and layers, and then across the evaluation set. Table cite80†4 shows that the model allocates more attention to text tokens than to audio tokens in the demonstrations.
L210: This is understand the condition that the audio representation is roughly three times longer than the text representation under the setting of Phi-4 model (Appendix cite38†J ). Referring to previous work cite81†Baldassini et al. (2024) ; cite82†Chen et al. (2025a) showing multimodal LLMs mainly learn from text context and needs to pay more attention on visual modality, our finding confirms a similar imbalanced attention distribution for the speech modality.
L211: This observation indicates the potential to improve speech processing performance by designing LLMs to pay more attention to audio.
L212: We further observe that increasing the number of in-context samples shifts relatively more attention toward the audio samples. A possible explanation is that LLM is capable of understanding the task by learning the correspondence between audio and text within each example pair, and thus tends to increase attention on audios, which are as important as texts. We also show this capability of understanding the correspondence within one pair in Section cite26†5.2 .

L240: ### 4.4 MICL enhances ASR hypothesis selection
L241: Direct ASR transcription: For uncovered languages, speech LLMs can be prompted to perform ASR directly. However, prior work shows that prompting-based ASR is generally weak cite58†Hsu and yi Lee (2025) ; cite57†Zheng et al. (2025) . We verify this in our setting by prompting speech LLMs to generate transcriptions, with and without in-context demonstrations, on the datasets used in this work.
L242: As shown in Table cite91†5 , WER remain consistently high across models, indicating that ICL prompting alone is insufficient to achieve usable performance on these uncovered languages.
L243: MICL hypothesis selection: To address this limitation, we combine an stronger acoustic model with a speech LLM through hypothesis selection^{4}^{4} 4 Results of fine-tuning Phi-4 on ASR task are available in Appendix cite36†H . As the results in Table cite92†6 show, MICL-based hypothesis selection improves over the acoustic model baseline, indicating that speech LLMs can provide useful contextual guidance when used for re-ranking.
L244: Inspecting the results of Phi-4 fine-tuning, we find that cross-lingual instruction fine-tuning improves not only perplexity (Section cite20†4.1 ) but also hypothesis selection performance. The best results are still achieved with target-language fine-tuning, highlighting the value of supervised data in the target language when available.
L245: Comparing to Qwen3-Omni, we conclude cross-lingual fine-tuning enables Phi-4 to achieve comparable performance, even though Qwen3-Omni benefits from broad multilingual and multimodal pre-training coverage. Target-language fine-tuned Phi-4 outperforms Qwen3-Omni on Kichwa and Mboshi, while the gains are smaller on Khinalug, where the amount of supervised data is the most limited. This suggests that target-language fine-tuning is most beneficial when sufficient supervised data is available.
L246: Text-only LM selection: We further include several text-only re-ranking baselines, including Llama3B and two languages that are trained on target-language text, to assess whether text-only models can better capture contextual information. We find multimodal speech LLMs outperform Llama across all languages in our setup.
L247: Although comparisons across LLMs are not fully controlled due to differences in architecture and pre-training data, these results suggest that multimodal conditioning is more effective for hypothesis selection than using a general-purpose text-only LLM. Besides, we observe that cross-lingual fine-tuning yields better or comparable ASR performance than a language model trained specifically for the target language but without access to target-language training data, demonstrating the effectiveness of MICL.
L248: Hypothesis selection VS Joint decoding: We compare our approach with the popular joint decoding approach with an n-gram LM ( details in Appendix cite35†G ). Overall, joint decoding performs better than hypothesis selection in our setup.
L249: Integrating MICL in joint decoding might bring the best performance, but it is substantially more computationally expensive because joint decoding combines acoustic and language model scores at every decoding timestep, which limits practicality for large scale experiments and deployment.
L250: ## 5 Ablation study
L251: ### 5.1 Benefits of language coverage
L252: We show that cross-lingual instruction fine-tuning improves MICL performance on unseen languages. To better understand the role of multilingual coverage, we fine-tune the model using varying numbers of training languages and evaluate it on the same set of unseen languages. As shown in Table cite93†7 , perplexity on unseen languages consistently decreases as the number of fine-tuning languages increases, suggesting that broader language coverage leads to better cross-lingual generalization.
L253: We note that this trend may be influenced by both the language diversity and the total amount of fine-tuning data. Nevertheless, the results consistently indicate that increasing language coverage during fine-tuning is beneficial for MICL performance on unseen languages.

L259: ### 5.2 Attention allocation reflects what model see in ICL
L260: 
L261: Although we observe clear layer-dependent attention allocation patterns, attention weights alone do not guarantee that the model actually learns from the attended context. We therefore perform an intervention by replacing one in-context sample with gold information. Concretely, we replace its transcription with gold text, its audio with gold audio, or both.
L262: As Table cite94†8 shows,replacing the text of sample 4 substantially reduces perplexity, and replacing both audio and text further improves performance. This aligns with the attention heatmap in Figure cite95†2 in Appendix cite31†C , where attention shifts more strongly toward sample 4 after replacement. Together, these results suggest that attention allocation reflects actual in-context usage and that the model relies on attended samples for prediction.
L263: Moreover, Table cite94†8 and Figure cite95†2 shows that replacing the text sample increases attention to the corresponding audio sample, suggesting that the model understands MICL task by connecting the paired text and audio. In contrast, replacing only the audio yields little improvement, possibly because text is easier for the model to exploit, or audio-only cues are harder to leverage without strong textual reference.

L271: ## 6 Conclusion
L272: This work shows that MICL is effective at learning unseen low-resource languages by leveraging both speech and text modalities. We demonstrate that prompt-based ASR performs poorly on unseen languages, and we propose a MICL-based hypothesis selection approach that effectively combines MICL’s contextual understanding with a strong acoustic model.
L273: We further find that cross-lingual transfer learning without seeing target languages achieves performance comparable to models trained with target-language data, indicating the benefits of broader language coverage during LLM development. Finally, we reveal that MICL exhibits layer-dependent attention allocation with a preference for text samples, offering insights for developing more interpretable and effective multimodal LLMs.
L274: ## Limitations
L275: We evaluate MICL on a single ASR task. While this task is challenging, evaluating MICL on additional speech and multimodal tasks would further validate its generality. In addition, our experiments are conducted on three languages. Although the results are consistent across them, the overall language coverage remains limited. Our evaluation is restricted to two state-of-the-art open-source speech LLMs, as the proposed hypothesis selection approach requires access to model parameters.
L276: As a result, we do not assess MICL on closed-source LLMs, such as Gemini or GPT, and our findings may not directly generalize to such models.
