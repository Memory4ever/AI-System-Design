Abstract.
Backdoored and privacy-leaking deep neural networks pose a serious threat to the deployment of machine learning systems in security-critical settings. Existing defenses for backdoor detection and membership inference typically require access to clean reference models, extensive retraining, or strong assumptions about the attack mechanism. In this work, we introduce a novel LoRA-based oracle framework that leverages low-rank adaptation modules as a lightweight, model-agnostic probe for both backdoor detection and membership inference.
Our approach attaches task-specific LoRA adapters to a frozen backbone and analyzes their optimization dynamics and representation shifts when exposed to suspicious samples. We show that poisoned and member samples induce distinctive low-rank updates that differ significantly from those generated by clean or non-member data. These signals can be measured using simple ranking and energy-based statistics, enabling reliable inference without access to the original training data or modification of the deployed model.
Keywords: LoRA, Membership Inference Attack, Backdoor Attack
1. Introduction
As the adoption of large-scale machine/deep learning models continues to grow in both industries and the public sectors, so is the attention towards verification processes for data provenance, regulations and model security aspects.
In fact, most of the existing AI-based solutions make use of large models that are typically pre-trained on massive and heterogeneous corpora of data. The complexity of the adopted data makes it very difficult to certify its composition and security, raising important concerns about the quality of training and the reliability of the model obtained.
In particular, the most relevant aspects to consider are related to whether specific data sources were used during training and whether malicious manipulations occurred in the learning process.
These issues are even more critical in scenarios where the adopted models are open-source or somehow shared among entities and deployed inside the boundaries of organizations with limited or no knowledge on the training process and adopted training data.
On the other hand, one of the strongest drivers behind the rapid diffusion of AI-driven solutions in today’s IT landscape is the easy access to publicly available pre-trained models, which makes the aforementioned challenges particularly difficult to address natively.
Consequently, there is a pressing need for auditing and security solutions that are both computationally efficient and capable of capturing global signals related to data usage and training-time threat injections and model manipulations.
Regarding the first aspect, recent research has focused extensively on the development of auditing techniques aimed at verifying whether specific data points were included in the training process of a target model. Contributions in this line of work are commonly referred to as Membership Inference Attack (MIA) approaches (1, 2).
However, despite the high level of maturity reached by the research community thanks to the numerous existing contributions, most of these approaches typically rely on extensive querying, auxiliary shadow models, or strong assumptions about the original training set and access to the target model (3). As a result, existing proposals are often poorly suited to generic deployment scenarios in which the above demanding actions (such as building support shadow models) cannot be guaranteed.
Concerning the second aspect, the security of artificial intelligence solutions has also attracted a very large community of researchers, and, in recent years, an impressive volume of proposals have been developed (4).
However, a large proportion of these approaches target scenarios in which defenses can be deployed during the training phase (5, 6). For the reasons discussed above, while still important and effective in many practical settings, such approaches are not applicable to the a posteriori evaluation of the reliability of pre-trained models, which are a common asset in modern industrial scenarios.
On the other hand, approaches focusing on the post-training detection of anomalies in the model typically require either access to, at least a portion, of the original training data, or adopt specialized detection pipelines that analyze the internal activations of the model to identify potential poisoning effects or backdoors (7).
However, as noted above, obtaining access to even a small fraction of the training data, which preserves general statistical properties, is often infeasible. Moreover, as target models increase in complexity, approaches relying on in-depth analyses of internal components become increasingly impractical, or, in any case, poorly applicable in scenarios with limited computational budgets.
These issues are even more evident in the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), whose architectures are typically very complex and consist of billions of parameters. The huge size and, therefore, black-box nature of such models make comprehensive security auditing extremely difficult.
Targeting the aforementioned gaps in the current reference literature, in this work, we consider a defensive auditing setting in which a data contributor or model user seeks to verify properties of a pre-trained model. In particular, the objective of the audit is twofold: (i) to determine whether a specific dataset was used during pretraining, and (ii) to detect the presence of a hidden threats (such as a backdoor) in the model.
Borrowing some ideas from the strategy used to reduce LLM fine-tuning costs, we exploit parameter-efficient fine-tuning via Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) to probe the model representations.
In our approach, rather than performing exhaustive internal inspections or retraining the original target model, we rely on lightweight, low-rank updates to study the model behavior in an efficient way.
In practice, we develop a lightweight framework that analyzes the behavior of a target model by fine-tuning low-rank adapters on top of it. Observing the evolution of these adapters when exposed to specific inputs, we uncover systematic differences in the parameter updates and representation shifts caused by the controlled fine-tuning. Specifically, samples originating from the training set or poisoned produce distinctive low-rank adaptation patterns compared to clean or unseen data. Leveraging simple ranking and energy-based statistics, our method allows for an effective post-training auditing without requiring access to the original training corpus or the construction of computationally expensive shadow models.
In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows:
•
We introduce a novel lightweight auditing approach using Low-Rank Adapters (LoRAcle).
•
We define a novel methodology for Membership Inference based on the comparative analysis of low-rank adapter values on a target batch of data.
•
We extend the auditing capability to backdoor detection through a synthetic generation of a proxy batch of inputs for each candidate class by optimizing a stochastic objective towards high-confidence classification.
•
We show that parameter-efficient adapters can capture global training-time signals related to data usage and malicious manipulation, demonstrating that effective auditing is possible even when direct inspection of full model parameters is infeasible.
To validate our proposal, we carried out a thorough experimental validation over three benchmarking datasets and four most common model architectures. The obtained results show very satisfactory performance for both the auditing task, reaching more than 90\% accuracy for both the membership analysis and accuracy for backdoor identification on most of the analyzed settings.
Finally, our tests on real-life pre-trained models demonstrate the efficiency of our methodology with respect to related existing approaches for both Membership Inference and Backdoor detection.
2. Background
This section is dedicated to presenting the core concepts relevant to this work, including low-rank adaptation methods for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, membership inference attacks that threaten training data privacy, and backdoor attacks that compromise model integrity.
2.1. Low-Rank Adapters
State of the art deep learning models, like Large Language Models, are typically pre-trained on a large amount of data and adapted to downstream tasks through fine-tuning. However, performing fine-tuning on complete model parameters is often computationally costly, as state-of-the-art models contain billions of parameters, making it impractical to perform fine-tuning without access to server-grade GPUs. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods address this challenge by updating only a small subset of parameters while keeping the pretrained weights fixed.
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a representative PEFT method that constrains weight updates to a low-rank subspace (8). Consider a neural network parameterized by
(1)
\theta=\{W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{L}\},
where each W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k} denotes a weight matrix in a linear transformation.
Instead of directly updating W, LoRA introduces a low-rank decomposition of the weight update:
(2)
\Delta W=BA,
where
(3)
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\quad A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\quad r\ll\min(d,k).
Where r defines the rank or the adapter.
The adapted weight matrix is then given by
(4)
\widetilde{W}=W+\alpha BA,
where \alpha>0 is a scaling factor defined as \alpha=\frac{1}{r}.
Given an input vector x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}, the output of a LoRA-augmented linear layer can be written as
(5)
h=Wx+\alpha BAx.
By reducing the number of trainable parameters, LoRA enables efficient fine-tuning while preserving model performance. The modular nature of adapter parameters also introduces new security considerations, as adapters can be independently trained, shared, or modified (9, 10).
Given the demonstrated effectiveness of steering pretrained large models toward desired behaviors by updating only a small number of parameters, we were motivated to investigate whether such adapters can efficiently encode essential information from the pretrained model itself.
To demonstrate that LoRA can be deployed and used to audit a model under this approach, we concentrate on applying LoRA to models that differ from those on which it is typically used.
2.2. Membership Inference Attacks
Membership inference attacks (MIAs) (11) aim to determine whether a particular data sample was included in the training dataset of a machine learning model, thereby violating data privacy (1).
Let \mathcal{D}_{\text{pre}} denote the training dataset used to learn a model f_{\theta}. Given a sample x and the model output f_{\theta}(x), the attacker tries to infer whether x\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{pre}} using the membership variable
(6)
m(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{pre}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
An attack is formalized as a function
(7)
\mathcal{A}:(x,f_{\theta}(x))\rightarrow\{0,1\},
which aims to maximize the inference accuracy
(8)
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(x,f_{\theta}(x))=m(x)].
A common class of MIAs exploits the discrepancy between training and non-training samples, often quantified using the model loss:
(9)
\ell(x)=\mathcal{L}(f_{\theta}(x),y),
where \mathcal{L}(\cdot) denotes a task-specific loss function. Samples that obtain lower loss values are more likely to be inferred as members (2). More advanced attacks leverage confidence scores, gradients, or shadow models to improve inference accuracy (12).
Nowadays, users of online platforms generate large amounts of data that these platforms can exploit to train models that are difficult to audit, making it hard to determine whether a particular user’s data was used. Therefore, the goal of this approach is to propose an efficient strategy that enables the execution of membership inference attacks as a defensive mechanism to audit such models with reduced complexity.
2.3. Backdoor Attacks
Backdoor attacks (13) are training-time attacks that aim to embed hidden malicious behavior into a model while preserving its normal performance on clean inputs. The attacker’s objective is to preserve its functionalities on benign, unmodified samples; second, the model should reliably exhibit attacker-chosen behavior when specific triggers are present.
Backdoors can be introduced through several attack vectors, including poisoning the training data, injecting malicious code during training, or directly modifying the model parameters. In this work, we focus on data poisoning–based backdoor attacks, which are the most commonly studied and widely applicable.
Let the clean training dataset be denoted as
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
where x_{i}\in\mathcal{X} represents an input sample and y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y} its corresponding label. The attacker selects a trigger t and applies it to a subset of the training data. The fraction of poisoned samples is given by
\epsilon=\frac{M}{N},\quad M\ll N,
where M denotes the number of poisoned samples.
A trigger injection function \phi(\cdot) is used to generate a poisoned input
\hat{x}=\phi(x,t).
Depending on whether the attacker modifies the label, backdoor attacks can be categorized as either dirty-label or clean-label attacks. In dirty-label attacks, the original label is replaced with an attacker-chosen target label \hat{y}, whereas in clean-label attacks the ground-truth label remains unchanged.
Triggers can further be classified based on their generation strategy. Static (sample-agnostic) triggers remain identical across all poisoned samples, while dynamic (sample-specific) triggers vary depending on the input. In addition, attacks may be source-specific, where the trigger only activates the backdoor for inputs from a particular class, or source-agnostic, where the trigger is effective regardless of the input class.
The effectiveness of a backdoor attack is typically evaluated using the attack success rate (ASR), defined as the fraction of triggered test inputs that are classified as the target label. At the same time, clean accuracy is reported to measure the model’s performance on unmodified samples, ensuring that the backdoor does not significantly degrade benign behavior.
Backdoor attacks have been demonstrated across vision and language models, including large language models and parameter-efficient fine-tuning pipelines (14, 15, 16).
Backdoor attacks are poisoning threats executed by a malicious party during the training phase of a machine/deep learning model.
The objective of the adversary is to inject a hidden pattern into the model’s representation space that can later be leveraged at inference time to influence model behavior (14, 17, 18).
Differently from other poisoning threats, a backdoor attack aims at altering the behavior of the model towards a target class in the presence of a controlled pattern in the input, while preserving an almost unaltered general accuracy for benign input.
3. Methodology
This section is devoted to presenting the methodology of the proposed approach, starting from the Threat Model in Section 3.1 that presents the considered scenario, followed by the section that presents the proposed approach LoRAcle in its two modalities: Membership Inference in Section 3.2.1 and Backdoor detection mode in Section 3.2.2.
3.1. Threat Model
This paper considers a defensive auditing setting in which a data contributor or model owner has the objective of verifying properties of a pre-trained model using parameter-efficient fine-tuning through Low Rank Adapters (LoRA). The objective of the audit is twofold: (i) determine whether a specific set of data was used during pretraining, and (ii) detect the presence of a hidden backdoor target embedded during training.
The auditor is assumed to have access to the pre-trained model f_{\theta}. The auditor does not have access to any subset of the original pre-training data D_{pre}, nor any metadata describing its composition, and cannot observe the original training process.
The auditor is only allowed to act in a post-training scenario, where only fine-tuning using LoRA adapters can be performed. This assumption aligns with standard practice: LoRA is broadly used as a safe, computationally efficient fine-tuning approach and is frequently permitted explicitly, even in settings where full retraining or access to gradients is limited or disallowed.
The auditor can fine-tune LoRA adapters on selected batches to observe the resulting LoRA updates and their evolution over time.
In the data-use verification setting (membership inference), the aim of the auditor is to determine whether their data was used during training.
This scenario arises, for example, when a data owner wishes to verify compliance with licensing agreements, consent restrictions, or data deletion requests.
Given a batch of data B, the auditor use them to perform a fine-tuning of a LoRA and distinguish between the hypotheses
(10)
H_{0}:\;B\cap D_{pre}=\varnothing,\qquad H_{1}:\;B\subseteq D_{pre}
Membership inference is therefore framed as a diagnostic signal indicating prior representational alignment rather than as an attempt to reconstruct private training data.
In the backdoor auditing setting, the aim of the auditor is to detect whether the target model contains a backdoor introduced by a poisoned D_{pre} used for the pre-training. The auditor does not assume knowledge of trigger patterns and cannot directly activate the backdoor at inference time. Instead, the auditor probes the model by performing LoRA fine-tuning on a synthetic set of data, generated using the pre-trained model, and analyzing parameters of the obtained adapter. The goal is to identify anomalous behavior consistent with the presence of a pre-aligned malicious subspace. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods, and LoRA in particular, provide a natural mechanism for probing this structure.
3.2. LoRAcle
Our methodology relies on the observation that fine-tuning a pre-trained model on data it has already encountered tends to adjust the parameters along existing directions, making them better conditioned and easier to re-activate. In contrast, directions associated with unseen or rare data demand larger, less stable parameter changes.
By constraining updates to a low-rank subspace, LoRA forces the model to express its adaptation through directions that are already present or easily accessible in the pretrained model. As a result, the geometry of LoRA updates functions as an oracle revealing the degree of prior alignment between the pretrained model and the queried data.

3.2.1. Membership Inference Mode
Figure 2. Overview of the Membership Inference mode.
As introduced in Section 3.1, we first examine the membership inference problem in the setting where we are given a pre-trained model f_{\theta}, but have no information about its training procedure or access to the pre-training data D_{pre}. The goal is to determine whether a batch B of data was included in the training of f_{\theta}, inspecting only LoRA weights fine-tuned on B.
Formally for a frozen layer W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}, lora produces an adapted matix,
(11)
W^{\prime}=W+\Delta W,\qquad\Delta W=BA,
(12)
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n},\;B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r},\;r\ll\min(m,n)
We perform LoRA fine-tuning on B and track the resulting LoRA updates \Delta W over training epochs.
Let \{\Delta W^{(t)}(\mathcal{B})\}_{t=1}^{T} denote the LoRA updates at epoch t. This sequence represents the temporal response of the pre-trained model to repeated exposure to the batch B.
Then, from the full history, we summarize the batch-induced response through its norm trajectory.
In particular, we obtain h_{t}=\left\lVert\Delta W^{(t)}(\mathcal{B})\right\rVert_{2} and compute the mean magnitude \mu of the updates and standard deviation \sigma as follows:
(13)
\mu=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t},\ \sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(h_{t}-\mu)^{2}}
These quantities reflect two complementary facets of adaptation: the overall magnitude of the parameter correction required by each batch, and how stable that update remains over successive optimization steps. Intuitively, batches that are well aligned with the pretrained representation lead to small, smooth update trajectories, while misaligned batches require larger adjustments as optimization drives the model in new directions. Let \lVert W\rVert_{2} denote the norm of the frozen layer. We reduce the dimensionality o the LoRA trajectory into a two-dimensional physics-inspired space defined by:
(14)
E=\frac{\mu}{\lVert W\rVert_{2}+\varepsilon},\ C=\frac{\sigma}{\mu+\varepsilon}.
Where E is the relative energy of the LoRA updates, C is the optimization chaos and \varepsilon is a small constant to avoid division by 0.
The resulting representation (E,C) is a batch-level statistic that characterizes how strongly and how stably the pretrained model must adapt to the given batch B. From an information-geometric perspective, low values of E correspond to directions of low curvature in the loss landscape, while low values of C indicate well-conditioned optimization trajectories, both of which are consistent with prior exposure to B during pretraining.
By examining how these two values behave under different conditions, we can distinguish three distinct regimes prototypes,
(15)
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{r}=(E_{r},C_{r})\mid r\in\mathcal{R}\}.
These three regimes can be considered the centroid of three distinct behaviours, and the distance of the observed (E,C) from each of them can be used to weight the contribution of three separate experts, each focusing on different dynamics of the two dimensions. Given an embedding (E,C), we compute weighted distances:
(16)
d_{r}=\sqrt{\lambda_{E}(E-E_{r})^{2}+\lambda_{C}(C-C_{r})^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{E}>\lambda_{C}.
and convert them into soft regime assignments,
(17)
w_{r}=\frac{\exp(-d_{r}/\tau)}{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\exp(-d_{r^{\prime}}/\tau)}.
These soft weights reflect uncertainty about the underlying adaptation behavior and avoid hard assignments.
Each regime is associated with a specialized expert predictor whose assumptions match the local geometry of physics space.
The first expert is designed for regimes where LoRA updates are coherent but may have high magnitude. Given a reference weight matrix W and the sequence of LoRA updates \{\Delta W(t)\}_{t=1}^{T}, it computes a raw membership score
(18)
s_{\mathrm{exp}}=\text{score}(W,\Delta W,\{\Delta W(t)\}_{t=1}^{T}),
which captures both the overall magnitude of updates and their temporal consistency across fine-tuning epochs.
The second targets regimes where layer scales vary, or absolute update magnitudes are unreliable. For a reference weight matrix W and batch-induced LoRA updates \{\Delta W(t)\}_{t=1}^{T}, we first compute the mean update magnitude
(19)
\mu=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\Delta W(t)\|_{2},
and normalize it by the reference norm to obtain the relative energy
(20)
E_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{\mu}{\|W\|_{2}}.
We also compute a log-norm ratio
(21)
R=\log\frac{\|\Delta W\|_{2}}{\|W\|_{2}},
which captures scale-independent changes in the layer. The raw membership score is then defined as a combination of these two scale-invariant signals:
(22)
s_{\mathrm{exp}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma(\beta_{1}(\kappa_{1}-E_{\mathrm{rel}}))+\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma(\beta_{2}(\kappa_{2}-R)),
where \beta_{1} and \beta_{2} control the steepness of the sigmoid, \sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1} is the sigmoid function, and \kappa_{1},\kappa_{2} are regime-specific pivots. This score quantifies membership likelihood independently of absolute layer scale.
These hyperparameters, along with the pivots, are dynamically estimated from the observed batch statistics to remain robust across layers and architectures.
The third expert handles regimes where LoRA updates exhibit high temporal variability. For a reference weight matrix W and batch updates \{\Delta W(t)\}_{t=1}^{T}, we compute the mean update magnitude
(23)
\mu=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\Delta W(t)\|_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\|\Delta W(t)\|_{2}-\mu)^{2}},
and define the coefficient of variation as
(24)
\mathrm{CV}=\frac{\sigma}{\mu+\varepsilon}.
We then compute the relative energy
(25)
E_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{\mu}{\|W\|_{2}},
and adapt the energy pivot according to chaos,
(26)
\kappa(C)=\kappa_{0}+\gamma\,\mathrm{CV}.
The raw membership score combines a chaos-weighted energy component with a scale-invariant ratio component
(27)
s_{\mathrm{exp}}=\omega(\mathrm{CV})\,\sigma\big(\beta_{1}(\kappa(\mathrm{CV})-E_{\mathrm{rel}})\big)+(1-\omega(\mathrm{CV}))\,\sigma\big(\beta_{2}(\kappa_{2}-\log\tfrac{\|\Delta W\|_{2}}{\|W\|_{2}})\big),
where \omega(\mathrm{CV}) decreases with increasing chaos to down-weight unreliable energy signals, and \beta_{1},\beta_{2} control the sigmoid slopes. All hyperparameters (\kappa_{0},\gamma,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}) are estimated dynamically from the batch statistics, enabling the expert to adapt its sensitivity to local trajectory instability like the previous expert.
The overall membership score is obtained by marginalizing over regimes,
(28)
S=\sum_{r,exp\in R}w_{r}s_{exp},
where w_{r} is the soft assignment to regime r based on the physics-space embedding (E,C), and s_{e}xp is the raw membership score produced by the corresponding expert. This formulation can be interpreted as a Bayesian model averaging over competing hypotheses about the underlying adaptation dynamics.
The final decision is based on a time-dependent threshold rather than a fixed cutoff. As LoRA fine-tuning proceeds, even non-member data may induce nontrivial updates due to stochastic drift and low-rank overfitting. To account for this effect, we define the threshold as
(29)
\theta(T)=\frac{1}{2}+\alpha\log(1+T),
where the base value \frac{1}{2} corresponds to an uninformative prior, and \alpha>0 controls sensitivity to prolonged optimization. The logarithmic form reflects the empirical saturation of drift effects: false-positive risk grows quickly at early epochs and then stabilizes. This threshold can be interpreted as an implicit estimate of the upper envelope of non-member scores achievable under continued fine-tuning.
Finally, a batch is classified as originating from the pretraining distribution if S>\theta(T).
3.2.2. Backdoor Detection
Figure 3. Overview of the Backdoor Detection mode.
We consider the problem of detecting whether a pre-trained model contains a backdoor target class c_{t}. For each candidate class c, we synthesize a proxy batch of inputs B_{c}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} by optimizing a stochastic objective toward high-confidence classification:
(30)
x_{i}^{(t+1)}=x_{i}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{x_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{proxy}}(f_{\theta}(x_{i}^{(t)}),y=c)+\sigma\,\epsilon_{t},
where \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{proxy}} combines cross-entropy with contrastive and smoothness terms, \eta is the learning rate, and \epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1) injects stochasticity. Multiple strategies are run in parallel:
(1)
Sparse / localized: targets small patches,
(2)
Smooth / global: targets diffuse patterns,
(3)
Hybrid: convex combination of the two.
The best-performing strategy is selected by maximizing
(31)
\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{strategy}}=\lambda\,\overline{p_{c}}+(1-\lambda)\,\overline{\mathrm{sim}}(B_{c}),
where \overline{p_{c}} is the mean model confidence on the target class, \overline{\mathrm{sim}}(B_{c}) is the mean pairwise cosine similarity among the generated inputs, and \lambda\in[0,1] balances confidence and convergence.
Once the proxy batch is obtained, we perform LoRA adaptation of the frozen model and extract global LoRA signatures. Let W denote the concatenated pretrained weights and \Delta W_{c} the LoRA update induced by B_{c}. We compute scale-invariant metrics:
(32)
E_{c}=\frac{\|\Delta W_{c}\|_{2}}{\|W\|_{2}},\quad C_{c}=\frac{\langle W,\Delta W_{c}\rangle}{\|W\|_{2}\,\|\Delta W_{c}\|_{2}}.
To allow comparison across classes within a trial, we normalize via robust statistics:
(33)
\displaystyle z_{E}(c)=\frac{0.6745\,(E_{c}-\mathrm{median}(E))}{\mathrm{MAD}(E)},\quad
(34)
\displaystyle z_{C}(c)=\frac{0.6745\,(C_{c}-\mathrm{median}(C))}{\mathrm{MAD}(C)},
where MAD denotes the median absolute deviation.
A backdoor is expected to have a distinguished energy and high alignment across the different classes.
Therefore we assign to each class a physics-based score.
According to the dynamics of the two features, we distinguish three regimes.
When a single class exhibits a statistically isolated positive alignment peak, for all competing classes c^{\prime}, the score is driven primarily by cosine alignment:
(35)
S_{c}=\beta_{1}z_{C}(c)+\beta_{2}|z_{E}(c)|,\qquad\beta_{1}>\beta_{2}.
This regime corresponds to backdoors that induce coherent, directionally consistent LoRA updates.
If alignment is ambiguous but a single class exhibits anomalously relative energy,
(36)
z_{E}(c)\leq\mathrm{median}(z_{E})-\mathrm{MAD}(z_{E}),\qquad|z_{E}(c)|-|z_{E}(c^{\prime})|\geq\tau_{E},
the score emphasizes energy concentration:
(37)
S_{c}=\beta_{1}|z_{E}(c)|+\beta_{2}\max\{z_{C}(c),0\}.
This captures stealthy backdoors that exploit low-rank overfitting with minimal parameter displacement. The masking on relative alignment \max\{z_{C}(c),0\}, even if not discriminative on its own is done to keep only the top-k possible classes with high alignment.
When neither signal is individually decisive, we adopt a combined conservative score:
(38)
S_{c}=\beta_{1}|z_{E}(c)|+\beta_{2}\max\{z_{C}(c),0\},
which favors classes exhibiting simultaneous energy concentration and positive alignment while suppressing anti-aligned updates.
The thresholds \tau_{C} and \tau_{E} are estimated dynamically from empirical score gaps within each trial using median-based dispersion, avoiding explicit clean calibration.
Finally, the procedure is repeated over multiple independent trials t=1,\dots,T, and the final decision is based on rank stability:
(39)
\displaystyle\mathrm{MeanRank}_{c}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{rank}_{t}(c),
(40)
\displaystyle\mathrm{RankVar}_{c}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathrm{rank}_{t}(c)-\mathrm{MeanRank}_{c})^{2}.
A class is flagged as a backdoor target if it consistently achieves the top rank with low variance, indicating a persistent, isolated attractor in LoRA adaptation space.
3.2.3. Scope and intent.
We emphasize that Loracle is not intended to propose a new state-of-the-art defense for either backdoor detection or membership inference. Instead, it is introduced as an analysis-driven framework that uses LoRA as a controlled diagnostic tool to probe how security-relevant information is encoded in pretrained models. By restricting adaptation to low-rank parameter subspaces, the approach exposes geometric signatures—such as energy concentration, directional alignment, temporal stability, and regime-dependent behavior that are indicative of both backdoor targets and data membership effects. In this sense, Loracle should be viewed as a principled starting point for the development of future methods that leverage LoRA not only for efficient fine-tuning, but also as an effective lens for extracting and interpreting latent vulnerability signals in large neural models.
4. Results
This section is devoted to presenting the results obtained from the experimental campaign, which assesses the performance of the proposed approach across the different scenarios and modalities, for a total of more than 4{,}000 experiments conducted to complete the collection of results.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets
We evaluate our method on MNIST (19), CIFAR-10 (20), CIFAR-100 (20), and GTSRB (21), which are selected for their diversity and varying levels of difficulty across the two considered scenarios. In particular, we include datasets that share many common features across different classes, such as MNIST and GTSRB, as well as datasets with a large number of labels, such as CIFAR-100 and again GTSRB, in order to test the limits of target label prediction in the backdoor detection mode.
MNIST consists of simple grayscale images; its inclusion ensures that grayscale data are considered in the evaluation, as such images frequently occur in real-world applications, including medical imaging. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 offer mid-level complexity with 10 and 100 classes, respectively, composed of RGB images representing different real-world objects. Finally, GTSRB provides a dataset of 43 classes, each corresponding to a distinct German traffic sign.
Models
In our experiments, we consider ResNet18 (22), VGG19 (23), ViT (24), and DenseNet (25). These cover both convolutional and transformer-based architectures, intentionally different from the common usage of LoRA adapters to prove the portability of the approach even in other domains different from Natural Language Processing.
Scenarios
To perform the experiments presented in the following, we define two different experimental setups with different assumptions, according to the modality under consideration: Membership Inference or Backdoor Detection mode.
Starting from the membership inference setting, in this scenario the auditor, having access to a batch of data to test, can better tune some parameters, and in particular the scaling factor \alpha, by selecting a higher or lower rank r according to the characteristics of the considered data, thus adjusting the “degrees of freedom” of the adapter. A higher rank corresponds to a larger amount of freedom. In the case of datasets sharing many common features, such as MNIST or GTSRB, we set r=2; for more diverse datasets, such as CIFAR-10, we instead set r=8.
Regarding the placement of the LoRA adapter, for ResNet18 and VGG19 we naively attach the adapter to the fully connected layer at the output of the feature extractor. For DenseNet, we instead connect the adapter to the transition blocks. In the case of ViT, we inject LoRA into the query and value projections of each transformer block, in order to better capture the information extracted from the image features.
To obtain the members to test, we split the training dataset into fractions of size 0.1 and pretrain two models for each split: one model includes the considered fraction during pretraining, while the other excludes it, thus simulating the member and non-member scenarios.
In the Backdoor Detection scenario, we adopted a completely blind scenario, naively injecting LoRA on the output of each feature extractor for every model, since backdoor attacks are usually more detectable on the output layers due to the confidence of the model given the trigger with rank r=8 since we don’t have information on D_{pre}.
For the backdoor detection experiments, we consider three widely used backdoor attack strategies: BadNets, Blended, and WaNet. BadNets (14) injects a fixed trigger pattern into a subset of the training samples, causing the model to associate the trigger with a target label. Blended (15) attacks generate more stealthy triggers by blending a trigger pattern with the original image, making the backdoor harder to visually detect. WaNet (16) introduces a warping-based trigger by applying a smooth spatial transformation to the input images, resulting in a backdoor that is input-agnostic and particularly challenging to identify due to the absence of explicit pixel-level patterns.
To reduce the risk of cherry-picking, we perform each run by varying the target label. In particular, we use all 10 labels for CIFAR-10 and MNIST, while for the remaining two datasets, which contain more than 10 labels, we randomly select 10 target labels.
We perform the experiments by varying the poisoning rate of the attacks over the range \{0.01,0.05,0.1,0.2\}, in order to assess the capability of the proposed approach even in the presence of weaker signals or non-optimally performing attacks.

As a first empirical validation, we analyze the individual geometric features used in the membership score.
Figure 4 shows the distributions of cosine alignment, relative update magnitude and trajectory chaos computed over LoRA adaptations induced by member and non-member data batches.
Member batches exhibit positive alignment with the pretrained weights smaller relative energy as expected.
In contrast, non-member batches produce weakly aligned or noisy updates that disperse energy across different directions and require larger parameter displacement.
These complementary effects motivate the combined score, as no single signal alone fully separates the two regimes, while their joint geometry yields a clear and stable separation.
Beyond static geometry, we further observe a systematic difference in their trajectories. Member-induced LoRA updates follow coherent and repeatable trajectories, indicating that optimization consistently exploits well-aligned directions already present in the pretrained model. Non-member batches, by contrast, induce more chaotic and less stable update trajectories, similar to energy, reflecting exploratory optimization in regions of parameter space not strongly supported by the learned representation. This dynamical contrast reinforces the geometric separation and suggests that membership information is encoded jointly in both the orientation and the stability of LoRA updates, rather than in their magnitude alone.

4.5. Computational Efficiency
A key motivation behind our approach is to enable practical backdoor and membership analysis on modern models without requiring datacenter-grade hardware.
All experiments in this section are conducted on models pretrained with 224\times 224 images, a regime that is representative of contemporary large-scale vision pipelines and significantly more demanding than classical low-resolution benchmarks.
We compare LoRAcle against several existing defenses, as in (4), we prioritize post-training defenses, as LoRAcle, with standard interfaces and minimal implementation dependencies to enable fair comparisons
The analysis is conducted in terms of GPU power consumption (watts) and peak memory usage (GB).
All methods are executed on a consumer-grade GPU with 16 GB of VRAM whenever possible.
Results are reported in Figure 7.
LoRAcle is the only method that consistently executes across all architectures within the memory constraints of a 16 GB GPU.
In contrast, most competing defenses either fail to initialize due to out-of-memory errors or exceed hardware limits during optimization.
This gap becomes particularly pronounced for transformer-based models, where full-parameter or activation-heavy defenses are prohibitively expensive.
Beyond feasibility, LoRAcle also exhibits favorable energy characteristics.
By restricting optimization to low-rank LoRA adapters and freezing the backbone, the method avoids repeated high-cost backward passes through the full network.
As a result, both power draw and memory usage remain stable across architectures, even for ViT models operating at high resolution.
This behavior stands in sharp contrast to defenses such as IBAU, NAD, and BadExpert, which require either full-model retraining or large intermediate buffers.
These results demonstrate that LoRA-based analysis is not merely an optimization convenience but also enables auditing strategies like the ones proposed in this paper.
It allows security diagnostics to be performed on widely available hardware, lowering the barrier to deployment and reproducibility.

This efficiency is especially critical for transformer-based vision models, where traditional defenses fail to scale even on moderately powerful consumer hardware.
Loracle thus provides a practical foundation for future security analysis methods that seek to exploit low-rank structure rather than fight against it.
5. Limitations and Future Work
As evidenced by the results, our intuition that LoRA adapters can be leveraged to audit models, both for detecting the presence of private training data and for identifying target backdoors in poisoned models, is supported in controlled experiments. While the observed performance is promising, there remains substantial room for improvement.
For backdoor detection, our current approach naively attaches a LoRA adapter to the fully connected layer at the output of the feature extractor, without considering model-specific characteristics, as is done in the membership inference setting. This simplification was intended to probe the limits of our method under constrained scenarios. In this context, the generation of proxy data constitutes a current bottleneck: in some cases, the synthesized inputs are insufficiently representative, limiting the effectiveness of the LoRA adaptation. More targeted and adaptive data generation strategies could substantially enhance performance beyond the generic approach used here.
Additionally, the fully blind setting, where no access to real target data is assumed, may be overly restrictive. Variants that exploit small batches of real data could improve detection rates and provide a more practical path toward real-world applicability. Another promising direction is to design a pretraining-time mode that monitors LoRA adaptation trajectories to detect poisoned or anomalous examples, assuming access to training data.
We emphasize that this analysis is not intended as a state-of-the-art membership inference attack. Performance is evaluated at the batch level and depends on access to LoRA adaptation trajectories, which may not be available in all threat models. Furthermore, the method is sensitive to architectural inductive biases: models with weaker geometric constraints, such as Vision Transformers, exhibit reduced recall. Finally, our study focuses on relatively small datasets and controlled fine-tuning regimes; extending these insights to large-scale, highly diverse pretraining corpora remains an important open direction for future work.
Overall, this work establishes LoRA-based auditing as a promising diagnostic tool and provides a foundation for developing more sophisticated, efficient, and architecture-aware security analyses in future research.
6. Related Work
Low-Rank Adapters (8) were introduced to enable efficient training of large models by updating only a small subset of parameters, making fine-tuning feasible even on less capable GPUs. As demonstrated by the authors, for GPT-3, LoRA can reduce the number of trainable parameters by up to four orders of magnitude and lower the computational hardware requirements by roughly a factor of three compared to full fine-tuning. Despite this substantial reduction, LoRA achieves performance that is on par with, or in some cases superior to, full fine-tuning on both GPT-3 and GPT-2, while benefiting from fewer trainable parameters, higher training throughput, and no additional inference latency.
LoRA proved its effectiveness in different scenarios and domains, including security, which is the focus of this paper. The authors of
(9) to address the challenges of robust fine-tuning in self-supervised learning, explored the integration of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) into adversarial training. One such approach performs adversarial training during the fine-tuning stage rather than during pre-training, mitigating the risk of catastrophic forgetting. LoFT further leverages LoRA to restrict updates to a low-rank parameter space, updating only 1%–5% of the pre-trained model parameters, thereby enabling efficient and memory-friendly fine-tuning while maintaining robustness against adversarial perturbations.
LoRA canbe effecently deploued also in distributed scenarios expsosing to different vulnerabilities. Recent efforts in secure and efficient fine-tuning of large language models have explored integrating cryptographic techniques with parameter-efficient training. In this context, FedShield-LLM (10) introduces a novel federated fine-tuning mechanism that combines fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) with model pruning. This integrates FHE with pruning in federated LLM fine-tuning, aiming to mitigate vulnerabilities during both training and inference, including scenarios with an honest-but-curious server. Furthermore, FedShield-LLM restricts updates to LoRA adapter layers rather than the full model parameters, significantly reducing computational and memory overhead and enabling secure fine-tuning even in resource-constrained environments such as edge devices.
More in line with this paper, approaches have also been proposed also in the direction of membership inference attacks.
LoRA-Leak (27) was proposed as a comprehensive framework to assess the vulnerability of LoRA-based fine-tuned models to MIAs. LoRA-Leak evaluates privacy risks across multiple research questions, including whether MIAs remain a threat for LoRA-based models, whether access to pre-trained model information amplifies attacks, and which LoRA fine-tuning strategies can mitigate risk.
Furthermore, the analysis demonstrates that incorporating pre-trained models into the attack pipeline consistently increases privacy risks.
Recent work (28) has investigated the privacy risks of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) in latent diffusion models (LDMs), which are used to adapt models on private datasets for targeted image generation. Despite their efficiency, LoRA-adapted LDMs remain vulnerable to membership inference (MI) attacks, which can reveal whether a particular data point was part of the private dataset. To mitigate this, MP-LoRA was proposed, formulating the adaptation as a min-max optimization problem where a proxy attack model maximizes its MI gain while the LDM minimizes the sum of the adaptation loss and the proxy’s MI gain.
Although the security of LoRA adapters against membership inference attacks has been studied, to the best of our knowledge, no work has investigated the use of LoRA adapters for explainability purposes, specifically to detect the presence of a batch of data during the pre-training of models.
On the backdoor front, several studies have investigated both vulnerabilities of LoRA adapters to attacks and strategies to defend them. While LoRA adapters offer parameter-efficient fine-tuning for large language models, their lightweight and shareable nature makes them susceptible to backdoor injection, which can cause malicious or incorrect outputs. To systematically study this risk, PADBench (29) was introduced as a large-scale benchmark containing over 13,000 benign and backdoored adapters across multiple datasets, attack strategies, PEFT methods, and LLMs. Building on this benchmark, PEFTGuard was proposed as the first framework specifically designed to detect backdoors in PEFT-based adapters, achieving near-perfect detection accuracy and demonstrating zero-shot transferability across attack types, adapter ranks, and fine-tuning methods.
The authors of LoBAM (30) introduce a method that intelligently amplifies malicious weights to achieve a high attack success rate while requiring minimal computational resources, demonstrating that LoRA-based models are still vulnerable under realistic constraints.LoBAM further demonstrates that by intelligently combining the weights of a benign and a malicious LoRA-fine-tuned model to amplify attack-relevant components, high backdoor success can be achieved even with minimal computational resources.
Even in the context of security, research has begun to explore the use of LoRA adapters, not only as targets of attacks, but also as tools to detect backdoors in poisoned models. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has fully leveraged the explainability potential of LoRA adapters to extract detailed information about the training data or model behavior.
The primary objective of this paper is to highlight and systematically investigate how LoRA adapters can serve as a lens for model interpretability, revealing insights about the presence of malicious patterns, data influence, and other hidden characteristics in fine-tuned models. By examining the information encoded within the adapters, we aim to demonstrate their utility beyond parameter-efficient fine-tuning, positioning them as a novel tool for both explainability and security analysis.
7. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a novel oracle framework for deep model audit after training, termed LoRAcle, with a focus on both data provenance and security. Our proposal seeks efficiency and feasibility by exploiting Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) and framing auditing as a lightweight fine-tuning problem.
To define our methodology, we performed a thorough study of the characteristics of optimization dynamics and representation shifts of task-specific LoRA adapters for the target model when exposed to controlled samples.
We then defined two different auditing strategies for Membership Inference and Backdoor detection using specific patterns in the LoRA adapter variations.
Due to the lightweight nature of LoRA, our approach addresses key practical constraints that limit the applicability of existing MIA and post-training Backdoor detection mechanisms.
Our experimental results on benchmarking datasets and popular model architectures demonstrate that our approach can effectively capture signals associated with the use of specific training datasets or the possible poisoned state of the model.
This suggests that LoRA is not just useful for lightweight parameter-efficient specializations of pre-trained models to specific tasks and domains but can also be exploited as a tool for post-training analysis of the model behavior and state.
We believe that this novel perspective can open new directions for model interpretability and security.
In particular, the proposed methodology can be extended in the future to create efficient and lightweight unlearning and backdoor sanitization solutions, thus allowing a further interesting step toward building more transparent, compliant, and trustworthy machine learning solutions.
