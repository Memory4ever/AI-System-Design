Agata Żywot Affiliation: University of Amsterdam Email: agata.zywot@student.uva.nl Xinyi Chen Affiliation: University of Amsterdam Email: x.chen2@uva.nl Maarten de Rijke Affiliation: University of Amsterdam Email: M.deRijke@uva.nl Abstract This report studies whether small, tool-augmented agents can match or outperform larger monolithic models on the GAIA benchmark. Using Qwen3 models (4B–32B) within an adapted Agentic-Reasoning framework, we isolate the effects of model scale, explicit thinking (no thinking, planner-only, or full), and tool use (search, code, mind-map). Tool augmentation provides the largest and most consistent gains. Using tools, 4B models can outperform 32B models without tool access on GAIA in our experimental setup. In contrast, explicit thinking is highly configuration- and difficulty-dependent: planner-only thinking can improve decomposition and constraint tracking, while unrestricted full thinking often degrades performance by destabilizing tool orchestration, leading to skipped verification steps, excessive tool calls, non-termination, and output-format drift. 

1 Introduction 
Agentic-AI systems are increasingly leveraging language models to autonomously plan, reason, and act. While Large Language Models (LLMs) have traditionally served as the backbone for these systems, recent work argues that smaller language models (SLMs) may be better suited for many agentic tasks due to their efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and modular architecture Belcak et al. (2025) . 
Despite these advantages, SLMs are often perceived as fundamentally limited by weaker reasoning and planning abilities compared to frontier-scale models. A promising alternative to pure scaling is to augment models with explicit tools and structured reasoning mechanisms. Frameworks such as Agentic-Reasoning demonstrate that equipping language models with specialized tool-agents—such as web search, code execution, and external memory—can substantially improve performance on multi-step reasoning tasks Wu et al. (2025) . This raises a question: can architectural and agentic design compensate for smaller model size? 
In this work, we investigate whether an agentic decomposition using small, tool-augmented models can match or outperform a single large monolithic model. We focus specifically on three factors: model size , thinking , and tool usage . Understanding their effects is crucial for designing efficient agentic systems that do not rely exclusively on scaling. 
We study these questions using the GAIA benchmark Mialon et al. (2023) , which targets agentic capabilities across three difficulty levels (L1–L3), including multi-hop reasoning, planning, and tool use. Specifically, we address the following research questions: 
• RQ1: What are the capabilities of small models when embedded in a collaborative agentic framework? 

• RQ2: Can a collaboration of small agents outperform a single larger model? 

• RQ3: How do explicit thinking and tool usage contribute to performance across model scales? 

Our contributions can be summarized as the following findings: 
• Small models equipped with tool-augmented agentic architectures can outperform much larger monolithic models on GAIA. 

• Tool usage provides larger and more consistent gains than explicit thinking alone, particularly for smaller models. 

• The effectiveness of thinking depends on both model scale and agent role: planner-only thinking benefits instruction-tuned small models (4B-Instruct), but does not consistently help base 4B models, while full thinking frequently degrades accuracy by disrupting tool coordination—particularly on computation-heavy and long-horizon coordination tasks. 

2 Related Work 
2.1 Tool-Augmented Language Models 
A growing body of work demonstrates that external tools significantly enhance the problem-solving abilities of language models. Systems integrating web search, code execution, or retrieval mechanisms enable models to overcome knowledge cutoffs and computational limitations. The Agentic-Reasoning framework Wu et al. (2025) exemplifies this approach by decomposing reasoning into planning and tool-invocation stages. While prior work shows strong gains for large models, it remains unclear to what extent such gains transfer to smaller backbones. 
2.2 Explicit Thinking and Planning 
Explicit reasoning mechanisms, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and planner-style decomposition, have been shown to improve performance on arithmetic and symbolic reasoning benchmarks by encouraging step-by-step problem decomposition Wei et al. (2023) . However, recent work highlights important limitations of explicit thinking. CoT prompting can obscure hallucination cues and complicate model behavior, e.g., leading models to overcommit to intermediate steps that are incorrect Cheng et al. (2025) . Moreover, explicit reasoning does not universally improve performance and can even degrade accuracy by introducing unnecessary or noisy intermediate steps, particularly when model capacity is limited Zheng et al. (2025) . As a result, when and where explicit thinking is beneficial—especially in tool-augmented agentic settings—remains an open research question. 
2.3 Multi-Agent Collaboration 
Multi-agent systems distribute reasoning across specialized components or roles. Approaches such as ToolOrchestra Su et al. (2025) and MaAS Zhang et al. (2025) show that collaboration can outperform monolithic systems while reducing cost. However, most existing work focuses on architectural search, leaving open how collaboration interacts with explicit thinking and tool usage across model sizes. Our work directly addresses this gap. 
3 Methodology 
3.1 Framework Adaptation 
We build on the Agentic-Reasoning framework Wu et al. (2025) , adapting it for local execution with open-source models. A key constraint is GPU memory, which motivates our decision to use a single shared model instance with locking. Instead of deploying separate models for each agent role, we dynamically re-role a single backbone via specialized system prompts. This preserves modular reasoning while remaining feasible on academic hardware. 
3.2 Tool-Agent Integration 
We integrated and adapted three primary tool-agents to handle the multi-step requirements of complex reasoning tasks, build upon the Agentic Reasoning implementation. The tools are defined as follows: 
1. Web-Search Agent: Utilizes a RAG-based query breakdown to decompose complex questions into searchable sub-queries. It iteratively retrieves and filters web content to provide the Planner with grounded context. 

2. Coding Agent: Provides a sandbox environment for algorithmic execution. It is triggered when tasks require precise mathematical calculations, data manipulation, or the parsing of structured formats. 

3. Mind-Map Agent: Designed for long-context management, constructs a dynamic knowledge graph (Mind-Map) during the reasoning process. It allows the system to store, link, and retrieve intermediate findings, preventing "forgetting" during long-horizon tasks. 

We compare standard single-model inference with an agentic setup where the same backbone model alternates between planning, searching, coding, and memory roles using different prompts. Tools are kept identical across all runs. 
4 Experiments 
4.1 Experimental Setup 
Models. We evaluate Qwen3 models ranging from 4B to 32B parameters Yang et al. (2025) . This range allows us to test whether scaling alone can replace explicit tool usage and whether smaller models benefit more from agentic augmentation. We additionally include the 4B-Instruct variant to assess the impact of tool- and instruction-focused fine-tuning. 
Datasets. We use the GAIA benchmark Mialon et al. (2023) , which is explicitly designed to evaluate agentic behavior. GAIA Level 1 primarily tests factual retrieval and shallow reasoning, Level 2 emphasizes multi-hop reasoning and planning, and Level 3 requires complex coordination between reasoning and tools. This stratification enables analysis of capability breakdowns. We use the validation split, comprising 165 questions (53, 86 and 26 per Level 1-3, respectively). 
Evaluation Metrics. We report accuracy (ACC) overall and per difficulty level. Accuracy directly reflects whether an agentic system successfully completes the task end-to-end, aligning with our research questions about practical capability rather than intermediate reasoning quality. 
Reasoning and Tool Usage. We evaluate three reasoning configurations: no explicit thinking, planner-only thinking, and full thinking across all agents. This allows us to isolate whether explicit reasoning helps planning, execution, or both.

4.2 Experimental Results 
We test the following hypotheses:
(1) tool usage provides larger gains than scaling alone, and
(2) explicit thinking benefits depend on model size and agent role.
The results of our experiments, summarized in Table 1 , demonstrate a consistent performance uplift when transitioning from monolithic execution to an agentic framework. 
Tool usage helps small agents beat bigger models. Models as small as 4B, when equipped with tool usage (e.g., 4B-Instruct + Agentic achieves 18.18% ACC), outperform a 32B model without tools (32B No-Tools: 12.73%). This finding challenges the assumption that scaling is the primary driver of the performance, highlighting the importance of structured interaction with external tools. 
Thinking generally helps but not always. Explicit thinking yields mixed effects that strongly depend on both model scale and the surrounding agentic structure. In No-Tools settings, enabling thinking provides consistent gains across all model sizes, suggesting that explicit reasoning partially compensates for the absence of external tools. In contrast, within the Agentic framework, explicit thinking is not universally beneficial. 
For smaller models (4B and 4B-Instruct), full thinking often degrades performance relative to planner-only or no-thinking configurations. We attribute this to reasoning–action misalignment: limited-capacity models tend to generate verbose or low-quality intermediate reasoning that diverges from the task objective, resulting in incorrect tool selection, poorly specified tool inputs, or hallucinated intermediate conclusions. 
Interestingly, even the largest model (32B) achieves its best performance with thinking disabled in the agentic setup. This suggests that sufficiently large models can internally coordinate tool usage in a near-zero-shot manner, and that additional explicit reasoning may introduce redundant steps or error-prone control loops. Overall, these findings may indicate that explicit thinking should be applied selectively: while beneficial for planning and for weaker models without tools, it can hinder performance when it interferes with reliable tool orchestration in multi-agent systems.

5 Analysis 
5.1 Model Size vs. Accuracy and Tool Efficiency 
We analyze how model scale interacts with tool usage patterns and efficiency, focusing on whether higher accuracy arises from better tool coordination rather than increased tool invocation. Figures 1 , 2 , and 3 jointly reveal that effective agentic behavior is characterized not by more tool calls, but by better-aligned and more selective tool usage. 
Across all configurations, the Web-Search Agent dominates tool usage, accounting for approximately 70–90% of total calls. This reflects the retrieval-heavy nature of GAIA tasks, particularly at lower difficulty levels. However, the relative distribution of tools shifts systematically with both model size and reasoning configuration. In the No Thinking setting, tool usage tends to be more conservative: models primarily rely on Search for direct fact retrieval, invoking the Coding Agent only when numerical computation is explicitly required. This behavior is especially pronounced for smaller models, which exhibit limited diversification beyond search. 
Introducing Planner Thinking leads to the highest tool diversity across scales. This effect is most visible for the 8B model, where Mind-Map usage rises sharply (up to 29.4% of calls), indicating an increased reliance on external memory for intermediate state tracking. Importantly, this diversification does not uniformly translate to higher accuracy, suggesting that tool variety alone is insufficient without proper tool coordination. 
In the Full Thinking configuration, larger models (14B and 32B) maintain relatively stable tool-use ratios, while smaller models exhibit increased volatility and less consistent gains. This suggests that higher-capacity models are better able to integrate explicit reasoning with tool execution, whereas smaller models may struggle to translate internal reasoning into effective external actions. 
Moreover, both planner-only and full thinking configurations consistently increase total tool calls across all model sizes, reflecting longer control loops and more frequent tool re-invocation. However, this increase does not reliably translate into higher accuracy, especially for smaller models. 
The difficulty of the task additionally shapes these effects. As shown in Figure 2 , Level 1 tasks are dominated by search-based retrieval, while Levels 2 and 3—although still search intensive—increasingly require coding-based computation. Correspondingly, Figure 3 shows the expected pattern: accuracy decreases with task level. However, total tool calls peak at Level 2 but decline at Level 3, despite the latter being more difficult. This pattern suggests that failures on the hardest tasks may arise from early planning breakdowns—where the agent fails to invoke the appropriate tools at all—rather than from errors during tool execution itself.
Overall, higher accuracy from scaling can be associated with more stable and better-aligned tool orchestration rather than increased tool usage. 
5.2 Thinking Effects in Multi-Agent Collaboration: Qualitative Analysis 
Across all models, thinking helps when it sharpens tool selection and constraint preservation, and hurts when it alters the controller’s tool policy or induces non-terminating action loops. 
We analyze when and why explicit thinking helps or hurts performance, linking outcomes to model size, task difficulty, and agent role (Web-Search, Coding, Mind-Map). Throughout this section, NT denotes the tool-enabled no-thinking setup, and T denotes the corresponding thinking-enabled setup (planner-only or full thinking, depending on the configuration). We focus on questions where the correctness outcome changes between NT and T. 
Effect of Thinking Across Models and Difficulty. Table 1 shows that thinking is not uniformly beneficial; its effects concentrate at specific GAIA levels and depend on model size. For 4B, full thinking reduces overall accuracy from 13.33% to 9.09% and collapses Level 2 from 16.28% to 3.49%, despite improving Level 1 (15.09% to 20.75%). For 8B, full thinking increases overall accuracy from 10.30% to 16.36% (Level 1: 18.87% to 30.19%; Level 2: 6.98% to 11.63%) but does not improve Level 3 (3.85% in both cases) and increases controller failures. For 32B, full thinking reduces overall accuracy from 25.45% to 23.03%, with the sharpest drop at Level 3 (11.54% to 3.85%). Overall, explicit thinking is most brittle when tasks require tight retrieval → \rightarrow compute → \rightarrow format coupling (Level 2 for small models) and long-horizon tool coordination (Level 3 for larger models). 
5.2.1 Why Thinking Hurts 
Thinking hurts when it fails to control or coordinate across tool-agents. The dominant failure modes are not factual inaccuracies but mismatches between reasoning and action: (1) skipping the correct tool, (2) tool-call thrashing/loops that never terminate in a final answer, and (3) output-contract drift (formatting/constraints). 
1. Skipping required tools. Thinking can change the controller’s tool policy and cause it to omit search/computation steps that were crucial in the NT run. The thinking-enabled run spends many calls assembling intermediate notes (e.g., lists of one-athlete countries and candidate IOC codes) via repeated Search and Mind-Map queries, but never performs the final step required by the prompt: outputting a single IOC code. Under full thinking, tool-agent outputs are often verbose and structured (e.g., Markdown-like headings/lists) rather than a planner-ready, decision-critical fact. As a result, the planner keeps re-querying for the missing key detail and is more likely to surface an intermediate heading as the final answer instead of the required IOC code. 

5.2.2 Why Thinking Helps 
Thinking usually helps with planning, which improves tool selection, constraint checking, and multi-hop decomposition. More specifically, it typically improves performance by (1) improving decomposition and tool selection instead of guessing/refusing, and/or (2) preserving hard constraints (units, shortest-path criteria, output format) across multiple steps. 
1. Multi-hop decomposition via coordinated tool use. For 8B (T = full thinking), thinking helps on a shortest-link-path question by distributing work across agents:

Implications for Agentic Systems. Across scales, explicit thinking helps when it improves tool orchestration and constraint preservation , but hurts when it destabilizes the controller ( tool omission, looping/non-termination, or format drift ). Practically, these examples support selective thinking and avoiding uncontrolled full thinking that increases tool-call depth without termination. This aligns with the broader finding that higher performance is driven by better-aligned tool usage, not more tool calls. 
6 Conclusion 
Tool augmentation provides the most reliable performance gains across model sizes: agentic configurations consistently outperform their no-tool counterparts, and small models with tools can rival or exceed much larger models without tools. However, explicit thinking is not always beneficial. Its effect depends on model capacity, task difficulty, and the agent role in the tool pipeline. In particular, we observe that full thinking can increase tool usage and latency while occasionally reducing accuracy, due to tool selection errors (skipping the Coding Agent when verification is needed), controller instability (tool-call loops ending in <tool_call> ), and output-contract drift (failing strict formatting requirements). These failure modes are most pronounced on tasks requiring tight retrieval → \rightarrow compute → \rightarrow format coupling (Level 2 for small backbones) and long-horizon coordination (Level 3 for larger backbones). 
More broadly, our results suggest that explicit reasoning should be treated as a controllable resource in agentic systems—applied selectively to planning or constraint checking, rather than uniformly across all agent roles. When model capacity is limited or tool interfaces are brittle, explicit reasoning can interfere with reliable execution. 
Limitations 
This study is limited to the GAIA benchmark and a small set of open-source backbones and prompting configurations; results may change with different tool implementations or retrieval quality. Future work should evaluate these findings on additional agentic benchmarks such as GPQA and AIME, and with alternative tool implementations and retrieval pipelines. Future work should explore dynamic thinking policies, where full explicit reasoning is enabled selectively based on task difficulty, model uncertainty, or agent role, reducing coordination failures while retaining the benefits of deliberation.
