Unit-Consistent (UC) Adjoint for GSD and Backprop in
Deep Learning Applications

Abstract
Deep neural networks constructed from linear maps and positively homogeneous nonlinearities (e.g., ReLU) possess a fundamental gauge symmetry: the network function is invariant to node-wise diagonal rescalings. However, standard gradient descent is not equivariant to this symmetry, causing optimization trajectories to depend heavily on arbitrary parameterizations. Prior work has proposed rescaling-invariant optimization schemes for positively homogeneous networks (e.g., path-based or path-space updates). Our contribution is complementary: we formulate the invariance requirement at the level of the backward adjoint/optimization geometry, which provides a simple, operator-level recipe that can be applied uniformly across network components and optimizer state. By replacing the Euclidean transpose with a Unit-Consistent (UC) adjoint, we derive UC gauge-consistent steepest descent and backprogation.
Keywords: UC GSD, UC adjoint. Unit Consistency (UC). Deep learning optimization. Parameterization invariance.
Positive homogeneity (ReLU networks).
1 Unit-Consistent Inverses to UC Adjoints
A unit-consistent (UC) generalized matrix inverse, M^{-\text{\tiny U}}, was developed in [1, 2] that is consistent
(alt., covariant or equivariant) with respect to arbitrary nonsingular diagonal scalings D and E:
M^{-\text{\tiny U}}~=~E\left(DME\right)^{-\text{\tiny U}}D.
or, equivalently:
(DME)^{-\text{\tiny U}}~=~E^{-1}M^{-\text{\tiny U}}D^{-1}.
This is not satisfied by the Moore-Penrose (MP) pseudoinverse, which provides consistency with respect to orthonormal/unitary (e.g., rotation) transformations rather that diagonal scale transformations. The MP pseudoinverse (and least-squares minimization more generally) is widely misapplied in contexts that do not satisfy its consistency properties, and it was noted in [1] that this includes gradient descent in machine learning in many applications. In this note, we apply analysis analogous to that used to derive the UC inverse to derive a unit-consistent matrix adjoint applicable to machine learning applications involving implicitly UC activation functions.
Related work.
Several lines of work recognize that standard Euclidean-gradient updates in deep networks can depend strongly on parameterization, including the rescaling symmetries induced by positive homogeneity. Natural-gradient methods address this in principle by defining steepest descent under a non-Euclidean metric [5], and path-normalized approaches such as Path-SGD target ReLU rescaling invariances more directly through a path-based geometry [4]. In practice, architectural normalizations (e.g., BatchNorm, LayerNorm) are widely used as effective stabilizers [7, 8], and more recent work shows that competitive training without normalization can be possible with carefully designed training rules [12]. The UC framework is complementary to these approaches. Specifically, rather than proposing a particular metric (as in natural-gradient variants), or a specific invariant norm (as in path-based methods), UC formulates the consistency requirement at the level of operator adjoints and coordinate changes, which provides a general recipe for constructing backward/updates that are equivariant to the underlying diagonal rescaling gauge while leaving the forward architecture unchanged.
2 The Geometry of Scale
Deep networks built from linear maps and positively homogeneous nonlinearities (e.g.,ReLU) exhibit a massive degree of gauge freedom. For example, consider a standard bias-free multilayer perceptron:
h_{0}=x,~z_{l}=W_{l}h_{l-1},~h_{l}=\sigma(z_{l}),
(1)
where \sigma=\text{ReLU} [3]. The identity \sigma(\alpha u)=\alpha\sigma(u) for \alpha>0 implies that the network function is unchanged if the hidden units are rescaled by diagonal matrices S_{l}>0 satisfying [4]:
\tilde{W}_{l}=S_{l}W_{l}S_{l-1}^{-1}.
(2)
While the parameterizations W and \tilde{W} are functionally identical, they are geometrically distinct. Standard optimization algorithms, such as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), implicitly assume global rotation sysmmetry. This means that their estimates of local gradients are computed based on an incorrect assumed geometry [5], which introduces a non-stochastic local bias. It is non-stochastic in the sense that it results from local structural artifacts of a gauge freedom that the SGD does not recognize11
1
This is analogous to trying to estimate relative distances between objects in an environment while observing through a warped lens., which means that deviations due to violated symmetry assumptions will not tend to “filter out” over a sequence of local steps.
The symptoms of the mismatch between the actual symmetry and that implicitly assumed by most SGD implementations are well known, but instead of correcting the disconnect, methods such as Batch Normalization (BN) [7] or Layer Normalization (LN) [8] are applied to scale activations during the forward pass in a way that moderates the symptoms, but not the underlying cause.
2.1 The Dimensional Flaw in the Euclidean Transpose
The geometric inconsistency22
2
Here “inconsistency” refers to symmetry/metric mismatch: W^{\top} is the Euclidean adjoint, but Euclidean geometry is not invariant to the diagonal rescalings that leave the network function unchanged. in standard backpropagation is use of the Euclidean adjoint, W^{\top}. To see this, consider a linear map y=Wx in a unit-analysis context. If inputs x have physical units of [Meters] and outputs y have units of [Seconds], then the operator W carries units [\text{Seconds}/\text{Meters}]. For the gradient update W\leftarrow W-\eta\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L} to be physically meaningful, the gradient must have the units as W. However, standard backpropagation derives the gradient via the transpose as [6]:
\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}=W^{\top}(\nabla_{y}\mathcal{L}).
(3)
The Euclidean transpose W^{\top} numerically retains the entries of W, but it blindly mixes the dimensions. If the input units are rescaled to (x\mapsto Ex), and output units to (y\mapsto Dy), the transpose transforms as:
\widetilde{W^{\top}}=(DWE^{-1})^{\top}=E^{-1}W^{\top}D,
(4)
which “mixes” the arbitrary diagonal scales D and E into the backward signal. The result is a gradient update that essentially subtracts incommmensurate units from the weights, which makes the assumed optimization landscape incorrectly sensitive to the choice of measurement units (the gauge).
2.2 Contribution: The Unit-Consistent Adjoint
The contribution of this work is to replace the Euclidean transpose with a Unit-Consistent (UC) Adjoint. We define this operator by projecting the weight matrix W into a canonical coordinate system that is invariant to arbitrary row/column scaling. Unlike the standard transpose, the UC adjoint is invariant to diagonal gauge transformations. It ensures that the backpropagated error signal and resulting parameter updates respect the intrinsic geometry of the network function instead of the arbitrary implicit scaling of its parameters.
3 The Unit-Consistent Adjoint and UC-GSD
To achieve full diagonal-scale consistency, both the backward signal and the resulting gradient update must be computed in a canonical coordinate system.
3.1 Canonical Decomposition
Let W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} be a linear operator. A canonical diagonal decomposition (e.g., via RZ scaling [1]) can be defined as:
W~=~DW^{\prime}E,
(5)
where D\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} are positive diagonal matrices, and W^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}(W) is the canonical representative (“gauge-fixed” form) of the equivalence class of W. The computation can be performed in optimal O(m,n) time for m\times n W [2, 1].
Definition 3.1 (Unit-Consistent Adjoint).
Given W=DW^{\prime}E, the Unit-Consistent Adjoint is defined as33
3
For notational convenience, we use D and E here to define positive canonical scalings instead of as arbitrary diagonal matrices as done earlier:
W^{*_{\text{UC}}}~:=~E^{-1}W^{\top}D^{-1}.
(6)
Using the identity W^{\top}=E(W^{\prime})^{\top}D, this simplifies to W^{*_{\text{UC}}}=(W^{\prime})^{\top}. This can be interpreted as: the UC adjoint is the transpose taken in canonical coordinates.
3.2 Derivation of the Equivariant Update
Standard gradient descent performs updates W using the Euclidean gradient G=\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}. To construct a gauge-equivariant update, steepest descent must be performed on the canonical variable W^{\prime}, with the result then mapped back to the original parameter space:
1.
Gradient in Canonical Coordinates -
Using the chain rule on W=DW^{\prime}E:
\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}~=~D(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L})E~=~DGE.
(7)
2.
Descent in Canonical Coordinates -
Perform a gradient step on W^{\prime}:
(W^{\prime})^{+}~=~W^{\prime}-\eta\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}.
(8)
3.
Push-forward to Original Coordinates -
Reconstruct the updated weight matrix using the original scales D,E:
\displaystyle W^{+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle D(W^{\prime})^{+}E
(9)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle D(W^{\prime}-\eta DGE)E
(10)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DW^{\prime}E-\eta D^{2}GE^{2}.
(11)
Substituting W=DW^{\prime}E then gives the Unit-Consistent Gauge-Equivariant Steepest Descent (UC-GSD) rule:
\boxed{W^{+}~=~W-\eta D^{2}GE^{2}}
(12)
3.3 Proof of Equivariance
It is straightforward to verify that this update preserves gauge equivalence. Let \tilde{W}=S_{out}WS_{in}^{-1}. The canonical factors transform as \tilde{D}=S_{out}D and \tilde{E}=ES_{in}^{-1}, and the Euclidean gradient transforms as \tilde{G}=S_{out}^{-1}GS_{in}. The preconditioned update term \tilde{U} transforms as:
\displaystyle\tilde{U}
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\tilde{D}^{2}\tilde{G}\tilde{E}^{2}
(13)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle(S_{out}D)^{2}(S_{out}^{-1}GS_{in})(ES_{in}^{-1})^{2}
(14)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{out}(D^{2}GE^{2})S_{in}^{-1}
(15)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{out}US_{in}^{-1}.
(16)
Thus:
\displaystyle\tilde{W}^{+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\tilde{W}-\eta\tilde{U}
(17)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{out}(W-\eta U)S_{in}^{-1}
(18)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{out}W^{+}S_{in}^{-1}.
(19)
This establishes that the optimization trajectory is identical regardless of the chosen gauge.
4 Gauge-Equivariant Extensions to Network Operations
The UC framework is extended here to obtain analogs for common deep learning primitives that
maintain global consistency.
4.1 Biases
Consider z=Wx+b. Forward equivariance requires b to scale identically to the output z. If z\to S_{out}z, then b\to S_{out}b. We define the canonical bias b^{\prime}=D^{-1}b using the same output scale D derived from W.
The update rule follows the same derivation:
b^{+}~=~b-\eta D^{2}(\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}).
(20)
4.2 Convolutions
A convolution kernel K of shape (C_{out},C_{in},kH,kW) transforms under the gauge via its channel dimensions:
\tilde{K}_{i,j,u,v}~=~(S_{out})_{ii}K_{i,j,u,v}(S_{in}^{-1})_{jj}.
(21)
We apply the canonical scaling decomposition to the matricized kernel (flattening spatial dimensions). The resulting update broadcasts the diagonal preconditioners D^{2} (acting on output channels) and E^{2} (acting on input channels) across the spatial dimensions of the gradient:
K_{i,j,u,v}^{+}~=~K_{i,j,u,v}-\eta(D^{2})_{ii}(\nabla_{K}\mathcal{L})_{i,j,u,v}(E^{2})_{jj}.
(22)
4.3 Residual Connections
In a residual block y=x+F(x), the addition operation forces the scale of the input and the output to match [9]: S_{y}=S_{x}=S_{F(x)}.
This implies that for any learnable layer within the residual branch F(x), the gauge freedom is constrained. Specifically, the input scaling matrix E and output scaling matrix D for the block must be coupled to maintain the identity skip connection. In practice, this simplifies the canonicalization process by “locking” the gauge across residual blocks.
4.4 Stateful Optimizers (Momentum/Adam)
Standard Momentum or Adam [11, 10] maintains state variables (velocity V, squared gradients M) that are linear or quadratic in the gradient units. To maintain diagonal-scale consistency, these states must be tracked in canonical coordinates. For Momentum with coefficient \mu:
1.
Map velocity to canonical: V^{\prime}=D^{-1}VE^{-1}.
2.
Update canonical velocity: (V^{\prime})^{+}=\mu V^{\prime}+\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}.
3.
Map back for weight update: W^{+}=W-\eta D(V^{\prime})^{+}E.
5 Discussion: Normalization by Geometry
5.1 The Role of Normalization Layers
As previously discussed, Batch Normalization (BN) and Layer Normalization (LN) are currently ubiquitous in deep learning. They function by actively manipulating the statistics of intermediate activations h during the forward pass (h\to\frac{h-\mu}{\sigma}). Essentially, BN is intended to operate as a dynamic data-dependent gauge-fixing mechanism.
However, BN introduces significant downsides:
•
Batch Dependence: It couples training examples, complicating small-batch or non-i.i.d. learning.
•
Train/Test Mismatch: The discrepancy between batch statistics (training) and running statistics
(inference) is a frequent source of bugs.
•
Symmetry Breaking: Normalization layers do not satisfy positive homogeneity
(BN(\alpha x)\neq\alpha BN(x)), breaking the clean algebraic structure of ReLU networks.
The UC-GSD framework proposes that the benefits of BN (i.e., stabilization of scale) can be achieved entirely through the optimization geometry. This suggests a path to Normalization-Free Networks [12] that rely on mathematical symmetry rather than patchwork architectural scaffolding.
6 Discussion
This work introduced the Unit-Consistent Adjoint as the geometrically-correct backward operator for networks with homogeneous nonlinearities. By acknowledging that diagonal rescalings are non-physical degrees of freedom, a preconditioned update rule was developed that is invariant to these symmetries. This formulation replaces heuristic “normalization by architecture” approaches with a rigorous “normalization by geometry,” which could potentially lead to deep learning models that are simpler and more robust. (See appendices for additional details and discussion.)

Appendix A Diagonal-Scale Equivariance via a Unit-Consistent Adjoint
Deep networks built from linear maps and positively homogeneous nonlinearities (e.g., ReLU) possess a large gauge freedom: many distinct parameterizations implement exactly the same input-output function. For example, in a bias-free ReLU multilayer perceptron:
h_{0}=x,~z_{\ell}=W_{\ell}h_{\ell-1},~h_{\ell}=\sigma(z_{\ell}),~\sigma=\mathrm{ReLU},
the identity \sigma(\alpha u)=\alpha\sigma(u) for all \alpha>0 implies a node-wise diagonal rescaling symmetry. If S_{\ell}\succ 0 are diagonal matrices with S_{0}=I and S_{L}=I, then the gauge transform:
\widetilde{W}_{\ell}~=~S_{\ell}W_{\ell}S_{\ell-1}^{-1}~~(\ell=1,\dots,L)
leaves the realized network function unchanged while rescaling hidden coordinates as \widetilde{h}_{\ell}=S_{\ell}h_{\ell}.
This freedom is benign at the level of representation but harmful for optimization: standard backpropagation and gradient descent are not equivariant to this symmetry and therefore can behave very differently on gauge-equivalent parameterizations.
A.1 Why the transpose is the bottleneck
For a linear map y=Wx, classical backpropagation propagates errors via the Euclidean adjoint W^{\top}:
g_{x}~=~W^{\top}g_{y},
where g_{y}=\nabla_{y}\mathcal{L} and g_{x}=\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}.
However, under a diagonal rescaling of coordinates:
\displaystyle x
\displaystyle\mapsto
\displaystyle\widetilde{x}=Ex,
\displaystyle y
\displaystyle\mapsto
\displaystyle\widetilde{y}=Dy,
\displaystyle W
\displaystyle\mapsto
\displaystyle\widetilde{W}=DWE^{-1},
for diagonal matrices D,E\succ 0. The transpose transforms as:
\widetilde{W}^{\top}~=~(DWE^{-1})^{\top}~=~E^{-1}W^{\top}D,
which mixes the arbitrary diagonal scales into the backward operator. This scale mixing is precisely what makes the backpropagated signal, and consequently the gradient field in parameter space, sensitive to the choice of gauge (or to the choice of measurement units in a unit-analysis interpretation).
A.2 Core Mechanism: The Unit-Consistent Adjoint
The main contribution of this note is to replace the Euclidean transpose in backpropagation by a unit-consistent adjoint that is invariant (or, more precisely, consistent) under positive diagonal scalings.
Let W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} be a linear operator. Define a canonical diagonal decomposition:
\displaystyle W
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DW^{\prime}E,~D\succ 0,\ E\succ 0\ \text{diagonal},
\displaystyle W^{\prime}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(W),
where \mathcal{C}(\cdot) is a canonical scaling map (e.g., RZ [2, 1]) that removes arbitrary positive row/column scalings so that W^{\prime} is a unique representative of the diagonal-scaling equivalence class of W.
(Informally: \mathcal{C}(W) is the “gauge-fixed” form of W.)
Definition (unit-consistent adjoint).
Given W=DW^{\prime}E as above, define:
W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}}~:=~E^{-1}W^{\top}D^{-1}.
Because W^{\top}~=~EW^{\prime\top}D, this construction simplifies to:
W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}}~=~W^{\prime\top},
i.e., take the transpose in canonical coordinates. In particular, if \widetilde{W} is obtained from W by arbitrary positive diagonal scalings, then \mathcal{C}(\widetilde{W})=\mathcal{C}(W), so:
\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\widetilde{W})^{\top}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(W)^{\top}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}}.
Thus the UC adjoint eliminates the backward-pass dependence on arbitrary diagonal gauge factors. Conceptually, this operator is the appropriate adjoint once one acknowledges that, in homogeneous networks, diagonal rescalings of intermediate coordinates are not physically meaningful degrees of freedom and therefore should not influence the learning dynamics.
A.3 From UC adjoint to diagonal-scale equivariant learning
Replacing W^{\top} by W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}} gives a diagonal-scale consistent error propagation rule for each linear map:
\displaystyle g_{x}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}}
\displaystyle g_{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(W)^{\top}g_{y}.
However, full diagonal-scale equivariance requires not only consistent backpropagated signals but also an update rule that respects the gauge action on parameters. The UC adjoint provides the missing ingredient needed to construct such updates systematically.
The key idea is to perform optimization in the same canonical coordinates that define the UC adjoint. With W^{\prime}=D^{-1}WE^{-1}, the chain rule gives:
\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}~=~D(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L})E.
A standard gradient step in W^{\prime}:
W^{\prime+}~=~W^{\prime}-\eta\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L},
pushes forward to the explicit preconditioned update in the original parameters:
W^{+}~=~DW^{\prime+}E~=~W-\eta D^{2}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L})E^{2},
which is equivariant under the diagonal gauge transformation W\mapsto S_{\mathrm{out}}WS_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1} when D,E are chosen canonically from W. The UC adjoint is therefore an anchor that determines the correct canonical coordinates, and canonical-coordinate descent then gives an update rule that preserves gauge-equivalence across iterations.
A.4 Implications: replacing normalization-by-architecture with normalization-by-geometry
Normalization layers (BatchNorm, LayerNorm, etc.) can be viewed as forward-pass mechanisms that forcibly control intermediate scales in order to stabilize optimization. The program developed here proposes a different principle:
Instead of modifying the architecture to manage scale, modify the backward operator and the optimization geometry so that learning is intrinsically insensitive to diagonal rescalings that do not change the function.
In summary, the UC approach makes stability a property of the learning rule rather than an artifact of additional layers and running statistics.
A.5 Contributions and roadmap
This program is organized around the UC adjoint as the central new mechanism:
1.
We define the unit-consistent adjoint W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}} by transposing in a canonically scaled (gauge-fixed) coordinate system.
2.
We use W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}} to construct backpropagation rules that are consistent with the diagonal rescaling symmetries implied by positive homogeneity.
3.
We derive diagonal-scale equivariant optimization updates by performing descent in canonical coordinates, giving explicit preconditioned updates in the original parameters.
4.
We extend the same canonical-coordinate principle to the remaining network operations (biases, convolutions, additions/residuals, concatenations/splits, and optimizer state), clarifying where exact equivariance is achievable and where architectural choices intentionally break the symmetry.
In summary, the UC adjoint is the conceptual and technical cornerstone: it removes arbitrary diagonal scales from the backward operator, thereby enabling a coherent, symmetry-consistent optimization geometry whose downstream consequence is diagonal-scale equivariant learning.

A condition stronger than equivariance can be obtained from the

each W_{\ell} to its canonical representative W_{\ell}^{\prime}=\mathcal{RZ}(W_{\ell}).
Appendix C Gauge-equivariant extensions to common network operations
We assume graph-wide viewpoint for all operations. Associate to every tensor node v
a (usually channel-wise) positive diagonal scale S_{v}.
Gauge action on tensors
\tilde{x}_{v}=S_{v}x_{v},~S_{v}\succ 0~~\text{(diagonal or block-diagonal by channels/groups)}.
A network is gauge-equivariant (and a family of parameterizations are gauge-equivalent)
iff every operation f satisfies:
\displaystyle\tilde{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle f(\tilde{x};\tilde{\theta})
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{y}f(x;\theta)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{y}y.
This induces constraints/transform rules for parameters \theta and (necessarily) for updates.
Linear maps (Dense / Matrix)
•
Forward:
y~=~Wx,~~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}},~y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}.
•
Gauge:
\displaystyle\tilde{x}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{in}}x,
\displaystyle\tilde{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}y,
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{in}},S_{\mathrm{out}}\succ 0~\text{diagonal}.
•
Parameter transform for forward equivariance:
\tilde{W}~=~S_{\mathrm{out}}WS_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1}.
•
Euclidean gradient transform:
\displaystyle G
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}
\displaystyle\Longrightarrow
\displaystyle\tilde{G}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}GS_{\mathrm{in}}.
•
RZ canonicalization for the layer:
\displaystyle W
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DW^{\prime}E,~~~D\succ 0,\ E\succ 0\ \text{diagonal},
\displaystyle W^{\prime}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathcal{RZ}(W).
•
Under the gauge W\rightarrow\tilde{W}=S_{o}utWS_{i}n^{-1}:
\displaystyle\tilde{W}^{\prime}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle W^{\prime},
\displaystyle\tilde{D}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}D,
\displaystyle\tilde{E}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle ES_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1}.
•
UC-adjoint:
\displaystyle W^{\ast_{\mathrm{UC}}}
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle E^{-1}W^{\top}D^{-1}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle(W^{\prime})^{\top}.
•
Gauge-equivariant gradient step obtained by descending in canonical coordinates W^{\prime}:
\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DGE,
\displaystyle W^{\prime+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle W^{\prime}-\eta\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L},
\displaystyle W^{+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DW^{\prime+}E.
•
Explicit preconditioned update in original coordinates:
\boxed{W^{+}~=~W-\eta D^{2}GE^{2}.}
(This is the same rule previously given. It is the canonical pattern to reuse.)
C.1 Bias addition (Dense / Conv bias)
z~=~Wx+b,\qquad b\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}.
•
Forward equivariance requires b to scale like the output:
\tilde{b}~=~S_{\mathrm{out}}b.
•
Bias gradient and its gauge transform:
\displaystyle g_{b}
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}
\displaystyle\Longrightarrow
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{b}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}g_{b}.
•
Canonical bias coordinate (using the same output scaling D as for W):
\displaystyle b
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle Db^{\prime},
\displaystyle b^{\prime}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle D^{-1}b,
\displaystyle\nabla_{b^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle Dg_{b}.
•
Gauge-equivariant bias update (descend in b’):
\displaystyle b^{\prime+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle b^{\prime}-\eta\nabla_{b^{\prime}}\mathcal{L},
\displaystyle b^{+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle Db^{\prime+}.
•
Explicit preconditioned rule:
\boxed{b^{+}~=~b-\eta D^{2}g_{b}.}
C.2 Elementwise positively-homogeneous nonlinearities
Let \phi be elementwise and positively homogeneous of degree 1:
\phi(\alpha u)~=~\alpha\phi(u)
for all \alpha>0, e.g., as holds for ReLU, leaky-ReLU, absolute value, max-pooling (channelwise scaling),
average-pooling (linear), and dropout mask (multiplicative).
\displaystyle h
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\phi(z),
\displaystyle\phi(\alpha z)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\alpha\phi(z)~~(\alpha>0),
then for any diagonal S\succ 0:
\displaystyle\phi(Sz)
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S\phi(z),
\displaystyle\Rightarrow
\displaystyle S_{h}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{z}.
Backprop through an elementwise \phi is:
g_{z}~=~\phi^{\prime}(z)\odot g_{h}.
The indicator pattern is unchanged for degree-1 homogeneous \phi and S\succ 0,
so the backprop map is gauge-consistent:
\tilde{g}_{h}~=~S^{-1}g_{h}~\Longrightarrow~\tilde{g}_{z}=S^{-1}g_{z}.
C.3 Addition / Residual connections (constraints on the gauge)
Addition node:
y~=~x_{1}+x_{2}.
Forward equivariance requires both summands to carry the same scale:
\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{1}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle Sx_{1},
\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{2}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle Sx_{2}
\displaystyle\Longrightarrow
\displaystyle\tilde{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S(x_{1}+x_{2})~=~Sy.
Therefore, an addition node imposes the constraint:
S_{x_{1}}~=~S_{x_{2}}=S_{y}.
Residual block example.
h_{\ell}~=~h_{\ell-1}+F(h_{\ell-1};\theta).
This forces the scale on the skip path and the residual branch output to match:
S_{h_{\ell-1}}~=~S_{F\text{-out}}~=~S_{h_{\ell}},
i.e., residuals reduce the available gauge freedom and preserve equivariant updates,
but they constrain the allowable S’s on the computational graph.
C.4 Concatenation / split / reshape / transpose of axes
•
Concatenation:
y~=~\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}.
•
Scale is block-diagonal:
\displaystyle S_{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mathrm{blkdiag}(S_{x_{1}},S_{x_{2}}),
\displaystyle\tilde{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{y}y.
•
Split is the inverse operation. Permuting axes corresponds to conjugating S
by the permutation operator P:
y~=~Px~\Rightarrow~S_{y}~=~PS_{x}P^{-1}.
These are essentially bookkeeping rules for how the gauge acts on tensor layouts.
C.5 Convolution (as a channelwise-scaled linear operator)
Consider conv kernel K with shape (C_{o}ut,C_{i}n,kH,kW).
•
Channelwise gauge:
\displaystyle\tilde{x}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{in}}x,
\displaystyle\tilde{y}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}y,
for S_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}},
S_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{out}}} diagonal.
•
Kernel transform (broadcasted over spatial indices):
\tilde{K}_{i,j,u,v}~=~(S_{\mathrm{out}})_{ii}K_{i,j,u,v}(S_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1})_{jj}.
Treat conv as a matrix W(K) after “im2col”, then reuse the linear-map rule.
RZ scaling can be applied to the channelwise matricization of K, e.g. flatten (C_{o}ut)
rows and (C_{i}n*kH*kW) columns, or impose separate scalings on in/out channels.
The gauge-equivariant update is the same pattern with broadcasted D^{2} and E^{2}:
\boxed{K^{+}_{i,j,u,v}~=~K_{i,j,u,v}-\eta(D^{2})_{ii}(\nabla_{K}\mathcal{L})_{i,j,u,v}(E^{2})_{jj},}
where D acts on output channels and E on input channels (both diagonal),
and (\nabla_{K}\mathcal{L}) is the usual conv-kernel gradient.
C.6 Elementwise affine channel transforms (e.g., learned gains)
Example.
y=a\odot x+c, with a,cinR^{n} (per-channel or per-feature).
y~=~a\odot x+c.
If x scales by S and y scales by S (degree-1 equivariance), then:
•
“a” should be scale-invariant (dimensionless) under the gauge,
•
“c” should scale like y.
\tilde{a}~=~a,~~\tilde{c}~=~Sc,~~S_{y}=S_{x}=S.
The bias-like update for c uses the same D^{2} preconditioning as in (2).
For a, the gauge places no requirement (it is invariant), so ordinary update
is already equivariant.
C.7 Normalization layers are NOT degree-1 homogeneous
LayerNorm / BatchNorm (in their usual form) may be approximately scale-invariant (training-mode, ignoring eps/running stats):
\displaystyle\text{LN}(\alpha x)
\displaystyle\approx
\displaystyle\text{LN}(x),
\displaystyle\text{BN}(\alpha x)
\displaystyle\approx
\displaystyle\text{BN}(x),
but they do not satisfy LN(Sx)=SLN(x).
In gauge language, they act like a “gauge-fixing” node:
y~=~\mathrm{Norm}(x)
which implies (approximately):
S_{y}~\approx~I~\text{even if}~~S_{x}\neq I.
The consequence of this is:
•
The ReLU positive-homogeneity gauge is broken or collapsed across normalization nodes.
•
Exact global gauge-equivariance cannot generally hold in a network containing such nodes.
However, the linear-layer canonical updates can still be applied locally around them.
C.8 Softmax / Sigmoid / Tanh are NOT positively homogeneous
These do not satisfy f(\alpha x)=\alpha f(x), so the multiplicative ReLU gauge
does not extend through them. (Softmax is shift-invariant: softmax(x+c1)=softmax(x).)
Therefore there is no nontrivial multiplicative-diagonal gauge that leaves such
subgraphs invariant.
C.9 Optimizers with state (Momentum / Adam): keep state in canonical coordinates
If momentum is used, then velocity must be maintained in the canonical W^{\prime} coordinate:
\displaystyle V^{\prime}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle D^{-1}VE^{-1},
\displaystyle V^{\prime+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\mu V^{\prime}+\nabla_{W^{\prime}}\mathcal{L},
\displaystyle W^{\prime+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle W^{\prime}-\eta V^{\prime},
\displaystyle W^{+}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DW^{\prime+}E.
This preserves gauge-equivariance exactly (the same logic as the GD step),
and nalogously for Adam/RMSProp: store and update all moments i.
Appendix D Toward Eliminating Normalization “Hacks” via Gauge-Equivariant Training
A central motivation for introducing gauge-equivariant (or unit-consistent) learning rules is precisely to avoid architectural “patches” such as Batch Normalization (BN), Layer Normalization (LN), and their variants. Normalization layers were originally adopted because deep networks with positively homogeneous nonlinearities (e.g., ReLU) exhibit severe scale pathologies: many distinct parameterizations represent the same function, yet standard Euclidean-gradient training behaves very differently across these equivalent parameterizations. In practice, this manifests as instability, sensitivity to initialization, depth-dependent ill-conditioning, and a strong dependence on manual tuning.
D.1 What normalization layers are really doing
Normalization layers mitigate training difficulties by actively controlling intermediate scales. For example, ignoring affine re-scaling parameters and \varepsilon-terms for intuition, a typical normalization step may be viewed as enforcing a constraint of the form
h~\mapsto~\widehat{h}~\approx~\frac{h-\mu(h)}{\sigma(h)},
so that the next layer receives an input whose typical magnitude is approximately fixed. This acts as an explicit gauge-fixing mechanism: it collapses or removes degrees of freedom corresponding to arbitrary scaling of hidden representations. However, this comes with costs::
•
BN introduces dependence on batch statistics, coupling examples and complicating small-batch, online, or non-i.i.d. settings.
•
BN adds running-state (moving averages) whose training/inference mismatch can be subtle.
•
LN and related normalizations alter the symmetry structure of the network (e.g., by mixing coordinates through shared statistics), which can interfere with a clean diagonal rescaling interpretation.
•
All normalization layers add architectural and implementation complexity, plus additional hyperparameters and failure modes.
D.2 Gauge-equivariant learning as a replacement principle
If the root cause of training instability is the mismatch between (1) the functional invariances implied by positive homogeneity and (2) the non-invariance of standard gradient descent to these reparameterizations, then it is natural to attempt a “first-principles” fix:
Instead of forcing activations to have convenient scale by inserting normalization layers, adjust the learning rule so that it is equivariant to the network’s intrinsic scaling symmetries.
Concretely, consider a linear map (dense or convolutional) written abstractly as:
y~=~Wx,
with a diagonal scaling gauge:
\displaystyle x
\displaystyle\mapsto
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{in}}x,
\displaystyle y
\displaystyle\mapsto
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}y,
\displaystyle W
\displaystyle\mapsto
\displaystyle\widetilde{W}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{out}}WS_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1},
which preserves the realized function in positively homogeneous networks when combined appropriately across layers.
A gauge-equivariant (canonical) learning rule is obtained by descending in a canonical coordinate W^{\prime} defined through a positive diagonal decomposition:
\displaystyle W
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle DW^{\prime}E,~~D\succ 0,~E\succ 0~\text{diagonal},
\displaystyle W^{\prime}
\displaystyle=
\displaystyle\text{(canonical representative)}.
Gradient descent in W^{\prime} induces the preconditioned update in the original parameters:
\boxed{W^{+}~=~W-\eta D^{2}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L})E^{2},}
and similarly for biases:
b^{+}~=~b-\eta D^{2}(\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}).
This update is equivariant under the diagonal rescaling gauge: if two parameter sets represent the same function via a gauge transform, then after one step of this update they remain gauge-related and thus represent the same function to first order in step size.
Interpretation.
In effect, the learning rule ensures that training dynamics are not affected by arbitrary parameterization choices. From this perspective, “normalization layers” attempt to solve a conditioning and gauge problem in the forward pass, whereas gauge-consistent learning entirely resolves the problem in the optimization geometry.
D.3 Conclusion: when gauge-equivariant learning can eliminate normalization
Under the hypothesis that normalization’s primary benefit is to control scale-induced ill-conditioning (rather than to provide some fundamentally different representational capability), a successful gauge-equivariant learning rule would imply:
1.
Normalization becomes optional: stable training can be achieved without BN/LN by correcting the
optimization geometry instead of altering the network architecture.
2.
Architecture simplification: networks can be built from simpler primitives (linear maps, additions,
homogeneous nonlinearities) without inserting normalization layers as “stability scaffolding.”
3.
Batch-size robustness: because the mechanism does not depend on batch statistics, performance
should degrade less when batches are small or data are streaming/online.
4.
Parameterization robustness: training should be less sensitive to rescalings of hidden units, to
initialization magnitudes, and to depth-induced scale drift.
Although BN/LN may be regarded as non-rigorous methods intended to impose scale control, this does
not necessarily mean they do not provide accidental benefits in the form of, e.g., implicit noise
injection. The message of this note is that if such benefits are unknowingly provided, then rigorous
alternative solutions should be derived to provide them.
