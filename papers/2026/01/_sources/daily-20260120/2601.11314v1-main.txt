1 Introduction 
The unprecedented success of Large Language Models (LLMs) is fueled by pretraining on massive-scale corpora ( Yang et al., 2025 ; OpenAI, 2025 ; Team, 2025 ) .
However, as state-of-the-art (SOTA) models become increasingly opaque, details regarding their training data are rarely disclosed.
This lack of transparency poses significant challenges for auditing potential copyright infringement, privacy leakage, and data contamination. 
A promising avenue to address these concerns is pretraining data detection ( Shi et al., 2024 ; Fu et al., 2023 ) , which typically employs Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) to determine whether a specific text was part of a model’s training set ( Shokri et al., 2017 ) .
The majority of existing MIA techniques for LLMs are logit-based , relying on the assumption that the model exposes per-token probabilities ( Zhang et al., 2024a ; Xie et al., 2024 ) .
While effective for open-weight models, this assumption breaks down for API-accessed LLMs, where typically only the generated text is returned. 
To mitigate reliance on model internals, early explorations proposed to estimate logits via linear regression ( He et al., 2025 ) or infer membership based on tokenizer distinctiveness ( Tong et al., 2025 ) .
Despite their utility, these methods often require knowledge of the target model’s tokenizer, information that remains proprietary for many closed-source systems.
Therefore, recent research has pivoted toward a strictly black-box setting, where the attacker has access only to generated text ( Dong et al., 2024 ; Kaneko et al., 2025 ) .
Current black-box methods predominantly rely on approximating likelihoods via N-gram matching.
However, we identify that these approximation methods suffer from two critical limitations: distribution drift , where long-form generation diverges from the original distribution, and signal sparsity , where exact N-gram matching fails to capture semantic retention, offering unstable estimates across domains. 
In this work, we propose SimMIA, a novel framework that significantly advances MIA performance in the black-box setting.
Unlike prior works that sample full sequences, SimMIA employs a word-by-word sampling strategy to eliminate error propagation (mitigating drift) and introduces a semantic scoring mechanism that captures soft membership signals beyond exact token matching (mitigating sparsity).
We evaluate SimMIA on two standard benchmarks, WikiMIA ( Shi et al., 2024 ) and MIMIR ( Duan et al., 2024 ) , across four representative open-source families.
Our results demonstrate that SimMIA outperforms existing black-box baselines by a significant margin of 15.7 AUC points on average and achieves parity with, or even surpasses, many logit-based approaches. 
To further validate SimMIA’s effectiveness on modern proprietary LLMs, we follow WikiMIA-24 ( Fu et al., 2025 ) to curate WikiMIA-25, a dataset derived from 2025 Wikipedia dumps.
Experiments on WikiMIA-25 with the four latest LLMs show that SimMIA establishes a new SOTA in the black-box setting, offering a practical tool for auditing the black-box LLMs of today. 
2 SimMIA 
2.1 Problem Statement 
Let M M be a target LLM trained on a dataset D D . Given a text x x , the goal of MIA is to determine whether x ∈ D x\in D .
Existing LLM MIAs formalize this as constructing a scoring function over x x , where a higher score indicates a higher likelihood of membership.
Based on the information exposed by M M , we distinguish between two threat models: 
• Gray-box Setting : In this scenario, the attacker has access to the model’s internal tokenization results and even per-token logits. Most existing MIAs operate under this assumption ( Shi et al., 2024 ; Mattern et al., 2023 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2025 ) . 

• Black-box Setting : This setting represents the restricted access typical of commercial APIs. The attacker has no access to model weights, logits, or the tokenization results. The attacker can only observe the generated textual continuation ( Dong et al., 2024 ; Kaneko et al., 2025 ) . 

In this work, we focus on the strictly constrained black-box setting, aiming to perform MIA solely through text-based interaction. 
2.2 Method 
The core idea behind SimMIA is that if a text x x has appeared in the training data of M M , then continuations sampled from M M given any prefix of x x should be more semantically consistent with the ground truth suffix than those for unseen examples.
SimMIA operationalizes this idea in three stages:
(1) word-by-word sampling,
(2) computation of word-level scores, and
(3) relative aggregation of these scores into a final membership score.
Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of SimMIA and compares it with the SOTA black-box MIA baseline. 
Word-by-Word Sampling 
Previous sampling-based MIA methods ( Dong et al., 2024 ; Kaneko et al., 2025 ) typically generate multiple full continuations x ≥ i x_{\geq i} conditioned on a fixed prefix x < i x_{<i} and use them to estimate likelihood-based scores.
However, such approaches suffer from distribution shift : as the generation proceeds, later tokens depend on both the true prefix and the model’s self-generated outputs, making the estimated distribution deviate from that of the original text x x .
SimMIA mitigates this issue by sampling only the immediate next word, which avoids self-conditioning and provides an unbiased estimate of the model’s local behavior without increasing the sampling cost. 
Specifically, we first tokenize x x into a sequence of words x 1 , x 2 , … , x L x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{L} using a standard word tokenizer ( Bird et al., 2009 ) , where L L is the number of words.
Starting from the second word, SimMIA draws N N samples of the next word x ^ i \hat{x}_{i} from the conditional distribution M ( ⋅ ∣ x < i ) M(\cdot\mid x_{<i}) , and computes a word-level score for x i x_{i} based on these samples. 
Semantic Scoring 
A straightforward method for scoring the target word x i x_{i} involves estimating its empirical probability using a set of generated samples { x ^ i ( j ) } j = 1 N \{\hat{x}^{(j)}_{i}\}_{j=1}^{N} . This formulation aligns with conventional logit-based baselines ( Yeom et al., 2018 ; Mattern et al., 2023 ; Shi et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ) : 
s ∗ ( x i ∣ x < i ) = 1 N ∑ j = 1 N 𝕀 [ x i = x ^ i ( j ) ] , s^{*}(x_{i}\mid x_{<i})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[x_{i}=\hat{x}^{(j)}_{i}\right], (1) 
where 𝕀 ⁡ [ ⋅ ] \mathbb{I}[\cdot] is the indicator function.
Laplace smoothing is applied to prevent zero-probability estimates.
We denote this version as SimMIA ∗ . 
However, this approach relies on a hard indicator, which is inherently sparse due to the high dimensionality of the vocabulary.
This way also fails to utilize the information present in sampled words that do not exactly match x i x_{i} . Consequently, it suffers from high variance across domains (See Section 4 ). To overcome this, SimMIA introduces a soft scoring function .
The key intuition is that if x x is part of the training data of M M , the model should assign high probabilities not only to x i x_{i} itself but also to words that are semantically similar to it.
To capture this proximity, we define the similarity between two words a a and b b as: 
sim ⁡ ( a , b ) = cos ⁡ ( Enc ⁡ ( a ) , Enc ⁡ ( b ) ) + 1 2 , \mathrm{sim}(a,b)=\frac{\mathrm{cos}\!\left(\mathrm{Enc}(a),\mathrm{Enc}(b)\right)+1}{2}, (2) 
where Enc ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{Enc}(\cdot) denotes the word embedding and cos ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{cos}(\cdot) computes cosine similarity.
The word-level score is then given by 
s ( x i ∣ x < i ) = 𝔼 x ^ i ∼ M ( ⋅ ∣ x < i ) [ sim ( x i , x ^ i ) ] , s(x_{i}\mid x_{<i})=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}_{i}\sim M(\cdot\mid x_{<i})}[\mathrm{sim}(x_{i},\hat{x}_{i})], (3) 
where the expectation is approximated by the sample mean over { x ^ i ( j ) } j = 1 N \{\hat{x}_{i}^{(j)}\}^{N}_{j=1} .
This soft formulation leverages both the empirical frequency of x i x_{i} and the semantic alignment of non-exact matches, thereby enhancing robustness across domains.

Relative Aggregation 
Finally, we aggregate word-level scores into an overall membership score for x x . Xie et al. (2024) demonstrated that the likelihood of member data is significantly lower than that of non-member data when perturbed by non-members.
We observe a similar phenomenon in the relative score distribution of s ⁡ ( x i ∣ x < i ) s(x_{i}\mid x_{<i}) in Eq. 3 , as illustrated in Figure 2 .
Drawing on this insight, we adopt a formulation similar to Xie et al. (2024) and define the final membership score as: 
SimMIA ( x ) = − 1 L ∑ i = 1 L s ⁡ ( x i ∣ P ⊕ x < i ) s ⁡ ( x i ∣ x < i ) , \mathrm{SimMIA}(x)=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{s(x_{i}\mid P\oplus x_{<i})}{s(x_{i}\mid x_{<i})}, (4) 
where ⊕ \oplus is the string concatenation and P = p 1 ⊕ p 2 ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ p T P=p_{1}\oplus p_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus p_{T} denotes the concatenation of T T non-member samples used to prefix the context.

3 WikiMIA-25 
While numerous MIA benchmarks have been proposed, they are often limited to testing specific LLMs.
For example, WikiMIA ( Shi et al., 2024 ) applies only to models released between 2017 and 2023, while MIMIR ( Duan et al., 2024 ) is applicable only to models trained on The Pile ( Gao et al., 2021 ) .
Given the rapid release cadence of LLMs, benchmarks require frequent updates to remain relevant.
For instance, WikiMIA-24 ( Fu et al., 2025 ) extends the validity period of WikiMIA to 2024. 
Moreover, evaluating proprietary LLMs presents a unique challenge: unlike open-source models, which remain accessible indefinitely, proprietary models are hosted on API endpoints subject to vendor deprecation.
This creates a gap: as vendors retire older model versions that fit a benchmark’s timeframe, they release newer versions trained on data that post-dates the benchmark.
This temporal misalignment renders the benchmark ineffective.
For instance, while WikiMIA-24 updates its data to March 2024, the oldest Gemini version available by the time of conducting experiments, Gemini-2.0-Flash, possesses a knowledge cutoff of June 2024.
Since the model has likely seen the benchmark’s data during training, WikiMIA-24 cannot reliably evaluate Gemini. 
To evaluate MIA on the latest LLMs, we introduce WikiMIA-25.
Similar to WikiMIA and WikiMIA-24, we construct datasets from recent Wikipedia dumps.
We designate events added from January 2015 to December 2016 as member data, and events from March 2025 to September 2025 as non-member data.
We truncate examples to L L whitespace-delimited tokens, creating buckets where L ∈ { 32 , 64 , 128 } L\in\{32,64,128\} .
The final benchmark comprises 759, 875, and 529 pairs of member and non-member examples for the respective lengths. 
4 Experiments 
4.1 Setup 
Benchmarks 
We evaluate our method on WikiMIA ( Shi et al., 2024 ) and MIMIR ( Duan et al., 2024 ) , alongside our WikiMIA-25.
For WikiMIA, we utilize subsets with sequence lengths of 32, 64, and 128.
For MIMIR, we report results on the 7-gram version.
To ensure cost efficiency on WikiMIA-25, we sample a balanced subset of 120 members and 120 non-members from the length-32 split.
For all benchmarks, we reserve 10 non-members to construct the non-member prefix P P and evaluate on the remaining examples. 
Consistent with prior work ( Shi et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024a ) , we report the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) as our primary metric, along with the True Positive Rate at 5% False Positive Rate (TPR@5%FPR). 
Baselines 
We compare SimMIA against ten MIA baselines across gray-box and black-box settings.
In the gray-box setting, we include: Loss , Zlib , and Lowercase ( Carlini et al., 2021 ) , which utilize the raw input loss, the input’s Zlib entropy, and the lower-cased input loss, respectively; Reference ( Carlini et al., 2022 ) , which calibrates loss using a smaller model; Neighbors ( Mattern et al., 2023 ) , which compares input loss to that of similar tokens; Min-K% ( Shi et al., 2024 ) and Min-K%++ ( Zhang et al., 2024a ) , which focus on low-probability tokens; ReCaLL ( Xie et al., 2024 ) , which measures relative conditional likelihood;
and PETAL ( He et al., 2025 ) , which uses regression based on token-label similarity.
In the black-box setting, we evaluate SaMIA ( Kaneko et al., 2025 ) , which approximates likelihood via ROUGE-N scores over sampled continuations.

Models 
For WikiMIA, we test OPT-6.7B ( Zhang et al., 2022 ) , Pythia-6.9B ( Biderman et al., 2023 ) , LLaMA-7B ( Touvron et al., 2023 ) , and GPT-NeoX-20B ( Black et al., 2022 ) .
For MIMIR, we follow Duan et al. (2024) and focus on the Pythia-dedup family (160M, 1.4B, 2.8B, and 6.9B).
For WikiMIA-25, we extend our evaluation beyond Pythia-6.9B to newer models, including Qwen3-8B-Base ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , Claude-4.5-Haiku ( Anthropic, 2025 ) , Gemini-2.5-Flash ( Team, 2025 ) , and GPT-5-Chat ( OpenAI, 2025 ) . 
Implementation Details 
We reproduce all baselines using their official hyperparameter settings.
For SimMIA, we set the sample size N = 100 N=100 , except for WikiMIA-25 where we reduce N N to 10 to mitigate API costs.
The number of shots T T is set to 7 for WikiMIA and WikiMIA-25, and 10 for MIMIR.
We use all-MiniLM-L6-v2 2 2 2 https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 for embedding ( He et al., 2025 ) .
A specific exception is applied to the DM Mathematics split of MIMIR, where we employ Exact Match for numerical tokens.
In mathematical reasoning, numerical proximity does not imply semantic equivalence (e.g., distinct result values indicate incorrect reasoning regardless of magnitude); therefore, we strictly enforce exact matching for numbers.
All experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA RTX 3090.

4.2 Main Results 
SimMIA achieves SOTA black-box MIA performance and rivals gray-box counterparts. Tables 1 and 2 demonstrate that SimMIA substantially outperforms existing black-box baselines, improving AUC by 16.6 and 14.9 on WikiMIA and MIMIR, respectively.
Remarkably, SimMIA bridges the gap with SOTA gray-box methods; it trails the best gray-box performance by only 6.1 and 3.4 AUC points on average.
This margin is comparable to the difference between the first- and second-best gray-box methods (a gap of 10.2 and 0.7 AUC), suggesting that SimMIA remains highly competitive despite lacking access to model internals.
It even surpasses the best gray-box method on specific LLMs, such as OPT-6.7B on WikiMIA.

The soft scoring function ensures robustness across benchmarks. Directly estimating label probabilities (SimMIA ∗ ) yields peak performance on WikiMIA-style datasets (WikiMIA and WikiMIA-25), outperforming SaMIA, the SOTA black-box baseline, by +19.2 AUC.
However, we find that employing our soft scoring function (SimMIA) offers superior generalization and stability across diverse benchmarks.
As shown in Tables 1 , 2 , and 3 , SimMIA consistently outperforms SaMIA by a significant margin in all settings.
Crucially, while SimMIA ∗ surpasses SaMIA by only +7.6 AUC on MIMIR, SimMIA remains robust, outperforming SaMIA by +14.9 AUC on MIMIR while maintaining high efficacy on WikiMIA-style datasets (+15.4 AUC).

Impact of Prefix Length 
Since black-box MIAs often rely on continuations generated from a prefix, we investigate how the length of this prefix affects performance.
Intuitively, performance is expected to be inversely proportional to the prefix ratio: as the prefix length increases (from 10% to 90%), the remaining target segment becomes shorter, providing less signal for the MIA to exploit.
We compare SimMIA (which defaults to conditioning on the first word) against SaMIA (which defaults to the first 50%).
As shown in the left panel of Figure 3 , SimMIA aligns with the expected behavior: performance peaks at a 10% prefix ratio and declines monotonically as the prefix grows.
In contrast, SaMIA shows no clear gains from shorter prefixes: it attains its best performance at a 20% prefix ratio, then fluctuates across intermediate ratios before dropping at 70%.
This suggests that SaMIA fails to exploit the rich signal available in the early portions of the text, whereas SimMIA remains robust even with minimal prefix conditioning. 
Impact of Sample Size 
We examine the trade-off between computational budget (sample size N N ) and inference accuracy.
The middle panel of Figure 3 demonstrates that SimMIA consistently outperforms SaMIA across all sample sizes.
Notably, SimMIA exhibits better scalability: it continues to accrue performance gains up to N = 30 N=30 . Conversely, SaMIA saturates rapidly, failing to yield improvements beyond N = 10 N=10 . 
Sensitivity to Hyperparameters 
SimMIA introduces two design choices: the number of non-member shots ( T T ) and the embedding model.
In the right panel of Figure 3 , we observe that inference performance improves as T T increases, stabilizing around T = 7 T=7 . This suggests that while non-member shots are crucial, a small, computationally efficient set is sufficient.
Regarding the embedding model, Table 5 reports performance across a spectrum of architectures, ranging from static embeddings (Word2Vec ( Mikolov et al., 2013 ) , fastText ( Bojanowski et al., 2017 ) ) to state-of-the-art dense retrievers (e.g., bge-large-en-v1.5 ( Xiao et al., 2023 ) , UAE-Large-V1 ( Li and Li, 2024 ) , and mxbai-embed-large-v1 ( Lee et al., 2024 ) ).

Table 7 confirms that performance improves as similarity increases (8.7 AUC on average).
Notably, however, the fixed prefix strategy yields performance comparable to the moderately similar and random settings.
This implies that dynamic retrieval is not strictly necessary; a simple, fixed set of non-member shots is sufficient to capture the primary benefits of SimMIA. 
Progressive Disclosure 
SimMIA has demonstrated its effectiveness in a strict black-box setting, where only generated text is available to the attacker.
However, real-world API access exists on a spectrum; some services may disclose metadata such as tokenization results or token probabilities.
We therefore ask: can SimMIA adapt to exploit this additional information for improved detection? 
We evaluate SimMIA using GPT-5-Chat and GPT-4.1-mini on WikiMIA-25. The former provides tokenization results for the input and output, while the latter provides both tokenization results and the log probabilities of generated tokens.
We adapt SimMIA as follows:
(1) When tokenization is visible, we shift from word-by-word sampling to token-by-token sampling.

5 Related Work 
Membership inference attacks (MIA) determine whether a data point was included in a model’s training set ( Shokri et al., 2017 ) .
While a large body of work on LLMs relies on model logits, either directly ( Mattern et al., 2023 ; Shi et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024a ; Zhang et al., 2024b ) or via relative log-likelihoods ( Xie et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) , these methods are inapplicable to proprietary LLMs where APIs typically restrict logit access. 
To address this, recent studies have explored more constrained gray-box settings. He et al. (2025) approximate target logits using an open-source proxy, while Tong et al. (2025) exploit tokenizer behaviors.
However, these methods generally presume access to the target’s tokenized outputs, which are often unavailable. 
Consequently, research has shifted toward black-box settings where only generated text is observable.
Existing approaches mostly target specialized scenarios.
Quiz-based methods ( Golchin and Surdeanu, 2025 ; Duarte et al., 2024 ) use manual templates to generate QA pairs for detection;
training-based methods ( Hu et al., 2025 ) train detectors on known members and non-members;
and set-based strategies ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Kuditipudi et al., 2025 ) infer membership at the dataset level. 
Closest to our work are sampling-based methods ( Kaneko et al., 2025 ; Dong et al., 2024 ) .
Yet, these general-purpose approaches heavily rely on surface-form statistics (e.g., N-gram matches), making them brittle across domains.
SimMIA advances this line of work by improving both the sampling strategy and the scoring function. 
6 Conclusion 
In this work, we propose SimMIA, a robust framework for MIAs in the strict black-box setting.
We also introduce WikiMIA-25, a curated MIA benchmark designed to evaluate modern proprietary LLMs.
Experimental results demonstrate that SimMIA achieves SOTA performance, effectively rivaling gray-box counterparts even when model internals are completely inaccessible. 
Limitations 
Despite its effectiveness, SimMIA shares a fundamental limitation with other sampling-based black-box MIAs on LLMs: the reliance on sampling to estimate membership signals.
Unlike gray-box counterparts that can derive metrics from a single forward pass, general-purpose black-box methods necessitate repeated querying to approximate model behavior.
This requirement inevitably incurs higher computational overhead, latency, and cost.
Consequently, a critical direction for future work is to enhance the sample efficiency of the entire class of sampling-based black-box MIAs, thereby reducing the budget required for reliable detection.
