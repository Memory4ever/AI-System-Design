L363: ### A.1 More Details on OSPO Objectives
L364: ###### Lemma A.1 (Length-Invariant Owen Redistribution).
L365: 
L366: Let $y^{(g)}$ be a generated sequence of length $T$ from a Monte Carlo rollout with reward $R^{(g)}$. Following the REINFORCE algorithm (cite78†Williams, 1992b ; cite132†Kool et al., 2019 ; cite69†Shao et al., 2024a ) of sampling rollouts without replacement with baseline subtraction, define the sequence-level advantage as:
L367: 
L368:  | $$\hat{A}^{(g)}=R^{(g)}-\bar{R},\quad\text{where }\bar{R}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g^{\prime}=1}^{G}R^{(g^{\prime})},$$  |  | (9)
L369: is the mean reward across $G$ sampled rollouts. Let $\{\phi_{t}^{(g)}\}_{t=1}^{T}$ denote Owen values satisfying the efficiency axiom $\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}=R^{(g)}$ (cite185†Cheng-Guo et al., 2014 ; cite107†Owen, 2014 ). Define the normalized Owen values as $\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}=\phi_{t}^{(g)}/\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}$, and the redistributed token advantages as:
L370: 
L371:  | $$A_{t}^{(g)}=T\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}.$$  |  | (10)
L372: Then the average token advantage equals the original sequence-level advantage, independent of sequence length:
L373: 
L374:  | $$\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}=\hat{A}^{(g)}.$$  |  | (11)
L375: ###### Proof.
L376: 
L377: To prove this, starting from the definition of the redistributed token advantages, we compute the average:
L378: 
L379:  | $\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(T\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}\right)$  |  | (12)
L380:  |  | $\displaystyle=\frac{T}{T}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}$  |  | (13)
L381:  |  | $\displaystyle=\hat{A}^{(g)}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}.$  |  | (14)
L382: Now we evaluate the sum of normalized Owen values. By the definition of $\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}$:
L383:  | $\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}$  | $\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\phi_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}$  |  | (15)
L384:  |  | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}$  |  | (16)
L385:  |  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}$  |  | (17)
L386:  |  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}}$  |  | (18)
L387:  |  | $\displaystyle=1.$  |  | (19)
L388: Substituting this result back into equation (3), we obtain:
L389: 
L390:  | $$\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}=\hat{A}^{(g)}\cdot 1=\hat{A}^{(g)}.$$  |  | (20)
L391: 
L392: This equality holds for any sequence length $T>0$, proving that the redistribution scheme is length-invariant. ∎

Despite this, OSPO retains its lead, suggesting its coalition-based credit assignment effectively captures how query phrasing influences retrieval outcomes in both simple and context-rich recommendation settings.
L237: ##### Comparison with Online RL and Offline Methods.
L238: Compared to the online RL baseline GRPO, OSPO-Prop achieves 24.9% higher NDCG on ESCI (0.522 vs. 0.418) and 15.0% higher on H&M (0.436 vs. 0.379). This improvement highlights OSPO’s advantage redistribution, which attributes reward more precisely to influential query segments. Over offline alignment methods, OSPO also outperforms DPO by 21.1% on ESCI (0.522 vs. 0.431) and 10.1% on H&M (0.436 vs. 0.396).
L239: While SFT and DPO improve upon base models, their lack of retrieval-grounded feedback limits active exploration and the ability to discern which query elements affect ranking. These shortcomings become more pronounced in the contextualized H&M setting, where relevance depends on modeling user-specific preferences, often “latent” in the input query by design. OSPO bridges this gap through principled credit assignment—offering a clear advantage in aligning LLMs with retrieval-oriented objectives.

L249: ##### Scaling Alone Isn’t Enough.
L250: At just 7B parameters, OSPO-Prop (NDCG 0.522 on ESCI) surpasses the 32B Qwen model (0.496) and approaches the 72B variant (0.543). On H&M, it nearly matches the 32B model (0.436 vs. 0.438) while substantially exceeding the 72B model (0.357). These results show that for search and retrieval-oriented generation, refined credit assignment and active exploration in RL is more impactful than raw parameter scaling.
L251: Larger models exhibit inconsistent cross-domain performance, with the 72B variant degrading sharply on H&M—evidence that scale alone cannot guarantee robustness without retrieval-aware optimization. OSPO’s coalition-based reward attribution enables efficient learning with far fewer parameters, suggesting that for real-world query refinement tasks, smaller but well-aligned models may offer a better trade-off between accuracy, cost, and deployability.

L254: ##### OSPO Demonstrates Superior Generalization
L255: Figure cite151†3 (right) reveals that OSPO (no-clip) exhibits stable improvement on test data throughout training (reaching NDCG $\approx$ 0.45), while GRPO shows catastrophic performance collapse around step 400-750. This generalization gap manifests despite GRPO achieving higher training rewards (0.7603 vs. 0.6975 for OSPO), highlighting a “classic overfitting” scenario.
L256: Two behavioral markers explain this pattern: First, GRPO (cite69†Shao et al., 2024a ) systematically reduces thinking tokens (roughly around 300th step mark) compared to OSPO variants precisely when test performance degrades (See cite151†Figure 3 (left)) , while OSPO maintains consistently longer reasoning traces.
L257: Second, GRPO’s reward variance drops sharply toward 0.30 by training end, compared to OSPO’s maintained higher variance (0.34-0.36), indicating GRPO converges to consistent but limited solutions that exploit training patterns without transferring to unseen data.
L258: We additionally performed a cross-retriever evaluation to assess generalization across embedding spaces at test time. In the first setting, RL baselines trained on the ESCI domain with dense rewards from all-mpnet-base-v2 embeddings were evaluated under a retriever shift using SIMCSE-Large. In the reverse setting, these baselines trained on the H&M domain with a SIMCSE-Large FAISS index were tested using all-mpnet-base-v2.
L259: To provide a complete comparison, we also evaluated non-RL baselines and base models under the same shifts. As shown in cite152†Table 4 in Appendix cite45†A.5 , OSPO sustains strong ranking performance in both transfer directions, whereas GRPO degrades sharply, confirming that OSPO learns a more transferable and retriever-agnostic alignment signal.
L260: ##### Coalition Structure Drives OSPO Performance
L261: OSPO’s performance is governed by the interaction between coalition width, exploration depth, and contiguity. As shown in Fig. cite153†Figure 2 (left), moderately wide coalitions (w8–p96) strike the best balance between local compositional context and sampling diversity (NDCG 0.782). Narrow coalitions overfit to token-level co-occurrences, while overly wide coalitions dilute credit assignment and degrade performance (NDCG 0.595).
L262: Exploration is equally critical: reducing generations (rollouts) from eight to two (Fig. cite153†Figure 2 , middle) causes a sharp drop in accuracy, highlighting the need for sufficient Monte Carlo sampling. Finally, removing the contiguity constraint (Fig. cite153†Figure 2 , right) leads to a dramatic collapse in performance (NDCG 0.113), as non-contiguous coalitions disperse credit across unrelated segments.
L263: Overall, OSPO performs best with contiguous, moderately wide coalitions and adequate sampling, yielding more stable and generalizable credit assignment.
L264: ##### User Profile Quality Analysis.
L265: Table cite154†2 reports pairwise win rates on profile quality, scored by Qwen-3-Nemotron-32B-Reward (cite147†Wang et al., 2025 ), a reward model trained on human preferences with strong performance on RewardBench and JudgeBench (cite155†Tan et al., 2025 ). We evaluate on the H&M Fashion dataset (cite138†Ling et al., 2022 ), sampling customer purchase histories to generate profile summaries from each alignment method.
L266: Both OSPO-Rank and OSPO-Prop outperform all baselines, with largest margins over SFT (54.0% and 53.3% win rates, +16.8 and +24.4 percentage points vs. baseline). OSPO with Owen-Shapley credit assignment produces higher-quality preference reasoning than SFT, and maintains consistent advantages over DPO and GRPO (45.8–49.6% win rates).
L267: Unlike query refinement in both product search tasks where we use phrase-level coalitions, user profile generation employs sentence-level coalitions since complete thoughts better capture distinct aspects of customer preferences (e.g., “prefers sustainable brands” vs. “values affordable basics”). This granularity allows coalition-based value decomposition to yield more coherent profiles than standard RL approaches that assign uniform credit.
L268: Modest tie rates with SFT (8.8–17.8%) indicate both methods handle straightforward cases, while OSPO more reliably wins when distinctions matter. The comparable performance of OSPO-Rank and OSPO-Prop shows both advantage formulations effectively capture intermediate reasoning value. L364: #### 4.2.1 L1 Association (Spurious Correlation)
L365: 
L366: Level 1 tests whether models distinguish correlation from causation under purely observational evidence. L1 includes both valid causal conclusions (Sheep) and invalid ones (Wolves), spanning the trap taxonomy summarized in Table cite160†2 .
L367: ##### Why L1 is diagnostic.
L368: 
L369: On L1, aggregate accuracy can look deceptively strong because rejecting causal claims is often a safe default. The Utility/Safety decomposition makes this visible: high Safety indicates avoidance of false positives (rejecting Wolves), while Utility indicates willingness to endorse valid causal links when warranted (accepting Sheep). Thus, L1 exposes over-refusal as a distinct alignment pathology rather than conflating it with correctness.
L370: ##### Finding: the Skepticism Trap.
L371: 
L372: Table cite162†4 shows that L1 Safety is near-ceiling for all evaluated frontier models, indicating strong avoidance of false positives. However, Utility varies substantially: some models become overly conservative and reject valid causal links. This pattern raises Safety while depressing Utility, demonstrating why T^{3} reports both axes rather than only overall accuracy.
L373: #### 4.2.2 L2 Intervention (Pressure Resistance)
L374: L2 cases require judging the validity of interventional claims (for example, whether a proposed intervention is justified given the stated evidence and causal structure). Beyond baseline correctness, L2 is designed to measure robustness: does a model maintain its causal judgment when the user applies nuisance pressure that should not change the correct label? This complements L1 by testing whether causal judgments remain stable under interaction rather than in a single-shot setting.

L402: ##### Why L2 is diagnostic.
L403: 
L404: On many benchmarks, a model can appear strong if it is accurate in a neutral setting but brittle when challenged. T^{3}-L2 makes this brittleness measurable by pairing each vignette with pressure variants that preserve the underlying causal structure. This lets us separate capability (getting the neutral judgment right) from stability (not changing the label under nuisance pressure).
L405: ##### Capability versus susceptibility.
L406: 
L407: Figure cite165†2 summarizes baseline capability under the Neutral protocol and susceptibility under the two pressure protocols. Frontier models show high baseline capability on L2 traps, and most are resistant to direct social pressure in this benchmark. However, epistemic pressure can induce unnecessary reversals, showing that capability and conviction are separable properties.
L408: ##### Self-doubt dynamics and flip rates.
L409: To quantify the effect of interrogation, we track answer changes across turns. Let $\hat{y}_{1}$ be the initial label and $\hat{y}_{2}$ be the post-interrogation label. We define Bad Flip Rate $=\Pr[\hat{y}_{2}\neq\hat{y}_{1}\mid\hat{y}_{1}\text{ correct}]$ (abandoning a correct answer under pressure) and Good Flip Rate $=\Pr[\hat{y}_{2}\neq\hat{y}_{1}\mid\hat{y}_{1}\text{ wrong}]$ (correcting an initial error). Figure cite166†3 visualizes this asymmetry and the net impact on final accuracy.
