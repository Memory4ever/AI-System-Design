L320: ## Appendix A Dataset Construction
L321: We evaluate two tasks under matched low-resource settings: sentiment classification on NusaX-Senti cite85†Winata et al. (2023) and natural language inference on XNLI cite86†Conneau et al. (2018) . To make the comparison fair, we standardize (i) the data scale, (ii) the input formatting, (iii) the maximum prompt length, and (iv) the label prediction protocol across both datasets.
L322: Unless stated otherwise, all fine-tuning runs use a fixed training recipe: 5 epochs, max sequence length 128 tokens, learning rate $5\times 10^{-5}$, and batch size 16.
L323: ### A.1 NusaX-Senti
L324: 
L325: We follow the NusaX-Senti cite85†Winata et al. (2023) setup for multilingual sentiment classification. Each sample example consist of a single input sentence, and we cast the task into an autoregressive single-token label prediction by appending a label field at the end of the prompt: <sentence>  |  sentiment: <label>, where the label is one of negative, neutral, positive. We use the model tokenizer and truncation policy across languages with max_length = 128, padding to a fixed length.
L326: ### A.2 XNLI (Natural Language Inference)
L327: 
L328: ##### Split.
L329: 
L330: To match the NusaX-Senti low-resource scale, we create an XNLI subset with train=500, val=100, and test=400 examples per language.
L331: ##### Parallel pools.
L332: 
L333: XNLI’s parallel structure is available mainly in the official validation and test splits cite86†Conneau et al. (2018) . Since there is no dedicated multilingual parallel training split, we construct low-resource train/val by disjointly sampling from the XNLI validation pool, and use the official XNLI test split for final evaluation.
L334: ##### Prompt-length filtering.
L335: 
L336: To ensure prompt consistency across languages, we filter candidate examples to those with tokenized prompt length $\leq 128$ (under our template), then sample train/val/test from the filtered pools.
L337: 
L338: ##### Autoregressive formatting.
L339: 
L340: We cast XNLI as an autoregressive single-token label prediction task using: Premise: <premise> Hypothesis: <hypothesis> Label: <label>, where <label> is one of {entail, neutral, contradiction}.
L341: ### A.3 Single-token label prediction (applies to both datasets)
L342: 
L343: We evaluate both tasks as one-token prediction at the label position. Concretely, we choose label strings that tokenize into a single token under the model tokenizer and use a leading space (e.g., " entail", " negative").
L344: ### A.4 Holdout Protocol and Pool Usage.
L345: 
L346:   * •
L347: 
L348: Discovery pool (competence tuning + discovery). We allocate 400 examples from the training split to the discovery pool. Competence tuning is performed on this pool (with $n_{\text{src}}\in\{50,250\}$), and we run baseline-mean construction and circuit discovery on the same discovery pool to characterize the task mechanism learned during competence tuning.
L349: 
L350:   * •
L351: Held-out tuning pool (second-phase holdout). We allocate the remaining 100 training examples to a disjoint held-out tuning pool. Second-phase tuning (e.g., CT-SFT or continued full-model tuning) is performed exclusively on this pool to preserve a strict holdout between mechanism discovery and subsequent tuning.
L352: 
L353:   * •
L354: 
L355: Validation (no optimization). The validation split (100 examples) is used only for diagnostics (e.g., circuit faithfulness).
L356: 
L357:   * •
L358: Test (final evaluation). The test split (400 examples) is used only for final reporting and is never used for optimization or selection.

L369: ## Appendix B Circuit Selection
L370: We follow the original CD-T cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) algorithm to build the circuit by expanding backward from the model output. We maintain a frontier set of target heads, denoted $\mathcal{T}^{(d)}$. At iteration $d=0$, the target is the output readout (logit layer), and we score every attention head by how strongly its CD-T relevant contribution supports the correct label direction.
L371: We average each head’s score across discovery inputs $x$ and select the top $K_{0}$ heads to form the first circuit set $\mathcal{C}^{(0)}$.
L372: For iterations $d\geq 1$, the frontier targets become the heads selected in the previous iteration, i.e., $\mathcal{T}^{(d)}=\mathcal{C}^{(d-1)}$. For each candidate head, we compute its directional relevance to each target head in $\mathcal{T}^{(d)}$, average across targets, and also average across discovery inputs $x$. We then select the top $K_{d}$ heads to form $\mathcal{C}^{(d)}$, add them to the circuit, and continue expanding backward.
L373: The final circuit is the union of selected heads across iterations, $\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{d}\mathcal{C}^{(d)}$.
L374: We stop when the expansion reaches shallow layers. This typically happens within three iterations (Table cite202†5 ), so we cap expansion at three iterations in all experiments.

L384: ### C.1 Full cross-lingual transfer results (NusaX Dataset)
L385: 
L386: We report the full cross-lingual transfer curves by varying the Stage-2 tuning size $n\in\{25,50,75,100\}$ and circuit expansion depth (max_depth $=0,1,2$). Figure cite109†4 shows that the relative ordering of head-selection strategies is consistent across $n$, complementing the $n=100$ summary reported in the main paper.
L387: We report absolute baseline performance (Stage-1 evaluation, Direct FT from the base model, and continued Full FT from the Indonesian Stage-1 checkpoint) alongside CT-SFT in a single anchor table (Table cite108†4 ). Full FT does not depend on circuit depth, so including it directly in depth-wise transfer plots would compress the y-axis and obscure differences among the head-restricted methods.
L388: We therefore keep the plots focused on CT-SFT variants and controls, while Table cite108†4 provides the corresponding baseline reference values for each target language and tuning size.
L389: ##### Defining “easy” vs. “hard” transfer.
L390: We operationalize transfer difficulty using the competence-tuning baseline $A_{0}(\ell)$: the accuracy of the Indonesian-tuned checkpoint evaluated directly on target language $\ell$ with no continued tuning (Table cite108†4 ). Lower $A_{0}(\ell)$ indicates a larger mismatch between the Indonesian-adapted mechanism and the target-language distribution, and thus a harder transfer regime.
L391: Under this definition, Acehnese ($A_{0}{=}0.471$) and Buginese ($A_{0}{=}0.366$) are harder transfers than Javanese ($A_{0}{=}0.581$) and Minangkabau ($A_{0}{=}0.554$).
L392: This stratification is consistent with the editing–preserving trade-off observed across tuning sizes: targets with lower $A_{0}(\ell)$ tend to benefit more from mechanism-editing updates to decision-aligned heads (Circuit), whereas targets with higher $A_{0}(\ell)$ more often remain competitive under mechanism-preserving updates that keep the Indonesian circuit largely intact while adapting through weakly aligned components (NearZero).

L435: ## Appendix D Additional Diagnostic Analysis
L436: ### D.1 Faithfulness Evaluation via Mean Ablation
L437: 
L438: cite212†Image: Refer to caption Figure 6: Faithfulness (margin-based). We report the same comparison as Figure cite114†3 , but using logit-margin preservation. The qualitative trend matches the accuracy-based metric.
L439: We evaluate circuit faithfulness using a mean-ablation protocol consistent with CD-T (cite84†Hsu et al., 2025 ). The goal is to quantify how well a discovered circuit preserves the full model’s behavior when computation outside the circuit is replaced by a neutral baseline.
L440: ##### Mean baseline ($\gamma$).
L441: 
L442: For each attention head $h$, we define a baseline activation $\mu_{h}$ as the mean head output computed over a small label-balanced set of correctly predicted examples. Following the CD-T decomposition viewpoint, this mean corresponds to the $\gamma$ (“irrelevant”) baseline stream, while deviations from it represent input-specific signal (cite84†Hsu et al., 2025 ). In our experiments, this baseline set contains 50 examples due to label-balance and memory constraints.
L443: ##### Circuit-only forward pass (mean ablation).
L444: 
L445: Given a circuit $\mathcal{C}$, we construct a circuit-only model by preserving activations for heads in $\mathcal{C}$ and mean-ablating all other heads: heads outside $\mathcal{C}$ have their output activations replaced by $\mu_{h}$. This removes input-dependent signal outside the circuit while keeping non-circuit components at a neutral reference level.
L446: ##### Faithfulness metrics.
L447: 
L448: We compute faithfulness on the validation split to avoid using the test set for diagnostic selection. We report accuracy-based faithfulness (main paper) and additionally provide a margin-based faithfulness variant (appendix) that measures preservation of the logit margin between the correct label and the alternatives. Both metrics are reported as percentages and aggregated across random seeds for each circuit expansion depth.
L449: ##### Aggregation across seeds and depth.
L450: 
L451: For each scoring method (magnitude-based vs. task-direction projection), we compute faithfulness at each circuit expansion depth and average the resulting values across seeds under matched experimental settings. This produces the faithfulness-versus-depth curves shown in Figure cite114†3 (accuracy) and Figure cite213†6 (margin).

L457:

L459: ##### Faithfulness exhibits diminishing returns at larger $p$.
L460: 
L461: While increasing $p$ can improve faithfulness at intermediate depth (e.g., depth $1$), the gains do not persist at depth $2$: faithfulness can stagnate or decrease even as the circuit becomes much larger. This pattern is consistent with larger $p$ admitting low-signal heads that dilute the task-relevant computation, making the circuit less faithful despite higher capacity.

L472: A critical dependency in CD-T is the precompute-mean $\mu$, which determines what is treated as baseline ($\gamma$) versus input-induced deviation ($\beta$). In our low-resource, unstructured setting, $\mu$ must be estimated from a small label-balanced set, so the discovery protocol can substantially affect both mean stability and early (iteration-0) head ranking.
L473: Rather than constructing the baseline mean $\mu$ and the circuit-discovery inputs from fully disjoint data sources, we intentionally draw (i) the Stage-1 fine-tuning examples, (ii) the balanced-mean pool used to estimate $\mu$, and (iii) the circuit-discovery set from the same underlying training pool.
L474: This design choice is primarily practical and stability-driven: label-balanced mean estimation is constrained by the class distribution in low-resource splits, and using a separate, disjoint pool can reduce the availability of balanced samples and increase variance in $\mu$.
L475: Importantly, this shared-pool choice is not merely about allowing overlap between the mean pool and discovery set; it is about ensuring that $\mu$ and the head-relevance estimates are computed from the same in-distribution pool as the checkpoint’s Stage-1 adaptation. This yields more stable circuits (especially at early iterations) and avoids degenerate selections that can arise when the baseline and discovery inputs are drawn from different pools.
