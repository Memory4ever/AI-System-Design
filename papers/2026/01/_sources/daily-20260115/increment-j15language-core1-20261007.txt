L43: Tabular Foundational Models are becoming increasingly popular as a tool to replace traditional machine learning techniques in real world prediction tasks. These models are trained at scale on large collections of synthetic datasets generated from structural causal models (cite18†Peters et al., 2017 ; cite19†Pearl, 2009 ) and related generative processes.
L44: This broad pretraining gives them strong generalization across many tabular distributions and has led to performance that often surpasses classical methods such as gradient boosted trees or shallow neural networks.
L45: As a result they are now seen as potential drop in replacements for a wide range of supervised pipelines in domains such as finance (cite20†Brauer, 2024 ; cite21†Chu et al., 2024 ; cite22†Nguyen, ), healthcare (cite23†Noda et al., 2024 ; cite24†Dyikanov et al., 2024 ; cite25†Alzakari et al., 2024 ; cite26†Karabacak et al., 2024 ), manufacturing and industrial engineering (cite27†Magadán et al., 2023 ; cite28†Bellarmino et al., 2025 ; cite29†He et al., 2025 ) and scientific data analysis (cite30†Chen et al., 2025 ; cite31†Sharma and others, 2025 ; cite32†Sharma, 2025 ).
L46: However, the growing use of such models introduces a significant challenge. The black box transformer based nature of their architecture makes their processing opaque. Their predictions are produced through stacked attention layers and MLP blocks that obscure the intermediate steps of the computation. This opacity stands in contrast to traditional tabular models, which usually provide clearer reasoning paths or explicit feature contributions.
L47: When TabFMs begin replacing these more transparent pipelines, the lack of interpretability becomes a central concern for trust, debugging, and compliance with regulatory requirements. Understanding what information TabFMs store internally and how they compute their outputs becomes increasingly important as these models move toward deployment.
L48: Although TabPFN and related models achieve strong accuracy across a wide range of tasks, there is still very limited understanding of how these architectures represent tabular structure or carry out the computations required for prediction. Existing interpretability work on tabular models mostly focuses on post hoc feature importance, which does not reveal the internal mechanisms that produce the final decision (cite33†Rundel et al., 2024 ).
L49: Meanwhile, the interpretability community studying large language models (cite34†Zhao et al., 2023 ) has shown that transformer representations often contain linearly readable information about tasks (cite35†Todd et al., 2024 ), intermediate computations, and concepts (cite36†Park et al., 2024 ). These results suggest that transformers learn structured internal algorithms rather than acting as unstructured black box approximators.
L50: Since TabPFN relies heavily on in context learning to approximate the target function implied by the provided training data, it becomes natural to ask whether similar algorithmic structure appears inside its hidden representations.
L51: In this work we investigate this question through a set of targeted probing experiments. We construct synthetic datasets where the true functional relationships are known, and we examine how TabPFN v2 (cite37†Ye et al., 2025 ) encodes these relationships inside it’s activations. Our probes target three categories of information. First, we test whether the coefficients of simple linear relationships are recoverable from the hidden states.
L52: Second, we probe for intermediate quantities that arise inside multi step arithmetic expressions, which allows us to study whether the model represents internal computational steps. Third, we analyze how the final predicted value forms throughout the network using both linear probes (cite38†Belinkov, 2022 ) and the logit lens (cite39†nostalgebraist, 2020 ) to track the evolution of the output across layers.
L53: Across the experiments we find interpretable structure in the residual stream. Linear coefficients and intermediate arithmetic terms are often linearly decodable in the middle layers, indicating that these quantities are represented explicitly during computation.
L54: Although the information encoded in the representations might not be consistent across different instances of the model with separate datasets as contexts, we can still see success in extracting different quantities from a single large context with all the datasets combined. We also observe that the correct answer emerges earlier in the forward pass than the final prediction head, showing that the model converges on the correct value before the last layer projects it into the output space.
L55: These findings suggest that TabFMs perform multi step structured computation internally, rather than relying on an opaque end to end mapping. Our contributions are as follows.
L56:   1. 1.
L57: 
L58: We present the first mechanistic and representational analysis of TabPFN models, to the best of our knowledge.
L59: 
L60:   2. 2.
L61: 
L62: We show that both linear coefficients and intermediate arithmetic quantities are embedded in hidden states in a linearly decodable manner.
L63: 
L64:   3. 3.
L65: 
L66: We trace how the model forms the final answer and characterize how this signal evolves through the depth of the transformer.
L67: 
L68:   4. 4.
L69: We provide empirical evidence that TabFMs implement structured internal computation, pointing toward the possibility of more interpretable and trustworthy tabular foundation models.
L70: ## 2 Related Work
L71: While tree-based methods like XGBoost have historically dominated tabular data tasks (cite40†Grinsztajn et al., 2022 ), recent work has shifted toward Deep Learning architectures designed for tabular modalities (cite41†Gorishniy et al., 2021 ). A significant paradigm shift was the introduction of Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs), most notably TabPFN (cite42†Hollmann et al., 2023 ).
L72: Unlike traditional transfer learning, TabPFN is a Transformer pre-trained on a vast corpus of synthetic datasets generated from Structural Causal Models (SCMs) (cite18†Peters et al., 2017 ; cite19†Pearl, 2009 ). It acts as a proxy for Bayesian inference, solving new classification tasks in a single forward pass via In-Context Learning (ICL) (cite37†Ye et al., 2025 ).
L73: Subsequent iterations, such as TabPFN-v2, have addressed scalability limitations regarding context length and feature counts, with a new improved architecture (cite43†Hollmann et al., 2025 ).However, interpretability for these models has largely remained restricted to post-hoc feature importance methods like Shapley Value’s or Representation Analysis and other classical techniques (cite37†Ye et al., 2025 ; cite43†Hollmann et al., 2025 ; cite33†Rundel et al., 2024 ) rather than mechanistic investigation.
L74: As TabPFN relies on ICL to approximate classification functions, understanding the mechanics of ICL is crucial. In Large Language Models (LLMs), ICL is hypothesized to emerge from specific attention circuits. cite44†Olsson et al. (2022) identified induction heads which are circuits that implement a ”copying” mechanism by attending to previous tokens that appeared in similar contexts as a primary driver of ICL. Furthermore, cite45†Hendel et al. (2023) and cite35†Todd et al.
L75: (2024) introduced the concept of Task Vectors (or Function Vectors), showing that the ”task” specified by the context (e.g., a classification rule) is compressed into a distinct, manipulable direction within the model’s residual stream.
L76: Our work is grounded in the methodology of linear probing (cite46†Alain and Bengio, 2018 ) seeking to decode information stored in intermediate layers. While this is well-established in NLP (cite47†Belinkov, 2021 ), recent work has extended these techniques to continuous-value domains closer to tabular data. cite48†Wiliński et al.
L77: (2025) analyzed representations in Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs), employing Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) to map layer-wise similarity and activation steering to identify steerable concepts (e.g., trend and seasonality) within the latent space. They demonstrated that, much like LLMs, continuous-domain Transformers learn structured, disentangled representations of high-level concepts.
L78: We extend this line of inquiry to Tabular FMs, investigating whether TabPFN similarly encodes decision boundaries and feature interactions in its residual stream. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first mechanistic interpretability study of TabPFN models.
L79: ## 3 Experiments and Results
L80: ### 3.1 Preliminaries
L81: Transformer models have been used extensively in both NLP and computer vision to capture relationships within sequential inputs. This paradigm has been extended to structured tabular data, where transformer-based approaches have become competitive with classical models.
L82: A tabular dataset is defined consisting of data in which each row $x_{i}$ consists of $d$ features or attributes, where $d$ typically varies across datasets with a label $y_{i}$ belongs to $[C]=\{1,\ldots,C\}$ for a classification task or is a continuous numerical value for a regression task.
L83: Among transformer models for tabular learning, TabPFN currently represents the state of the art, with widespread adoption in both industry and academia. TabPFN performs in-context learning to predict the test set given the train set, without requiring further parameter updates. To enable this it is pretrained on a large collection of synthetic datasets and learns to approximate Bayesian inference over this prior distribution.
L84: The primary model used in this paper is TabPFN v2, thus necessitating a deeper dive into its architecture.
L85: #### TabPFN v2.
L86: The first step of the pre-processing pipeline includes numerical encoding of categorical features and normalisation of all the features. The model then embeds the $d$ features into a $k$-dimensional space, adding perturbations to differentiate the features. Alongside the label embedding $\tilde{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}$, each training instance $x_{i}$ is represented by $(d+1)$ tokens with dimension $k$. For a test instance $x^{*}$, a dummy label is used to generate the label embedding $\tilde{y}^{*}$.
L87: The full shape of the input is thus represented as a tensor of shape $(N+1)\times(d+1)\times k$. TabPFN v2 uses two types of self-attention, one after the other, the first being over samples known as sample attention and the other over attributes known as feature attention, which combine to enable in-context learning across either axis.
L88: At the end of the transformer layers the output token corresponding to the dummy label $\tilde{y}^{*}$ is extracted and mapped to a 10-class logit for classification or is mapped to a single number in case of regression.

L97: As TabPFN is an In-Context Learning based model, previous work has shown that coefficients of linear relationships can be extracted from transformers when they are given train dataset as context and asked to predict the answer for test samples. Building on this we investigate whether TabPFN’s internal representations encode the coefficients of the modeled linear function
L98: 
L99:  | $$z=\alpha x+\beta y$$  |
L100: . To recover these coefficients from the activations, we use two distinct probing setups. A probe is a function
L101: 
L102:  | $$\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{Y},$$  |
L103: 
L104: which maps an activation tensor $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ to a target property, in this case $\alpha\in\mathcal{Y}.$ Probes are typically chosen to be simple, lightweight functions, such as linear maps or a shallow neural network.
L105: First configuration. We vary $\alpha$ and $\beta$ across multiple datasets and then fit separate instances of TabPFN for each of these datasets. We can write dataset $n$ thus as:
L106: 
L107:  | $$f_{n}(x)=\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n}x$$  |
L108: We then extract forat a single layer for all datapoints activation $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times k}$ and flatten it to get a activation $A\in\mathbb{R}^{ndk}$ vector, trying to learn the probe. We collate these activations for all the different datasets to construct the probing dataset.
L109: 
L110: The probing dataset is a mapping from this activation vector to the coefficient $\alpha$ or $\beta$.
L111: 
L112:  | $$\phi:\mathbb{R}^{ndk}\to(\alpha,\beta)$$  |
L113: This approach yields poor training accuracy. The low probe performance indicates that each TabPFN fit encodes the coefficients differently, so when activations from many fits are pooled, the coefficients no longer align in any common representation that a probe can read out.
L114: Second configuration. In this setup we combine the multiple datasets with varying $\alpha$ and $\beta$ into one large dataset with another added input variable which is unique and maps directly to the different coefficient combinations. So, this added variable acts like a switch input that switches between the coefficient pairs.
L115: 
L116:  | $$f(x;u)=\alpha(u)\,x+\beta(u)\,x$$  |
L117: With this dataset we fit the TabPFN model with dataset containing different coefficient pairs and then extract activations from a holdout test set. From these extracted activations we construct the probing dataset which is a mapping from the activations of all the tokens but the switch token to the coefficients. The individual activations which are of shape $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$ are flattened to $A\in\mathbb{R}^{dk}$.
L118: The probing dataset thus is a mapping from the activations of a single data point to the coefficient $\alpha$ or $\beta$.
L119:  | $$\phi:\mathbb{R}^{dk}\to(\alpha,\beta)$$  |
L120: 
L121: The results for this setup, as show in cite51†Figure 1 , indicate high train and test performance for the probes, showing that we are able to extract the coefficients from the internal activations from a single fit.
L122: This performance disparity between the first and the second configuration indicates that the successful probes in the second configuration setting likely rely on distinguishing between dataset-specific signatures in the coefficients of the linear expressions rather than decoding the coefficient themselves.
L123: We posit from this evidence that the coefficients are not encoded as a universal quantity in the representation as shown by the low training accuracy on different fits, but it can be extracted from the representations to a degree as shown by the same fit probing results.
L124: We also run the above probing experiments with increasingly complex probes. The complexity of the probes is controlled by adding more hidden layers into the MLP. The results in cite51†Figure 2 show that with increasing complexity, the $R^{2}$ scores decrease. This gives evidence for the linear encoding of the coefficients within the activations of the model.

L78: # Mechanisms are Transferable: Data-Efficient Low-Resource Adaptation via Circuit-Targeted Supervised Fine-Tuning
L79: 
L80: Khumaisa Nur’aini Affiliation: Monash University Indonesia    Ayu Purwarianti Affiliation: Institute Teknologi Bandung    Alham Fikri Aji Affiliation: MBZUAI    Derry Wijaya Affiliation: Monash University Indonesia Affiliation: Boston University
L81: ###### Abstract
L82: Adapting LLMs to low-resource languages is difficult: labeled data is scarce, full-model fine-tuning is unstable, and continued cross-lingual tuning can cause catastrophic forgetting.
L83: We propose Circuit-Targeted Supervised Fine-Tuning (CT-SFT): a counterfactual-free adaptation of CD-T (Contextual Decomposition Transformer) that uses a label-balanced mean baseline and task-directional relevance scoring to identify a sparse set of task-relevant attention heads in a proxy-language checkpoint, then transfer learns to a target language by updating only those heads (plus LayerNorm) via head-level gradient masking.
L84: Across NusaX-Senti and XNLI, CT-SFT improves cross-lingual accuracy over continued full fine-tuning while updating only a small subset of model parameters. We find an editing-preserving trade-off: harder transfers favor editing circuit heads, while easier transfers often favor near-zero (i.e., low-relevance heads) updates, preserving the source mechanism. CT-SFT also substantially reduces catastrophic forgetting, preserving proxy/source-language competence during transfer.
L85: ## 1 Introduction
L86: 
L87: Most of the world’s languages sit in the long tail: they have limited digitized text, limited labeled data, and limited tooling compared to a handful of high-resource languages. This imbalance matters because many standard NLP recipes implicitly assume abundant supervision and stable evaluation, leaving low-resource adaptation underserved in practice (cite66†Joshi et al., 2020 ; cite67†Hu et al., 2020 ; cite68†Aji et al., 2022 ).
L88: In this regime, full-model supervised fine-tuning is often fragile: model capacity vastly exceeds what small datasets can constrain, making training sensitive to sample choice and hyperparameters and prone to measurable instability in small-data settings (cite69†Somayajula and others, 2024 ; cite70†Du et al., 2023 ; cite71†Mosbach et al., 2021 ; cite72†Dodge et al., 2020 ).
L89: Cross-lingual continued tuning adds another failure mode, catastrophic forgetting of the source competence, hindering multilingual deployment where we want to add capability without destroying what was already learned (cite73†Li et al., 2024 ; cite74†Vu et al., 2022 ; cite75†Pfeiffer et al., 2020 ).
L90: A natural response is to restrict learning capacity via parameter-efficient or sparse updates (cite76†Ding and others, 2023 ). The lottery-ticket view suggests large networks contain many trainable subnetworks (cite77†Frankle and Carbin, 2019 ; cite78†Mehta and others, 2019 ; cite79†Chen et al., 2020 ), and composable sparse tuning can reduce interference in cross-lingual transfer (cite80†Ansell and others, 2022 ).
L91: However, sparsity alone does not answer the central question: which parameters should move, and which should be preserved. This motivates mechanism-aware selection. Mechanistic interpretability tools can localize computation in transformers (cite81†Rai et al., 2024 ), often identifying small sets of causally important heads in realistic behaviors (cite82†Wang et al., 2023 ), while also highlighting open challenges in extending methods beyond controlled settings (cite83†Sharkey et al., 2025 ).
L92: We employ mechanistic interpretability for diagnosis and as a training method for low-resource adaptation. We introduce Circuit-Targeted Supervised Fine-Tuning (CT-SFT), a two-stage process where (i) a task-competent checkpoint is learned in a higher-resource proxy/source language and (ii) adaptation to a low-resource target occurs by updating a small, relevant circuit found in that checkpoint.
L93: Our discovery procedure enhances Contextual Decomposition for Transformers (CD-T) (cite84†Hsu et al., 2025 ) by using methods compatible with unstructured text.
L94: We evaluate CT-SFT on NusaX-Senti (cite85†Winata et al., 2023 ) and XNLI (cite86†Conneau et al., 2018 ). Using competence-tuning as a proxy for transfer difficulty, we find an editing–preserving trade-off: larger source–target mismatches benefit from editing decision-aligned heads, while smaller mismatches favor preserving the source mechanism. Overall, CT-SFT improves data efficiency and reduces destructive drift, complementing evidence of component reuse (cite87†Merullo et al., 2024 ).
L95: Our contributions in this work are as follows: (i) We propose the adaptation of CD-T (cite84†Hsu et al., 2025 ) to unstructured low-resource text using a label-balanced statistical baseline and a task-directional relevance score, avoiding handcrafted counterfactuals.
L96: 
L97: (ii) Our proposal: Circuit-Targeted Supervised Fine-Tuning (CT-SFT) is a mechanism-guided fine-tuning method that restricts updates to a discovered set of task-relevant attention heads.
L98: (iii) We identify an editing–preserving trade-off in cross-lingual transfer that tracks source–target mismatch measured by competence tuning.
L99: 
L100: (iv) We show that CT-SFT substantially reduces catastrophic forgetting compared to continued full fine-tuning.
L101: ## 2 Related Work
L102: Mechanistic interpretability (MI) has led to an expanding set of tools for localizing computation in transformers (cite81†Rai et al., 2024 ; cite83†Sharkey et al., 2025 ). Techniques like path patching and causal interventions have been used to identify the components responsible for specific behaviors in real-world tasks, such as the IOI task cite82†Wang et al. (2023) . Research has shown that only a small subset of heads can be causally important in these contexts cite82†Wang et al. (2023) .
L103: Automated methods such as ACDC recover sparse causal graphs via activation patching cite88†Conmy et al. (2023) , while attribution-based approaches, EAP, and variants like integrated gradients and GradPath, scale circuit identification through edge-level scoring (cite89†Nanda, 2023 ; cite90†Syed et al., 2024 ; cite91†Hanna et al., 2024 ; cite92†Zhang et al., 2025b ).
L104: Contextual Decomposition for Transformers (CD-T) complements these with a mathematically grounded decomposition into “irrelevant” and “relevant” streams that can efficiently recover circuits cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) .
L105: Many methods are validated on template-based tasks (e.g., IOI, greater-than, docstring) where clear counterfactuals and controlled reference states are available. However, circuit discovery is more challenging with unstructured, diverse data lacking faithful counterfactuals. Recent reviews highlight that evaluations on real-world tasks are still uncommon, raising questions about the transferability of current interpretability methods beyond controlled settings cite83†Sharkey et al. (2025) .
L106: Most research on mechanistic interpretability has primarily focused on English cite93†Yu et al. (2025) . However, recent studies have started to explore multilingual behaviors and the internal structures that exist across different languages cite94†Resck et al. (2025) ; cite95†Zhang et al. (2025c) .
L107: Investigations into cross-lingual circuit similarity suggest that models maintain structurally similar pathways across languages, while also demonstrating language-specific specializations when necessary cite95†Zhang et al. (2025c) .
L108: Several lines of research connect mechanistic structure to adaptation. Circuits and components can be reused across tasks cite87†Merullo et al. (2024) , and circuit analyses of fine-tuning suggest that substantial changes are concentrated in a small subset of heads, motivating mechanism-aware adaptation strategies such as circuit-informed LoRA cite96†Wang et al. (2025) .
L109: Relatedly, Li et al. cast fine-tuning as subgraph search and propose circuit-tuning, which iteratively prunes an EAP-based circuit and updates only the associated parameters cite97†Li et al. (2025) . Mechanism-guided tuning has also been explored for translation, where selectively tuning identified components can outperform full or random tuning, though these approaches typically rely on large-scale datasets and carefully designed interventions cite98†Zhang et al. (2025a) .
L110: In contrast, we modify CD-T for unstructured, low-resource datasets by using counterfactual-free baseline construction and a task-relevance score to aid component selection. Additionally, while multilingual MI primarily serves a diagnostic purpose, we leverage the discovered mechanisms to guide low-resource adaptation by reusing these circuits to fine-tune models for lower-resource languages.
L111: cite99†Image: Refer to caption Figure 1: CT-SFT overview. Our method has two phases. Mechanism Discovery (left): starting from a base model, we perform competence tuning on a higher-resource proxy language to obtain a task-competent checkpoint $\theta_{1}$. From a discovery pool, we form (i) a correctly-predicted discovery set $\mathcal{X}$ under $\theta_{1}$ and (ii) a label-balanced statistical baseline $\mu$ for CD-T.
L112: We then run a modified CD-T procedure to score components and select a task-relevant circuit $\mathcal{C}$ (a subset of attention heads). We optionally compute circuit-faithfulness diagnostics on a validation set. Mechanism-Guided Tuning (middle): for a low-resource target language, we fine-tune only the parameters corresponding to heads in $\mathcal{C}$ (and LayerNorm), using a held-out tuning pool, producing the CT-SFT checkpoint $\theta_{\text{ct-sft}}$.
L113: Evaluation (right): we evaluate $\theta_{\text{ct-sft}}$ on the test set and report task metrics.
L114: ## 3 Contextual Decomposition Transformer (CD-T)
L115: Contextual Decomposition Transformer (CD-T) cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) links model behavior to specific internal components by decomposing the activation of $h$ of every component into two additive terms: an irrelevant component ($\gamma$) representing the baseline processing, and a relevant component ($\beta$) representing the deviation induced by the input. Let $a_{x}(s)$ denote the activation produced by the attention head $s$ on input $x$.
L116: Given the pre-computed mean $\mu$ for head $s$, i.e., $\mu(s)$, CD-T defines:
L117:  | $\displaystyle\gamma_{s}=\mu(s);\qquad\beta_{s}=a_{x}(s)-\mu(s).$  |  | (1)
L118: where the precomputed mean $\mu$ is constructed from faithful counterfactual examples, such as swapping names or years, when applied to templated tasks like IOI, greater-than, and docstring cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) . These streams are then propagated through the network using contextual decomposition rules to produce contribution $\beta_{s\to t}(x)$ from head $s$ to a downstream target $t$, including contributions at the logit readout or at the intermediated residual-stream sites.
L119: CD-T rank components using an unsigned magnitude ratio between relevant and irrelevant streams cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) :
L120: 
L121:  | $$R(s,t)=\frac{\lVert\beta_{s\to t}\rVert_{1}}{\lVert\gamma_{s\to t}\rVert_{1}}.$$  |  | (2)
L122: 
L123: This relevance score $R$ favors heads whose “relevant” signal ($\beta$) is large relative to the background mean ($\gamma$).
L124: ##### Circuit Selection.
L125: Let $\mathcal{T}^{(d)}$ denote the frontier targets at depth $d$. At $d{=}0$, $\mathcal{T}^{(0)}$ is the logit readout, and we select the top $K_{0}$ heads based on relevance score (averaged over discovery inputs) to form $\mathcal{C}^{(0)}$. For $d{\geq}1$, we set $\mathcal{T}^{(d)}=\mathcal{C}^{(d-1)}$ and select the top $K_{d}$ heads by mean directional relevance to $\mathcal{T}^{(d)}$ (averaged over targets and inputs), forming $\mathcal{C}^{(d)}$.
L126: We stop the iteration at layer 0 and output the selected heads $\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{d}\mathcal{C}^{(d)}$.
L127: ## 4 Adapting CD-T for Unstructured Text in Low-Resource Languages
L128: Our approach consists of two distinct phases (Figure cite100†1 ): (1) Mechanism Discovery, where we identify a task-relevant circuit in a higher-resource proxy language, and (2) Mechanism-Guided Tuning, where we adapt the model to low-resource languages by training only the parameters corresponding to the discovery circuit. In our setting, a component is an attention head $(l,h)$ at layer $l$ and head index $h$.
L129: The circuit is a subset of heads selected using a relevance score aggregated across the discovery set.
L130: ### 4.1 Phase 1: Mechanism Discovery
L131: 
L132: We utilize Contextual Decomposition for Transformers (CD-T) cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) to identify the task-relevant circuit.
L133: #### 4.1.1 Precomputed-mean Construction: Establishing a "Neutral State" (Label-Balanced Mean)
L134: A critical dependency in this formulation is the choice of $\mu$: the precomputed-mean determines what is treated as “background” ($\gamma$) versus “signal” ($\beta$). In templated tasks (e.g., IOI, greater-than, docstring), $\mu$ can be defined using faithful counterfactuals (e.g., swapping names or years) cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) . However, for unstructured real-world text, generating such counterfactuals is impractical without expensive human annotation.
L135: Accordingly, we approximate $\mu$ from in-distribution samples rather than explicit counterfactuals, and enforce label balance in the mean-estimation set $\mathcal{M}$ (i.e., using equal number of examples per class) to reduce leakage of class-directional signal into the baseline. Specifically, for each head $s$, we compute:
L136: 
L137:  | $\displaystyle\mu(s)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{M}}[a_{x}(s)].$  |  | (3)
L138: Balancing ensures that class-directional information cancels in expectation, and the baseline approximates a neutral operating point.
L139: #### 4.1.2 Scoring Protocol: Directional Relevance over Magnitude
L140: 
L141: Original CD-T scores components using an unsigned magnitude ratio (Eq. cite101†2 ) cite84†Hsu et al. (2025) , but high magnitude does not necessarily mean task support: a head can be non-discriminative or even anti-aligned with the gold label. This can cause magnitude-based selection to include components that amplify spurious features or push probability mass away from the correct label.
L142: To address this limitation, we propose a directional relevance scoring protocol that explicitly measures whether a head’s contribution supports the correct prediction direction (i.e., increases the logit of the gold label relative to alternatives), rather than merely having large activation magnitude. We use this directional score for circuit selection throughout our experiments.
L143: ##### Directional relevance at the logit readout (classification margin).
L144: 
L145: Let $y_{c}$ be the correct label and $\mathcal{Y}_{\text{other}}=\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y_{c}\}$. When the target $t$ is a logit readout, we score head $s$ using the relevant contribution to the correct logit relative to the average contribution to incorrect logits:
L146: 
L147:  | $$R(s,t)=\beta_{s\to t}(y_{c})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}_{\text{other}}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{other}}}\beta_{s\to t}(y).$$  |  | (4)
L148: This measures discriminative contribution at the decision boundary: positive scores indicate that the head increases the correct class logit more than the competing classes.
L149: ##### Directional relevance beyond the logit layer (task-direction projection).
L150: Intermediate layers do not write directly to logits; instead, they update the residual stream, and the final logits are obtained by a linear readout via the unembedding matrix $W_{U}$ (cite102†Belrose et al., 2023 ; cite103†Nanda, 2024 ). This makes $W_{U}$ a natural label-specific reference direction for judging whether an intermediate residual update increases the gold-label logit relative to competing labels.
L151: To evaluate whether an intermediate contribution supports the correct class, we define a task direction using the unembedding matrix $W_{U}$:
L152:  | $$\mathbf{v}_{\text{task}}=W_{U}(y_{c})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}_{\text{other}}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{other}}}W_{U}(y).$$  |  | (5)
