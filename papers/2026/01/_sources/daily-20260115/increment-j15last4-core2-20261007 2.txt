L161: ### 2.3 Guiding Optimisation Toward Transfer via Direct Ascent Synthesis
L162: 
L163: Unconstrained optimisation in high-dimensional pixel space can discover adversarial patterns that exploit quirks of the specific model rather than recovering genuine visual content. These images transfer poorly. To encourage transfer, we thus use direct ascent synthesis (DAS), a technique from the adversarial robustness literature (cite53†Fort and Whitaker, 2025 ). See cite63†Figure 2 for an overview.
L164: cite64†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2: Our image synthesis pipeline based on direct ascent synthesis (cite53†Fort and Whitaker, 2025 ). The final image consists of a base image (neutral grey) plus a trainable perturbation. (1) Instead of training the perturbation pixels directly, we re-parametrise the perturbation via a set of trainable multi-resolution components. (2–3) All multi-resolution components are upscaled to the target size, summed, and added to the base image.
L165: (4) The resulting image is augmented with random shifts and noise.
L166: DAS, at its core, is an adversarial attack method: it starts with a base image (in our case, neutral grey) and generates a perturbation to be added to it, optimising the resulting image to minimise some loss function (in our case, the dissimilarity between the image’s representation and the target concept direction). During optimisation, it applies random shift and noise augmentations to guide the process toward more natural-looking images.
L167: However, the key difference is that instead of directly optimising the pixels of the perturbation, DAS re-parametrises it as a sum of components at multiple resolutions, which are then optimised simultaneously.
L168: #### Step 1: Obtain the base image.
L169: 
L170: We chose a target resolution for our synthesised image to be
L171: 
L172:  | $$R\times R=448\times 448\,,$$  |
L173: 
L174: The base image $I_{\text{base}}\in[0,1]^{R\times R\times 3}$ is a neutral grey image with all pixel values set to $0.5$.
L175: #### Step 2: Compute the perturbation as a sum of multi-resolution components.
L176: 
L177: We do not optimise the perturbation pixels directly. Instead, we maintain a set of separate trainable components at a range of resolutions,
L178: 
L179:  | $$\{\text{layer}_{r\times r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r\times 3}\}\text{ for }r\in\{R,R-20,R-40,\ldots\},$$  |
L180: 
L181: with the smallest resolution being $8\times 8$. We construct the final perturbation by upscaling each component to the target resolution and summing them:
L182:  | $$\text{perturbation}=\sum_{r}\text{upscale}_{R\times R}\left(\text{layer}_{r\times r}\right).$$  |
L183: 
L184: We scale this sum with $\tanh$ and add it to the base image:
L185: 
L186:  | $$I_{\text{final}}=I_{\text{base}}+\frac{1}{2}\tanh\left(\text{perturbation}\right).$$  |
L187: 
L188: Due to the $\tanh$ scaling and $I_{\text{base}}$ being a neutral grey image, $I_{\text{final}}$ is guaranteed to be in $[0,1]$ and thus a valid image.
L189: This multi-resolution reparameterisation encourages the optimisation to capture both coarse semantic structure (in low-resolution components) and fine details (in high-resolution components) simultaneously.
L190: #### Step 3: Augment the image.
L191: 
L192: Before passing the image to the VLM, we apply random augmentations.
L193: 
L194: We shift the image horizontally and vertically by an amount sampled uniformly from $\{-\Sigma_{\text{DAS}},\ldots,\Sigma_{\text{DAS}}\}$ pixels. We first upscale the image $R+\Sigma_{\text{DAS}}$ pixels in each direction, apply the shift, then centre-crop back to the target resolution $R\times R$. We then add Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{DAS}}$ to each pixel.
L195: Following the original DAS paper, we use $\Sigma_{\text{DAS}}=56$ and $\sigma_{\text{DAS}}=0.1$, and when optimising in batches, we apply the augmentations independently to each batch element.
L196: #### Transfer to other models and recognisability by humans.
L197: Our approach to regularisation involves trade-offs. For our current application — testing whether alignment exists — getting transfer is sufficient: if an independent model recognises the synthesised image, we have evidence of alignment. However, potential future applications of this method as an interpretability tool — a visual lens into a model’s representation space — would require not just transfer but human recognisability, which is a stronger requirement.
L198: Our current method already produces human-recognisable images for many concepts (cite17†Section 3 ), suggesting it is partway toward this goal, but does not do so reliably. On the other hand, the regularisation techniques we use may already sometimes be too restrictive, causing us to miss genuine alignment that does not manifest in the forms that DAS-regularised optimisation favours. Adjusting the strength and type of the transfer-inducing techniques is thus a natural direction for future work.

L181: ### 5.2 Stage I: SFT
L182: The goal of the supervised stage is to teach the model the structure of multimodal aggregation: how to articulate intermediate reasoning, verbalize evidence via visual keys, and format the final answer. Given $(\mathbf{x},q,\{\tau_{i}\})$, where $\{\tau_{i}\}$ are reference trajectories in the structured aggregation format, the model is trained to imitate a structured aggregation output $(r,K,a)$.
L183: This stage does not primarily optimize correctness; rather, it provides: (1) a stable and structured textual interface for subsequent RL training, with a well-defined and parseable output format, and (2) a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used for KL regularization in GSPO. Without this SFT initialization, RL training often collapses into malformed or degenerate trajectories.
L184: ### 5.3 Stage II: RL via GSPO
L185: 
L186: While SFT establishes the structural format of multimodal aggregation, it relies primarily on imitation and does not explicitly teach the model to distinguish correct evidence from hallucinated or noisy candidate trajectories. To explicitly optimize for effective aggregation, we employ reinforcement learning to train the model as an aggregator.
L187: #### Aggregation Policy.
L188: 
L189: During RL, the model is treated as a policy
L190: 
L191:  | $$\pi_{\theta}(a,K,r\mid\mathbf{x},S),$$  |
L192: where $\pi_{\theta}$ is initialized from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ learned during SFT and $S=\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}$ denotes a set of candidate trajectories drawn from a mixture of offline cached trajectories and on-policy rollouts, following a curriculum-based teacher–on-policy trajectory mixing strategy. Each rollout consists of a reasoning trace $r$, a set of predicted visual keys $K$, and the final aggregated answer $a$.
L193: #### Curriculum-Based Teacher-On-Policy Trajectory Mixing.
L194: The candidate pool $S$ evolves during RL training and is constructed as a mixture of teacher-generated trajectories and on-policy trajectories sampled from the current model $\pi_{\theta}$.
L195: We employ a staged curriculum that gradually increases the proportion of on-policy candidates: early stages rely primarily on teacher trajectories to provide clear and reliable evidence, while later stages increasingly incorporate noisier and more diverse self-generated trajectories that the policy must ultimately aggregate at inference time. This dynamic mixture mitigates distribution shift and improves the robustness of the learned aggregation policy.
L196: The specific curriculum schedule and mixing ratios are detailed in Section cite29†6 .
L197: #### Reward Design.
L198: 
L199: Each rollout is evaluated by a composite reward that accounts for answer correctness, evidence selection quality, and adaptive reasoning efficiency:
L200: 
L201:  | $$R(a,K,r\mid\mathbf{x})=w_{\text{acc}}\,R_{\text{acc}}+w_{\text{key}}\,R_{\text{key}}+R_{\text{len}}.$$  |  | (7)
L202: 
L203: (1) Answer correctness ($R_{\text{acc}}$) We measure answer accuracy using an exact-match criterion against the ground-truth answer $y^{\ast}$:
L204: 
L205:  | $$R_{\text{acc}}=\mathbf{1}[a=y^{\ast}].$$  |  | (8)
L206: This binary signal provides stable, task-agnostic supervision during RL optimization.
L207: 
L208: (2) Evidence selection quality ($R_{\text{key}}$) To explicitly encourage faithful visual grounding, we reward the selection of relevant visual evidence via a balanced precision-recall formulation. Let $G$ denote the ground-truth visual-key set derived from dataset annotations, and let $K$ be the model-predicted key set. We define:
L209:  | $$R_{\text{key}}=(1-\alpha)\frac{|K\cap G|}{|G|+\epsilon}+\alpha\frac{|K\cap G|}{|K|+\epsilon}.$$  |  | (9)
L210:

L212: (3) Difficulty-Aware Length Penalty ($R_{\text{len}}$) . L213: 
L214:  | $$\hat{p}_{\text{solve}}(q)=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{1}[a_{j}=y^{\ast}],$$  |  | (10)
L215: which is smoothed across training steps using an exponential moving average (EMA):
L216: 
L217:  | $$\tilde{p}_{t}\leftarrow\gamma\tilde{p}_{t-1}+(1-\gamma)\hat{p}_{\text{solve}}.$$  |  | (11)
L218: 
L219: Let $N_{\text{tok}}$ denote the number of tokens within the <think> region of the rollout. The length penalty is defined as:
L220: 
L221:  | $$R_{\text{len}}=-\beta\,N_{\text{tok}}\cdot\max(\tilde{p}_{t},\,1/J).$$  |  | (12)
L222: Intuitively, easy prompts that are consistently solved (high $\tilde{p}_{t}$) incur stronger penalties for extended reasoning, encouraging concise aggregation, while difficult prompts retain flexibility for longer computation. The lower bound $1/J$ prevents the penalty from vanishing entirely when no rollout succeeds, ensuring stable optimization. The scalar $\beta$ controls the relative strength of this term.
L223: Overall, this composite reward encourages the policy to produce correct answers grounded in minimal yet sufficient visual evidence, while allocating reasoning effort adaptively based on task difficulty.

L143: 
L144: The previous sections introduce the one-step drag editing using our method. As the long-distance drag operation often requires more than one step to avoid corruption, we propose a multi-step editing scheduler to solve such problems. Specifically, we split the drag operation into $T$ intervals and set the progressive target points $\{p_{t}^{\prime}(u)|u=1,2,...,T\}$. L145:  | $$p_{t}^{\prime}(u)=p_{h}+\frac{u}{T}(p_{t}-p_{h}),$$  |  | (7)
L146: 
L147: However, the actual handle point position usually changes when training 3D Gaussians. In addition, we further conduct history-aware diffusion fine-tuning to improve the ability for more aggressive editing.
L148: Handle Point Relocation. The handle point relocation is performed after each interval’s training process. To keep track of the handle points, we use the Gaussians associated with each handle point. Specifically for handle point $p_{h}^{i}$, we update it with the averaged position change of Gaussians $P_{h}^{i}$. As shown in Figure cite96†4 , the dragged part can be successfully relocated.

L150: History-Aware Diffusion Fine-Tuning. For long-distance drag operations, the edited 2D images can shift out of the diffusion model’s domain since it is fine-tuned on the original images, resulting in degeneration back to the original images. We build an image buffer to fine-tune the diffusion model. The diffusion model will be fine-tuned with the image buffer every interval. Initially, the buffer only contains original images, and the newly edited result will be added to the buffer during intervals.

Specifically, we choose the views with a larger visible area on the handle points’ Gaussians, which is determined by the local editing mask on each view. L157: We use batch size 4 and train for 200 iterations. After each dragging step, we continue fine-tuning the diffusion model for 50 iterations with the updated image buffer in each interval. Each time, the newly edited image will be enqueued. The loss weight of $\lambda_{1}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{ssim}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{lpips}}$ are set to $8,2,1$ respectively. Note that the $\lambda_{1}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{ssim}}$ are 10 times bigger than normal to ensure the background.
L158: Datasets. our experiments include edits on eight scenes, using the published datasets from Instruct-NeRF2NeRF cite48†Haque et al. (2023) , PDS cite76†Koo et al. (2024) , Mip-NeRF360 cite98†Barron et al. (2022) , and Tank and Temple cite99†Knapitsch et al. (2017) .
