L349@P3: Consistency

L370@P3: 5. Methodology and Experimental Setup
L371@P3: This section describes the overall experimental design
L372@P3: used to study the relationship between semantic
L373@P3: segmentation degradation and vision–language
L374@P3: misalignment. We first outline the datasets and perception
L375@P3: models used for semantic segmentation, followed by the
L376@P3-4: corruption protocol for controlled perception degradation. 5
L377@P4: We then describe the Vision–Language Models (VLMs),
L378@P4: prompting strategy, and evaluation protocol used to
L379@P4: quantify semantic misalignment.
L380@P4: 5.1. Datasets
L381@P4: For semantic segmentation, Cityscapes [11] provides
L382@P4: high-resolution urban street images with fine-grained
L383@P4: pixel-level annotations across 19 semantic classes. This
L384@P4: dataset serves as canonical benchmark for evaluating
L385@P4: semantic segmentation performance under controlled
L386@P4: visual degradation.
L387@P4: 5.2. Perception Degradation Protocol
L388@P4: To evaluate the robustness of semantic segmentation
L389@P4: models under realistic perceptual degradation, we
L390@P4: introduce three classes of image corruptions—motion blur,
L391@P4: low-light degradation, and partial occlusion—each applied
L392@P4: at three increasing severity levels, shown in Table 1.
L393@P4: Motion blur is simulated using a linear convolutional
L394@P4: kernel whose size increases with severity, modeling
L395@P4: camera shake or rapid ego-motion. Low-light conditions
L396@P4: are simulated via gamma correction combined with
L397@P4: additive Gaussian noise, capturing illumination loss and
L398@P4: sensor noise typical of night-time urban driving. Occlusion
L399@P4: is implemented by masking a randomly positioned
L400@P4: rectangular region whose area increases with severity,
L401@P4: reflecting partial blockage by dynamic objects such as
L402@P4: vehicles or pedestrians. These corruptions are applied
L403@P4: independently to each test image, resulting in nine
L404@P4: degraded variants per image in addition to the original
L405@P4: clean input. The corruption parameters are chosen to reflect
L406@P4: realistic operational conditions encountered in autonomous
L407@P4: driving scenarios while maintaining consistent and
L408@P4: reproducible degradation levels across the dataset.
L409@P4: 5.3. Semantic Segmentation Evaluation
L410@P4: Semantic segmentation performance is evaluated using
L411@P4: standard metrics, including mIoU and per-class IoU. Let
L412@P4: 𝑄clean and 𝑄deg denote segmentation quality on clean and
L413@P4: degraded images, respectively. We define the
L414@P4: segmentation performance drop as: Δ𝑄 = 𝑄clean − 𝑄deg .
L415@P4: This quantity captures the magnitude of perception
L416@P4: degradation under controlled corruption.
L417@P4: 5.4. Vision-Language Models Evaluation
L418@P4: We evaluate multiple Vision–Language Models that
L419@P4: represent different classes of multimodal architectures,
L420@P4: including both contrastive vision–language representation
L421@P4: model and instruction-tuned vision–language model. All
L422@P4: VLMs are used in a zero-shot inference setting, without
L423@P4: fine-tuning or prompt adaptation. Importantly, all VLMs
L424@P4: receive only the raw visual inputs (clean or degraded
L425@P4: images). Each model is evaluated on clean images and
L426@P4: three corruption types (low-light, motion blur, occlusion)
L427@P4: with increasing severity. All generative models are
L428@P4: evaluated under a fixed visual token budget to ensure
L429@P4: computational stability and fair comparison. Semantic
L430@P4: segmentation outputs are used exclusively for analysis and
L431@P4: metric computation and are not provided to VLMs.
L432@P4: For each image, we query the VLM using prompts
L433@P4: aligned with our proposed metrics:
L434@P4: • Object grounding: object presence or top-K
L435@P4: object ranking
L436@P4: • Safety reasoning: “Is it safe to proceed
L437@P4: forward?”
L438@P4: • Hallucination probing: object predictions not
L439@P4: supported by ground truth
L440@P4: Contrastive models (CLIP, SigLIP) use Top-K object
L441@P4: selection, while Qwen2-VL generates free-form text
L442@P4: parsed into structured outputs.

L928@P8: 7. Conclusion
L929@P8:  This paper presents a systematic study of semantic
L930@P8: misalignment in Vision–Language Models under realistic
L931@P8: degradation of upstream visual perception. Using semantic
L932@P8: segmentation on the Cityscapes dataset as a representative
L933@P8: perception module, we demonstrate that modest
L934@P8: degradation in conventional segmentation metrics can
L935@P8: trigger severe failures in downstream multimodal
L936@P8: reasoning. These failures manifest as hallucinated object
L937@P8: mentions, omission of safety-critical entities, and incorrect
L938@P8: or uncertain safety judgments, even when segmentation
L939@P8: outputs remain visually plausible.
L940@P8:  To capture these phenomena, we introduce languagelevel misalignment metrics that explicitly quantify
L941@P8: hallucination, critical omission, and safety
L942@P8: misinterpretation. Through extensive experiments across
L943@P8: contrastive and generative VLMs, we show that
L944@P8: conventional perception metrics such as mIoU exhibit
L945@P8: weak and inconsistent correlation with multimodal
L946@P8: semantic reliability. In particular, safety reasoning failures
L947@P8: persist even under relatively high segmentation quality,
L948@P8: revealing a fundamental disconnect between pixel-level
L949@P8: robustness and semantic reliability.
L950@P8:  Our findings highlight a critical limitation of current
L951@P8: VLM-based systems for autonomous and embodied
L952@P8: applications. Evaluating perception and language
L953@P8: components in isolation is insufficient to guarantee reliable
L954@P8: multimodal behavior under real-world conditions. Future
L955@P8: work should explore tighter integration of perception
L956@P8: uncertainty into vision–language reasoning, robustnessaware training objectives, and evaluation frameworks that
L957@P8: directly reflect safety-relevant semantic failures. We hope
L958@P8: this work motivates a shift toward holistic, uncertaintyaware evaluation of multimodal systems in safety-critical
L959@P8: domains.
L960@P8: 8. Future Work
L961@P8:  While this work focuses on single-frame semantic
L962@P8: segmentation to isolate the relationship between perception
L963@P8: degradation and vision–language misalignment, an
L964@P8: important direction for future research is extending the
L965@P8: proposed evaluation framework to sequential semantic
L966@P8: segmentation and video-based perception. Temporal
L967@P8: aggregation and consistency mechanisms may stabilize
L968@P8: pixel-level predictions under dynamic conditions, but it
L969@P8: remains unclear whether such temporal robustness
L970@P8: translates to improved semantic reliability in downstream
L971@P8: vision–language reasoning. Applying the proposed
L972@P8: misalignment metrics to temporally coherent perception
L973@P8: pipelines would enable systematic study of error
L974@P8: accumulation, temporal hallucination, and safety drift over
L975@P8-9: time. We leave this investigation to future work.10
