L96: Entropy serves as a fundamental uncertainty metric widely used for static reasoning evaluation, including answer selection cite74†Ren et al. (2023) ; cite75†Raj et al. (2025) , confidence calibration cite76†Chen and Mueller (2024) , and error detection cite77†Farquhar et al. (2024) ; cite78†Liu et al. (2025a) . As reasoning scales to multi-step paradigms where responses are decomposed into atomic steps cite79†Guo et al. (2025) , the role of entropy extends to training.
L97: Particularly in reinforcement learning, entropy not only reflects sampling diversity to facilitate exploration cite80†Wang et al. (2025c) ; cite81†Zheng et al. (2025) , but also acts as a fine-grained signal for step-level credit assignment in long-horizon reasoning cite82†Wang et al. (2025b) ; cite83†Wang et al. (2025a) , and further extends to incentivize exploration across multi-turn interactions cite84†Dong et al. (2025) . This demonstrates the potential of entropy as a self-trigger signal for web agents.
L98: ## 3 Preliminaries
L99: 
L100: Agent Framework. Following cite85†Wu et al. (2025b) , we construct web agents based on the ReAct framework cite86†Yao et al. (2023) , modeling problem-solving as an interleaved sequence of reasoning and acting. A trajectory $\tau$ is defined as:
L101: 
L102:  | $$\tau=(q,R_{1},O_{1},\dots,R_{t},O_{t},\dots,R_{T}),$$  |  | (1)
L103: 
L104: where $q$ is the query, $R_{t}$ is the agent’s response at step $t$, and $O_{t}$ is the environment observation. We distinguish two types of steps:
L105: 
L106:   * •
L107: Process Step ($S^{p}_{t}=(R_{t},O_{t})$, $t<T$): The response $R_{t}=\langle z_{t},a_{t}\rangle$ contains reasoning thoughts $z_{t}$ (enclosed in <think> tags) and an action $a_{t}$ (in <tool_call> tags), followed by the tool’s response $O_{t}$.
L108: 
L109:   * •
L110: 
L111: Answer Step ($S^{a}_{T}=R_{T}$): The terminal step produces $R_{T}=\langle z_{T},\texttt{y}\rangle$, where the final answer y is wrapped in <answer> tags.
L112: Step Entropy. To quantify the agent’s confidence in each step of interaction, we compute the step entropy as the average token entropy across each response $R_{t}$. Specifically, the entropy of the $i$-th token $x_{i}$ with preceding context $h_{i}$ is defined as $H(x_{i})=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P(v\mid h_{i})\log{P(v\mid h_{i})}$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the vocabulary and $P(\cdot\mid h_{i})$ is the model’s predicted distribution. Step entropy is computed:
L113:  | $$\bar{H}_{t}=\frac{1}{|R_{t}|}\sum_{x\in R_{t}}H(x),$$  |  | (2)
L114: 
L115: where $|R_{t}|$ denotes the number of tokens in $R_{t}$.
L116: 
L117: Experience Intervention. We formally define experience intervention as a class of methods comprising two phases:
L118: 
L119:   * •
L120: Construction Phase: Given a training corpus $\mathcal{D}_{train}=\{(q_{i},\tau_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ with queries $q_{i}$, trajectories $\tau_{i}$, and ground truth $y_{i}$, experience acquisition is formalized as $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D}_{train})$, where $\mathcal{F}$ is a function that extracts an experience base $\mathcal{E}$ from $\mathcal{D}_{train}$^{1}^{1} 1 Self-evolution methods have the same $\mathcal{D}_{train}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{test}$, but typically do not provide ground truth $y$..
L121:   * •
L122: 
L123: Utilization Phase: At step $t$ with context $h_{t}\!=\!(q,\ldots,R_{t},O_{t})$, the agent obtains applicable experience $e_{t}\!=\!\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E},h_{t})$ through mapping function $\mathcal{G}$, which serves as additional input for reasoning.
L124: Traditional methods represent a special case where experience is concatenated at the beginning of reasoning, with $e=\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E},q)$. The function $\mathcal{G}$ typically returns the entire base or retrieves relevant cases, providing static experience. In contrast, our method invokes $\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E},h_{t})$ at any step $t$ to allow the agent to seek appropriate guidance.
L125: ## 4 Methodology
L126: 
L127: This section formally introduces ExpSeek, elaborating on three key components: experience base construction (§ cite12†4.1 ), self-triggering mechanism (§ cite13†4.2 ), and step-level guidance (§ cite16†4.3 ).
L128: 
L129: cite87†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2: The overall architecture of ExpSeek, including experience base construction and actively seeking experience guidance during inference. The step entropy threshold calculation process is not depicted here.
L130: ### 4.1 Experience Base Construction
L131: 
L132: The core of guiding experience lies in recreating the problem behavior and simulating guidance.
L133: 
L134: Guiding Experience Schema. We design experience triplets containing: (1) Behavior: objectively describes the state and action at the current step; (2) Mistake: identifies errors by contrasting with correct trajectories; (3) Guidance: provides directional guidance based on error analysis, without directly offering answers or specific clues.
L135: Construction Process. As shown in Figure cite88†2 (A), construction involves three steps. First, for each query in $\mathcal{D}_{train}$, we sample $k$ trajectories with the agent model and pair successful and failed ones. Second, for each trajectory pair $(\tau^{+},\tau^{-})$, we use a tool model to analyze the failed trajectory $\tau^{-}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{T}\}$ by contrasting with the successful one $\tau^{+}$.
L136: The tool model assigns each step $S_{t}$ a binary correctness label $y_{t}\in\{0,1\}$ and outputs triplets for incorrect steps (where $y_{t}=0$). Finally, we prompt the tool model to induce topics for triplets using an iterative batch processing approach: when processing each new batch, the model takes all previously generated triplets with their assigned topics as input, then either assigns existing topics, modifies them, or creates new topics for the current batch.
L137: This yields a guiding experience base $\mathcal{E}$ organized into topics, with separate collections $\mathcal{E}_{p}$ and $\mathcal{E}_{a}$ for process and answer steps respectively.
L138: ### 4.2 Entropy as Self-Trigger
L139: #### 4.2.1 Entropy Analysis for Step Correctness
L140: Determining when to trigger intervention is challenging. Using reward models to analyze each step incurs prohibitive costs. Inspired by prior research on entropy-based error detection and confidence calibration cite76†Chen and Mueller (2024) ; cite78†Liu et al. (2025a) , we hypothesize that the step entropy of web agents has the potential to reflect the agent’s inherent state.

L250: ### 6.2 Why Self-Trigger and Generation?
L251: 
L252: To validate entropy-based self-triggering, we compare two variants: Reward Model-based^{2}^{2} 2 We employ claude-sonnet-4-20250514 to judge intervention necessity at each step based on the full history. (RM) and rule-based (continuous intervention from step one), both with one-step post-trigger silence.
L253: Table cite100†3 shows similar accuracy but divergent efficiency. Rule-based triggers result in 1.7$\times$ step and 2.6$\times$ time overhead on GAIA (1.5$\times$ and 2.1$\times$ on xbench). RM reduces step overhead (1.3-1.5$\times$) but increases time (2.2-2.9$\times$), suggesting over-intervention. In contrast, self-trigger balances efficiency and performance by adapting intervention intensity to problem difficulty.
L254: On the more challenging xbench, its trigger rate increases 25.6% compared to GAIA while maintaining similar accuracy, confirming its ability to precisely identify intervention timing based on internal state.
L255: We also explore retrieval-based guidance, selecting the most similar experience (via text-embedding-v4^{3}^{3} 3 https://www.alibabacloud.com/help/en/model-studio/embedding) instead of generation. Table cite100†3 shows that retrieval increases time without improving accuracy, confirming the necessity of generative guidance.
L256: ### 6.3 Does ExpSeek Scale and Transfer?
L257: We experiment with smaller 4B and 30B models^{4}^{4} 4 Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct as $\mathcal{M}_{e}$ to explore the scaling of intervention models. As shown in Figure cite101†5 , the three model sizes exhibit scaling law on both GAIA and xbench, all achieving substantial performance gains. Notably, the 4B guidance model improves the 32B agent by 5.2% and 9.7% points respectively, validating the feasibility of weak-to-strong guidance given reasonable experience.
L258: Additionally, we swap the experience pools between 8B and 32B Agents. Results in Table cite102†4 show that while experience has model dependency, the abstract guidance knowledge it contains still holds transfer value.
L259: Furthermore, Figure cite103†7 shows we reduce experiences per topic. Even with just one experience per topic, $\mathcal{M}_{e}$ still identifies key intervention points and maintains high accuracy, demonstrating that the experience model can understand and generalize to current reasoning even with only a few high-quality seed experiences, highlighting the necessity of experience topics.
L260: While removing the repository entirely degrades performance, the remaining substantial accuracy indicates that the $\mathcal{M}_{e}$’s inherent world experience alone is beneficial.
L261: ### 6.4 What Is the Efficiency Trade-off?
L262: Figure cite104†6 illustrates the relationship between accuracy and reasoning steps under varying intervention frequencies and thresholds. We expand the intervention interval from 1 to $\{0,1,2\}$ and shift the threshold by $\pm$ 0.05 three times, yielding 21 configurations. Results show that as the trigger threshold decreases, reasoning steps increase rapidly while accuracy rises then plateaus with diminishing returns.
L263: With ~2 interventions, accuracy reaches 43.01%; beyond 6 interventions, performance barely improves, indicating that while increasing intervention intensity does not degrade performance, it also fails to yield higher gains. The results also demonstrate that in practice, the web agent produces stable performance even when thresholds fluctuate at a small scale.
L264: ## 7 Conclusion
L265: 
L266: We propose ExpSeek, a framework enabling web agents to actively seek step-level guidance based on step entropy. Even with small open-source agents, ExpSeek demonstrates significant performance improvements and excellent properties on complex real-world web reasoning tasks, showing great potential for future development.

L245: ### 5.1. Evaluation on Clarification Interaction
L246: 
L247: Clarification interaction does not involve task execution; instead, it focuses on assessing the model’s ability to clarify user intents during the interaction process.
L248: #### 5.1.1. Test Datasets and Settings
L249: 
L250: To ensure a comprehensive evaluation, we select test instances from three intent understanding datasets. Each dataset contains annotations for user instructions, missing intent details, clarification processes, and final outputs.
L251: 
L252:   * •
L253:

L255: 
L256:   * •
L257: IN3^{6}^{6} 6  cite98†https://github.com/OpenBMB/Tell_Me_More†github.com (cite64†Qian et al., 2024b ) comprises 108 test instances, this dataset includes a greater number of clarification questions.
L258: 
L259:   * •
L260:

L262: Following the definition of complex intent (ref. Section cite11†3 ), we manually categorize test instances into simple and complex intent scenarios with the assistance of an LLM. Comprehensive test data statistics are provided in Appendix cite43†C.2 . For each instruction, users interact with the model in an open-ended setting, during which the model actively seeks clarification regarding the user’s intent.
L263: To ensure diversity in responses, we recruit 20 graduate students to participate in the interactions and provide responses. Participants first list their intents outlines and then express them under the model’s guidance. The entire clarification process is recorded and evaluated using specific metrics.
L264: #### 5.1.2. Baselines and Competitors
L265: We compare Prism against three previous SOTA interactive intent understanding methods: Mistral-Interact^{cite101†6 } (cite73†Qian et al., 2024a ), ITIU^{cite102†5 } (cite66†Liao et al., 2024 ), and CollabLLM ^{8}^{8} 8  cite103†https://github.com/Wuyxin/collabllm†github.com (cite58†Wu et al., 2025 ). To ensure a fair comparison and control for differences in backbone models, we standardize all methods by adopting the same baseline models (i.e., Mistral-7b-Instruct-v0.3 and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct).
L266: #### 5.1.3. Implementation Details
L267: 
L268: Prism performs self-evolved intent tuning based on baseline models. We train two model variants: Prism-SFT and Prism-DPO, each fine-tuned on a pre-generated multi-turn clarification dataset guided by the intent-aware reward (ref. Section cite18†4.4 ). The entire training process is conducted on eight 80GB A100 GPUs and completed in 14.5 hours, with the final checkpoint used for subsequent evaluation.
L269: #### 5.1.4. Metrics
L270: We utilize the metrics developed by cite64†Qian et al. (2024b) to transform subjective user intents from user-model clarification interactions into objective numerical values, simplifying data analysis and comparison. These include Intents Cover Rate, Average Interaction Turns, Average Questions Per Turn, Options Presenting Rate, Options Reasonable Rate, and Average Options Per Question. Additionally, we introduce a Logical Conflict Rate metric to measure logical conflicts between clarification questions.
