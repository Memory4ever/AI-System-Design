S4.SS2  4.2 Archive The archive serves as an evolutionary starting point in AgenticRed . Previous works adapt an archive that mostly consider agentic systems designed for commonly-used reasoning tasks such as LLM-Debate ( Du et al., 2023 ) and Step-back Abstration ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) . These methods cannot be directly transfered to the red-teaming task, as they lack interfaces to obtain critical feedback including target model responses and judge evaluations. We instead crafted an archive that consists of Self-Refine (Reflexion) ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) and JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning. We constructed this archive as our starting point because the baseline systems in the provided archive; (1) effectively use the response and feedback signal from the target model and judge function; (2) cover a wide range of modular components and helper functions; (3) their different workflow and corresponding fitness shows how different system design can lead to big differences in fitness. JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning. We created JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning (JS-Guided AdvReasoning) for our initial archive. We took inspirations from the SOTA red-teaming method, Adversarial Reasoning ( Sabbaghi et al., 2025 ) . We apply the “Proposer and Verifier” principle ( Snell et al., 2024 ) by iteratively ranking and refining the reasoning string provided to the attacker model. Previous work requires the logits of a white-box target model, while in JS-Guided AdvReasoning, we instead develop a black-box compatible implementation. Its details can be found in Appendix C.2 . 
S4.SS3  4.3 Evolutionary Pressure We apply evolutionary pressure to the search algorithm by selecting the ’fittest’ design from multiple candidate agentic systems in each generation. To reduce computational cost, each design is evaluated on a small-scale subset d ⊂ D ~ d\subset\tilde{D} . Only the system with the highest fitness score in this generation is kept and given a chance for a full evaluation, and is added to the archive later. S4.SS4  4.4 AgenticRed Search Framework Previous works have proved that textual and numerical feedback signals are critical to attack optimization in red-teaming systems, and the suboptimal results we observed from the naïve approaches (see Appendix B.1 ) are partially due to a lack of feedback signals. We thus provide the meta agent with a structural guidance and basic helper functions to interact with target models and judge functions. Our helper functions include (1) a query function to the target model, where we set the temperature to 0 so that the model always selects the highest-probability token and therefore produces a reproducible response for a given prompt, and (2) a query function to the judge function to validate if a target model’s response adequately address the provided intention and its corresponding log probability. These functions are critical for crafting an effective red-teaming system, as they provide a verifiable dense signal, and allow the system to return once a jailbreaking attempt succeeds. Similar to existing open-endedness algorithms that encourage novelty and exploration ( Zhang et al., 2023 ; Lu et al., 2024 ) , we encourage the meta agent to design novel and original systems while maximizing the fitness score. The full prompt is provided in Appendix E.1 . 
S5.SS1  5.1 Implementation We use gpt-5-2025-08-07 as the meta agent to generate individual agentic red-teaming systems. Each red-teaming system is evaluated using locally-deployed Mistral-8x7B ( Jiang et al., 2024 ) as attacker model, Llama-2-7B ( Touvron et al., 2023 ) as target model, and HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls classifier as judge function unless otherwise specified. The intent and target phrase pairs are extracted from HarmBench Standard dataset ( Mazeika et al., 2024 ) . We include more implementation details in Appendix C .

S5.SS7  5.7 Ablation Study We perform ablations to examine whether and how our method benefits from the (1) evolutionary pressure; (2) high-quality initial archive; (3) diversity incentive. Evolutionary pressure. We are interested in how evolutionary pressure mechanism described in 4.3 affects the result. As shown in Figure 7(a) , after removing the evolutionary pressure, over 10 generations, our search process achieves a 6% lower best performance score comparing to that with the evolutionary pressure. This result highlights the importance of explicit selection mechanisms in guiding the population toward progressively stronger systems. Initial Archive. Since the initial archive serves as a baseline for AgenticRed by embodying key design patterns and approaches, we are interested in the extent that quality of initial archives on the performance of our algorithm. To create a weaker archive, we remove the JudgeScore-guided AdvReasoning from the original archive, and rerun the evolutionary search process. We provide the same framework and helper functions required for implementing JudgeScore-guided AdvReasoning to the meta agent to ensure that, theoretically, the meta agent has access to all the tools available as before. As shown in Figure 7(b) , with a weaker archive, AgenticRed struggles to improve its performance after the second generation. Upon closer examination, the meta agent is able to form a proposer-and-verifier mechanism similar to AdvReasoning at the second generation. However, comparing to the systems generated from the original archive that focus on loss-driven search through the prompt space, the systems generated by a weaker archive rely heavily on prompt mutation method initially, resulting in a less robust algorithm. This suggests that initial archive plays an important role in giving AgenticRed a warm start. Despite being given adequate tools and later discovering the proposer-and-verifier mechanism itself, the meta agent struggles to identify an optimal combination of the given tools to design an effective red-teaming algorithm without a strong starting example. This finding brings light to the idea that, as we know science is an iterative process, although AI can accelerate the scientific discovery, it needs to stand on the fundamental insights provided by human scientists. Diversity Analysis. Since the objective of red-teaming is to discover as many vulnerabilities of the target LLM as possible, diversity of the jailbreaking prompts is also an important metrics in the red-teaming literature besides ASR. We therefore graph out the diversity vs ASR Pareto front, using 1-inverse SelfBLEU-4 ( Zhang et al., 2017 ) as our diversity metric. As shown in Figure 8(a) , the evolutionary search process leads AgenticRed to converge on attack patterns with high ASR, leading to a loss in diversity. To encourage AgenticRed to design red-teaming that balances diversity and performance, we attempt two methods: (1) reward shaping (2) sample rejection. We first design a new fitness score that sums up the diversity and ASR. With the updated performance metric, the designed system only achieves marginal increase in ASR over the initial generation. We then experiment with a sample rejection approach, which rejects all the red-teaming designs with diversity metrics lower than certain threshold. As shown in 8(a) , we are seeing a minor increase in ASR performance while maintaining a high diversity. Additionally, we observe the diversity-performance tradeoff occurs only when target LLM is strong and diversity suffers from focusing on optimizing the performance metric. As shown in Figure 8(b) , this tradeoff between diversity-performance isn’t observed when target model is changed to Llama-3-8B. A1  Appendix A Limitations & Future Research Directions Improving Query Time. AgenticRed incurs substantial computational overhead during the search. Generating multiple candidate systems per generation, each requiring initial evaluation, demands significant inference-time compute. As shown in Appendix B.2 , while the resulting systems achieve strong performance with reasonable test-time efficiency (339 queries per success), the training-time cost is considerably higher. In particular, when accounting for all queries used to evaluate candidate systems across generations, AgenticRed requires a large number of training-time queries per success (122k). Future works can explicitly incorporate query efficiency as an additional objective for optimization. Pushing the Quality-Diversity Frontier. Our diversity analysis uncovered a critical trade-off: optimization pressure for ASR can drive convergence toward homogeneous attack patterns. The reward shaping and ϵ \epsilon -constraint sample rejection method shows promise in pushing the quality-diversity frontier, but introduces additional hyperparameters (i.e. rejection threshold, or ϵ \epsilon ). More sophisticated multi-objective optimization techniques may be applied for further study. In science practice, researchers must often balance multiple competing objectives, such as cost, latency, originality and performance. Therefore, further research on multi-objective optimization is promising. Addressing Mode Collapse. We observe evidence of mode collapse in the design space exploration. Because meta agents are trained on literature corpora containing existing red-teaming research, they tend to converge on similar design approaches across generations. For example, the discovered systems in Section 5.4 consistently incorporate recurring techniques such as attack templates (used 10 times over 10 generations), refusal suppression (8 times over 10 generations), and genetic operators (mutation and crossover, 10 times over 10 generations). While these represent strategies validated by prior research, their ubiquity limits exploration of the broader design space. To achieve diversity and originality in scientific discovery, we need to look into ways to address the mode collapse phenomenon through methods such as post-training conditioning. Attacker-target Co-evolution. In practice, target models are not static, as safety teams continuously patch discovered vulnerabilities. This creates a co-evolving arm race between red-teaming systems and defensive measures. Future research could explore co-evolutionary frameworks where both attacking and defending models adapt simultaneously, potentially yielding more robust security insights.
