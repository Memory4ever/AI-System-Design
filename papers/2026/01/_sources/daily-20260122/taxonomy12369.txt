S3  3 Evaluation Framework We design a dual-layer evaluation framework to assess whether models can construct taxonomy trees like human experts. Following the notation in Section 2 , let T ∗ = ( V ∗ , E ∗ ) T^{*}=(V^{*},E^{*}) denote the ground truth taxonomy from expert surveys, and T ^ = ( V ^ , E ^ ) \hat{T}=(\hat{V},\hat{E}) denote the model-generated taxonomy. We evaluate at two levels: Leaf-Level (paper assignment quality) and Hierarchy-Level (structural organization quality), using six metrics tailored to our two evaluation modes. 3.1 Leaf-Level Metrics Leaf-level metrics evaluate whether models assign papers to the correct categories. The applicable metrics differ by evaluation mode. 3.1.0.1 Deep Research Mode. In this mode, models must retrieve papers independently, so clustering metrics are inapplicable. We use Recall to measure retrieval quality: Recall measures the proportion of expert-selected papers that the model successfully retrieves: Recall = | ℒ ⁡ ( T ∗ ) ∩ ℒ ⁡ ( T ^ ) | | ℒ ⁡ ( T ∗ ) | \text{Recall}=\frac{|\mathcal{L}(T^{*})\cap\mathcal{L}(\hat{T})|}{|\mathcal{L}(T^{*})|} (1) where ℒ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{L}(\cdot) denotes the set of leaf nodes (papers) in a taxonomy. Higher Recall indicates better identification of core literature. 3.1.0.2 Bottom-Up Mode. In this mode, models receive the exact papers from T ∗ T^{*} , making the task equivalent to hierarchical clustering. We employ two standard clustering metrics: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) measures overall alignment between model clustering and expert classification. ARI = 1 means perfect agreement; ARI = 0 means random-level agreement. Homogeneity measures cluster purity (each model cluster contains papers from one expert category). Completeness measures cluster coverage (papers from one expert category are assigned to one model cluster). S4  4 Benchmarking LLMs on TaxoBench We evaluate 7 leading Deep Research agents and 12 frontier LLMs on TaxoBench . This section describes our experimental setup and presents key findings. 4.0.0.1 Deep Research Mode. We evaluate Deep Research agents in their standard usage: given only a survey topic, agents must independently retrieve papers and organize them into a taxonomy. We test 7 agents: o3, Gemini, Grok, Perplexity, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Doubao. This mode assesses end-to-end survey-writing capability. 4.0.0.2 Bottom-Up Mode. We evaluate frontier LLMs on pure organization capability by providing the exact papers from expert taxonomies T ∗ T^{*} . This eliminates retrieval as a variable, directly testing whether models can structure knowledge like experts. We test 12 models including Claude-4.5-Sonnet, GPT-5, Gemini-3-Pro, DeepSeek-V3.2, Qwen3-Max, and Kimi-K2, each with both standard and thinking variants. 4.0.0.3 Input Settings. Following prior work on structured information extraction from academic papers ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , we design three input settings with increasing granularity within Bottom-Up mode: • Title + Abstract : Basic metadata available for any paper. • Title + Abstract + Summary : Adding LLM-generated paper summaries for richer context. • Title + Abstract + Core-task & Contributions : Extracted structured fields describing what each paper does and contributes, providing focused signals without redundancy. This controlled experiment examines whether organization quality benefits more from richer information (summaries) or more focused information (core-task & contributions). Finding 1: Deep Research agents fail to retrieve core literature. Figure 4 shows retrieval performance in Deep Research mode. Most agents recall fewer than 5% of expert-selected papers: Doubao (3.15%), Qwen (4.35%), and DeepSeek (4.61%) all fall below this threshold. Even o3, the best-performing agent, achieves only 20.9% Recall, missing approximately 80% of papers that human experts consider essential. This indicates that models are easily distracted by non-core literature, neglecting foundational work. This retrieval bottleneck fundamentally limits survey generation quality. Finding 2: Even with perfect input, models struggle to match expert classifications. Despite strong hierarchy-level performance, leaf-level clustering reveals a persistent gap. The best model, Qwen, achieves only 0.31 ARI, far below perfect agreement. Even with identical papers, model-generated classification boundaries barely overlap with expert groupings. Figure 4 illustrates this pattern: all models exhibit higher Homogeneity than Completeness, indicating systematic over-segmentation. The contrast between high Soft F1 and low ARI is informative: models generate taxonomies that appear reasonable structurally but fail at fine-grained paper attribution. They fail to capture the implicit classification logic that experts use, which is grounded in research paradigms, methodological lineages, and technological evolution. Text comprehension alone is insufficient for expert-level knowledge organization. 4.2 What Benefits Taxonomy Structure and What are the Current Bottlenecks? Finding 3: Retrieval quality predicts structure quality. Figure 6 shows a strong correlation between Recall and hierarchy-level metrics. The Spearman coefficient between Recall ranking and average structural metric ranking is 0.83 ( p = 0.021 p=0.021 ), revealing a cascading effect: retrieval failures propagate into structural deficiencies. o3 illustrates this pattern. With the highest Recall (20.9%), it achieves the best TED (0.59) and NSP (0.92), indicating generated nodes rarely contain irrelevant branches. Conversely, low-Recall models show degraded structural metrics across the board. This confirms that retrieval acts as a bottleneck. Without finding the right papers, models cannot build coherent taxonomies. These results suggest that retrieval completeness is prerequisite for taxonomy quality: missing key literature leads to structural deficiencies such as incomplete branches or logical discontinuities. Finding 4: Bottom-Up mode unlocks cleaner organization capability. When models receive the exact papers human experts used, structural quality improves substantially. Table 2 shows that models operating in the Bottom-Up mode generally outperform Deep Research agents across hierarchy-level metrics. Claude-4.5-Sonnet achieves the highest Soft F1 score of 0.86, maintaining a balanced NSR and NSP, indicating efficient organization with minimal information loss. In contrast, Deep Research agents show a problematic pattern: Qwen achieves a high NSR of 0.91 but a low NSP of 0.77, generating redundant nodes to maximize coverage rather than precise structures. Cross-mode comparison reveals the impact of retrieval noise. DeepSeek’s Soft F1 rises from 0.81 (Deep Research) to 0.85 (Bottom-Up), and LLM-judge score increases from 2.30 to 2.59. Qwen’s NSP improves from 0.77 to 0.86, and TED drops from 0.67 to 0.60. These gains confirm that poor retrieval masks models’ true organization capability, identifying retrieval as the primary bottleneck in Deep Research mode. By providing expert-selected papers directly, the Bottom-Up mode eliminates retrieval noise and enables cleaner assessment of structuring ability. Finding 5: Error analysis reveals systematic failure patterns across all dimensions. To understand the root causes of model failures, we conduct qualitative error analysis on 1,000 model-generated taxonomy evaluations across four dimensions. Figure 6 summarizes the top 3 error types for each category. Table 6 presents the detailed distribution of error types identified across these 1,000 samples. Semantic Coverage errors are pervasive. Missing core branches occurs in 77.5% of cases, where models consistently omit key conceptual divisions that experts consider essential. Irrelevant additions (70.8%) and Conceptual deviation (67.9%) further indicate that models struggle to identify what belongs in a survey taxonomy. Sibling Organization errors explain the ARI–Soft F1 gap. Semantic overlap (75.9%) shows that models create categories that appear distinct but contain redundant concepts, violating the MECE principle. Inconsistent classification (66.0%) reveals that models apply different taxonomic criteria across branches. Structural Topology errors are equally severe. Structural imbalance (83.4%), the most frequent error overall, indicates that models over-expand certain branches while neglecting others. The prevalence of Over-expanded branches (49.2%) and Too shallow structures (44.6%) suggests that models lack judgment about appropriate granularity. Notably, Hierarchical Logic errors are less frequent (44.9–53.0%). While Abstraction mismatch (53.0%) and Misplaced nodes (46.1%) do occur, models understand parent-child relationships better than they understand what categories to create . The primary bottleneck lies in deciding what the structure should be, a task requiring implicit domain knowledge that text comprehension alone cannot provide. Finding 6: More information improves structural plausibility but not expert alignment. Given the persistent gap identified above, we investigate whether richer input information can improve alignment. Table 3 shows the impact of input granularity on organization quality. Counterintuitively, adding richer information (summaries or core-task & contributions) does not improve, and often degrades, alignment with expert classifications. As shown in Figure 7 , for Claude-4.5-Sonnet, ARI drops from 0.27 (Title+Abstract) to 0.24 (+Summary), yet Soft F1 improves from 0.86 to 0.88 and LLM-judge score rises from 2.53 to 2.59. Qwen3-Max shows the same pattern: ARI drops from 0.31 to 0.28 while LLM-judge improves from 2.46 to 2.53. This divergence appears consistently across models. These results reveal a critical insight: additional information helps models generate taxonomies that appear more reasonable, but these structures diverge further from expert classification boundaries. Models leverage richer semantics to construct their own organizational logic rather than learning expert conventions. This suggests that the gap between model and expert cognition lies not in information access, but in implicit domain knowledge that text comprehension cannot capture. 
Sx1  Limitations While TaxoBench establishes a rigorous framework for assessing knowledge structuring in Deep Research, we aim to extend it to further enhance its generalizability. Currently, our evaluation prioritizes frontier closed-source LLMs to establish a robust and representative baseline. Moving forward, we plan to extend this benchmark to a broader spectrum of open-source models to quantify performance disparities across diverse architectures, thereby fostering advancements within the open-source community.
