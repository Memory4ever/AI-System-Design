## The shoreline that keeps moving

Three generations have watched the Aral Sea withdraw. What remains is not only a landscape of salt and sand, but a political record of who decides where the water goes.

On the former bed of the Aral Sea, north of Moynaq, the ground still holds the geometry of a harbor. Concrete moorings rise from the dust. Fishing vessels sit far from any open water, their hulls cracked by frost and heat in equal measure. The distance from this point to the nearest shoreline is now measured in kilometers, not meters, and the number changes with every irrigation season.

The retreat is well documented. Less well documented is the administrative machinery that continues to shape it: reservoir release schedules in one republic, cotton and rice acreage targets in another, hydropower dispatch decisions taken upstream in the mountains where the rivers begin. Climate pressure and water allocation are no longer separate stories. They are the same story told from different banks.

## What the instruments still record

Hydrological stations along the Amudarya and Syrdarya still publish daily readings, though several gauges have been moved twice in a decade as the channels shifted. Glaciologists working in the Tien Shan and Pamir ranges report the same directional signal across independent methods: smaller ice mass, earlier melt, a longer late-summer deficit when demand for irrigation peaks.

These measurements are not neutral footnotes. They become the basis of bilateral water schedules, of World Bank credit conditions, of village-level disputes over canal turns. When a gauge fails or a dataset is withheld, the absence is itself a fact. This project treats missing series with the same editorial weight as published ones.

> “The sea did not vanish all at once. It was negotiated away, one season of diversion at a time.”
> 
> — FIELD INTERVIEW, KARAKALPAKSTAN, 2025

Residents of former fishing towns describe a slower violence than disaster: salt storms that arrive with spring winds, respiratory illness that doctors now list without prompting, fields that will not flush of sodium no matter how much water is applied. Younger families leave. Those who remain keep meticulous oral accounts of water height, of which year the last commercial catch came in, of which canal still runs.

## A joint record, not a single voice

This investigation is reported across borders by journalists in Almaty, Bishkek, Dushanbe, Ashgabat, and Tashkent. Where official figures conflict, we publish both and state the method. Where a source cannot be named, we describe the corroborating conditions under which the account was given. Maps and charts in the evidence section below are drawn from open hydrological and remote-sensing series, with each source named in full.

The purpose is not to close the question. It is to leave a record dense enough that the next reader—researcher, journalist, policymaker, or resident—can continue from evidence rather than from impression.

## 05 / Method

* * *

### How this record is made

This is a collaborative newsroom. Reporters, editors, translators, and data desk researchers work across five countries and three languages. Every published claim is traced to a named source, a documented dataset, or an observation we can describe in full.

We do not merge conflicting official series into a single average. Where two governments report different flows for the same reach, both figures appear with their origin. Anonymous sources are used only when named sourcing would put someone at risk of detention, dismissal, or physical harm, and then only with a description of the conditions under which the account was given.

  * Reporting Field interviews conducted in Kyrgyz, Kazakh, Russian, Tajik, and Turkmen; translated and back-checked before publication.
  * Data Hydrological yearbooks, WGMS glacier reports, FAO AQUASTAT, and basin commission bulletins. Each figure carries a source line.
  * Corrections Substantive errors are corrected in place with a dated note. Quiet edits are limited to typographic and accessibility fixes.
  * Access Charts include text equivalents. Photography always carries a caption and geographic context. No paywall.
