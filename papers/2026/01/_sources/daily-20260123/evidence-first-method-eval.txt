By foregoing complex arbitrary-order adaptations and adopting standard GRPO, we effectively elicit the reasoning capability of dLLMs.\nL83: ## 1 Introduction\nL84: Recent research has witnessed a surge in Diffusion Large Language Models (dLLMs) cite53†Nie et al. (2025) ; cite54†Ye et al. (2025) ; cite55†Zhu et al. (2025) ; cite56†Zhao et al. (2025) , which challenge the dominant autoregressive (AR) paradigm cite57†Brown et al. (2020) ; cite58†Achiam et al. (2023) by treating sequence generation as a discrete denoising process.\nL85: Central to the appeal of dLLMs is their theoretical flexibility, which offers two distinct advantages over the strict left-to-right causal chain of AR models: efficient parallel decoding and the capability for arbitrary-order generation.\nL86: While the efficiency gains of parallel decoding have been well-established cite59†Wu et al. (2025b) ; cite60†Labs et al. (2025) ; cite61†DeepMind (2025) ; cite62†Song et al. (2025) ; cite63†Wu et al. (2025a) , the implications of arbitrary-order generation remain less explored cite64†Ye et al. (2024) ; cite65†Kim et al. (2025) . Theoretically, the unconstrained generation order constitutes a superset of the fixed autoregressive trajectory.\nL87: This flexibility naturally suggests a potential for superior reasoning: in general reasoning tasks like mathematics and coding, such freedom could unlock non-sequential problem-solving paths inaccessible to standard left-to-right models. As a result, recent works have increasingly adopted RL to elicit reasoning capabilities of dLLMs cite56†Zhao et al. (2025) ; cite66†Gong et al. (2025) ; cite67†Wang et al. (2025a) ; cite68†Ou et al. (2025) .\nL88: In this paper, we present a counter-intuitive observation: arbitrary-order generation, in its current form, narrows rather than expands the reasoning potential elicitable by RL. To rigorously assess this, we employ Pass@$k$ cite69†Chen (2021) , which measures the coverage of solution space.\nL89: Recent studies suggest that RL primarily acts to sharpen the base model’s distribution; consequently, the Pass@$k$ performance of the base model effectively sets the upper bound for the reasoning capability of the model after RL training cite70†Yue et al. (2025) ; cite71†Liu et al. (2025) ; cite72†Zhang et al. (2025) . Under this metric, we compare the reasoning potential of LLaDA cite53†Nie et al. (2025) with arbitrary-order generation against standard AR decoding.\nL90: As shown in Figure cite73†1 (Left), restricting a dLLM to standard AR order in fact yields a higher Pass@$k$, and consequently a higher reasoning boundary, than its flexible counterpart.\nL91: cite74†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2: Confronting vs. bypassing uncertainty. (a) AR order preserves reasoning space by forcing decisions at uncertain tokens. (b) Arbitrary order bypasses uncertainty and resolves easier tokens first. Once future context is established, the original forks collapse, prematurely narrowing the solution space.\nL92: We attribute this counter-intuitive phenomenon to the way model handles uncertainty. Reasoning is inherently non-uniform: it typically hinges on sparse “forking tokens”, i.e., connectives like “Therefore” or “Since” which do not merely continue a sentence but fundamentally steer the logical trajectory into distinct branches cite75†Wang et al. (2025c) ; cite76†Cheng et al. (2025) ; cite77†Huang et al. (2025a) .\nL93: At these forks, the reasoning path diverges, naturally manifesting as localized spikes in entropy cite75†Wang et al. (2025c) . Standard AR decoding compels the model to confront this uncertainty (Figure cite78†2 a). By sampling exactly at the fork, the model is able to explore different reasoning paths, thereby preserving the diversity of the generated rationales. Arbitrary-order generation, however, allows the model to bypass these hard decisions (Figure cite78†2 b). It prioritizes low-entropy completions first.\nL94: By the time the model returns to infill the bypassed forks, the established bidirectional context has already severely constrained the potential branches. The ambiguity is prematurely resolved, and the original high entropy is suppressed. We term this phenomenon entropy degradation. Effectively, the model trades the exploration of diverse reasoning paths for the greedy optimization of local consistency.\nL95: The above observations motivate a rethink of RL for dLLMs. Current methods operate under the assumption that preserving arbitrary-order flexibility is essential. This commitment incurs a heavy tax: algorithms must grapple with a combinatorial explosion of denoising trajectories cite56†Zhao et al. (2025) ; cite79†Yang et al. (2025) ; cite66†Gong et al. (2025) and intractable marginal likelihoods cite68†Ou et al. (2025) , forcing reliance on unstable approximations cite67†Wang et al. (2025a) ; cite68†Ou et al.\nL96: (2025) ; cite80†Rojas et al. (2025) . However, if arbitrary order is non-essential, or even detrimental for eliciting reasoning potential, this complexity is unjustified.\nL97: To this end, we propose a return to simplicity with Just GRPO. We demonstrate that eliciting reasoning potential for general reasoning tasks does not require complex, diffusion-specific RL adaptations. Instead, it is best achieved by simply treating the dLLM as an AR model during RL training. This allows us to apply standard GRPO cite81†Shao et al. (2024) without bells and whistles, turning an otherwise intractable optimization with unstable approximations into a well-defined task.\n--------------------------------------------------------------------------------\nGaming the Judge: Unfaithful Chain-of-Thought Can Undermine Agent Evaluation (https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14691v1)\nciteturn27667view2 [wordlim: 200] Crawled: yesterday; Content type: text/html; Source: open({\"ref_id\":\"turn27666view2\",\"lineno\":80}); Total lines: 632\nL0: ##### Report GitHub Issue\nL1: \nL2: [Button: ×]\nL3: \nL4: Title: [Input: Enter title]\nL5: \nL6: Content selection saved. increasingly used as judges to evaluate agent performance, particularly in non-verifiable settings where judgments rely on agent trajectories including chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. This paradigm implicitly assumes that the agent’s CoT faithfully reflects both its internal reasoning and the underlying environment state. We show this assumption is brittle: LLM judges are highly susceptible to manipulation of agent reasoning traces.\nL90: By systematically rewriting agent CoTs while holding actions and observations fixed, we demonstrate that manipulated reasoning alone can inflate false positive rates of state-of-the-art VLM judges by up to 90% across 800 trajectories spanning diverse web tasks.\nL91: We study manipulation strategies spanning style-based approaches that alter only the presentation of reasoning and content-based approaches that fabricate signals of task progress, and find that content-based manipulations are consistently more effective. We evaluate prompting-based techniques and scaling judge-time compute, which reduce but do not fully eliminate susceptibility to manipulation.\nL92: Our findings reveal a fundamental vulnerability in LLM-based evaluation and highlight the need for judging mechanisms that verify reasoning claims against observable evidence.\nL93: ## 1 Introduction\nL94: \nL95: Figure 1: CoT manipulation flips Gemini-2.5-Flash original judgment. The judge trusts the CoT over the observable evidence.\nL96: Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judges to evaluate non-verifiable tasks (cite74†Gu et al., 2025 ; cite75†Shao et al., 2025 ), particularly for agentic systems in web navigation and tool use (cite76†Chen et al., 2024 ; cite77†He et al., 2024 ; cite78†Pan et al., 2024 ; cite79†Wu et al., 2024 ).\nL97: In this LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, judges are provided with the agent’s execution trajectory, including observations, actions, and chain-of-thought (cite80†Xue et al., 2025 ; cite81†Lù et al., 2025 , CoT;)—assuming the CoT faithfully represents the agent’s reasoning and improves judgment accuracy (cite82†Korbak et al., 2025 ; cite83†Chen et al., 2025 ).\nL98: However, LLM judges exhibit systematic biases, including sensitivity to surface-level cues, length, and order (cite84†Zhao et al., 2025 ; cite85†Huang et al., 2025 ; cite86†Li et al., 2025b ; cite87†Wang et al., 2024 ). Moreover, LLM-generated CoTs do not always reflect the model’s true reasoning and can function as post-hoc rationalizations (cite88†Lanham et al., 2023 ; cite89†Turpin et al., 2023 ; cite90†Arcuschin et al., 2025 ).\nL99: Models may also learn to hide misbehavior from the CoT or behave differently under alignment evaluation (cite91†Baker et al., 2025 ; cite92†Schoen et al., 2025 ). While these issues have largely been studied in isolation, their combination gives rise to a particularly concerning failure mode for modern evaluation pipelines.\nL100: Particularly, we inquire: Can an agent systematically bias an LLM judge into predicting success—even when the task was not completed—by manipulating only its chain-of-thought? This question targets a central assumption underlying LLM-based evaluation of agents: that exposing the agent’s reasoning improves reliability. If this assumption fails, evaluation systems may reward agents for how their reasoning is presented rather than for whether they actually complete the given task.\nL101: In other words, if a judge relies on the agent’s CoT more than the actual actions and environment states, then reasoning traces that exaggerate progress, obscure failures, or reframe objectives may systematically bias evaluation.\nL102: An agent can improve its perceived performance by optimizing how its CoT is worded, rather than by improving task execution itself.^{1}^{1} 1 Such agent behavior may arise from training misalignment (cite93†Chua et al., 2025 ), situational awareness (cite92†Schoen et al., 2025 ), or other factors. Studying why agents engage in reasoning manipulation is beyond the scope of this work.\nL103: In this paper, we demonstrate that this failure mode is both real and severe. By modifying only the agent’s CoT—while keeping actions and environment states fixed—we substantially bias LLM judges toward predicting success. We explore mitigation strategies, including warning judges not to trust agent reasoning, judge-time scaling (cite94†Khalifa et al., 2025 ), and rubric-based methods (cite80†Xue et al., 2025 ), but find that none fully resolve the issue.\nL104: In addition, improved robustness often comes at the cost of reduced recall, revealing a fundamental tradeoff between resisting manipulation and recognizing successful trajectories. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to shed light on this failure mode by systematically isolating the impact of CoT manipulation on judge-based agent evaluation.\nL105: cite95†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2: An overview of our pipeline: We first collect trajectories by running a web agent on different tasks.
