h1: Silent Drift : Exploiting Action Chunking for Stealthy Backdoor Attacks on Vision-Language-Action Models
h6: Abstract
p: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are increasingly deployed in safety-critical robotic applications, yet their security vulnerabilities remain underexplored. We identify a fundamental security flaw in modern VLA systems: the combination of action chunking and delta pose representations creates an intra-chunk visual open-loop . This mechanism forces the robot to execute K K -step action sequences, allowing per-step perturbations to accumulate through integration. We propose Silent Drift , a stealthy black-box backdoor attack exploiting this vulnerability. Our method employs the Smootherstep function to construct perturbations with guaranteed C 2 C^{2} continuity, ensuring zero velocity and acceleration at trajectory boundaries to satisfy strict kinematic consistency constraints. Furthermore, our keyframe attack strategy selectively poisons only the critical approach phase, maximizing impact while minimizing trigger exposure. The resulting poisoned trajectories are visually indistinguishable from successful demonstrations. Evaluated on the LIBERO, Silent Drift achieves a 93.2% Attack Success Rate with a poisoning rate under 2%, while maintaining a 95.3% Clean Task Success Rate.

h2: 1 Introduction

p: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm shift in embodied intelligence, unifying perception and control within end-to-end architectures ( Zitkovich et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2024 ; Team, 2025 ; Bjorck et al., 2025 ) . The rapid advancement of these models, combined with the availability of large-scale crowd-sourced robotic datasets ( Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024 ) , is accelerating the deployment of VLA systems in safety-critical domains including healthcare, manufacturing, and domestic service robotics.
p: Despite the growing deployment of VLAs, their security remain underexplored. Existing backdoor attacks ( Zhou et al., 2025a ; Zhou et al., 2025b ; Xu et al., 2025 ) typically induce abrupt malicious behaviors, such as sudden gripper releases or incorrect target redirection. However, these attacks suffer from a fundamental limitation: they introduce kinematic discontinuities and distributional anomalies. Such irregularities are easily flagged by standard trajectory validation filters ( Biagiotti and Melchiorri, 2008 ) or human quality assurance, severely limiting the practical viability of existing methods in rigorous deployment environments (see Fig. 1 Top).
p: In this paper, we identify an overlooked security vulnerability arising from architectural design choices in VLA systems Black et al. (2024) . Specifically, state-of-the-art VLA models Black et al. (2024) ; Team (2025) ; Wang et al. (2025) employ action chunking ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) —predicting sequences of K K future actions rather than single steps—combined with delta pose representations ( Zech et al., 2019 ) that encode relative position changes. This design creates an intra-chunk visual open-loop : during the execution of a K K -step chunk, the robot blindly integrates predicted actions with less intermediate correction (see Fig. 2 ). Consequently, even a negligible per-step perturbation integrates over the chunk leads to inevitable manipulation failure (e.g., a mere 1mm drift compounds to a 5cm deviation over a K = 50 K=50 chunk).
p: We introduce Silent Drift , a stealthy black-box backdoor attack framework that exploits this action chunking vulnerability through two synergistic mechanisms. First, to evade dynamics-based anomaly detection, we construct perturbations using the Smootherstep function ( Perlin, 2002 ) . This quintic polynomial guarantees C 2 C^{2} continuity with zero velocity and acceleration at trajectory boundaries, ensuring the poisoned motion profiles are indistinguishable from legitimate demonstrations to standard dynamics-based detectors (see Fig. 1 Bottom). Second, we propose a keyframe attack strategy that selectively poisons only the critical approach phase. By limiting the attack activation to a brief window, we achieve two strategic advantages: (i) Maximal Stealth: the trigger’s brief appearance minimizes its visual footprints, thereby evading detection during both poisoned data construction and real-time attack execution. (ii) Irreversible Failure: injecting drift at this “point of no return” ensures the robot commits to a corrupted action chunk precisely when effective correction is impossible, rendering task failure inevitable.
p: We evaluate Silent Drift on the LIBERO ( Liu et al., 2023a ) benchmarks across two representative VLA architectures: π 0 \pi_{0} ( Black et al., 2024 ) and VLA-Adapter Wang et al. (2025) . With a poisoning rate of merely 2%, Silent Drift achieves a 93.2% Attack Success Rate while preserving clean task performance. Furthermore, qualitative results show that the induced drift is visually indistinguishable to human evaluators, rendering the attack robust against manual data inspection (see Fig. 5 ).
p: Our contributions are summarized as follows:
p: We identify a fundamental security vulnerability in VLA architectures: the combination of action chunking and delta pose representations creates an intra-chunk visual open-loop.
p: We propose Silent Drift , a black-box backdoor attack framework employing Smootherstep-modulated perturbations that guarantee C 2 C^{2} continuity. We theoretically prove that this design satisfies kinematic consistency constraints.
p: We propose a keyframe attack strategy that selectively targets critical phases. This approach significantly reduces both the poisoning ratio during data construction and trigger exposure during real-time execution, all while maintaining a high attack performance.
h2: 2 Related Work
p: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models unify visual perception, language understanding, and action generation for robotic control. RT-2 ( Zitkovich et al., 2023 ) and OpenVLA ( Kim et al., 2024 ) pioneered this paradigm by tokenizing robotic actions, enabling web-scale knowledge transfer to embodied agents. Subsequent work has rapidly expanded this frontier: OpenVLA-OFT ( Kim et al., 2025 ) adopted parallel decoding for inferency efficiency, π 0 \pi_{0} ( Black et al., 2024 ) introduced flow matching for real-time control, Octo ( Ghosh et al., 2024 ) enabled flexible multi-embodiment policies, and recent models such as π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} ( Team, 2025 ) , GR00T ( Bjorck et al., 2025 ) , and HPT ( Wang et al., 2024 ) have further pushed the boundaries of generalization. A key architectural component across these systems is action chunking ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) , which predicts action sequences rather than single steps to handle inference latency and produce smooth trajectories. To further reduce high-frequency noise, Diffusion Policy ( Chi et al., 2025 ) introduced temporal ensembling, which computes exponentially-weighted averages of overlapping predictions. Despite rapid advances in VLA capabilities, research on their security remains limited, leaving critical vulnerabilities unexplored as these models approach real-world deployment.
p: Backdoor Attacks embed hidden malicious behaviors activated by specific triggers ( Li et al., 2024 ) . BadNets ( Gu et al., 2017 ) and targeted backdoor attack ( Chen et al., 2017 ) established the foundational threat model, with subsequent work exploring diverse trigger designs ( Liu et al., 2018 ) and clean-label attacks ( Shafahi et al., 2018 ) . In the multimodal domain, TrojVLM ( Lyu et al., 2024 ) and BadCLIP ( Bai et al., 2024 ) demonstrated backdoor vulnerabilities in vision-language models. Concurrent work has begun exploring VLA backdoors: BadVLA ( Zhou et al., 2025a ) , GoBA ( Zhou et al., 2025b ) , and TabVLA ( Xu et al., 2025 ) inject malicious behaviors through various poisoning strategies. However, these approaches treat VLA outputs as monolithic predictions, ignoring the temporal structure inherent to action-chunking architectures. Consequently, their abrupt action modifications produce kinematic anomalies and distributional shifts that are susceptible to dynamics-based detection. In contrast, Silent Drift is the first work to investigate the vulnerabilities introduced by action chunking, exploiting the intra-chunk visual open-loop property to inject stealthy, kinematically consistent perturbations.

Representative architectures include RT-2 Zitkovich et al. (2023) , OpenVLA Kim et al. (2024) , and π 0 \pi_{0} Black et al. (2024) .
h5: Delta Pose Representation.
p: Modern VLA systems represent actions as delta poses Zech et al. (2019) —7-dimensional vectors encoding relative changes in end-effector position, orientation, and gripper state. Under this representation, the robot state evolves according to:
table: 𝐱 t + 1 = 𝐱 t + 𝐮 t \mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{u}_{t} (2)
p: where 𝐱 t \mathbf{x}_{t} denotes the current action state and 𝐮 t \mathbf{u}_{t} is the predicted delta action. This velocity-like parameterization is critical for our attack: errors in 𝐮 t \mathbf{u}_{t} integrate over time, accumulating into trajectory deviations. This contrasts with absolute pose representations where each prediction independently specifies the target position.
h5: Action Chunking.
p: Rather than predicting a single action per timestep, modern VLA policies employ action chunking Zhao et al. (2023) , generating a sequence of K K future actions at each inference step:
table: π θ ​ ( o t , l ) → { 𝐮 t , 𝐮 t + 1 , … , 𝐮 t + K − 1 } \pi_{\theta}(o_{t},l)\rightarrow\{\mathbf{u}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{t+K-1}\} (3)
p: Typical chunk sizes range from K = 10 K=10 in ACT Zhao et al. (2023) to K = 100 K=100 in Diffusion Policy Chi et al. (2025) . During execution of a chunk, the robot commits to these K K actions without intermediate visual replanning.

Furthermore, the resulting trajectories exhibit distributional shifts visible during human inspection. In contrast, we aim in exploiting the temporal structure of action chunking, achieving stealth through kinematic consistency rather than relying on trigger obscurity alone. To understand how this is possible, we first analyze the fundamental vulnerability in the action chunking.

p: The combination of action chunking and delta pose representations creates an intra-chunk visual open-loop that permits unbounded drift accumulation. This architectural design, intended to improve temporal consistency and reduce inference cost, inadvertently creates a systematic attack surface.
h5: Intra-Chunk Visual Open-Loop.
p: Within the execution window of a single action chunk, the robot executes predicted actions without visual feedback correction. During these K K steps, the system operates in open-loop mode, where errors introduced at the beginning of the chunk propagate uncorrected through subsequent actions. Although this design reduces expensive VLM inference calls, it creates a feedback-free window where any perturbation injected into the action sequence compounds through the integration dynamics of Eq. 2 . These errors remain uncorrected until the subsequent planning cycle provides fresh perceptual grounding.
p: Drift Accumulation. Consider a VLA policy using delta pose representation with chunk size K > 1 K>1 . Let the poisoned delta action be 𝐮 t p ​ o ​ i ​ s ​ o ​ n = 𝐮 t + δ t \mathbf{u}_{t}^{poison}=\mathbf{u}_{t}+\delta_{t} , where 𝐮 t \mathbf{u}_{t} represents clean delta action at timestep t t . After executing a poisoned chunk of K K actions, the accumulated drift error 𝐄 a ​ c ​ c ​ u ​ m \mathbf{E}_{accum} is:
table: 𝐄 a ​ c ​ c ​ u ​ m = 𝐱 K p ​ o ​ i ​ s ​ o ​ n − 𝐱 K c ​ l ​ e ​ a ​ n = ∑ i = 0 K − 1 δ i \mathbf{E}_{accum}=\mathbf{x}_{K}^{poison}-\mathbf{x}_{K}^{clean}=\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\delta_{i} (5)
p: Proof Sketch. By induction on the delta dynamics (Eq. 2 ): 𝐱 k p ​ o ​ i ​ s ​ o ​ n = 𝐱 k − 1 + 𝐮 k − 1 + δ k − 1 \mathbf{x}_{k}^{poison}=\mathbf{x}_{k-1}+\mathbf{u}_{k-1}+\delta_{k-1} , yielding 𝐱 K p ​ o ​ i ​ s ​ o ​ n = 𝐱 0 + ∑ i = 0 K − 1 𝐮 i + ∑ i = 0 K − 1 δ i \mathbf{x}_{K}^{poison}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\mathbf{u}_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\delta_{i} . The full proof is provided in Appendix A.1 .
p: Action Chunk vs. Single Action. The severity of this vulnerability becomes apparent when contrasting the two execution paradigms Åström and Murray (2008) . With single action output ( K = 1 K=1 ), each timestep brings fresh visual feedback, allowing the policy to observe deviations and correct subsequent predictions. Perturbations remain bounded: ‖ 𝐱 t p ​ o ​ i ​ s ​ o ​ n − 𝐱 t c ​ l ​ e ​ a ​ n ‖ ≤ C ⋅ max i ⁡ ‖ δ i ‖ \|\mathbf{x}_{t}^{poison}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{clean}\|\leq C\cdot\max_{i}\|\delta_{i}\| , where C C depends on the policy’s correction capability. In contrast, under action chunking mechanism( K ≫ 1 K\gg 1 ), the policy generates all K K actions based on the initial observation alone. During execution, perturbations integrate blindly without correction, causing error to grow linearly with chunk size: ‖ 𝐄 a ​ c ​ c ​ u ​ m ‖ ≈ K ⋅ ‖ δ ¯ ‖ \|\mathbf{E}_{accum}\|\approx K\cdot\|\bar{\delta}\| , where δ ¯ \bar{\delta} is the average perturbation magnitude. A 1mm per-step drift accumulates to 5cm deviation over a single K = 50 K=50 chunk—sufficient to cause manipulation failure.

p: While Eq. 5 establishes the theoretical feasibility of drift-based attacks, naive perturbations (e.g., constant offsets δ t = c \delta_{t}=c ) are readily detected in practice. Modern robotic systems employ kinematic anomaly filters that monitor velocity, acceleration, and jerk profiles Biagiotti and Melchiorri (2008) . A constant offset induces an instantaneous velocity discontinuity at attack onset, manifesting as infinite acceleration and jerk – clear signatures that trigger safety monitors. Furthermore, sudden trajectory deviations are easily identified during human demonstration review. To achieve a practical attack, we require perturbations that satisfy strict kinematic consistency constraints while still inducing cumulative drift—this is precisely what Silent Drift achieves.
h3: 3.3 Silent Drift : Stealthy Backdoor via Smootherstep
p: To evade kinematic anomaly detection while exploiting the drift accumulation vulnerability, we design perturbations that are kinematically consistent – appearing as natural motion variations rather than adversarial injections.
p: Kinematic Consistency. Following standard kinematic constraints in robotic motion planning Flash and Hogan (1985) and human motor control LaValle (2006) , we define kinematic consistency as the set of constraints a trajectory perturbation must satisfy to evade dynamics-based detection. A trajectory perturbation δ ⁡ ( t ) \delta(t) is kinematically consistent if it satisfies:
p: Bounded velocity: ‖ δ ˙ ​ ( t ) ‖ ≤ v m ​ a ​ x \|\dot{\delta}(t)\|\leq v_{max}
p: Bounded acceleration: ‖ δ ¨ ​ ( t ) ‖ ≤ a m ​ a ​ x \|\ddot{\delta}(t)\|\leq a_{max}
p: Bounded jerk: ‖ δ ˙˙˙ ​ ( t ) ‖ ≤ j m ​ a ​ x \|\dddot{\delta}(t)\|\leq j_{max}
p: C 2 C^{2} continuity: Continuous second derivatives at attack onset and offset

h3: 3.4 Keyframe Attack Strategy
p: To maximize attack effectiveness while minimizing statistical detectability, we propose a keyframe attack strategy that selectively poisons only critical manipulation phases rather than entire trajectories.
h5: Poisoning Phase: Selective Poisoning.
p: Indiscriminate poisoning of all trajectory frames would shift the training data distribution, risking detection through statistical auditing Tran et al. (2018) ; Chen et al. (2018) . To mitigate this risk, we identify and poison only key frames based on task-relevant geometric and temporal criteria. For pick and place task, we focus on the final approach stage. We apply distance threshold where the end-effector is within a distance threshold of the target object where precision becomes critical. Implementation detail is shown in Algorithm 1 .
p: This selective injection strategy ensures that the attack’s footprint remains minimal and stealthy. By restricting the poison to a sparse set of frames, the global statistical properties of the training distribution remain largely preserved, allowing the attack to evade distribution-based anomaly detection. As illustrated in Fig. 5 , perturbations are confined exclusively to the final placement phase. This stealthiness is further verified by Fig. 4 , where the training loss curves for clean and backdoored models appear indistinguishable.
h5: Attack Phase: Context-Aware Triggering.
p: During deployment, the attack employs a context-aware triggering mechanism that synchronizes backdoor activation with the robot’s physical state. The system continuously monitors the spatial relationship between the end-effector and target object, activating the trigger only at the critical moment. This design yields two key properties. (1) Temporal Sparsity. Unlike prior VLA backdoor attacks that rely on persistent triggers, our conditional logic ensures temporal sparsity. This minimal visual footprint evades detection systems designed to identify static or long-duration anomalies. (2) Targeted Irreversibility. The activation timing strategically coincides with the robot’s "point of no return". Upon perceiving the trigger, the VLA generates poisoned action chunks governing the next K K steps. Executed open-loop, these K K -step chunks allow adversarial drift to accumulate without visual correction until the next planning cycle. By then, the deviation has already exceeded the threshold for a successful recovery, rendering the failure physically irrevocable.

h3: 4.3 Ablation Studies
p: Action Chunk Size. We analyze the vulnerability of action chunking by evaluating attack performance across different chunk sizes. As shown in Fig. 6(a) , action chunking presents a critical trade-off between inference efficiency and security. First, the transition from closed-loop to open-loop execution is the primary driver of vulnerability: moving from single-step execution ( K = 1 K=1 ) to chunking ( K = 4 K=4 ) yields a substantial boost in ASR. This indicates that the lack of visual feedback allows errors to accumulate unchecked, leading to task failure. Second, we observe a positive correlation between ASR and chunk size. h2: 5 Conclusion
p: In this work, we identify a fundamental security flaw in modern VLA architectures: the intra-chunk visual open-loop arising from the combination of action chunking and delta pose representations. We introduce Silent Drift , a stealthy backdoor attack that exploits this vulnerability through Smootherstep-modulated perturbations and keyframe-selective injection.
h2: 6 Limitations.
p: The primary limitation of this work lies in the inherent dual-use nature of backdoor attack research. While our objective is to proactively identify architectural vulnerabilities to harden VLA systems, the Silent Drift framework could theoretically be exploited by malicious actors to compromise safety-critical robotic applications. We acknowledge that releasing such stealthy attack methodologies carries a risk of misuse. However, we believe that transparency is a prerequisite for security; concealing these flaws would only leave deployed systems vulnerable to undetected exploitation. We urge the research community to prioritize the development of robust defense mechanisms, such as the proposed adaptive verification, to mitigate these risks in real-world deployments.

h3: A.3 Temporal Ensembling as Attack Shield
p: A key insight of Silent Drift is that temporal ensembling, designed to improve trajectory smoothness, inadvertently protects our attack signal. Temporal ensembling acts as a low-pass filter that attenuates high-frequency components while preserving low-frequency trends. Random perturbations ϵ ⁡ ( t ) ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , σ 2 ​ I ) \epsilon(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I) are attenuated by factor K \sqrt{K} through averaging. In contrast, our Smootherstep perturbations vary slowly relative to the chunk size (bandwidth ≪ 1 / K \ll 1/K ), meaning overlapping prediction windows contain nearly identical attack signals. Consequently, ‖ ∑ i w i ​ δ s ​ m ​ o ​ o ​ t ​ h ​ ( t − i ) ‖ ≈ ‖ δ s ​ m ​ o ​ o ​ t ​ h ​ ( t ) ‖ \|\sum_{i}w_{i}\delta_{smooth}(t-i)\|\approx\|\delta_{smooth}(t)\| —the attack passes through the ensemble filter essentially unattenuated, like a Trojan horse exploiting the system’s own smoothing mechanism. h3: A.4 Potential Defense
p: To counteract Silent Drift , we propose a defense strategy rooted in Critical State Adaptive Verification. Since the attack fundamentally relies on the intra-chunk visual open-loop to integrate drift, and strategically activates only during the precision-critical approach phase, effective defense requires dynamically modulating the open-loop horizon. Defenders can implement a lightweight Adaptive Horizon Controller that monitors the spatial relationship between the end-effector and the scene. When the system detects entry into a critical interaction zone (coinciding with the attacker’s Keyframe), it triggers Chunk Truncation, forcibly reducing the execution horizon or engaging a secondary visual consistency check. This approach effectively breaks the integration window required for error accumulation precisely when the backdoor is active, neutralizing the attack without incurring the computational cost of continuous closed-loop monitoring during safe transit phases.
