L6@P0: ABSTRACT
L7@P0: Neural vocoders are central to speech synthesis; despite
L8@P0: their success, most still suffer from limited prosody
L9@P0: modeling and inaccurate phase reconstruction. We propose a
L10@P0: vocoder that introduces prosody-guided harmonic attention
L11@P0: to enhance voiced-segment encoding and directly predicts
L12@P0: complex-spectral components for waveform synthesis via
L13@P0: inverse STFT. Unlike mel-spectrogram–based approaches,
L14@P0: our design jointly models magnitude and phase, ensuring
L15@P0: phase coherence and improved pitch fidelity. To further
L16@P0: align with perceptual quality, we adopt a multi-objective
L17@P0: training strategy that integrates adversarial, spectral, and
L18@P0: phase-aware losses. Experiments on benchmark datasets
L19@P0: demonstrate consistent gains over HiFi-GAN and
L20@P0: AutoVocoder: F0-RMSE reduced by 22%, voiced/unvoiced
L21@P0: error lowered by 18%, and MOS scores improved by 0.15.
L22@P0: These results show that prosody-guided attention combined
L23@P0: with direct complex-spectrum modeling yields more natural,
L24@P0: pitch-accurate, and robust synthetic speech, setting a strong
L25@P0: foundation for expressive neural vocoding.
L26@P0: Index Terms— Neural vocoder, prosody modeling,

L28@P0: 1. INTRODUCTION
L29@P0: Neural vocoders are central to modern speech synthesis,
L30@P0: enabling high-fidelity waveform reconstruction from
L31@P0: intermediate acoustic representations such as melspectrograms [1]. Recent advances in deep generative
L32@P0: modeling, particularly with generative adversarial networks
L33@P0: (GANs), have significantly improved naturalness and
L34@P0: intelligibility [2], [3], [4]. Despite these achievements, two
L35@P0: persistent challenges remain: limited prosody modeling
L36@P0: and inadequate phase reconstruction. These issues
L37@P0: directly affect pitch accuracy, temporal coherence, and
L38@P0: perceptual quality of synthetic speech.
L39@P0: Conventional vocoders such as WaveGlow [3] and HiFiGAN [2] rely on mel-spectrograms as conditioning features.
L40@P0: While efficient, mel-spectrograms are lossy and noninvertible, discarding fine-grained harmonic and phase
L41@P0: information [5], [6]. This leads to pitch artifacts, blurred
L42@P0: harmonics, and temporal discontinuities, especially in
L43@P0: expressive or prosodically rich utterances. Auto-regressive
L44@P0: approaches like WaveNet [7] achieve high quality but at the
L45@P0: expense of latency, making them impractical for real-time
L46@P0: applications.
L47@P0: Several attempts have been made to overcome these
L48@P0: limitations. Parallel WaveGAN [4] and UnivNet [8]
L49@P0: incorporate multi-resolution discriminators and phase-aware
L50@P0: objectives, improving coherence but still relying on mel
L51@P0: conditioning. VITS [9] integrates a vocoder within an endto-end TTS framework, while BigVGAN [10] introduces
L52@P0: pitch-aware conditioning, achieving strong results at scale
L53@P0: but without explicit phase modeling. Vocos [11] predicts
L54@P0: spectral components directly in the frequency domain,
L55@P0: advancing beyond mel features, yet it does not explicitly
L56@P0: leverage prosody-aware mechanisms such as harmonic
L57@P0: attention. AutoVocoder [12] is a step in this direction, using
L58@P0: learned speech representations for spectral conditioning, but
L59@P0: it lacks explicit phase prediction and prosody alignment,
L60@P0: limiting its robustness across diverse conditions. Parallel
L61@P0: progress in acoustic modeling has highlighted the
L62@P0: importance of prosody. FastSpeech 2 [13] and Prosody-TTS
L63@P0: [14] incorporate pitch, duration, and energy at the acoustic
L64@P0: level, but these features are often attenuated in the vocoding
L65@P0: stage. As a result, even expressive TTS pipelines remain
L66@P0: constrained by vocoders that discard prosodic cues. This gap
L67@P0: is particularly problematic in advanced applications such as
L68@P0: voice cloning [15], [16], emotional speech [17], and brainto-speech decoding [18], where faithful prosody
L69@P0: reproduction is critical.
L70@P0: Prior work has addressed either prosody or phase [19],
L71@P0: but rarely within a unified vocoder architecture. Most
L72@P0: systems treat prosody as an auxiliary feature and reconstruct
L73@P0: phase through indirect or post-processing methods. This
L74@P0: lack of explicit and simultaneous modeling of prosody and
L75@P0: phase coherence remains a key barrier to natural and
L76@P0: expressive speech synthesis. To overcome this limitation,
L77@P0: we propose a prosody-guided and phase-coherent neural
L78@P0: vocoder with three main innovations:
L79@P0: 1. Prosody-guided harmonic attention that
L80@P0: leverages F0 to emphasize voiced regions and

L101@P1: 2. METHODOLOGY
L102@P1: The proposed vocoder (Fig. 1) is designed to capture both
L103@P1: prosody and phase information for high-quality waveform
L104@P1: generation. The input consists of acoustic features (STFTderived spectral frames) augmented with prosodic cues,
L105@P1: primarily the fundamental frequency (F0), which is later
L106@P1: injected via the harmonic attention module. These are first
L107@P1: processed by a convolutional–residual encoder that extracts
L108@P1: local time-frequency patterns. A prosody-guided harmonic
L109@P1: attention module then refines the representation,
L110@P1: emphasizing voiced regions and harmonic structure while
L111@P1: leaving unvoiced parts unaffected. The decoder operates
L112@P1: directly in the complex spectral domain, predicting real and
L113@P1: imaginary components. Unlike mel-spectrogramconditioned vocoders, which discard phase information and
L114@P1: reconstruct waveforms indirectly, the proposed approach
L115@P1: preserves phase coherence and improves pitch accuracy.
L116@P1: The predicted spectrum is converted into waveform by
L117@P1: inverse short-time Fourier transform (ISTFT). Training is
L118@P1: guided by a multi-objective perceptual loss that combines
L119@P1: adversarial, spectral, and phase-aware criteria. Together,
L120@P1: these components create a unified alternative to mel-based
L121@P1: models such as HiFi-GAN and representation-driven
L122@P1: systems like AutoVocoder.
L123@P1: 2.1. Prosody-Guided Harmonic Attention
L124@P1: A central novelty of the proposed vocoder is the integration
L125@P1: of prosody-guided harmonic attention, which explicitly
L126@P1: conditions spectral modeling on the F0. While prior neural
L127@P1: vocoders often rely on mel-spectrograms or learned
L128@P1: representations, they treat prosody as an auxiliary feature
L129@P1: and fail to enforce harmonic alignment in voiced segments.
L130@P1: This often leads to pitch drift, blurred harmonics, and
L131@P1: reduced expressiveness in synthetic speech.
L132@P1: In our approach, F0 is extracted from the reference
L133@P1: waveform using the Harvest algorithm [20]. The extracted
L134@P1: F0 contour is then embedded into a continuous
L135@P1: representation and injected into the encoder-decoder
L136@P1: pathway through a harmonic attention mechanism.

L180@P2: 2.3. Multi-Objective Perceptual Optimization
L181@P2: To align training with human perceptual quality, the
L182@P2: proposed vocoder is optimized with a multi-objective loss
L183@P2: that combines established spectral constraints with a novel
L184@P2: phase-aware term.
L185@P2: Multi-resolution STFT loss: As a widely adopted baseline
L186@P2: in neural vocoding [2], [4], the multi-resolution short-time
L187@P2: Fourier transform (MR-STFT) loss is included to provide
L188@P2: stable spectral supervision. It evaluates reconstruction
L189@P2: fidelity across multiple analysis windows, capturing both
L190@P2: fine-grained harmonic structure and broader spectral
L191@P2: envelopes. While this objective has proven effective for
L192@P2: improving spectral sharpness, it operates purely on
L193@P2: magnitude and does not explicitly penalize phase
L194@P2: mismatches, which can still lead to temporal smearing and
L195@P2: reduced pitch clarity. In our framework, MR-STFT serves as
L196@P2: a complementary constraint, while phase coherence is
L197@P2: directly enforced through the proposed phase-aware loss.
L198@P2: Adversarial loss: To enhance perceptual realism, we adopt
L199@P2: a lightweight adversarial setup inspired by HiFi-GAN,
L200@P2: combining multi-period and multi-resolution discriminators.
L201@P2: While the structure follows prior work, in our framework
L202@P2: these discriminators operate directly on waveforms
L203@P2: reconstructed from the predicted complex spectrum,
L204@P2: ensuring that both magnitude and phase modeling benefit
L205@P2: from perceptual supervision. This design complements the
L206@P2: spectral and phase-aware losses, sharpening harmonic
L207@P2: details without overshadowing the novel contributions of
L208@P2: prosody-guided attention and direct complex-spectrum
L209@P2: prediction.

L226@P2: 3. EXPERIMENTS
L227@P2: 3.1. Datasets and Setup
L228@P2: Experiments were conducted on two standard corpora. The
L229@P2: LJSpeech 1.1 dataset [21] contains 13,100 utterances
L230@P2: (approximately 24 hours) of a single female English speaker
L231@P2: at 22.05 kHz. For speaker generalization, we additionally
L232@P2: used the VCTK corpus [22], which includes 109 English
L233@P2: speakers with diverse accents, downsampled to 22.05 kHz.
L234@P2: Waveforms were transformed into the spectral domain using
L235@P2: a 1024-point FFT, Hann window of size 1024, and hop size
L236@P2: of 256. Fundamental frequency was extracted with the
L237@P2: Harvest algorithm [19], aligned to the STFT frame rate. We
L238@P2: compared six systems: (a) Original (ground truth), (b)
L239@P2: Anchor (Griffin–Lim reconstruction), (c) HiFi-GAN [2], (d)
L240@P2: AutoVocoder [12], (e) Vocos [10], and (f) Proposed. All
L241@P2: models were trained with identical preprocessing for
L242@P2: fairness, using AdamW optimizer with an initial learning
L243@P2: rate of 2×10−4, batch size of 16, and weight decay of 0.01
L244@P2: (𝛽1 = 0.8, 𝛽2 = 0.99). Experiments were run on a single
L245@P2: NVIDIA GPU.

L277@P3: a similar trend, which supports the claim that direct
L278@P3: complex-spectrum prediction helps retain sharper harmonics

L332@P3: 5. CONCLUSION
L333@P3: This work introduced a prosody-guided and phase-coherent
L334@P3: neural vocoder that integrates harmonic attention with direct
L335@P3: complex-spectrum prediction. By conditioning on F0
L336@P3: through harmonic attention and predicting both real and
L337@P3: imaginary spectral components, the model effectively
L338@P3: bridges the long-standing gap between prosody modeling
L339@P3: and phase reconstruction. A multi-objective perceptual loss,
L340@P3: combining MR-STFT, adversarial, and phase-aware terms,
L341@P3: further improves naturalness and robustness. Experiments
L342@P3: on LJSpeech and VCTK confirm consistent improvements
L343@P3: over strong baselines such as HiFi-GAN, AutoVocoder, and
L344@P3: Vocos, with reductions in F0 RMSE and V/UV error, and
L345@P3: higher MOS ratings. Analysis of residual mel energy further
L346@P3: supports the model’s ability to closely follow reference
L347@P3: harmonics and preserve temporal coherence.
L348@P3: Future directions include extending the framework to
L349@P3: multilingual and expressive speech, integrating semantic or
L350@P3: linguistic cues for richer prosody control, and adapting the
L351@P3: system for real-time deployment. These extensions would
L352@P3: broaden the applicability of the proposed architecture to
L353@P3-4: scenarios such as emotional TTS, voice cloning, and brainto-speech decoding.6. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
