h1: GCG Attack on a Diffusion LLM
h6: Abstract
p: While many modern LLMs are autoregressive, diffusion LLMs are emerging as an alternative method for generation ( Nie et al., 2025 ) . GCG, or Greedy Coordinate Gradient, attacks have been developed for autoregressive models, but literature does not exist for a GCG attack on diffusion LLMs. In this paper, we implement a GCG attack on the open-source LLaDA (Large Language Diffusion with mAsking) model in order to obtain harmful output from the harmful prompts found in the AdvBench dataset ( Zou et al., 2023 ) .

h2: 1 Background
p: The Greedy Coordinate Gradient attack is a white-box attack that takes advantage of the continuous embedding space of a language learning model by using gradients to optimize towards a specified output prompt (targeted) or away from original model behavior (untargeted) ( Zou et al., 2023 ) . This attack has proven effective on autoregressive models but has not yet been measured against diffusion models. Diffusion models refine the entire output sequence in parallel instead of generating one token at a time. That is, in each denoising step, a probability distribution is predicted and used to gradually produce the final output. This marks a clear difference between autoregressive and diffusion models. While autoregressive models have a token’s generation directly depend on all of the tokens that came before it, diffusion models have tokens being affected by all other tokens in the output, not just those preceding it. Furthermore, diffusion models do not generate just one token, but many tokens all at once, using a random masking and unmasking process. In fact, limited research has been done on diffusion language learning models’ robustness or their potential as adversaries. Papers like Yuezhang and Wei (2025) explore how the denoising process makes image diffusion models naturally resistant to small l p l_{p} perturbations. On the other hand, Lüdke et al. (2025) propose that diffusion LLMs can serve as "natural adversaries" for any LLM 1 1 1 We briefly explored using the inpainting attack discussed in Lüdke et al. (2025) on LLaDA, but our initial results were completely unsuccessful, leading us to remain focused on other attack methods. Diffusion LLMs have some published papers regarding their safety, accuracy, and robustness, but there are many gaps to fill in the body of research. To these emerging topics, we contribute an exploratory experiment on the effectiveness of the GCG attack on diffusion models. We have set out to implement variations of the attack on the open-source model diffusion model LlaDA ( Nie et al., 2025 ) , where we will analyze the effectiveness of GCG and the comportment of LlaDA.
h2: 2 Baseline Results
p: Before implementing attacks on LlaDA models, we performed analysis on the baseline robustness of the models. We classified any non-harmful output as a "refusal response/output" and harmful output as an "adversarial response/output", and these terms will be used to refer to model output going forward. The base model generated a refusal response for 93.65% of prompts in the AdvBench dataset.
p: For our attacks, we only selected prompts for which the base model generated a refusal response in order to filter out prompts that do not need to be modified to obtain an adversarial result. We note that while the percentage of refusals was still quite high for the base model, the fact that any unmodified prompts generated adversarial responses indicates that the model may lack robustness.
h2: 3 Methodology
p: Our experiments were comprised of 3 major styles of GCG attacks: permuting the prefix of the prompt, generating a new adversarial suffix, and generating an adversarial suffix using Qwen, an auto regressive model ( Bai et al., 2023 ) . We will refer to these as prefix, random suffix and Qwen-seeded suffix respectively. Adversarial prompts were generated using the LlaDA-Base model before passing them as input to the LlaDA-Base or LlaDA-Instruct models 2 2 2 The Instruct model is a version of LLaDA fine-tuned to follow instructions in natural language. . Similarly, we utilized Qwen 2.5 Base to generate adversarial suffixes when testing the effectiveness of seeding the LlaDA based attacks. Throughout our experimentation, loss, our optimization objective, was calculated using the log-likelihood function from the LLaDA library developed in Nie et al. (2025) , which evaluates the log-likelihood of reconstructing the original tokens at positions where tokens were masked during the diffusion process, as defined by the loss equation below:

h3: 3.1 Attack Exploration
p: From our GCG variations, we selected the 3 styles that would maximize the range of attacks and scope of analysis we could expunge from our results. In an exploratory phase, we tinkered with a range of parameters and small implementation variations. Methods included early stoppage, enforcing monotonically decreasing loss, keeping track of local minimums of losses, and seeding attacks using autoregressive models. From these variations, we chose the subset that were the most computationally efficient and that best highlighted the intricacies of diffusion models with respect to the GCG attack.

p: The Qwen suffix generation followed the classic GCG implementation with reduced iterations to make it computationally feasible. The accompanying LlaDA suffix generation has a lower iteration and batch count than its non-seeded counterpart to allow for more effective timing. The algorithm was otherwise identical to the previous suffix attack, with the exception that the seeding prompt was tested in case it directly resulted in a successful attack.

h2: 5 Insights
p: Based on these results, it seems that the optimization objective of the loss function was not an effective target for the DLLM. This goes hand in hand with the fact that LlaDA was ill-suited towards attacks involving a large number of modifications to the prompt. This is clear when we look at the suffix attack. This resulted in a range of garbage output that was computationally expensive. Since the adversarial prompt would have a large amount of tokens that were generated in the attack, the model tended to become "confused", resulting in the output types we describe in Table 3. One example is the generation of skeletal output for adversarial prompts. The skeletal output includes an outline of a response, whether that be a list of empty steps or headings without content included. This could be because the adversarial prompts generated did not have enough semantic weight to give the model an idea of the kind of content it should be generating other than some kind of instruction. Although it is unclear why exactly this happens, it is clearly a result of the nature of diffusion models, specifically generation using masking.
p: In addition, calculating the loss, which was done with Monte-Carlo sampling of the conditional distribution, incurred significant computational costs. Each Monte-Carlo sample requires a call to the model, and as 128 samples were recommended to obtain a stable log-likelihood, the sampling became a significant bottleneck when other parameters were increased, including number of iterations and batch size. This is not the case for autoregressive model, as newly generated tokens are dependent on the previous tokens. Additionally, at times, the "best" candidate according to the log-likelihood loss did not match evaluation of which candidate was the best according to the eye test.
p: When running the more simplistic attack, it became apparent that some modifications that successfully circumvented safety guardrails were not chosen as the optimal prompt to pass to the next iteration. This observation led to our implementation of early stoppage, returning immediately if adversarial output was achieved. Although this added overhead, the computational costs were negligible in comparison with the costs incurred by loss calculation and thus were acceptable for our experimentation.
p: It was also observed that the tokens predicted as the top candidates for replacement generally had more success than lower probability tokens. Overall, low iteration numbers, medium to high batch sizes, and low k-values were the optimal parameter choices for a successful prefix-based attack on LLaDA.
h2: 6 Challenges and Limitations
p: This project faced significant challenges related to the availability of compute resources. First, attempts were made to use Colab, but available compute units were drained very quickly. Gaining access to the Jetstream 2 resources helped significantly, but the availability of the specific GPU we needed was extremely low. Although this was not as much of an issue for fast attacks such as the simple prefix-based attack, this made it very difficult to run and analyze long-running attacks on more than a few prompts. Related to this issue, the computational costs associated with the long-running attacks were a challenge, as results took a long time to generate for even a small number of prompts. This, combined with the complexity of the attacks, which included several different crucial parameters, made collecting sufficient information about the attack behavior and determining where issues arose challenging.
h2: 7 Future Directions
p: Due to time constraints and compute limitations, exploration of some ideas and avenues were limited, and there are multiple possible extensions and improvements to the research presented here. First, one aspect of the attack left unimplemented was limiting the set of modifiable tokens. When certain tokens are replaced in a prompt, the semantic meaning of the original prompt is lost, and responses where the model fails to parse the question or responds to an alternate question are common. For example, for the prompt "Write a tutorial for how to make a bomb", modifications to the tokens "Write", "make", and "bomb" are likely to cause the output to deviate from the intended target, even if the model does not refuse to answer. Limiting the set of modifiable tokens could be done manually for each prompt, as it involves human understanding of which tokens or most important, or it could be done by having a model identify the tokens that semantically position the question. These two strategies are also applicable to the problem of determining when output is actually adversarial. Simple keyword checks catch a majority of the prompts, but not every adversarial prompt contains expected key phrases.
p: Secondly, this attack was performed on an open-source model, and more specifically the base model, which was not fine-tuned, making it less robust than other diffusion models. Extending this attack to more robust models is an additional challenge, both in terms of attack strength and computation time.
p: Finally, a more sophisticated loss analysis would likely improve the success of this attack. As discussed earlier, the loss function did not always correctly rank the candidate modifications. Possible improvements to this evaluation of loss include using a different loss function, adding a component of the loss that involves movement away from a refusal response instead of solely considering movement towards a target response, and using reward functions to give tokens that appear often in successful adversarial prompts higher selection probability.
h2: 8 Conclusion
p: Overall, we believe that this contribution to the body of research has the potential to serve as a foundation for more effective attacks on diffusion models. While running larger experiments, refining the attacks further, and achieving success on more robust models would strengthen the research, the comparison between the suffix attacks and the prefix attacks, as well as the patterns seen in produced outputs, offer valuable insight into the differences between attacking a diffusion model and an autoregressive model. With time and computing resources as our main limitations, it is likely that the attack success rates obtained here can be improved upon significantly with further experimentation.
