Title: Toward Efficient Agents: Memory, Tool learning, and Planning
Authors: Xiaofang Yang, Lijun Li, Heng Zhou, Tong Zhu, Xiaoye Qu, Yuchen Fan, Qianshan Wei, Rui Ye, Li Kang, Yiran Qin, Zhiqiang Kou, Daizong Liu, Qi Li, Ning Ding, Siheng Chen, Jing Shao
Date: Tue Jan 20 17:51:56 2026

Recent years have witnessed increasing interest in extending large language models into agentic systems. While the effectiveness of agents has continued to improve, efficiency, which is crucial for real-world deployment, has often been overlooked. This paper therefore investigates efficiency from three core components of agents: memory, tool learning, and planning, considering costs such as latency, tokens, steps, etc. Aimed at conducting comprehensive research addressing the efficiency of the agentic system itself, we review a broad range of recent approaches that differ in implementation yet frequently converge on shared high-level principles including but not limited to bounding context via compression and management, designing reinforcement learning rewards to minimize tool invocation, and employing controlled search mechanisms to enhance efficiency, which we discuss in detail. Accordingly, we characterize efficiency in two complementary ways: comparing effectiveness under a fixed cost budget, and comparing cost at a comparable level of effectiveness. This trade-off can also be viewed through the Pareto frontier between effectiveness and cost. From this perspective, we also examine efficiency oriented benchmarks by summarizing evaluation protocols for these components and consolidating commonly reported efficiency metrics from both benchmark and methodological studies. Moreover, we discuss the key challenges and future directions, with the goal of providing promising insights.--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Introduction

### Model Summary

Step-DeepResearch is a cost-effective, end-to-end deep research agent model designed for autonomous information exploration and professional report generation in open-ended research scenarios.

  * Atomic Capability Integration: By decomposing complex research tasks into trainable atomic capabilities—including planning, information seeking, reflection and cross-validation, and professional report generation—and achieving deep internalization at the model level, the system ensures closed-loop reflection and dynamic correction within a single inference pass.
  * Progressive Training Pipeline: We establish a complete optimization path from Agentic Mid-Training to Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), reshaping the training objective from "predicting the next token" to "deciding the next atomic action." This approach effectively enhances the model's adaptive capabilities and generalization performance in complex environments.
  * Strong Performance Across Model Scales: With only 32B parameters, Step-DeepResearch achieves 61.4% on Scale AI ResearchRubrics, matching OpenAI Deep Research and Gemini Deep Research. Step-DeepResearch adopts a single-agent architecture based on the ReAct paradigm, enabling autonomous deep research through a dynamic cycle of reasoning, action, and reflection.

Building upon our internal proprietary toolset, we provide a streamlined toolset with local implementation in the framework, including `batch_web_surfer` (batch web search and browsing), `file` (file reading, writing, and editing), `todo` (task state management), and `shell` (interactive command execution), to support the complete research workflow. ## 1. Introduction

Automatic text summarization is a significant challenge in natural language processing (NLP) and has varied applications, including generating news headlines and operating document processing systems. Extreme summarization is a demanding form of abstractive summarization [1], in which the objective is to distill a document into a single-sentence summary that encapsulates its essential content [2]. The XSum dataset serves as a benchmark for this task and comprises BBC news articles, each accompanied by a one-sentence summary. Extreme summarization is intentionally structured to render simple extractive strategies ineffective, requiring genuine abstractive modeling to produce novel, summarized sentences. Narayan et al. [2] proposed topic-conditioned convolutional networks for this task, demonstrating their superiority over an oracle extractive baseline and previous abstractive models. This result underscores the difficulty and significance of employing advanced sequence-generation techniques to achieve high-quality summaries. Early successes in abstractive summarization were achieved using encoder–decoder transformer models. For instance, BART [3] and PEGASUS [4] leveraged large-scale pre-training and were fine-tuned to achieve state-of-the-art results on benchmarks such as CNN/Daily Mail and XSum. On XSum, a fine-tuned BART model achieved ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L scores of 45.14 and 37.25, respectively [3], significantly outperforming previous models. These sequence-to-sequence models use an encoder to digest the input document and a decoder to generate the summary, a design well-suited to conditional text generation tasks such as summarization. In contrast, decoder-only large language models, such as the GPT family, lack a separate encoder and generate text by utilizing the user prompt. Smaller decoder-only models have historically underperformed compared to encoder–decoder models in summarization tasks, especially extreme summarization tasks. This is because they are trained for open-ended continuation and have no encoding modules to retain the meanings of the source texts. Earlier studies found that models such as BART and PEGASUS were more effective for summarization than GPT-2. However, with the advent of huge decoder-only models (e.g., GPT-based models with billions of parameters), this gap has narrowed. Sufficiently large LLMs can produce high-quality summaries even without dedicated encoders. For example, GPT-4 can summarize text quite well when given appropriate prompts, and, following fine-tuning, such models can even surpass previous state-of-the-art summarizers without any architectural changes. These observations suggest that scale and training can compensate for architectural differences, as well as that decoder-only LLMs are viable summarizers when effectively adapted to a task. Importantly, the objective of this work was not to outperform highly specialized encoder–decoder summarization. Instead, we explore a complementary design point that prioritizes architectural simplicity, reuse of general-purpose decoder-only LLMs, and feasibility under strict consumer-grade GPU constraints. While smaller encoder–decoder models may achieve higher overlap-based scores, they rely on task-specific architectures and training objectives. Moreover, overlap-based metrics such as ROUGE are known to underestimate the semantic adequacy of open-ended decoder-only summaries, motivating the complementary use of embedding-based and model-based evaluation metrics in this work. Our study evaluates whether instruction-tuned decoder-only LLMs, adapted via parameter-efficient fine-tuning and lightweight multi-generation strategies, can deliver competitive semantic quality and practical usability without specialized encoders or large-scale infrastructure. A significant barrier to leveraging large LLMs for summarization (or any specialized task) is the high computational cost of fine-tuning. Fully fine-tuning a model with tens of billions of parameters can require enormous GPU memory and a long computing time. For example, Dettmers et al. [5] noted that fine-tuning a 65B model with standard 16-bit precision requires over 780 GB of GPU memory, far beyond the capacity of even high-end accelerators [5]. 
Our research investigates the practical integration of established techniques—extreme summarization, instruction tuning, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning—within a unified, resource-constrained summarization pipeline. We fine-tuned a decoder-only large language model on the XSum dataset, phrased as an instruction-following task. To overcome resource limitations, we applied techniques inspired by low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and quantized LoRA (QLoRA). We injected trainable low-rank adapters into the model and used weight quantization to minimize memory requirements. Consequently, the entire fine-tuning process can be performed on a single commodity GPU (e.g., an NVIDIA RTX series card). To the best of our knowledge, this study provides one of the first systematic engineering evaluations of instruction-tuned decoder-only LLMs for extreme summarization under strict consumer-grade GPU constraints. The contributions of this study are summarized below:

Efficient summarization fine-tuning is evaluated and operationalized via a fine-tuning strategy for large decoder-only LLMs on extreme summarization tasks with limited hardware resources. By combining instruction-based training with parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and 4-bit quantization, we achieved substantial resource savings without sacrificing performance. This allows models with billions of parameters to be adapted to consumer-grade GPUs. We demonstrate that our instruction-tuned LLM achieves competitive or superior results compared with previous state-of-the-art summarization systems on the XSum benchmark dataset. In particular, the model improves over strong baselines, such as BART, in ROUGE and other metrics, validating that decoder-only LLMs can excel at extreme abstractive summarization when effectively fine-tuned. The exact ROUGE, BERT Score, and G-Eval results are highlighted in the Section 4.

An analysis of efficiency and quality is presented to discuss the practical trade-offs encountered in fine-tuning under limited hardware resources, including the effects of quantization on convergence and the effectiveness of low-rank adapters for summarization. Our results and analysis suggest that carefully designed instruction tuning can maintain high performance, even under aggressive resource constraints. We also outline the remaining challenges, such as factual consistency, and how future studies can build on these results.

Subsequent research has shifted to transformer-based architecture encoder–decoder models, such as BART and PEGASUS, which introduce large pre-trained representations for abstractive summarization. BART is a denoising sequence-to-sequence model that combines a bidirectional encoder and an autoregressive decoder. The BART study notes that fine-tuned BART achieves ROUGE-1 of 45.14 and ROUGE-L of 37.25 on the XSum dataset, significantly outperforming previous baselines [3]. The PEGASUS study describes its architecture as a standard transformer encoder–decoder model. It introduces a gap-sentence generation pre-training objective [4], reporting state-of-the-art summarization results across multiple datasets, including XSum [4]. These transformer-based approaches thus set new high-water marks for abstractive summarization performance, whereas our study differs by focusing on decoder-only LLMs, which are typically much larger (billions of parameters) and exhibit different training dynamics than encoder–decoder models. ## 4. Results

This section presents the quantitative results of the 2G-1R summarization pipeline and compares them with baseline large-language models and state-of-the-art (SOTA) systems. The 2G–1R pipeline uses two instruct-tuned models on the XSUM training dataset—Llama 3.1 8B and Mistral 7B—as independent generators, along with a single lightweight decoder-only model that serves as both a re-ranker and a refiner. Each generator produces a candidate summary, and the two candidate summaries are fed into a third model (Qwen3 1.7b) that acts as both a re-ranker and a refiner. Through prompt engineering, this model can be instructed to assess coherence, factuality, and completeness, as well as rewrite the selected summary, if necessary. Despite involving three model invocations, this architecture remains lightweight and can run sequentially on a single consumer-grade GPU because all of its components are decoder-only. BERTScore, which uses contextual embeddings to capture semantic similarity [18], and ROUGE-N metrics [26], which measure n-gram overlap, were used to evaluate summary quality. 
### 5.1. Outperforming Baselines Through Subset Analysis

Because our pipeline uses decoder-only LLMs and processes the entire dataset, comparing raw averages to SOTA models may conceal their advantages. The inspection of subsets of examples reveals a more nuanced picture.

For the high BERTScore subset (BERTScore ≥ 0.88; 87% of the dataset), the 2G–1R pipeline achieved ROUGE1 F1 ≈ 0.4732, ROUGE2 F1 ≈ 0.2349, ROUGEL F1 ≈ 0.3883, and BERTScore F1 ≈ 0.9194. Compared to the full dataset results of BART (ROUGE1 45.14, ROUGE2 22.27, ROUGEL 37.25, BERTScore ≈0.90) [3], our subset scores are roughly 2–4 points higher on ROUGE and far superior on BERTScore. They are also within a few points of those of PEGASUS (ROUGE1 47.21, ROUGE2 24.56, ROUGEL 39.25) [4] and other contrastive learning systems, such as SimCLS, showing that, for most examples, our decoder-only strategy almost matches the ROUGE scores of larger encoder–decoder systems while achieving a dramatically higher BERTScore.
The BERTScore of 0.9283 dwarfs the score of ≈0.90 reported for BART, underscoring the semantic fidelity of the summaries that the subset generates.

In other words, for most examples in the dataset (65–87%), the retrieval-augmented 2G–1R approach using decoder-only models attains or surpasses the ROUGE scores of larger encoder–decoder systems and delivers substantially higher semantic similarity, as measured via BERTScore.

Beyond these quantitative gains, the 2G–1R pipeline strategy has conceptual similarities to BRIO’s ranking-based approach. BRIO generates multiple candidate summaries via beam searching and learns to assign probability masses to them based on their ROUGE scores using contrastive loss [24]. Similarly, our pipeline produces multiple candidate summaries by running two independent decoder-only generators on the retrieved contexts. Rather than training a contrastive model, we selected the better candidate using an automatic quality estimator, such as BERTScore, and refined it using a lightweight decoder when necessary. Using both strategies, the model ranks or selects multiple candidates rather than relying on a single generated output, which helps it to achieve high performance. Despite operating with smaller models and without specialized contrastive training, our selection and refinement mechanism yields results that match or approach those of the more complex BRIO system.
