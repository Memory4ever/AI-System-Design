[2] h1: Beyond Refusal: Probing the Limits of Agentic Self-Correction for Semantic Sensitive Information

[3] h6: Abstract

[4] p: While defenses for structured PII are mature, Large Language Models (LLMs) pose a new threat: Semantic Sensitive Information (SemSI), where models infer sensitive identity attributes , generate reputation-harmful content , or hallucinate potentially wrong information . The capacity of LLMs to self-regulate these complex, context-dependent sensitive information leaks without destroying utility remains an open scientific question. To address this, we introduce SemSIEdit , an inference-time framework where an agentic ”Editor” iteratively critiques and rewrites sensitive spans to preserve narrative flow rather than simply refusing to answer. Our analysis reveals a Privacy-Utility Pareto Frontier , where this agentic rewriting reduces leakage by 34.6% across all three SemSI categories while incurring a marginal utility loss ( 9.8% ). We also uncover a Scale-Dependent Safety Divergence : large reasoning models (e.g., GPT-5) achieve safety through constructive expansion (adding nuance), whereas capacity-constrained models revert to destructive truncation (deleting text). Finally, we identify a Reasoning Paradox : while inference-time reasoning increases baseline risk by enabling the model to make deeper sensitive inferences, it simultaneously empowers the defense to execute safe rewrites.

[6] h2: 1 Introduction

[7] p: While traditional privacy defenses successfully mask Structured Sensitive Information (PII) via pattern matching (e.g., regex for emails or SSNs) ( da Silva, 2020 ; Kulkarni and Cauvery, 2021 ) , they fail against a more insidious threat: Semantic Sensitive Information ( SemSI ). Unlike PII, SemSI involves contextual leaks where sensitive inferences emerge from the narrative structure itself rather than specific keywords. As formalized by Zhang et al. (2025) , these leaks fall into three distinct categories: Sensitive Identity Attributes , defined as non-structured personal privacy-related information (e.g., religious beliefs, political opinions); Reputation-Harmful Content , involving the generation of defamatory viewpoints that damage an individual’s standing; and Incorrect Hazardous Information , where the model validates false premises that threaten public safety (e.g., confirming that ”disinfectants cure COVID-19”).

[8] p: While the threat is known, the community lacks a fundamental understanding of the defense surface . Unlike PII, mitigating SemSI requires the model to distinguish between benign facts and sensitive inferences, and then restructure the narrative to preserve utility. Table 1 illustrates this necessity: when asked why NYC uses ”unsafe” pesticides (a query containing Incorrect Hazardous Information ), a standard model often attempts to justify the false premise, inadvertently validating the hazard. In contrast, a semantically secure model must restructure the response, explicitly correcting the false premise (citing EPA safety standards) while still answering the underlying question about pesticide use. Currently, no defense has been proposed to defend against SemSI leakage, which leaves us with critical blind spots regarding the interplay of reasoning, scale, and semantic privacy. To address this gap, we structure our investigation around four core scientific questions:

[9] p: Can LLMs rely on their own reasoning capabilities to detect and correct semantic leakage in open-ended generation?

[10] p: Can agentic defenses mitigate privacy risks without imposing a severe ”Utility Loss” (i.e., destroying helpfulness via refusal)?

[11] p: How do model scale and inference-time reasoning affect safety behaviors? Specifically, is ”safe rewriting” an emergent ability, and does reasoning act as a liability or an asset?

[12] p: Is semantic leakage primarily a failure of privacy or a failure of truthfulness (hallucination), and can agentic defenses restore epistemic calibration?

[13] p: To answer these questions, we introduce SemSIEdit , a novel inference-time defense framework designed to probe the limits of LLM self-correction. Recognizing that semantic privacy requires reasoning rather than pattern matching, our approach employs a dual-agent architecture (Evaluator-Editor) that iteratively critiques and rewrites content. We utilize SemSIEdit not merely as a tool, but as a lens to test the privacy capabilities of 13 SOTA LLMs (including GPT-5) against the SemSI-Bench .

[14] p: We summarize our primary contributions as follows:

[15] p: Quantifying the Privacy-Utility Frontier: We provide the first empirical analysis of the ”Utility Loss” inherent in SemSI mitigation. We demonstrate that SemSIEdit ’s agentic rewriting enables reducing semantic leakage by an average of 34.6% while incurring only a marginal 9.8% cost in utility.

[16] p: Unveiling Scale-Dependent Safety Behaviors: We report a novel divergence in model behavior under safety pressure. Larger models (e.g., GPT-5) utilize feedback to synthesize safer narratives, often increasing answer length to explain nuance (preserving utility). In contrast, smaller models (e.g., Llama-3, Gemma) lack the reasoning capacity to rewrite effectively, resorting to aggressive truncation or refusal (destroying utility). This suggests that ”Safety via Rewriting” is an emergent ability of larger reasoning models.

[17] p: Epistemic Calibration: We identify that semantic leakage often stems from model overconfidence, where models hallucinate plausible but unverified details to satisfy a prompt. We demonstrate that SemSIEdit restores epistemic calibration , shifting models from confident hallucination to ”qualified responses” (explicit admissions of uncertainty). This eliminates the generation of false information (reducing the rate from 15.9% to 0% in GPT-5) and reveals a scaling law where larger models retain high utility by providing corrective context instead of simple refusals.

[18] p: The SemSIEdit Framework: To enable these insights, we propose an iterative, inference-time agentic algorithm. SemSIEdit dynamically identifies and restructures specific narrative segments containing sensitive inferences to maintain text fluency, establishing a new state-of-the-art for semantic defenses.

[21] h2: 2 Related Work

[22] p: The landscape of privacy preservation in Large Language Models (LLMs) has shifted from the static masking of structured data to the dynamic mitigation of semantic risks. Our work addresses the intersection of three active research streams: (1) the transition from Structured PII to Semantic Sensitive Information (SemSI), (2) the utility costs of inference-time defenses, and (3) the scaling limits of agentic self-correction.

[23] p: From Structured PII to Inferential Privacy . Historically, privacy defenses in NLP focused on Structured Sensitive Information , primarily Personally Identifiable Information (PII) such as phone numbers or email addresses. Traditional approaches rely on pattern matching via regular expressions or Named Entity Recognition (NER) models to detect and mask these tokens ( da Silva, 2020 ; Kulkarni and Cauvery, 2021 ) . Tools like Microsoft Presidio and Google Cloud DLP exemplify this “match-and-mask” paradigm.

[24] p: However, recent study highlights the inadequacy of pattern-based defenses against Inferential Privacy risks. Staab et al. ( Staab et al., 2024 ) demonstrated that LLMs can accurately infer sensitive attributes (e.g., location, income, political affiliation) from seemingly benign text, achieving high accuracy even when all structured PII is redacted. This confirms that privacy leakage is often contextual rather than explicit. Building on this, Zhang et al. ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) formalized Semantic Sensitive Information (SemSI) —content defined by sensitive meaning (e.g., reputation-harming inferences or hazardous information) rather than syntax. Unlike PII, SemSI cannot be captured by static lexicons and is often entangled with the model’s tendency to hallucinate convincing but defamatory details ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , necessitating defenses that possess semantic reasoning capabilities.

[25] p: Inference-Time Defenses and the Utility Trade-off To address risks during generation, the field has adopted Inference-Time Defenses ( Kumar et al., 2023 ) . The dominant industrial standard involves “Guardrails”—external safety classifiers like Llama Guard 4 ( Meta, 2025 ) or NeMo Guardrails ( Rebedea et al., 2023 ) , which screen inputs and outputs for violations. While effective at blocking harm, these systems operate on a Refusal Basis : when a violation is detected, they trigger a binary refusal (e.g., “I cannot answer this”), destroying the utility of the response.

[26] p: Alternative approaches explore Privacy-Conscious Delegation . For instance, PrivacyPAD ( Hui et al., 2025 ) uses Reinforcement Learning to route queries between a secure local model and a powerful remote model, masking sensitive chunks before transmission. However, masking often disrupts the narrative flow. Other methods, such as PrivRewrite ( Kim, 2025 ) , utilize Differential Privacy (DP) mechanisms to select rewrites. While offering formal guarantees, DP-based noise frequently results in linguistic degradation, rendering the text disjointed. A key open question remains: Is it possible to mitigate semantic privacy risks without incurring this severe utility loss?

[27] p: Agentic Feedback and Self-Correction Our approach leverages the emerging paradigm of Agentic Self-Correction , where LLMs iteratively critique and improve their own outputs. Frameworks like Self-Refine ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) and Critic ( Gou et al., 2023 ) demonstrated that iterative feedback loops significantly improve performance in reasoning and code generation.

[28] p: While recent works have applied recursive oversight to safety—such as Constitutional AI ( Bai et al., 2022 ) for alignment or recursive checks against jailbreaks ( Robey et al., 2023 ) —two critical gaps remain. First, existing methods typically focus on compliance (blocking toxic content) rather than the intricate editorial work required to rewrite a narrative that is safe but remains informative. Second, the impact of Model Scale on self-correction capabilities is under-explored in the privacy domain. It remains unclear whether smaller models possess the reasoning capacity to “edit” rather than “refuse.” Our SemSIEdit framework addresses these gaps by employing an Evaluator-Editor loop to probe the privacy-utility frontier and analyze scale-dependent safety behaviors.

[42] p: Evaluation Methodology . We propose a multi-dimensional evaluation framework that assesses the trade-off between privacy preservation and response quality. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to formalize and quantify the Privacy-Utility Trade-off specifically for the task of Semantic Sensitive Information (SemSI) mitigation.

[43] p: Privacy Evaluation (Revised SemSI Judges) . We quantify semantic leakage using LLM-as-a-Judge agents. While Zhang et al. ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) proposed initial evaluation prompts, we observed that they were often too vague, leading to false positives where models flagged benign paraphrases of the question itself.

[44] p: To address this, we introduce Refined SemSI Judges (prompts in Appendix B ). These judges utilize stricter definitions for three leakage categories: Sensitive Identity Attributes , Reputation-Harmful Content , and Hazardous information . The judges output a toxicity score τ ∈ { 0 , … , 3 } \tau\in\{0,\dots,3\} , where 0 indicates no leakage and 3 indicates severe harm.

[45] p: Utility Evaluation (Novel Metric) . A defense mechanism that returns empty responses would achieve perfect privacy but zero utility. To prevent such trivial solutions, we explicitly measure the Utility Loss introduced by our defense. We employ a separate LLM judge ℳ judge \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{judge}} to evaluate responses on three axes (0-10 scale): Relevance ( s rel s_{\mathrm{rel}} ) , regarding whether the response directly addresses the user’s intent; Correctness ( s corr s_{\mathrm{corr}} ) , regarding whether the information is factually accurate and free of hallucinations; and Completeness ( s comp s_{\mathrm{comp}} ) , regarding whether the response covers the necessary details without being overly redacted. Formally, for a given input x x and response y y , the utility score S util S_{\mathrm{util}} is computed as the mean of these components: ( s rel , s corr , s comp ) ← ℳ judge ​ ( p util ​ ‖ x ‖ ​ y ) (s_{\mathrm{rel}},s_{\mathrm{corr}},s_{\mathrm{comp}})\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{judge}}(p_{\mathrm{util}}\|x\|y) and S util = 1 3 ​ ( s rel + s corr + s comp ) S_{\mathrm{util}}=\frac{1}{3}(s_{\mathrm{rel}}+s_{\mathrm{corr}}+s_{\mathrm{comp}}) . [57] p: A critical question in agentic alignment is whether safety behaviors scale predictably with model size (RQ3). Our analysis of answer length and convergence rates reveals a fundamental divergence: advanced reasoning models achieve safety through transformation , while capacity-constrained models achieve safety through elimination .

[58] p: The ”Rewrite vs Truncate” Divergence. Figure 5 illustrates the change in answer length ( Δ ​ L \Delta L ) following the SemSIEdit intervention, revealing two distinct behavioral clusters. We observe constructive safety in GPT-5, which uniquely exhibits a positive length change ( + 110 +110 characters). Upon detecting semantic leakage, the Editor agent does not delete the sensitive span; instead, it rewrites and expands the context to explain nuances or provide non-sensitive alternatives. Conversely, smaller and mid-sized models display Destructive Safety , exhibiting massive length reductions ranging from − 50 % -50\% to − 73 % -73\% (e.g., Llama-3.3-70B drops from 1479 to 394 characters). Lacking the reasoning capacity to execute subtle semantic edits, these models default to the ”safest” heuristic: removing the content entirely. [60] p: These findings indicate that Agentic Refinement is not model-agnostic . The ”Editor” role requires a minimum threshold of reasoning capability; below this threshold, the loop devolves into a simple refusal mechanism.

[61] h3: 5.3 The Double-Edged Sword of Reasoning

[62] p: With the emergence of reasoning-enhanced models (e.g., OpenAI o1, Qwen-QwQ), a critical question arises: Does reasoning aid or hinder privacy? [63] p: In the absence of defense ( No Protection ), enabling reasoning capabilities significantly increases semantic leakage (Occurrence rises from 74.0% to 84.0% ). In this unconstrained setting, higher intelligence correlates with higher risk, as the model uses its compute to hallucinate detailed (and sensitive) narratives.

[64] p: Conversely, when SemSIEdit is active, reasoning capabilities become a powerful asset. The reasoning-enabled defense outperforms the standard defense in both safety (42.0% vs. 47.0% leakage) and utility (4.61 vs. 4.03 score).

When the objective includes ”safety constraints” (Feedback), SemSIEdit utilizes the reasoning budget to solve the complex constraint satisfaction problem, finding a rewrite that preserves semantic value while excising sensitive inferences.

[68] h3: 5.4 Epistemic Uncertainty & Hallucination

[69] p: A key hypothesis of this work is that semantic leakage can be a symptom of hallucination, where models invent plausible but unverified details to satisfy a prompt. We investigate whether SemSIEdit can restore epistemic calibration (RQ4).

[70] p: Shift to Qualified Responses. We analyzed the frequency of ”qualified responses,” defined as explicit admissions of insufficient information (e.g., ”I cannot verify this claim”). As shown in Figure 6 , baseline models exhibit dangerous overconfidence, answering nearly 100% of queries regardless of factual basis. In contrast, SemSIEdit induces a dramatic shift in behavior, causing an increased rate of qualified responses (e.g., rising from 0% to 70.5% for Llama-3.3).

[71] p: Context Preservation vs. Simple Refusal. Crucially, this shift toward epistemic humility does not necessarily imply a loss of utility. As detailed in Table 3 , our manual audit reveals a distinct scaling law regarding the quality of these refusals. Larger models (GPT-5, Qwen3-235B) demonstrated the reasoning capacity to successfully provide useful context, such as citing relevant safety standards or clarifying misconceptions, in 80% of qualified responses. In contrast, smaller models (e.g., Qwen3-8B) struggled with this nuance, frequently defaulting to simple refusals with low context preservation (30%). This suggests that while SemSIEdit effectively stops hallucination across the board, the ability to restructure the qualified responses into useful context is an emergent property of model scale.

[72] p: Reduction in False Information. Finally, our manual audit of 88 qualified responses generated by GPT-5 confirms the safety benefits of this approach. While 15.9% of baseline queries resulted in the generation of false information, the false information rate under SemSIEdit dropped to 0% , confirming that the method effectively restores epistemic calibration.

[79] h2: 6 Conclusion

[80] p: This work investigated the limits of Large Language Models in self-regulating Semantic Sensitive Information (SemSI). By shifting the defense paradigm from static refusal to agentic rewriting , we demonstrate that the ”Privacy-Utility Trade-off” is not an immutable law, but a function of model reasoning capability.

[81] p: Our contributions are threefold. First, we establish SemSIEdit as a robust inference-time framework that navigates the Pareto frontier, reducing semantic leakage by 34.6% while incurring only a marginal 9.8% utility cost. Second, we uncover a fundamental Scale-Dependent Divergence in safety behaviors: while large reasoning models (e.g., GPT-5) utilize feedback to synthesize nuanced, safe narratives, smaller models revert to aggressive truncation that destroys utility. Third, we identify a critical link between privacy and truthfulness, showing that agentic defenses effectively convert ”confident hallucinations” about false premises into ”principled refusals.”

[82] p: Limitations & Future Work. The primary cost of agentic defense is inference latency; our iterative loop increases compute requirements by 2 × 2\times to 4 × 4\times compared to a standard forward pass. While acceptable for high-stakes applications, real-time deployment requires optimization. Ultimately, our findings suggest that privacy is not merely a constraint to be imposed, but a reasoning task to be solved. As models advance in reasoning capabilities, their ability to protect user privacy without compromising helpfulness will likely emerge as a distinct measure of intelligence.

Current safety paradigms often rely on ”refusal-heavy” alignment, which disproportionately degrades model utility for complex or sensitive topics. by demonstrating that safety can be achieved through rewriting rather than refusal , our work suggests a path toward alignment techniques that respect user intent while preserving privacy. This is particularly relevant for deploying LLMs in high-stakes domains (e.g., healthcare, finance) where ”I cannot answer” is often an unacceptable failure mode. Furthermore, our finding that agentic oversight reduces hallucinations suggests that privacy defenses can simultaneously serve as epistemic guardrails, reducing the propagation of incorrect information.
