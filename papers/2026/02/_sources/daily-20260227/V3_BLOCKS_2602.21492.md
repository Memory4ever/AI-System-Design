[2] h1: GradAlign: Gradient-Aligned Data Selection for LLM Reinforcement Learning

[3] h6: Abstract

[4] p: Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central post-training paradigm for large language models (LLMs), but its performance is highly sensitive to the quality of training problems. This sensitivity stems from the non-stationarity of RL: rollouts are generated by an evolving policy, and learning is shaped by exploration and reward feedback, unlike supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with fixed trajectories. As a result, prior work often relies on manual curation or simple heuristic filters (e.g., accuracy), which can admit incorrect or low-utility problems. We propose GradAlign , a gradient-aligned data selection method for LLM reinforcement learning that uses a small, trusted validation set to prioritize training problems whose policy gradients align with validation gradients, yielding an adaptive curriculum. We evaluate GradAlign across three challenging data regimes: unreliable reward signals, distribution imbalance, and low-utility training corpus, showing that GradAlign consistently outperforms existing baselines, underscoring the importance of directional gradient signals in navigating non-stationary policy optimization and yielding more stable training and improved final performance. We release our implementation at https://github.com/StigLidu/GradAlign .

[5] h6: Keywords:

[6] h2: 1 Introduction

[7] p: Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an important tool for enhancing the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) in complex tasks and environments ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2025b ) . However, the current success of LLM RL is critically dependent on high-quality human-labeled training corpora ( Hu et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2025 ) , which are expensive to obtain. To support large-scale RL training, researchers increasingly turn to massive, automatically collected corpora ( Yue et al., 2024 ) . These collections inevitably mix high-quality instances with trivial or misleading ones and noisy labels. This raises two key challenges: (1) how to identify instances that are suitable for a given downstream task, and (2) how to design mechanisms that prevent low-quality or misleading data from corrupting the RL training signal.

[8] p: Existing RL data selection strategies for LLM training are relatively simple and primarily rely on rule-based heuristics. For example, Yu et al. (2025) retains problems with intermediate accuracy to alleviate gradient vanishing, whereas Sun et al. (2025c) prioritizes samples with accuracy near 50%, analogous to selecting samples near the decision boundary in active learning. Although such partially solved problems can be informative, these methods rely on scalar difficulty signals and do not consider whether the resulting updates move the policy toward improved downstream performance. Other approaches score training problems using gradient-based criteria, often emphasizing large parameter updates ( Li et al., 2025b ) , internal consistency within the training distribution ( Li et al., 2025a ) , or gradient diversity ( Liang et al., 2025 ) . However, these strategies do not explicitly verify alignment with downstream optimization objectives and are typically static, failing to adapt the selection criterion as the policy evolves during RL training.

[9] p: In this work, we propose GradAlign , an automated gradient-informed algorithm to select training problems for LLM reinforcement learning. GradAlign leverages a small, trusted validation set and uses the policy gradient as a first-order surrogate to approximate the expected improvement in validation performance induced by policy updates. For each candidate training problem, we compute an alignment score defined as the cosine similarity between its policy gradient and the aggregated validation gradient. Training problems are then ranked by this alignment score, favoring updates that are most consistent with improving validation performance.

[10] p: To address RL non-stationarity, GradAlign adopts an adaptive curriculum that periodically recomputes validation gradients and re-scores candidate problems under the current policy, using the validation set as a directional probe rather than a direct scoring or retrieval signal. GradAlign is particularly effective in challenging data regimes common in large-scale LLM RL, including unreliable reward signals ( Skalse et al., 2022 ) , distribution mismatch between training and downstream problems ( Kirk et al., 2023 ) , and datasets with low-utility training instances ( Dodge et al., 2021 ) . In these settings, scalar accuracy-based heuristics often fail to distinguish useful learning signals from misleading ones, whereas GradAlign consistently prioritizes updates that improve downstream performance.

[11] p: In summary, our contributions include the following.

[12] p: We propose GradAlign , an online RL data-selection method that selects training samples whose updates are aligned with downstream performance improvement.

[13] p: We identify three challenging data regimes for RL data selection and conduct targeted experiments showing that GradAlign consistently outperforms prior baselines in these settings.

[14] h2: 2 Related Work

[15] h3: 2.1 Data Selection for LLMs

[16] p: Data selection has emerged as a critical lever for enhancing the efficiency and performance of LLMs ( Koh and Liang, 2017 ) . Early approaches largely relied on static heuristics to filter low-quality web text, utilizing metrics such as perplexity thresholds, language identification, deduplication, and rule-based filters ( Penedo et al., 2023 ; Raffel et al., 2019 ; Abbas et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2023b ; Lin et al., 2024 ) . Beyond filtering, optimizing the mixture of data sources has been standard practice to balance general capabilities with specific domain knowledge ( Xie et al., 2023a ; Ye et al., 2024 ) . More recent strategies employ diversity-based sampling ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) or proxy models to score data quality ( Wettig et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) . However, these methods predominantly rely on human intuition or general-purpose heuristics rather than a principled evaluation of how specific data points contribute to the model’s learning trajectory, often leading to suboptimal alignment with downstream tasks ( Xia et al., 2024 ) .

[17] h3: 2.2 Influence Estimation and Gradient Alignment

[18] p: To transcend heuristic selection, researchers have adopted Influence Functions ( Koh et al., 2019 ; Grosse et al., 2023 ) to theoretically quantify the impact of a training instance on validation loss. In the context of LLMs, gradient-based methods have been successfully adapted for data selection tasks during pre-training and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) ( Engstrom et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) . Specifically, gradient alignment techniques—which select training samples whose gradient updates maximize the cosine similarity with a trusted validation set—have proven effective for instruction tuning ( Zhang et al., 2024 ; Xia et al., 2024 ; Yu et al., 2024 ; Sun et al., 2025a ) . Despite these advances, the application of gradient-based selection is largely confined to imitation learning paradigms (SFT). The efficacy of such influence estimation in RL, where the model learns from self-generated rollouts and sparse rewards rather than ground-truth targets, remains significantly understudied.

[19] h3: 2.3 LLM Reinforcement Learning

[20] p: Reinforcement Learning (RL) is increasingly central to unlocking advanced LLM capabilities, particularly for complex reasoning ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) and autonomous agentic tasks ( Du et al., 2025 ) . While standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) remains a cornerstone, recent advances such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ( Shao et al., 2024 ) and the reasoning-focused DeepSeek-R1 ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ) demonstrate that RL can substantially outperform supervised baselines. However, RL algorithms are notoriously sensitive to data quality and reward noise; for example, training on trivial or unsolvable problems can induce instability or reward hacking ( Hu et al., 2025 ; Gao et al., 2022 ; Lightman et al., 2023 ) . As a result, although considerable effort has been devoted to selecting valuable data using heuristic signals such as gradients or parameter distributions ( Li et al., 2025b ; Li et al., 2025a ; Liang et al., 2025 ) , the curriculum of which problems to present to the agent, and when , is typically treated as static. This overlooks the potential of dynamic, quality-aware data selection to stabilize and accelerate RL training. In this work, we propose gradient alignment as a principled approach for dynamically selecting high-quality data during the RL process.

[22] h2: 3 Problem Formulation

[23] p: We study an online reinforcement learning (RL) setting in which, at each data selection round , the learner is presented with a large pool of candidate training problems that may be noisy, unlabeled, or even incorrect. The learner selects a subset of these problems for training, aiming to filter out low-quality candidates and maximize performance on a downstream evaluation task.

[25] p: Because the candidate pool may be highly noisy, naively training on all candidates can be inefficient and yield limited gains. We evaluate performance on a downstream benchmark split into a validation set 𝒫 v \mathcal{P}_{v} and a held-out test set 𝒫 t \mathcal{P}_{t} , where 𝒫 v \mathcal{P}_{v} contains a limited number of representative or i.i.d. examples of the downstream task. Having a validation set is common in real-world tasks, such as using few-shot examples in in-context learning or public test cases in code generation tasks.

[37] h2: 4 GradAlign

[38] p: We propose GradAlign , an online data selection method for reinforcement learning. In realistic RL settings, training data is abundant but highly heterogeneous, and reward signals can be noisy or uninformative to learning. Accuracy-based filtering methods are commonly used in prior work ( Yu et al., 2025 ) , based on the intuition that LLMs learn most from problems that are neither too easy nor too hard and lie near the decision boundary. However, accuracy alone does not distinguish which problems induce beneficial policy updates. GradAlign addresses this limitation by selecting high-quality samples through gradient alignment.

[39] p: Our key insight is that a training problem is valuable only if updating the policy on it improves performance on a trustworthy validation set representing the downstream task. We approximate this improvement using first-order policy gradients: GradAlign treats the average policy gradient on the validation set as a target direction and ranks candidate problems by their alignment with this direction. As illustrated in Figure 1 , GradAlign operates iteratively by estimating validation gradients, selecting aligned training problems, and updating the policy, while resampling rollouts in each round to adapt to the evolving policy.

[40] h3: 4.1 Validation Improvement as a Proxy

[41] p: Unlike LLM fine-tuning, where detailed solutions are provided in training data, in RL settings, we only observe reward signals. This makes it difficult to estimate the utility of individual training problems directly. We therefore use validation improvement as a proxy for problem utility: a training problem is useful if updating the policy on it improves performance on the validation set 𝒫 v \mathcal{P}_{v} , which is more likely to transfer to the held-out test set. A direct approach would be to perform RL updates on each candidate problem and measure the resulting change on 𝒫 v \mathcal{P}_{v} , but this is computationally prohibitive.

[69] h3: 4.4 On-Policy Resampling

[70] p: Because the policy evolves during RL training, both the expected validation accuracy and the validation GRPO gradients change over time. To mitigate the bias introduced by stale rollouts in advantage estimation, we resample all validation problems and recompute the average validation gradient at the beginning of each data-selection round.

[71] p: This design differs from Zhu et al. (2025) , which estimates gradients using offline rollouts with importance sampling. Although importance sampling can be unbiased, as policy evolves, newly emerging behaviors may not be covered by the offline rollout distribution, preventing reliable estimation of their gradients. Moreover, importance sampling typically incurs high variance when the behavior and target policies diverge, requiring substantially more samples for stable estimation and increasing computational cost.

[76] h2: 5 Considerations

[77] h3: 5.1 Applicable Scenarios

[78] p: GradAlign is particularly well-suited for settings where the training rewards are noisy or unreliable, as the gradients are more dispersed. We highlight three common scenarios, with illustrations in Figure 2 .

[79] h5: Unreliable Reward Signals

[80] p: In many RL settings, the reward signal can be noisy or unreliable, providing an imperfect proxy for true task performance. For example, in LLM RL, rewards are often obtained from model-based judges, and such rewards can be inaccurate on ambiguous problems or inputs beyond the judge’s capabilities. These cases often exhibit intermediate accuracy and are therefore difficult to remove with accuracy-based filtering, which can waste compute and destabilize training. GradAlign is well-suited to this setting: gradients induced by misjudged problems tend to be weakly aligned with the validation gradient (often near-orthogonal in high-dimensional space), so GradAlign naturally downweights them. In Section 6 , we show that GradAlign improves the stability of the training and the final performance under unreliable reward signals.

[81] h5: Distribution Imbalance

[82] p: When the training data set contains a wide range of problems (e.g., math problems containing different categories ( Albalak et al., 2025 ) ) and the target downstream task is more specific (e.g., one specific puzzle game), many training problems may be correct and of moderate difficulty, but uninformative for the target task, resulting in accuracy-based methods failing in filtering such problems. GradAlign addresses this mismatch by favoring problems whose gradients align with the validation gradient, while downweighting target-irrelevant ones with low alignment.

[83] h5: Low-Utility Training Data

[84] p: As RL training scales up, practitioners increasingly rely on large web-mined datasets ( Yue et al., 2024 ; Du et al., 2025 ) . Unlike distribution imbalance, where useful data is underrepresented, the challenge here is that many training problems are on-distribution but overly simple, yielding valid rewards while providing limited learning signal for the target task. GradAlign addresses this setting by prioritizing problems whose gradients align with validation performance and downweighting low-utility samples.

[85] h3: 5.2 Computational Overhead

[86] p: GradAlign introduces additional computations for estimating gradient alignment, but the overhead is modest for three reasons. First, the validation set is small, so computing the target gradient incurs a negligible cost relative to policy training. Second, gradient computation for training candidates adds only a constant-factor overhead compared to accuracy-based filtering ( Kaplan et al., 2020 ) . Finally, by prioritizing more informative training problems, GradAlign improves sample efficiency, reducing the number of ineffective updates and leading to better final performance than training on the full dataset.

[87] h2: 6 Experiments

[88] p: In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of GradAlign across diverse scenarios. Through these experiments, we aim to verify the capability of GradAlign to select high-quality training data targeting specific domains from a large and noisy training dataset.

[89] h3: 6.1 Experimental Setup

[90] h5: RL Framework

[91] p: All experiments are conducted using GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) . We use a sparse binary reward that indicates whether the final answer is correct, and all rewards are provided using Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as a judge. Detailed RL training hyperparameters can be found in Appendix A .

[92] h5: Scenario 1: Distinguishability Test with Noisy Rewards

[93] p: To rigorously assess the selector’s capacity to discriminate valid learning signals from stochastic noise, we establish a controlled experiment involving systematic perturbation of the training set. Utilizing the DAPO training corpus ( Yu et al., 2025 ) , we introduce controlled corruptions to 50% of the samples, whereby rewards are decoupled from model outputs and instead sampled from a Bernoulli distribution ( p = 0.5 p=0.5 ). This procedure generates a subset of data that statistically mimics the difficulty of “complex” problems (50% pass rate) while devoid of helpful gradient information.

[94] h5: Scenario 2: Unbalanced Dataset

[95] p: To evaluate alignment capabilities under distributional shift, we construct a heterogeneous dataset comprising 4k Countdown Game problems ( Gandhi et al., 2024 ) , 20k WebInstruct problems, and 10k DAPO problems ( Yu et al., 2025 ) . Given that the target domain (Countdown Game) constitutes merely ≈ \approx 10% of the aggregate pool, this setting challenges methods to retrieve domain-specific knowledge from imbalanced distributions. We employ a selection ratio of q = 20 q=20 (selecting the top 5%), with validation and test sets consisting exclusively of Countdown Game problems.

[99] p: SuperGPQA & TheoremQA: We investigate scenarios where the training distribution is broad but the target domain is narrow. Specifically, we train Qwen3-8B-Base on WebInstruct . We utilize SuperGPQA ( Team et al., 2025 ) and TheoremQA ( Chen et al., 2023 ) as the downstream target domains.

[100] p: Mathematical Reasoning Task: We train Qwen3-8B-Base on the same pre-filtered WebInstruct subset described above. This setting tests whether GradAlign can leverage a small, competition-level validation set to extract similarly challenging reasoning problems from a broad and noisy training distribution.

[101] h3: 6.2 Baselines

[102] p: We compare GradAlign with three data selection strategies:

[103] p: Random Selection: Uniformly samples training data. Serves as a lower bound.

[104] p: Accuracy Greedy (AccGreedy): Prioritizes samples with pass rates closest to 50%, based on the heuristic that problems near the decision boundary provide the strongest signal ( Sun et al., 2025c ) .

[105] p: LearnAlign (Align) ( Li et al., 2025a ) : A method that selects data based on gradient similarity within the training set without using external validation guidance.

[129] h5: Measuring Gradient Noise

[130] p: We conduct an ablation study to determine the number of response samples required for a reliable estimation of the cosine similarity between policy gradients. Specifically, we estimate the gradient cosine similarity using k v ∈ { 32,128,512 } k_{v}\in\{32,128,512\} samples per problem, computing each estimate twice with different random seeds. We then calculate the Pearson correlation between similarity scores obtained from these independent runs using the DAPO training set and the AMC22 validation set.

[131] p: Table 9 shows that the correlation increases with sample size, indicating that larger k v k_{v} produces more stable and accurate estimates. k v = 512 k_{v}=512 achieves the most accurate estimation with consistent similarity scores but requires high computational cost. k v = 32 k_{v}=32 reduces the computational cost significantly but produces noisy estimates. Since GradAlign relies on relative ranking rather than exact gradient values, a moderate correlation (e.g., 0.49) already provides a reliable directional signal in high-dimensional gradient space.

[133] h2: 8 Conclusion

[134] p: We presented GradAlign, a gradient-aligned data selection approach for LLM reinforcement learning that uses a small validation set to identify training problems whose policy-gradient updates most consistently improve downstream performance. Across multiple challenging regimes, including noisy rewards, distribution imbalance, and low-utility web data, GradAlign produces more stable training and stronger final results than heuristic and prior gradient-similarity baselines, suggesting policy gradient direction as a reliable signal for the attribution of RL data and the construction of the curriculum, which can be broadly used to improve the efficiency of training downstream tasks in LLM reinforcement learning.
