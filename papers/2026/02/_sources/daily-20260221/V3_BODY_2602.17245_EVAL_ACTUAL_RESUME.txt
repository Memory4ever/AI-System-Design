4.3. Task Evaluation
To further validate the effectiveness of the verb layer beyond the case studies, we conducted a larger-scale evaluation. We constructed a benchmark of 100 tasks that reflect common scenarios users might perform on the web. Examples include planning a romantic dinner that involves checking the weather, finding restaurants, shopping for supplies, and creating a playlist, or organizing a trip that requires combining information from travel, mapping, and booking services.
Across the full set of 100 tasks, our approach successfully completed every task. All outputs were manually verified for correctness, and repeated runs of a subset of tasks produced consistent results. These findings demonstrate that the verb layer, combined with program synthesis, can reliably support diverse web tasks at scale.
Consistent with the case study results, the baselines were unable to handle complex cross-website tasks that required multi-step reasoning and structured data processing. To provide a clearer comparison, we decomposed the workflows into simpler tasks and selected 10 representative single-site tasks from different websites, as summarized in Table 1. This setup avoided cross-website coordination and reduced planning complexity, more friendly to the baseline agents.
Our method succeeded on all 10 representative tasks, whereas the two baselines failed to solve the full set. The baselines completed some relatively straightforward tasks (e.g., retrieving astronomical images from NASA or querying weather forecasts), but consistently failed on tasks requiring multi-step decision making and structured intermediate outputs. For example, they were unable to complete the furniture shopping and travel planning tasks, which require decisions across multiple steps, or tasks such as budgeting supplies on Costco and searching news articles, which require organizing and filtering intermediate results.
We observed two primary error modes. First, some tasks produced incorrect results because the baselines were unable to manage intermediate outputs consistently or perform the additional data processing required. Second, execution was hampered by memory limitations, especially for tasks with longer action sequences. For example, browser-use frequently generated warnings of memory breakdown when histories grew too large, as shown in Figure 8.

For the subset of tasks that the baselines were able to complete, we also measured execution efficiency. Figure 9 reports relative execution times normalized to our prototype, which is set to 1.0× for reference. Across all four websites where baselines succeeded, our method consistently finished fastest. In contrast, the baselines were several times slower, ranging from 2.7× to 8.3× depending on the task. For example, both baselines required more than seven times longer than our prototype on AlaskaAir and OpenLibrary, and still more than twice as long on simpler lookups such as NASA image retrieval and OpenWeather queries.
The inefficiency arises because baselines rely on long traces of GUI actions interleaved with step-by-step reasoning, which slows execution and introduces delays in managing intermediate states. By contrast, our approach expresses the required logic directly through a small number of high-level verbs, condensing both reasoning and execution into short, predictable programs.
5. Why the Agentic Web Needs the Verb Layer
The case studies highlight not only the practical benefits of the verb layer but also the deeper reasons why it is necessary. We summarize these reasons along three dimensions: reliability, efficiency, and verifiability.
First, verbs improve reliability by providing stable semantics.
Each verb has a predefined implementation, so an agent does not need to infer concrete GUI actions from a combination of the task description and the current webpage structure. This design yields more stable behavior and avoids the variability that arises when reasoning step by step over raw interfaces. GUI-level actions lack semantic meaning because each click or keystroke is bound only to layout coordinates or interface styling without conveying the intended operation. By contrast, verbs expose explicit semantics through typed inputs and outputs, decoupling the agent’s reasoning from surface-level features such as page layouts or element identifiers. As a result, workflows remain robust even when interfaces evolve. For example, retrieving directions between a hotel and a museum through raw interactions would require dozens of fragile steps that could fail at any point, whereas the verb get_direction encapsulates the entire operation as a single stable call that works consistently across interface variations.
Second, verbs improve efficiency through structured composition.
Executing a task at the GUI level expands into long traces of clicks and keystrokes, and even API-based agents often need custom code to bridge gaps. Verbs instead expose operations as high-level building blocks that can be composed into concise programs with explicit control flow and data flow. This reduces the number of reasoning cycles and perception steps, turning tasks that once required dozens of manipulations into a handful of stable calls. The result is faster execution and better reuse of knowledge across sites.
