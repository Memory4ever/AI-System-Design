In- and Out-of-Context Availability
As described in Section 1, we draw a distinction between in- and out-of-context availability, both of which are measured by concept prevalence in large corpora. Briefly, in the MCQ setting, in-context availability asks: Given this question, which option comes most readily to mind? Meanwhile, out-of-context availability asks: Regardless of the question stem, which of these options is inherently more prevalent? Using our methodology, we can capture in- and out-of-context availability by modifying the passage retrieval query, to either include or exclude the question stem. As expected, in-context availability favours the correct answer even more clearly. This pattern is confirmed by the results in Table 3, where in-context availability using Wikipedia as the retrieval corpus is consistently higher than out-of-context availability. Using BEIR this pattern is not observed, reinforcing the suggestion that, despite its large size, BEIR does not contain relevant passages to the questions. The implications of this result are further discussed in Section 4.
Number of Retrieved Passages
We furthermore vary the number of passages we retrieve to determine the availability. We do this to assess whether our methodology of measuring availability is affected by the degree of specialisation of different topics: Presumably, retrieving a large number of passages using a highly specialised query might lead to the retrieval of irrelevant passages, thus obstructing our measurement of availability. We find that tripling the number of retrieved passages (from 20 to 60) has minimal impact on measured availability for both niche subjects (e.g., Immunopharmacology) and general subjects (e.g., SciQ), highlighting the robustness of this method.

4 Discussion
In this work we explored a computational method of quantifying the availability heuristic in the MCQ setting by measuring the prevalence of concepts in large text corpora. We found that the availability of the correct answer is significantly higher than that of the distractors, even when not accounting for the question (RQ1). We furthermore find that availability is, by itself, not sufficient to explain why some distractors are more often selected than others (RQ2). Lastly, we find that these conclusions do not only hold for expert-created distractors, but also extend to non-expert crowdsourced and LLM-generated distractors (RQ3).
A consistent finding across all experiments is the discrepancy in availability depending on the retrieval corpus used. While availability differences are pronounced when the Wikipedia corpus is used, they are negligible using BEIR. Given Wikipedia’s highly specialised, factual nature compared to BEIR’s broader but shallower scope, this suggests an intriguing distinction: Wikipedia allows us to approximate the availability heuristic of an expert (who possesses prior domain knowledge), whereas BEIR mirrors a novice’s perspective. In the broader context, this distinction highlights the care that needs to be given in selecting appropriate datasets when using computational approaches for education.
Shifting the focus to MCQ evaluation, we propose that our corpus-based methodology for quantifying the availability heuristic can assess how competitive distractors are.
