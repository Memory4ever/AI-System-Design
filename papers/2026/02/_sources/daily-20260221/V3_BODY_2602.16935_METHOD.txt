3 Methodology: Stateful Intent Tracking via Recurrent Latent Embeddings
To address the “Contextual Blind Spot” where atomic guardrails fail to detect distributed adversarial intent, we introduce DeepContext. Unlike stateless classifiers that evaluate queries in isolation (P⁡(unsafe∣xt)P(\text{unsafe}\mid x_{t})), DeepContext models safety as a trajectory through a semantic latent space, maintaining a continuous memory state that captures the accumulation of risk over time.
3.1 Problem Formulation: Adversarial Accumulation
We define a conversation history at turn tt as ℋt={(u1,r1),(u2,r2),…,ut}\mathcal{H}_{t}=\{(u_{1},r_{1}),(u_{2},r_{2}),\dots,u_{t}\}, where uu represents user inputs and rr represents assistant responses.
Current defenses approximate safety detection as a function f⁡(ut,rt−1)f(u_{t},r_{t-1}). This assumes the Markov property, that the current turn contains sufficient information to determine safety. As shown in recent attacks like Crescendo [6], this assumption is false for adversaries who fragment malicious payloads.
Safety Detection as a State-Space Problem
We reformulate safety detection as a state-space problem, where the model maintains a persistent representation of user intent. We define a hidden intent state hth_{t} which is updated recursively:

The crucial innovation is that RtR_{t} is not merely a summary of the text, but a risk vector oriented in the safety latent space, synthesized from both the projected historical intent and the immediate features of the current turn.
3.2 Model Architecture: The DeepContext Pipeline
DeepContext consists of three primary modules designed to decouple context understanding from safety enforcement, allowing for high-throughput monitoring without the latency of full context re-processing.
Figure 1: The DeepContext Architecture. The pipeline consists of three main stages: (1) Turn-level embedding extraction using a fine-tuned BERT and task-specific weighted pooling; (2) Recurrent intent tracking via a GRU to maintain conversation state; and (3) A trajectory classifier with a hybrid residual connection for final safety scoring.
3.2.1 Task-Attention Weighted Encoder
Following the architectural principles of the Mahendra and Raudra models [17], we utilize a fine-tuned BERT encoder to convert utu_{t} into a dense vector. Unlike generic embeddings, we apply a Task-Attention mechanism that weighs specific semantic tokens based on their relevance to known safety policies (e.g., prompt injection or jailbreak). This produces a weighted embedding ete_{t} that prioritizes “high-signal” adversarial markers.
3.2.2 Recurrent Intent Tracking (RIT) via Gated Recurrent Units
The core of our stateful tracking lies in a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) architecture. While Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are a traditional choice for sequence modeling, they suffer from a higher parameter count and three distinct gates, which introduces unnecessary computational overhead for real-time security applications.
In contrast, we utilize the GRU as a computationally efficient alternative to the LSTM. By merging the forget and input gates into a single “update gate,” the GRU minimizes the floating-point operations (FLOPs) required per conversational turn while effectively mitigating the vanishing gradient problem. This streamlined architecture is essential for achieving DeepContext’s sub-20ms latency target.

3.2.3 Projection Layer and Residual Shortcuts
To ensure the model retains high sensitivity to both long-range adversarial drift and immediate, single-turn violations, we implement a Hybrid Residual Architecture. Following the GRU state update, the hidden state hth_{t} is passed through a linear projection layer. To avoid the potential "smoothing" of high-intensity signals within the recurrent state, we introduce a residual connection that concatenates the raw task-attention weighted embedding ete_{t} directly with the projected hidden state.
The input to the final Trajectory Classifier, or risk vector RtR_{t}, is formulated as:
Rt=[ϕ⁡(ht);et]R_{t}=[\phi(h_{t});e_{t}]
(8)
Where ; denotes the concatenation operation. This "short-circuit" mechanism provides two primary benefits:
1.
Instantaneous Detection: It allows the model to identify blatant, single-turn attacks (e.g., explicit hate speech or direct dangerous queries) using the raw semantic signal from ete_{t}, without dependency on the temporal context stored in hth_{t}.
2.
Contextual Anchoring: It provides the classification head with a dual-perspective: the long-term intent trajectory (the stateful component) and the immediate prompt context (the stateless component).
By combining the recurrent memory of the GRU with the direct signal of the task-attention embeddings, DeepContext achieves a robust defense against both “slow-burn” grooming attacks like Crescendo and sudden, high-intensity adversarial injections.
3.2.4 Trajectory Classifier
The final component of the DeepContext pipeline is a specialized classifier. The head consists of a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with LayerNorm, GELU activations, and residual feed-forward blocks. The model is optimized using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) Loss, integrated with a focal loss component to handle class imbalance.

3.2.6 Training Procedure
DeepContext employs a supervised learning framework optimized for binary safety classification. By focusing on a single, unified safety objective, the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is able to concentrate its 2048-dimensional recurrent state on detecting the subtle transition from benign to adversarial intent within a conversation’s temporal context. To address the inherent class imbalance typically found in adversarial datasets we utilize a Focal Loss function with γ=2.0\gamma=2.0. This strategically weights the gradient descent process toward “hard” examples that reside near the safety boundary, ensuring the model does not become complacent with easily classified benign samples.
The optimization objective is defined by the following loss function:
ℒ=−(1−pt)γ​log⁡(pt)\mathcal{L}=-(1-p_{t})^{\gamma}\log(p_{t})
(10)
where ptp_{t} represents the model’s estimated probability for the ground-truth class. This approach prioritizes learning from sophisticated adversarial “drift” rather than over-represented, superficial patterns.
Hyperparameters and Configuration
To preserve the model’s ability to generalize to novel attack vectors, training is strictly limited to a single epoch. This prevents the GRU from over-indexing on the specific phrasing of historical attacks, which could lead to catastrophic forgetting of broader safety principles. We utilized a large batch size of 512 to ensure gradient stability and to provide a representative distribution of adversarial samples in every update step.

By pairing a high-capacity GRU with a conservative learning rate and substantial dropout, we ensure DeepContext develops a sharp sensitivity to intent trajectories without memorizing fixed prompt templates. 4 Results and Evaluation
4.1 Evaluation Dataset
To provide a rigorous evaluation, we curated a benchmark suite designed to isolate specific failure modes of modern guardrails. As detailed in Table 3, the benign datasets: LMSYS, Glaive, Nemotron, and Anthropic HH-RLHF were selected to represent "high-noise" environments like function calling and complex instruction following, where stateless filters often trigger false positives. To test defensive depth, we incorporated human-led red teaming data from HarmBench and DEFCON 34, capturing the creative and non-standard linguistic patterns humans use to bypass safety alignment. Finally, we prioritized automated multi-turn frameworks like Red Queen, XGuard, and Automated-Multi-Turn specifically to stress-test DeepContext’s ability to detect "slow-burn" adversarial drift such as the Crescendo attack which remains the primary blind spot for current enterprise-grade safety solutions. Overall the dataset includes 210 multiturn jailbreaks with a median of 7 turns per conversation and a max of 49 turns. We also have 800 benign conversations with a median of 4 turns per conversation and a max of 18 turns.
While some samples from the HH-RLHF, XGuard, and DEFCON datasets were present in our training data, we ensured that the specific samples used for evaluation were not seen during training.

4.2 Evaluation Baselines
To provide a comprehensive view of the safety landscape, we selected a diverse set of baselines that represent the three primary pillars of modern LLM guardrails: specialized classifiers, generative safety models, and managed enterprise services.
1. Specialized Task-Specific Encoders
We included Llama-Prompt-Guard-2-86M and Deberta-v3-Prompt-Injection to represent the "lightweight" layer of defense. These models are widely used for their extreme low latency and high precision on known, explicit attack patterns. They serve as a benchmark for whether a small, non-recurrent encoder can catch multi-turn drift through raw pattern matching.
