2 Methodology
We introduce Amber-Image, an efficient compression framework for large-scale Diffusion Transformers. Built upon Qwen-Image, Amber-Image reuses its text encoder (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) and VAE with official weights unchanged. Our compression targets exclusively the 60-layer dual-stream MMDiT backbone, where image and text latents are processed through separate transformer streams.
Figure 2: Overview of the Amber-Image compression pipeline.
The overall framework and the hierarchical compression procedure are illustrated in Fig. 2, while the precise execution flows for the two stages are summarized in Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2, respectively. The pipeline consists of two sequential stages: (1) depth pruning to derive a 30-layer model, Amber-Image-10B—where the first and last layers are excluded from pruning due to their essential roles in encoding the raw input and decoding the final output, and (2) deep-layer single-stream conversion to obtain a hybrid-stream variant, Amber-Image-6B, which retains dual-stream processing in early layers and adopts a unified single stream in deeper layers (initialized from the image branch). Each stage employs a two-phase recovery strategy: first, targeted knowledge distillation; second, lightweight full-parameter fine-tuning.

2.1 Depth Pruning and Recovery for Amber-Image-10B
2.1.1 Layer Importance Estimation
To guide structured depth reduction, we estimate the importance of each of the 60 MMDiT layers via a global ablation-based scoring protocol. Unlike gradient-based Taylor approximations (Molchanov et al., 2019), which rely on local linear assumptions and often fail to capture nonlinear inter-layer dependencies in deep transformers, our approach directly measures the impact of layer removal on overall generation fidelity.
Specifically, we define the ablation of the ll-th layer as the selective truncation of its residual contribution, and measure the resulting change in fidelity (e.g., loss) as a global sensitivity indicator. Formally, for a given input hlh_{l}, the modified output h~l+1\tilde{h}_{l+1} is expressed as:
h~l+1=(1−ml)⋅ℱl​(hl)+hl\tilde{h}_{l+1}=(1-m_{l})\cdot\mathcal{F}_{l}(h_{l})+h_{l}
(1)
where ℱl\mathcal{F}_{l} denotes the ll-th Transformer block and ml∈{0,1}m_{l}\in\{0,1\} is a binary mask. During the assessment of layer ll, we set ml=1m_{l}=1 (disabling the block) while maintaining mi≠l=0m_{i\neq l}=0.
We quantify this effect via the layer-wise prediction discrepancy δl\delta_{l}, defined in the noise prediction space as the difference between the original and ablated noise predictions:
δl​(zt,t,p)=ϵθ​(zt,t,p)−ϵθ∖{l}​(zt,t,p)\delta_{l}(z_{t},t,p)=\epsilon_{\theta}(z_{t},t,p)-\epsilon_{\theta\setminus\{l\}}(z_{t},t,p)
(2)
where ztz_{t} denotes the latent representation at timestep tt, and ϵθ,ϵθ∖{l}\epsilon_{\theta},\epsilon_{\theta\setminus\{l\}} represent the noise predictions of the original and the model with the ll-th layer ablated, respectively.
Following observations in Diff-Pruning (Fang et al., 2023) that prediction errors at different timesteps contribute unequally to final generation quality, we employ a dynamic weighting kernel ωt\omega_{t}. We explicitly account for the chained effect inherent in the reverse diffusion process: errors introduced at early timesteps (high-noise) tend to disrupt global semantic structures, whereas those at later timesteps primarily affect fine-grained texture details. Accordingly, we assign higher weights to larger tt values to prioritize the preservation of fundamental semantic layouts during assessment. The final importance score IlI_{l} is defined as:
Il​(𝒫)=1|𝒫|​∑p∈𝒫(∑t∈𝒯s​u​bωt⋅‖δl​(zt,t,p)‖22∑t∈𝒯s​u​bωt)I_{l}(\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{sub}}\omega_{t}\cdot\|\delta_{l}(z_{t},t,p)\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{sub}}\omega_{t}}\right)
(3)
where 𝒫\mathcal{P} is a representative prompt set, and ωt\omega_{t} is a timestep-dependent weighting factor.
The resulting score Il​(𝒫)I_{l}(\mathcal{P}) constitutes our layer importance estimation, which holistically quantifies each layer’s contribution to semantic integrity and visual fidelity. Layers with the lowest IlI_{l} scores are identified as redundant and slated for pruning.
2.1.2 Depth Pruning and Local Weight Averaging
Guided by the importance scores, we compress the 60-layer MMDiT backbone by removing the 30 least important layers, as ranked by their scores IlI_{l} from Section 2.1.1. This stage effectively reduces the model depth by 50%\%, significantly decreasing the computational overhead.
The local weight average ensures that the preserved layers inherit the transformation capabilities of their pruned neighbors. For any preserved layer l∈ℒk​e​e​pl\in\mathcal{L}_{keep}, let kk denote the number of consecutive layers immediately following ll that have been assigned to ℒr​e​m\mathcal{L}_{rem}. We initialize the weights of this preserved layer, 𝐖li​n​i​t\mathbf{W}_{l}^{init}, as the arithmetic mean of the original weights from the cluster of layers it now represents:
𝐖li​n​i​t=1k+1​∑j=0k𝐖l+jo​r​i​g\mathbf{W}_{l}^{init}=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbf{W}_{l+j}^{orig}
(4)
For layers where no subsequent pruning occurs (i.e., k=0k=0), the weights remain unchanged from their original state (𝐖li​n​i​t=𝐖lo​r​i​g\mathbf{W}_{l}^{init}=\mathbf{W}_{l}^{orig}).
The core idea is that adjacent layers in MMDiT typically perform incremental refinements on latent features, and their collective transformation can be well approximated by the local mean in weight space. With this strategy, we effectively mitigate the sharp loss spike commonly observed at the start of training, and provide a high-quality warm start. This significantly accelerates the recovery of generation fidelity compared to random or naive initialization strategies.

To bridge the fidelity gap introduced by layer reduction and weight fusion, we propose a two-stage recovery protocol. This process initiates with selective layer-wise distillation for rapid alignment and concludes with global fine-tuning to further improve generation quality.
