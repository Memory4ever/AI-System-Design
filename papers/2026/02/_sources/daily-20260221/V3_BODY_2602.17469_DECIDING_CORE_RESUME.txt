3. Methodology
We employed a controlled experimental framework to quantify cross-lingual sentiment alignment in multilingual transformer architectures. We adopt a within-model comparative design where each transformer processes parallel Bengali-English text pairs independently, to enable direct measurement of semantic divergence without inter-model architectural confounds.
3.1. Dataset Specification
We utilized a parallel corpus comprising n=7,350n=7{,}350 Bengali-English sentence pairs, sourced from the publicly available “BanglaBlend” (Ayman et al., 2025). Formally, the dataset 𝒟\mathcal{D} is defined as a set of tuples:
(1)
𝒟={(Bi,Ei,Di)∣i∈[1,n]}\mathcal{D}=\{(B_{i},E_{i},D_{i})\mid i\in[1,n]\}
where:
•
Bi∈ΣBengali∗B_{i}\in\Sigma_{\text{Bengali}}^{*} represents the ii-th Bengali sentence (original text)
•
Ei∈ΣEnglish∗E_{i}\in\Sigma_{\text{English}}^{*} represents the corresponding English translation
•
Di∈{Sadhu,Cholito}D_{i}\in\{\text{Sadhu},\text{Cholito}\} denotes the Bengali dialect classification
Bengali exhibits diglossia with two primary written forms:
(1)
Sadhu Bhasha: Formal/literary register, characterized by Sanskrit-derived vocabulary and archaic verb conjugations.
(2)
Cholito Bhasha: Colloquial/standard register, with relatively simplified morphology and contemporary vocabulary.
We stratify the dataset into dialect-specific subsets:
(2)
𝒟Sadhu\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{Sadhu}}
={(Bi,Ei,Di)∈𝒟∣Di=Sadhu}|𝒟Sadhu|=3,675\displaystyle=\{(B_{i},E_{i},D_{i})\in\mathcal{D}\mid D_{i}=\text{Sadhu}\}\hskip 10.00002pt|\mathcal{D}_{\text{Sadhu}}|=3{,}675
(3)
𝒟Cholito\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{Cholito}}
={(Bi,Ei,Di)∈𝒟∣Di=Cholito}|𝒟Cholito|=3,675\displaystyle=\{(B_{i},E_{i},D_{i})\in\mathcal{D}\mid D_{i}=\text{Cholito}\}\hskip 10.00002pt|\mathcal{D}_{\text{Cholito}}|=3{,}675
This balanced stratification enables statistical comparison of model performance across linguistic registers.
3.2. Experimental Design
For each model MM and sentence pair (Bi,Ei)(B_{i},E_{i}), we perform independent inference on both languages using the same model weights θM\theta_{M}:
(4)
y^B,i\displaystyle\hat{y}_{B,i}
=Mθ(Bi)[Bengali stream]\displaystyle=M_{\theta}(B_{i})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{[Bengali stream]}
(5)
y^E,i\displaystyle\hat{y}_{E,i}
=Mθ(Ei)[English stream]\displaystyle=M_{\theta}(E_{i})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{[English stream]}
This design eliminates architectural differences as a confounding variable, isolating the effect of language on sentiment prediction. Any divergence between y^B,i\hat{y}_{B,i} and y^E,i\hat{y}_{E,i} is attributable to cross-lingual representation, calibration, or decision boundary alignment within the same parameter space.
3.3. Score Normalization and Metric Formulation
3.3.1. Universal Score Normalizer
To enable direct comparison, we define a universal normalization function φ:Predictions→[−1,1]\varphi:\text{Predictions}\rightarrow[-1,1]:
(6)
φ⁡(y^)={y^.scoreif ​y^.label∈{positive}−y^.scoreif ​y^.label∈{negative}0if ​y^.label∈{neutral}\varphi(\hat{y})=\begin{cases}\hat{y}.\text{score}&\text{if }\hat{y}.\text{label}\in\{\text{positive}\}\\
-\hat{y}.\text{score}&\text{if }\hat{y}.\text{label}\in\{\text{negative}\}\\
0&\text{if }\hat{y}.\text{label}\in\{\text{neutral}\}\end{cases}
*Note: Standard “Positive” and “Negative” labels map to ±s\pm s for 2-class and 3-class models, while intermediate positive/negative classes are scaled by 0.5 in the 5-class Tabularis model to accommodate the “Very Positive” and “Very Negative” extremes.
After normalization, we obtain continuous sentiment scores:
(7)
SB,i\displaystyle S_{B,i}
=φ(y^B,i)∈[−1,1](Bengali sentiment score)\displaystyle=\varphi(\hat{y}_{B,i})\in[-1,1]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{(Bengali sentiment score)}
(8)
SE,i\displaystyle S_{E,i}
=φ(y^E,i)∈[−1,1](English sentiment score)\displaystyle=\varphi(\hat{y}_{E,i})\in[-1,1]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{(English sentiment score)}
3.3.2. Sentence-Level Alignment Metrics
For each sentence pair ii, we compute four alignment metrics:
M1. Alignment Divergence
(9)
Di=|SB,i−SE,i|∈[0,2]D_{i}=|S_{B,i}-S_{E,i}|\in[0,2]
Interpretation: Di=0.0D_{i}=0.0 indicates perfect alignment (identical sentiment), Di=0.5D_{i}=0.5 indicates moderate divergence, Di=2.0D_{i}=2.0 indicates maximal divergence (opposite extremes).
M2. Directional Bias
(10)
Bi=SE,i−SB,i∈[−2,2]B_{i}=S_{E,i}-S_{B,i}\in[-2,2]
Interpretation: Bi>0B_{i}>0 indicates that the English text is predicted as more positive (or less negative) than its Bengali counterpart; Bi<0B_{i}<0 indicates the reverse; Bi≈0B_{i}\approx 0 indicates minimal cross-lingual divergence for that pair.
M3. Polarity Inversion (Safety Metric)
(11)
Ii=𝟙​[(SB,i>τ∧SE,i<−τ)∨(SB,i<−τ∧SE,i>τ)]I_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\left[(S_{B,i}>\tau\land S_{E,i}<-\tau)\lor(S_{B,i}<-\tau\land S_{E,i}>\tau)\right]
where 𝟙​[⋅]\mathbbm{1}[\cdot] is the indicator function and τ=0.1\tau=0.1 is a noise threshold to avoid false positives from near-zero scores.
Interpretation: Ii=1I_{i}=1 indicates sentiment inversion (e.g., Bengali=Positive, English=Negative); Ii=0I_{i}=0 indicates polarity preserved. Inversion is the most severe failure mode, as it indicates misalignment of sentiment direction.
3.4. Population-Level Aggregation and Statistical Computation
To characterize model-level performance, we aggregate sentence-level metrics across stratified subsets (𝒟Sadhu,𝒟Cholito)(\mathcal{D}_{\text{Sadhu}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{Cholito}}) or the entire dataset 𝒟\mathcal{D}.
For each model MM, we compute the following population-level statistics:
P1. Mean Alignment Error (Divergence)
(12)
μD​(M)=1n​∑i=1nDi\mu_{D}(M)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
Lower μD\mu_{D} indicates better cross-lingual consistency.
P2. Standard Deviation of Divergence
(13)
σD​(M)=1n−1​∑i=1n(Di−μD​(M))2\sigma_{D}(M)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(D_{i}-\mu_{D}(M))^{2}}
Quantifies variability in alignment quality across the dataset.
P3. Robustness Index
(14)
ℛ(M)=1n∑i=1n𝟙[Di<0.1]×100%\mathcal{R}(M)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}[D_{i}<0.1]\times 100\%
Percentage of sentence pairs with negligible divergence (Di<0.1D_{i}<0.1). Higher robustness indicates a larger “safe operating zone.”
P4. Inversion Rate
(15)
ℐrate​(M)=1n​∑i=1nIi×100%\mathcal{I}_{\text{rate}}(M)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}\times 100\%
Percentage of sentence pairs with sentiment polarity flips.
P5. Mean Directional Bias
(16)
μB​(M)=1n​∑i=1nBi\mu_{B}(M)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}
Average positive value indicates that, on average, the model assigns higher sentiment scores to English inputs than to their Bengali counterparts; negative value indicates the reverse. Values close to zero suggest no systematic language-conditioned directional skew.
To investigate the dialect gap, we quantified the “formal penalty”:
(17)
Δdialect​(M)=μD​(M,Sadhu)−μD​(M,Cholito)\Delta_{\text{dialect}}(M)=\mu_{D}(M,\text{Sadhu})-\mu_{D}(M,\text{Cholito})
Interpretation: Δdialect>0\Delta_{\text{dialect}}>0 indicates the model struggles more with formal Bengali; Δdialect<0\Delta_{\text{dialect}}<0 indicates struggles with colloquial Bengali; Δdialect≈0\Delta_{\text{dialect}}\approx 0 indicates dialect-invariant performance. The “Dialect Gap” here is a measure of representational inequity that quantifies the performance degradation for formal (Sadhu) Bengali and operationalizes the cost of alignment for users who prefer traditional linguistic norms.
Additionally, we compute the percentage increase in error when processing Sadhu vs. Cholito:
(18)
Δdialect%(M)=Δdialect​(M)μD​(M,Cholito)×100%\Delta_{\text{dialect}}^{\%}(M)=\frac{\Delta_{\text{dialect}}(M)}{\mu_{D}(M,\text{Cholito})}\times 100\%
This metric normalizes the absolute gap by the baseline Cholito error rate, enabling comparison across models with different overall error magnitudes.

4.1. Finding 1: Sentiment Inversion and Alignment Instability
We define a sentiment inversion as a case where a Bengali–English translation pair (similar meaning) receives opposite polarity classifications (positive vs. negative). Such inversions represent major alignment failures, as semantic equivalence is preserved but affective interpretation is reversed.
Across model architectures, inversion rates vary dramatically, revealing a potential relationship between compression, divergence magnitude or optimization (Figure 1).
