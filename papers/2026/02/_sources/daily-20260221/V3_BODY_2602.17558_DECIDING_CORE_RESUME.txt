4.3 Training
Supervised Fine-Tuning Stage.
We first perform supervised fine-tuning so the GRM learns to produce metrics and corresponding scores consistent with annotations.
The training examples contain input image I0I_{0}, editing instruction gg, two after edit image II and IwI_{w}, and annotated metric mm and scores (m,s,sw)(m,s,s_{w}). The training objective is an autoregressive loss that fits the annotation:
ℒSFTreward=−∑tlogpϕ(yt|y<t,I0,I,Iw,g).\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}^{\text{reward}}=-\sum_{t}\log p_{\phi}(y_{t}\,|\,y_{<t},\,I_{0},I,I_{w},g).
(3)
This stage trains the model to explain aesthetic metrics in natural language and to produce scalar scores accordingly.
Policy-Guided Reward Training (PGRT) in RL.
The previous SFT stage relies on perturbed sub-optimal images, which introduces intrinsic bias toward a limited set of edit categories. As illustrated in Figure 2, we observe that many perturbations involve single adjustments to exposure and temperature, while the results generated by policy model often involve combined and complex edits. This reveals a training distribution shift, which may cause inferior performance of the reward model when predicting real outputs from policy models.
Our goal is to align the reward model with the policy distribution during training. To this end, we propose policy-guided reward training (PGRT), which trains the reward model on data consistent with the policy’s editing distribution. As shown in Figure 3 (right), the RL stage replaces perturbed images with policy-generated results, shifting IwI_{w} from the perturbed to the policy-generated distribution. Given paired examples (I0,I,Iw)(I_{0},I,I_{w}), the reward function is defined as
𝒥(ϕ)=𝔼m,r,rw∼π​ϕ[𝕀[r>rw]+rformat(m,r,rw)],\mathcal{J}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{m,r,r_{w}\sim\pi{\phi}}\left[\mathbb{I}[r>r_{w}]+r_{\text{format}}(m,r,r_{w})\right],
(4)
where 𝕀⁡[⋅]\mathbb{I}[\cdot] is a 0-1 indicator, and rformatr_{\text{format}} is a format reward ensuring that both metrics and scores are properly generated.
Essentially, this reward function penalizes the model when it incorrectly assigns a higher score to a weaker edit.
PGRT relies on the outputs of the policy model. Therefore, when training RetouchIQ, we adopt an alternating training strategy in which the policy model and the reward model are optimized in turn to progressively enhance each other’s performance. It is worth noting that when training the reward model for the first time, the RL stage uses only perturbed edits, since the policy model is not yet trained.

5 Experiments
In this section, we evaluate RetouchIQ under various retouching settings and compare it with state-of-the-art methods.
We focus on the following questions:
•
Can RetouchIQ consistently provides high-quality image retouching results?
•
How does the generalist reward model overcome the challenges in conventional verifiable reward?
•
How does the policy-guided reward training improves the reward model and policy model in RetouchIQ?
5.1 Experiment Settings
Datasets.
We curate large-scale, high-quality datasets for training both the policy model and the reward model.
For the policy model, the final training set contains 190K image–instruction pairs covering a wide range of global and local retouching tasks.
Each input image is associated with three instruction variants that differ in length and complexity, providing diversity during training.
For the reward model, we construct a dataset of 10K perturbed samples, where each datum consists of an original image, its ground-truth editing, and a corresponding sub-optimal version.
During alternating training, an additional 5K samples are generated to further fine-tune the reward model under the RL objective.
Implementation.
Both the policy and reward models are built upon Qwen2.5-VL-7B [2].
For the MLLM annotator and perturber, we employ GLM-4.5V [16].
During training, we first fine-tune the reward model using the perturbed dataset.
We then perform alternating fine-tuning of the policy and reward models, as described in Sec. 3 and 4.
For the editing platform, following prior work [22], we leverage Adobe Lightroom [1] for its professional-grade image retouching capabilities.
Benchmarks. To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing public benchmark for evaluating image retouching quality using professional editing tools.
Therefore, we introduce RetouchEval, a dedicated benchmark designed to assess retouching quality.
RetouchEval consists of 300 instruction–image pairs curated from real user editing histories.
The dataset is divided into three splits: quality enhancement, which focuses on enhancing the overall visual quality of the input image; style transformation, which aims to alter the artistic or tonal style of the image; and local retouching, which targets localized adjustments rather than global edits.
In addition, we also evaluate on MIT-Adobe5K [3], a public benchmark for general image enhancement.
It is worth noting that, unlike our setting, MIT-Adobe5K does not include explicit user intentions; as its primary objective is to improve perceptual quality rather than to achieve a customized editing style. Following previous work [10], we conduct evaluations on 400 randomly selected test images.
Baselines. We compare RetouchIQ with three categories of state-of-the-art baselines:
❶ General-purpose MLLMs: We carefully design editing instructions and prompt leading MLLMs, including Gemini-2.5 [6] and GPT-5 [27], to evaluate their zero-shot ability to interpret user instructions and generate valid editing plans.
❷ MLLM Agents: JarvisArt [22] serves as a representative state-of-the-art agent that leverages tools for image editing.
We also include MonetGPT [10], a strong baseline focusing on image quality enhancement using tools.
❸ Diffusion-based Models: We further evaluate how advanced diffusion-based models, flux-pro-1.1 [21], respond to editing instructions and perform corresponding modifications. Note that Google’s NanaBanana and Adobe Firefly are popular image editing solutions but are closed-source. Since their underlying architectures (e.g., pure diffusion or hybrid systems) remain undisclosed, we instead compare against the leading open-source diffusion model, flux-pro-1.1.
Metrics. Following prior work [22], we adopt the following metrics:
❶ L1 and L2 differences between the output and the ground-truth images;
❷ Perceptual quality and semantic consistency scores evaluated by GLM-4.5V [16].
For the MIT-Adobe5K dataset, we report PSNR, LPIPS, and SSIM, following the original benchmark setting.
