6 Experiments
We evaluate the proposed policy inference framework across a
range of domains designed to expose different structural aspects
of decision making under uncertainty, and compare it to
entropy-regularized policy optimization. Throughout the
experiments we contrast inference over distributions of
deterministic policies (VSMC) with direct optimization of
entropy-regularized stochastic policies (SAC). The experiments
are designed to examine (i) qualitative structure of inferred
policy distributions, (ii) the role of multi-particle inference,
(iii) differences between deterministic-policy inference and
entropy-regularized optimization, and (iv) behavior under
increasing stochasticity and planning complexity.
We begin by exploring the proposed policy inference framework
and the algorithm on a grid world domain. The ease of static
visualisation and apparent simplicity of grid worlds facilitates
introspection into the properties of inferred policy
distributions and the induced policies. Then, we use the grid
world domain and three other domains from the
literature—Academic Advising, Blackjack, and Triangle
Tireworld—to compare policy VSMC to discrete Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC) Haarnoja et al. (2018); Christodoulou (2019), the state-of-the-art algorithm for
learning of entropy-regualized stochastic policies, outlining
differences in the resulting policies and suitability to
particular MDP types.
The selected domains span complementary structural properties of
Markov decision processes that are particularly relevant to policy
inference. Grid worlds provide fully observable environments with
transparent geometry, allowing direct visual inspection of policy
distributions. Blackjack represents a stochastic decision problem
with small state space but strong outcome randomness and well-known
optimal policies, enabling calibrated comparison to ground truth.
Triangle Tireworld introduces irreversible stochastic failures and
risk-sensitive planning, where early decisions affect downstream
recoverability. Academic Advising represents a large combinatorial
decision space with long horizons and delayed consequences.
Together, these domains allow us to examine how policy inference
behaves under multimodality, outcome stochasticity, risk management,
and combinatorial planning complexity.
Throughout the experiments, we run VSMC with 10 particles for
50 00050\,000 iterations (SMC sweeps), adjusting the initial
learning rate between 10−510^{-5} and 3⋅10−43\cdot 10^{-4} for each
particular domain. We apply cosine learning rate decay to 0.10.1
of the initial rate. We adapt SAC implementation from
CleanRL (Huang et al., 2022) for discrete domains through replacing
the critic networks by multi-layer perceptrons with two hidden
layers. We train SAC for 1 000 0001\,000\,000 time steps. In both VSMC
and SAC, the width of the hidden layers in all networks is set
to 64. Increasing the width of the hidden layers to either 128
or 256 slows down training and policy execution but does not
result in a noticeable change in performance.
Each algorithm–domain pair is evaluated over 2525 independent
training runs. Policies obtained from each run are evaluated
using 10 00010\,000 trajectories, allowing estimation of both
expected performance and variability across runs. The
temperature for VSMC and the entropy weight for SAC are kept at
11 except in the studies where the influence of the temperature
or the entropy weight is explored explicitly.
6.1 Grid Worlds
Grid worlds provide a controlled setting in which policy
distributions can be visualized directly, allowing qualitative
inspection of multimodality, uncertainty, and variability across
runs. They therefore serve as a diagnostic domain for understanding
the behavior of the inference procedure itself.
In the grid world domain we use in this study, the environment
is a rectangular grid. Four actions—Right, Up, Down, and
Left—advance the agent to the corresponding adjacent cell.
An action that would take the agent out of the grid has no
effect. The environment is slippery: upon any action the agent
moves, with probability p𝑠𝑢𝑐𝑐=0.8p_{\mathit{succ}}=0.8, in the direction of
the action, and otherwise in an adjacent direction. The reward
collected by the agent upon each action is determined by the
color of the cell to which the agent moves: grey (pavement) —
0, red (gravel) — -1, yellow (goal) — +5, green (swamp) —
-5. Yellow and green cells are absorbing: once the agent reaches
a yellow or a green cell, no further reward is collected and no
action moves the agent out of the cell.
Inferred policies are represented by policy and
occupancy maps in the figures below
(Figure 1–5). A black border denotes
the initial cell. The darkness of the middle part of a cell
represents the occupancy: the darker a cell, the more
trajectories passed through the cell. The white cross with
unevenly sized beams in the center of each cell represents the
policy distribution in that cell, with the length of each beam
denoting the probability of the corresponding direction. The
maps are averaged over 25 runs: blurrier crosses mean more
variation in the inferred policies across runs.

Figure 2: Grid worlds: VSMC variations on multimodal world
Two crucial features of VSMC are multiple particles and
resampling. Figure 2 compares VSMC to
its degenerate variants — no resampling, corresponding to
variational importance sampling (VIS), and a single particle,
which reduces VSMC to variational structured approximation
(VSA). VIS (Figure 2(a) converges to a similar
policy distribution, but with a much higher variance across
runs, as manifested by blurry policy beams. VSA
(Figure 2(b)) consistently converges to a
different solution with a much lower expected return. This
qualitative gap is expected, since a single-particle variational
objective optimizes a different bound than its multi-particle
counterpart Burda et al. (2016); Cremer et al. (2017).

CDFs of return distributions for VSMC, VIS, and VSA.
