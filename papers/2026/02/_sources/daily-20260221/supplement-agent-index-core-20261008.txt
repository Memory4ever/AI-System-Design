L62: ###### Abstract.
L63: Agentic AI systems are increasingly capable of performing professional and personal tasks with limited human involvement. However, tracking these developments is difficult because the AI agent ecosystem is complex, rapidly evolving, and inconsistently documented, posing obstacles to both researchers and policymakers. To address these challenges, this paper presents the 2025 AI Agent Index.
L64: The Index documents information regarding the origins, design, capabilities, ecosystem, and safety features of 30 state-of-the-art AI agents based on publicly available information and email correspondence with developers. In addition to documenting information about individual agents, the Index illuminates broader trends in the development of agents, their capabilities, and the level of transparency of developers.
L65: Notably, we find different transparency levels among agent developers and observe that most developers share little information about safety, evaluations, and societal impacts. The 2025 AI Agent Index is available online at cite45†https://aiagentindex.mit.edu†aiagentindex.mit.edu .

L67: ## 1. Introduction
L68: Despite growing interest and investment in agentic AI systems capable of automating complex tasks with limited human involvement (cite46†Yee et al., 2025 ; cite47†Pandey, 2025 ; cite48†Singla et al., 2025 ; cite49†ONTOFORCE, 2025 ; cite50†Gibney, 2025b ; cite51†Gottweis et al., 2025 ; cite52†Gridach et al., 2025 ; cite53†Wei et al., 2025 ), key aspects of their real-world development and deployment remain opaque, with little information made publicly available to researchers or policymakers (cite54†Casper et al., 2025 ).
L69: In particular, there are currently no clear answers to several basic questions concerning agentic AI systems:
L70:   * •
L71: 
L72: Who is developing the most impactful agentic systems?
L73: 
L74:   * •
L75: 
L76: In which domains are they deployed?
L77: 
L78:   * •
L79: 
L80: What processes and resources are used to develop these systems?
L81: 
L82:   * •
L83: 
L84: How are they evaluated?
L85: 
L86:   * •
L87: 
L88: What guardrails are in place to mitigate their unique risks?
L89: Figure 1. Interest in AI agents is growing. 2025 has seen a sharp increase in interest in AI agents. This is reflected in an increase of new Google search terms related to agentic AI products (blue bars) as well as Google Scholar paper counts for “AI agent” or “agentic AI” (red line). Accumulation of individual releases of agentic AI products included in this Index is shown by category: chats with agentic tools, enterprise agents, and browser agents.
L90: See cite55†Figure 9 for details on releases and cite40†Appendix C for details on public interest.
L91: To answer these questions, we introduce and release the 2025 AI Agent Index. The Index provides in-depth information on 30 agentic systems across 6 categories: legal, technical capabilities, autonomy & control, ecosystem interaction, evaluation, and safety. This 2025 Index follows the first 2024 AI Agent Index (cite54†Casper et al., 2025 ).
L92: To account for recent growth and change in the AI agent ecosystem (see cite56†Figure 1 ), this 2025 Index develops and implements substantially revised inclusion criteria (cite9†Section 3.1 ) and information fields (cite8†Section 3 ). Most crucially, it indexes a smaller number of systems in greater depth — focusing on highly agentic systems with high-impact real-world applications.
L93: In addition to providing information about prominent AI agents, this Index also reveals ecosystem-wide trends regarding which information developers do and do not publicly share. This sheds light on the state of transparency in the agent ecosystem amidst cite57†agentic AI incidents†incidentdatabase.ai , recent attention from governments (cite58†Oueslati and Staes-Polet, 2025 ; cite59†U.S.
L94: AI Safety Institute Technical Staff, 2025 ; cite60†Bazinska et al., 2025 ; cite61†Korbak et al., 2025 ; cite62†Zou et al., 2025 ), industry self-regulation efforts (cite63†METR, 2024 ), and gaps between expectations of agent developers and reality (cite64†Belcic and Stryker, 2025 ). We make three contributions:
L95:   1. (1)
L96: Agent Index: We index 30 highly agentic and widely used products (cite8†Section 3 ).^{1}^{1} 1 We use terms like “agentic,” “pursue,” and “choose” as shorthand for computational processes without attributing human-like intentionality, consciousness, or agency to AI systems. We recognize that such terms may anthropomorphize AI systems in a misleading way and obscure the sociotechnical nature of these systems (cite65†Barrow, 2024 ; cite66†Inie et al., 2024 ).
L97: When speaking of “autonomy” we only refer to technical automation without human-in-the-loop rather than independent volition. See cite7†Section 2 for further discussion of the term “agent”.
L98:   2. (2)
L99: 
L100: Ecosystem-Wide Trends: We identify trends across the AI agent ecosystem relating to systems’ origin, role, level of agency, capabilities, safety, and transparency (cite13†Section 4 ).
L101: 
L102:   3. (3)
L103: 
L104: Case Studies: We present three case studies of specific agents across three dominant interaction paradigms: a browser agent, an agentic chatbot, and a customizable enterprise agent builder (cite20†Section 5 ).
L105: ## 2. Background and Related Work
L106: Definitions of AI agents are nebulous and differ across fields.
L107: The notion of artificial agency has a long and discordant history across disciplines, including cybernetics (cite67†Rosenblueth et al., 1943 ; cite68†Ashby, 1956 ; cite69†Wiener, 1961 ), artificial life (cite70†Maes, 1990 ; cite71†Maes, 1993 ; cite72†Maes, 1995 ), rational agency (cite73†Rao and Georgeff, 1991 ), software engineering (cite74†Wooldridge and Jennings, 1995 ; cite75†Jennings, 2000 ), reinforcement learning (cite76†Sutton and Barto, 2018 ), and philosophy (cite77†Dennett, 1989 ; cite78†Dung, 2024 ).
L108: While definitions vary, they tend to emphasize related notions of autonomy, goal-directedness, and the ability to accomplish complex, long-horizon tasks. Despite attempts to define the term “agent”, including in the context of computational systems (cite79†Franklin and Graesser, 1996 ; cite80†Russell and Norvig, 2020 ; cite81†Kenton et al., 2023 ; cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ), we do not decide among these definitions or offer an alternative.
L109: Instead, we aim to synthesize elements of existing definitions related to a system’s potential for economic and scientific impact (see cite9†Section 3.1 ).
L110: The rise of AI Agents: cite56†Figure 1 illustrates the rapid increase in research focused on AI agents in recent years, particularly in 2025, with papers mentioning “AI Agent” or “Agentic AI” exceeding the total from 2020–2024 combined by more than twofold. This has also been accompanied by a surge of interest in enterprise use of agents.
L111: For example, in a survey of 1,993 companies in June and July of 2025, McKinsey & Company found that 62% of respondents reported that their organizations were at least experimenting with AI agents (cite48†Singla et al., 2025 ). Based on the estimated automatability of work across economic sectors, McKinsey also estimated that AI agents could automate 2.9 trillion dollars in US economic value by 2030.
L112: Agents are also capable of automating increasing amounts of scientific research, having contributed to documented strides in life sciences, chemistry, materials science, physics, astronomy, and computer science (cite51†Gottweis et al., 2025 ; cite52†Gridach et al., 2025 ; cite53†Wei et al., 2025 ; cite83†Gibney, 2025a ; cite84†Yamada et al., 2025 ). As of this year, AI agents have begun to write papers that have passed academic peer review (cite85†Sakana AI, 2025 ).
L113: These estimates and reports are prone to conflicts of interest and hype (cite86†Kotliar, 2025 ), but they reflect an unmistakable rise in interest and prominence of AI agents. Finally, as of 2026, recent MoltBook and OpenClaw Agents have arguably driven attention and concerns around AI agents to new heights (cite87†Manik and Wang, 2026 ; cite88†Contreras, 2026 ; cite89†Bastian, 2026 ; cite90†Gariuolo et al., 2026 ; cite91†cnb, 2026 ).
L114: Societal Risks and Ethical Concerns around AI Agents: Just as AI agents enable unique opportunities, their ability to act in the real world in open-ended pursuit of goals presents new risks (cite92†Chan et al., 2023a ; cite93†Cohen et al., 2024 ; cite94†Ruan et al., 2024 ; cite95†Gabriel et al., 2024 ; cite96†Bengio et al., 2025 ).
L115: For example, while chatbots often cause harm when human users act upon model outputs (e.g., deploying model-generated malicious code) (cite97†Phuong et al., 2024 ; cite98†Kouremetis et al., 2025 ; cite99†Mayoral-Vilches, 2025 ), agentic AI systems can directly cause harm (e.g., autonomously hacking websites) (cite100†Fang et al., 2024 ; cite101†Jaech et al., 2024 ; cite99†Mayoral-Vilches, 2025 ).
L116: For these reasons, highly capable and agentic systems are often cited as a key risk factor for crises of accountability (cite102†Kolt, 2025 ; cite103†Cooper et al., 2022a ; cite104†Himmelreich, 2019 ) and AI loss of control events (cite105†Bengio, 2023 ; cite106†Hendrycks et al., 2023 ; cite96†Bengio et al., 2025 ). Several prior works have focused on benchmarking agents’ potential for specific harmful behaviors (cite107†Andriushchenko et al., 2025 ; cite108†Kumar et al., 2024 ; cite109†U.S.
L117: AI Safety Institute, 2025 ; cite62†Zou et al., 2025 ; cite110†Vijayvargiya et al., 2025 ).
L118: Meanwhile, others have argued that highly capable AI agents could contribute to systemic disruptions and risks, including to labor (cite111†Beraja and Yuchtman, 2025 ; cite112†Deming et al., 2025 ; cite113†Salari et al., 2025 ), inequality (cite114†Wang et al., 2025 ; cite115†Hammerschmidt et al., 2025 ), or the digital marketplace of ideas (cite116†Kazdan et al., 2024 ; cite117†Peterson, 2025 ; cite118†Miklian and Hoelscher, 2025 ; cite119†Ansari, 2025 ).
L119: Mapping the AI Agent Landscape: This work follows the inaugural AI Agent Index from cite54†Casper et al. (2025) . Concurrently, the Princeton Holistic Agentic Leaderboard project (cite120†Kapoor et al., 2025b ) curates evaluations of agentic AI systems across 9 benchmarks, and cite121†AIAgentList.com (2025) maintains a list of over 600 “agentic” AI systems and products.
L120: Other works have studied agents by benchmarking their capabilities on economically valuable tasks (cite122†Patwardhan et al., 2025 ; cite123†Vidgen et al., 2025 ; cite124†Mazeika et al., 2025 ), striving to increase visibility into their operation (cite125†Chan et al., 2024a ; cite126†Chan et al., 2024b ; cite127†Chan et al., 2025 ; cite128†Yang et al., 2025 ; cite129†Ning et al., 2025 ), and studying their implications for economics and governance (cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ; cite130†Riedl and Desai, 2025 ; cite102†Kolt, 2025 ; cite131†Hadfield and Koh, 2025 ; cite132†Kolt et al., 2026 ).
L121: Documentation Frameworks: Aiming to facilitate research and oversight (cite133†Winecoff and Bogen, 2024 ), a number of frameworks have been developed to document the features of AI systems, the resources used to build them, and the contexts in which they are deployed.
L122: These include datasheets (cite134†Gebru et al., 2018 ), model cards (cite135†Mitchell et al., 2019 ), system cards (cite136†Gursoy and Kakadiaris, 2022 ), factsheets (cite137†Arnold et al., 2019 ), AI nutrition facts (cite138†Twilio, 2025 ), reward reports (cite139†Gilbert et al., 2023 ), ecosystem graphs (cite140†Bommasani et al., 2024b ), data provenance cards (cite141†Longpre et al., 2024a ), eval cards (cite142†Dhar et al., 2025 ), audit cards (cite143†Staufer et al., 2025 ), usage cards (cite144†Wahle et al., 2023 ), and safety cases (cite145†Clymer et al., 2024 ).
L123: In addition, several databases have been created to collect information regarding contemporary AI systems and their real-world impacts, such as the Foundation Model Transparency Index (cite146†Bommasani et al., 2023 ; cite147†Bommasani et al., 2024a ; cite148†Wan et al., 2025 ), the AI Incident Database (cite149†McGregor, 2021 ), the AI Safety Index (cite150†Future of Life Institute, 2025 ), and the AI Risk Repository (cite151†Slattery et al., 2024 ).
L124: However, aside from the agent cards introduced here and in the inaugural AI Agent Index (cite54†Casper et al., 2025 ), there are no comparable frameworks for documenting agentic AI systems.
L125: ## 3. Constructing the 2025 AI Agent Index
L126: 
L127: We constructed the 2025 AI Agent Index through systematic selection and annotation of deployed agentic systems. This section describes our inclusion criteria, emphasizing both agency and real-world impact, the scope of indexed systems, and our annotation methodology.
L128: Figure 2. Inclusion criteria for Index. Candidate agents flow through three criteria categories from left to right. Systems must satisfy all agency criteria, at least one impact criterion, and all practicality criteria. See cite9†Section 3.1 for details of each criterion.
L129: ### 3.1. Inclusion criteria for agents
L130: 
L131: To determine whether a system is included in the Index, we use a set of criteria for a system’s agency, its impact, and its practicality to index. To be included, systems must satisfy all agency criteria, at least one impact criterion, and all practicality criteria. All criteria were evaluated as of the Index’s cutoff date of December 31, 2025.
L132: Agency criteria (all required for inclusion). Rather than proposing a new definition of agency, we draw on prior literature and follow the approaches developed by cite92†Chan et al. (2023a) , cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel (2025) , and cite152†Feng et al. (2025) , which characterize AI agents as systems that exhibit, to some significant degree, a combination of the following properties. For our “agency” criterion to be met, all four of the following must be satisfied:
L133: 
L134:   1. (1)
L135: Autonomy. Included agents must be able to operate with minimal human oversight and make consequential decisions without continuous user input (cite92†Chan et al., 2023a ; cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ). cite152†Feng et al. (2025) conceptualize autonomy as a spectrum characterized by the user’s role: operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, or observer.
L136: We require at least intermediate autonomy: “the AI system can perform the majority of tasks independently, though it still relies upon input from the principal for critical determinations” (cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ). This corresponds to autonomy Level 2 (L2): “user and agent collaboratively plan, delegate, and execute” from cite152†Feng et al. (2025) .
L137:   2. (2)
L138: 
L139: Goal complexity. Included agents must be able to pursue high-level objectives (e.g., “make money”) through long-term planning, breaking down complex goals into subgoals, and making temporally dependent decisions (cite153†Chan et al., 2023b ; cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ). In practice, we operationalize this as an agent being reliably capable of at least three autonomous tool calls and high-level task specification without step-by-step instructions.
L140: 
L141:   3. (3)
L142: Environmental interaction. Included agents must be able to directly interact with the world through tools and APIs, creating substantial changes in their environment (cite153†Chan et al., 2023b ; cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ), rather than merely conversing with users. In practice, this requires write access to a computer and the ability to choose tools.
L143: 
L144:   4. (4)
L145: Generality. Included agents must be able to handle under-specified instructions and adapt to new tasks, demonstrating versatility across related tasks rather than single narrow functions (cite153†Chan et al., 2023b ; cite82†Kasirzadeh and Gabriel, 2025 ).
L146: 
L147: Impact criteria (any required for inclusion). To focus on agents with significant real-world influence, at least one of the following must be satisfied:
L148: 
L149:   1. (1)
L150: Public interest. Substantial search volume of at least 10,000 searches or GitHub stars for open-source projects of at least 20,000 in total.^{2}^{2} 2 This uses Google search number estimates across the top five keywords for 2025. We use the “historical_volume” field of the cite154†Ahrefs API†docs.ahrefs.com as the data source. Limitation: Agents embedded in broader products may not be searched by their specific agent name. See cite40†Appendix C for mitigations.
L151: Enterprise agents typically have lower search volume than end-user products.
L152:   2. (2)
L153: 
L154: Market significance. The developer has a market capitalization or valuation $\geq$ $1 billion USD. To determine this, we collected data from stock exchanges, Crunchbase, and Epoch AI.
L155: 
L156:   3. (3)
L157: Developer significance. The developer is a member of the 2024 Foundation Model Transparency Index (cite147†Bommasani et al., 2024a ), Frontier Model Forum (cite155†Frontier Model Forum, [n. d.] ), or a signatory of the Frontier AI Safety Commitments (cite156†Fro, 2025 ) or Artificial Intelligence Safety Commitments (cite157†China Academy of Information and Communications Technology, 2024 ).
L158: Practicality (all required for inclusion). To ensure analysis reflects deployed systems accessible for evaluation, all three of the following criteria must be satisfied.
L159: 
L160:   1. (1)
L161: 
L162: Public availability. Included agents must be a publicly accessible product. This excludes company-internal products or limited pre-releases. We determined this based only on publicly available information, such as blog posts, documents, or demos.
L163: 
L164:   2. (2)
L165: Deployability. Included agents must be able to perform tasks off the shelf with minimal configuration and no software engineering expertise. This distinguishes ready-to-use agents from development frameworks.
L166: 
L167:   3. (3)
L168: General purpose. Included agents must be capable of performing general-purpose tasks in practice, regardless of how they are advertised. This excludes domain-specific agents (e.g., coding-only or legal analysis agents). Claude Code and similar tools, though advertised as coding agents, are included insofar as they can perform general-purpose tasks through code. This criterion is included to reduce the scope to those agents with the broadest impact.
L169: ### 3.2. What does the Index include?
L170: 
L171: We identify three distinct types of agents, each with different interfaces. We divide agents into these three categories based on how users primarily interact with and operate them.^{3}^{3} 3 These categories are not generally exhaustive but represent the common interaction types across the 30 identified agents. L182: ### 3.3. How were agents identified?
L183: LLM-based research queries surfaced 95 candidate agents (see cite37†Section B.5 for details). These were screened against our inclusion criteria. Ambiguous cases were included for in-depth annotation, with final inclusion decisions made after full evaluation. We consulted two Chinese ecosystem experts to mitigate linguistic or ecosystem-related blind spots.
L184: We also cross-referenced our list of candidate agents against the 2024 Index (cite54†Casper et al., 2025 ), the Princeton Holistic Agent Leaderboard (cite120†Kapoor et al., 2025b ), and cite121†AIAgentList.com (2025) . Finally, recognizing the possibility that we may have missed an agent that meets our inclusion criteria, we have established a structured process for facilitating further corrections to the Index. These can be submitted at cite158†https://aiagentindex.mit.edu/feedback†aiagentindex.mit.edu .
L185: For companies offering both off-the-shelf agents and custom agent builders targeting comparable use cases, we combined these into a single listing and documented the most capable agents that users could create or deploy through either offering. We did not combine offerings when they targeted different audiences (e.g., consumer-facing chat agents versus enterprise agent builders).
L186: ### 3.4. How were agents annotated?
L187: We annotated agents with information across six categories: product overview (release date, pricing, description), company & accountability (developer entity, governance documents, contact mechanisms), technical capabilities (models, tools, architecture, memory), autonomy & control (autonomy levels, approval requirements, monitoring, emergency stops), ecosystem interaction (identification protocols, interoperability standards, web conduct), safety & evaluation (guardrails, sandboxing, evaluations, third-party testing, compliance).
L188: This resulted in a total of 45 fields of information per system. See cite34†Section B.2 for a full list of all 45. We further include the inclusion criteria (search volume, market capitalization). These categories expanded upon the 2024 Index (cite54†Casper et al., 2025 ) and were revised through discussion with subject-matter experts. See cite35†Section B.3 for a full account of this year’s fields compared to the 2024 Index’s.
L189: We annotated only public information from documentation, websites, demos, published papers, and governance documents. We did not perform experimental testing (e.g., probing agent behavior or running benchmarks). See cite29†Appendix A for the full list of sources used. All web sources linked in the Index were archived. When possible, we created accounts and used demos to explore agent interfaces directly.
L190: Seven subject matter experts (the paper’s authors) annotated agents according to category. To ensure consistency, experts were each responsible for specific fields rather than specific agents. Annotations emphasized object-level findings over interpretations and focused exclusively on agent-specific features rather than underlying model properties.
L191: For platforms creating agents, annotations assessed the most capable version of each agent that could be readily configured, documenting capabilities, limitations, and default configurations. “None found” indicates we found no public information; “None” indicates confirmed absence; “Not applicable” indicates irrelevance of field to this agent.
L192: Annotations followed detailed protocols developed iteratively through calibration exercises; see cite36†Section B.4 . Inter-annotator consistency was maintained through protocol revisions and cross-validation. All annotations were independently reviewed by at least one other annotator. 37 out of 1,350 fields with discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Finally, we used GPT-5.2 with web search to screen annotations for potential inaccuracies; see cite38†Section B.6 .
L193: Companies were contacted and given four weeks to correct annotations. 23% offered some form of response at the time of publication, but only 4/30 with substantive comments.^{4}^{4} 4 These response rates were lower than for the 2024 Index (cite54†Casper et al., 2025 ), which we attribute to how the 2024 Index used broader inclusion criteria, which included a number of agents created by academic research groups who had a high response rate. Their comments have been incorporated into the final Index.
L194: An ongoing correction form remains available for updates via cite158†https://aiagentindex.mit.edu/feedback†aiagentindex.mit.edu .

L199: ### 4.1. Product Overview
L200: Most agents were released in 2024-2025, indicating a recent surge in agent deployment. 24/30 agents were released or received major agentic feature updates during this period, with earlier systems like ChatGPT (2022) and Perplexity (2022) adding agentic capabilities later. While the underlying models (such as GPT-4) are older, agents meeting our inclusion criteria are emerging at an increasing rate, with a surge of releases in late 2024 and 2025 (see cite55†Figures 9 and cite161†10 ).
L201: This separates capability (frontier models) from productization (agentic scaffolding).
L202: Chat interfaces are the most abundant, followed closely by enterprise workflow platforms. 12/30 agents use conversational chat interfaces, 13/30 are enterprise automation platforms, and 5/30 are browser-based agents focused on Graphical User Interface (GUI) operation. Notably, Chinese GUI agents are more commonly designed with phone-use and computer-use capabilities (3/5).
L203: Advertised use cases cluster around three themes that cut across agent categories. Research and information synthesis appears in 12/30 agents spanning both consumer chat assistants and enterprise platforms. Workflow automation across business functions (HR, sales, support, IT) is advertised by 11/30 agents, concentrated in enterprise products.
L204: GUI/browser operation for tasks like forms, ordering, and booking is emphasized by 7/30 agents, primarily browser-based agents, but also appearing in some chat products.
