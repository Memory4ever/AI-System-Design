Abstract.
Music generation has advanced markedly through multimodal deep learning, enabling models to synthesize audio from text and, more recently, from images. However, existing image-conditioned systems suffer from two fundamental limitations: (i) they are typically trained on natural photographs, limiting their ability to capture the richer semantic, stylistic, and cultural content of artworks; and (ii) most rely on an image-to-text conversion stage, using language as a semantic shortcut that simplifies conditioning but prevents direct visual-to-audio learning. Motivated by these gaps, we introduce 𝒜​rt​𝒮​ound\mathcal{A\textit{rt}S\textit{ound}}, a large-scale multimodal dataset of 105,884 artwork–music pairs enriched with dual-modality captions, obtained by extending 𝒜​rt​𝒢​raph\mathcal{A}\textit{rt}\mathcal{G}\textit{raph} and the Free Music Archive. We further propose 𝒜​rt2​ℳ​us\mathcal{A}\textit{rt2}\mathcal{M}\textit{us}, the first framework explicitly designed for direct artwork-to-music generation, which maps digitized artworks to music without image-to-text translation or language-based semantic supervision. The framework projects visual embeddings into the conditioning space of a latent diffusion model, enabling music synthesis guided solely by visual information. Experimental results show that 𝒜​rt2​ℳ​us\mathcal{A}\textit{rt2}\mathcal{M}\textit{us} generates musically coherent and stylistically consistent outputs that reflect salient visual cues of the source artworks. While absolute alignment scores remain lower than those of text-conditioned systems—as expected given the substantially increased difficulty of removing linguistic supervision—𝒜​rt2​ℳ​us\mathcal{A}\textit{rt2}\mathcal{M}\textit{us} achieves competitive perceptual quality and meaningful cross-modal correspondence. This work establishes direct visual-to-music generation as a distinct and challenging research direction, and provides resources that support applications in multimedia art, cultural heritage, and AI-assisted creative practice.
Keywords: Artwork-to-music generation, Multimodal learning, Cross-modal alignment, Audio synthesis, Diffusion models.
1. Introduction
Music generation has long been one of the most expressive forms of computational creativity. Music combines structure, emotion, and cultural background, making its automatic generation intrinsically challenging. Translating visual artworks into music is even more demanding, not because the two modalities are unrelated, but because their correspondences are indirect and highly structured. Visual art encodes meaning through composition, color, texture, style, and historical context, evoking emotions and narratives that are central to musical expression (Zhao et al., 2020). These shared perceptual and affective dimensions suggest meaningful cross-modal correspondences between images and sound. However, such relationships are rarely one-to-one or directly observable; they emerge from higher-level abstractions shaped by human perception and artistic conventions, making them difficult to model without sufficiently expressive representations.
Recent advances in multimodal deep learning have substantially improved generative models, enabling the synthesis of symbolic music (Shih et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024) and natural audio from text, images, or multimodal inputs (Agostinelli et al., 2023; Copet et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b). Text-based conditioning provides strong semantic grounding, and Large Language Models (LLMs) further reinforce this paradigm by converting heterogeneous inputs into rich textual descriptions (Liu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024). However, two limitations remain.
First, most image-conditioned music generation systems are trained primarily on natural photographs, whose semantics are comparatively literal and object-centric. Such data only partially reflect the symbolic, stylistic, and culturally layered semantics of artworks, limiting the applicability of existing approaches to the artistic domain.
Second, many pipelines rely on an image-to-text conversion stage. While effective, this strategy introduces language as an intermediate semantic bottleneck. Linguistic descriptions inevitably abstract away ambiguity, texture, and stylistic details—elements that often resist concise verbalization but are central to artistic expression.
