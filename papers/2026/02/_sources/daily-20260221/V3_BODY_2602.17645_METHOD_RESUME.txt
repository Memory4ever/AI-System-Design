3.1 Preliminaries and Limitations in Prior Local-level Matching-Based Methods
Local-level matching in 𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack} (Li et al., 2025). Consider a clean source image 𝐗~sou\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{sou}} and a target image 𝐗tar\mathbf{X}_{\text{tar}}. The objective of black-box transfer attacks is to minimally perturb the source image by δ\delta so that the perturbed image 𝐗sou=𝐗~sou+δ\mathbf{X}_{\text{sou}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{sou}}+\delta aligns semantically with the target under an inaccessible black-box model fξf_{\xi}. Due to the inaccessibility of fξf_{\xi}, surrogate models fϕf_{\phi} approximate the semantic alignment via cosine similarity:
arg⁡max𝐗sou⁡CS⁡(fϕ​(𝐗sou),fϕ​(𝐗tar))s.t.∥δ∥p≤ϵ,\arg\max_{\mathbf{X}_{\text{sou}}}\mathrm{CS}(f_{\phi}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{sou}}),f_{\phi}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{tar}}))\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\lVert\delta\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon,
(1)
where CS\mathrm{CS} denotes cosine similarity.
𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack} enhances Eq. (1) using local-level matching. At iteration ii, it applies predefined local transformations 𝒯s\mathcal{T}_{s} and 𝒯t\mathcal{T}_{t} to extract local area 𝐱^is\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{s}_{i} from the source 𝐗sou\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{sou}} and 𝐱^it\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{t}_{i} from the target 𝐗tar\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{tar}}, respectively. These transformations satisfy essential properties, such as spatial overlap and diversified coverage of extracted local regions {𝐱^i}\{\hat{\bf x}_{i}\} (Li et al., 2025). Formally, the local-level matching optimizes:
ℳ𝒯s,𝒯t=𝔼f​ϕj∼ϕ​[CS​(fϕj​(𝐱^is),fϕj​(𝐱^it))],\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{s},\mathcal{T}_{t}}=\mathbb{E}_{f{\phi_{j}}\sim\phi}[\text{CS}(f_{\phi_{j}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{s}_{i}),f_{\phi_{j}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{t}_{i}))],
(2)
where fϕjf_{\phi_{j}} is sampled from an ensemble of surrogate models ϕ\phi. Intuitively, matching local image regions instead of entire images enhances the semantic precision of perturbations by directing optimization towards semantically significant details. Despite its effectiveness, 𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack} encounters a critical challenge of unexpectedly low gradient similarity, which we investigate in detail next.
Extremely low gradient overlap.

Our finding reveals an exponential decay that plateaus below 0.10.1 once the overlap is smaller than 0.80 IoU.
We find that this unexpectedly low gradient overlap mainly stems from two factors:
ViTs’ inherent translation sensitivity and an overlooked asymmetry in the local
matching framework. We first examine the translation effect.
1) Patch-wise, spike-like gradient sensitive to translation. Because ViTs tokenize images on a fixed, non-overlapping grid, even sub-pixel changes each patch’s token mix. These token changes ripple through self-attention, altering weights and redirecting gradients for all tokens, so the resulting pixel-level gradient pattern diverges sharply. Worse, gradient magnitudes are uneven. Therefore, even similar patterns but missing a few pixels might completely break gradient similarity (Fig. 3(b)).
2) Asymmetric Transform Branches.
In 𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack}, both the source and target images are cropped, yet playing distinct roles. Cropping the source acts directly in pixel space: it rearranges patch embeddings and attention weights in the forward pass, ending up with guidance of different views. By contrast, cropping the target solely translates the target representation, thereby shifting the reference embedding in feature space. One sculpts the perturbation, while another moves the goalpost, formulating asymmetric matching. 𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack} overlooked this and implementations target translation alternate between a radical crop and an identity map, struggles between explore-exploitation trade-off and potentially risk in high variance of target embedding.
3.2 Asymmetric Matching over Expectation
To mitigate the issues above, we begin by systematically reformulating the original objective function as an expectation over local transformations within an asymmetric matching framework:
min∥𝐗sou∥p≤ϵ⁡𝔼𝒯∼𝒟,y∼𝒴​[ℒ⁡(f⁡(𝒯⁡(𝐗sou)),y)],\min_{\lVert\mathbf{X}_{\text{sou}}\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\mathcal{Y}}\left[\mathcal{L}(f(\mathcal{T}({\bf X}_{\text{sou}})),y)\right],
(3)
where 𝒟\mathcal{D} represents the distribution of local transformations, and 𝒴\mathcal{Y} denotes the distribution over target semantics. ∥⋅∥p\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p} is ℓp\ell_{p} constraint for imperceptibility. Conceptually, this formulation corresponds to embedding specific semantic content yy into a locally transformed area 𝒯⁡(𝐗sou)\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{sou}}), thus highlighting the intrinsic asymmetry compared to 𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack}’s original formulation. Within this framework, our proposed enhancements, i.e., Multi-Crop Alignment (MCA) and Auxiliary Target Alignment (ATA), can be interpreted as strategies to improve the accuracy of the expectation estimation and the sampling quality of the semantic distribution 𝒴\mathcal{Y}.

3.4 Improved Sampling Quality via Auxiliary Target Alignment
Selecting a representative target embedding y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y} is challenging because the underlying distribution 𝒴\mathcal{Y} is not observable.
𝙼​-​𝙰𝚝𝚝𝚊𝚌𝚔\mathtt{M\text{-}Attack} mitigates this by seeding at the unaltered target embedding f⁡(𝐗tar)f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{tar}}) and exploring its vicinity with transformed views f​(𝒯t​(𝐗tar))f(\mathcal{T}_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{tar}})) thereby sketching a locally semantic manifold that serves as a proxy for 𝒴\mathcal{Y}. However, the exploration–exploitation trade-off remains problematic. Radical transformations leap too far, dragging yy outside the genuine target region; conservative transformations, while semantically faithful, barely shift the embedding, leaving the optimization starved of informative signal.
To stabilize this process, we introduce PP auxiliary images {𝐗aux(p)}p=1P\{\mathbf{X}_{\text{aux}}^{(p)}\}_{p=1}^{P} as an auxiliary set that acts as additional anchors, collectively forming a richer sub-manifold of aligned embeddings. During each update, we apply a mild random transformation 𝒯~∼𝒟~\tilde{\mathcal{{T}}}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}} to every anchor, nudging the ensemble in a coherent yet restrained manner and thus providing low-variance, information-rich gradients for optimization.
