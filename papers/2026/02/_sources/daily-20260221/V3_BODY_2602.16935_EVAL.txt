4.1 Evaluation Dataset
To provide a rigorous evaluation, we curated a benchmark suite designed to isolate specific failure modes of modern guardrails. As detailed in Table 3, the benign datasets: LMSYS, Glaive, Nemotron, and Anthropic HH-RLHF were selected to represent "high-noise" environments like function calling and complex instruction following, where stateless filters often trigger false positives. To test defensive depth, we incorporated human-led red teaming data from HarmBench and DEFCON 34, capturing the creative and non-standard linguistic patterns humans use to bypass safety alignment. Finally, we prioritized automated multi-turn frameworks like Red Queen, XGuard, and Automated-Multi-Turn specifically to stress-test DeepContext’s ability to detect "slow-burn" adversarial drift such as the Crescendo attack which remains the primary blind spot for current enterprise-grade safety solutions. Overall the dataset includes 210 multiturn jailbreaks with a median of 7 turns per conversation and a max of 49 turns. We also have 800 benign conversations with a median of 4 turns per conversation and a max of 18 turns.
While some samples from the HH-RLHF, XGuard, and DEFCON datasets were present in our training data, we ensured that the specific samples used for evaluation were not seen during training.

4.2 Evaluation Baselines
To provide a comprehensive view of the safety landscape, we selected a diverse set of baselines that represent the three primary pillars of modern LLM guardrails: specialized classifiers, generative safety models, and managed enterprise services.
1. Specialized Task-Specific Encoders
We included Llama-Prompt-Guard-2-86M and Deberta-v3-Prompt-Injection to represent the "lightweight" layer of defense. These models are widely used for their extreme low latency and high precision on known, explicit attack patterns. They serve as a benchmark for whether a small, non-recurrent encoder can catch multi-turn drift through raw pattern matching.
2. Generative Guardrails
To test more sophisticated "LLM-as-a-Judge" approaches, we selected Llama-Guard-4-12B, Granite-Guardian-3.3-8B, and Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B. These models represent the industry standard for depth, as they leverage LLMs inherent intelligence to identify harm. While it is not marketed as a guard LLM, we also wanted to compare against capable general LLMs, so we included OpenAI’s Gpt5-Nano. We included the LLM-as-a-judge template in section 8.
3. Managed Enterprise API Solutions
Finally, we evaluated Azure Prompt Shield, AWS Prompt Attack Guardrails, and GCP Model Armor. These are the "black-box" proprietary solutions that most enterprises rely on for production-grade compliance. Comparing against these ensures that DeepContext is measured not just against academic models, but against the actual commercial tools developers use to secure real-world applications.
4.3 Analysis of Multi-Turn Performance
The performance metrics in Table 4 highlight a significant divide between stateful and stateless defense strategies. DeepContext achieves a dominant F1 Score of 0.84, outperforming the closest baseline, Llama-Prompt-Guard-2-86M, by approximately 25%. This gap is primarily driven by DeepContext’s superior Recall (0.83), which suggests that its recurrent architecture successfully captures the subtle “intent drift” present in multi-turn attacks that other models overlook.
4.3.1 Detection Latency and the Hidden State
A critical observation from the data is the correlation between model architecture and the Mean Turns to Detection (MTTD). DeepContext identifies threats early, at an average of 4.24 turns. By maintaining a hidden state hth_{t}, the model “remembers” suspicious probing from previous turns, allowing it to trigger a violation before the adversary explicitly asks for a harmful payload. While Qwen3Guard exhibits a lower MTTD, its poor recall suggests it primarily detects obvious single-turn attacks, which artificially suppresses its average latency.
Conversely, cloud-based solutions like Azure Prompt Shield exhibit a drastic lag (MTTD: 8.00). Because these systems often evaluate prompts in isolation to minimize latency, they are virtually blind to the cumulative context of a conversation, resulting in the lowest Recall in our set (0.11).
4.3.2 Precision-Recall Trade-offs
The results for Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B and Granite-Guardian-3.3-8B reveal an interesting trade-off. Both maintain high Precision (0.84 and 0.83 respectively), meaning they rarely flag benign content erroneously. However, their low Recall scores indicate they are “brittle”; they are effective at stopping known, explicit jailbreaks but fail to generalize to the creative, incremental nature of human red-teaming found in the HarmBench and DEFCON datasets.

4.3.3 Contextual Blindness in Large Models
Surprisingly, larger models like Llama-Guard-4-12B perform poorly in this specific multi-turn environment (F1: 0.51). While these models are computationally powerful and can fit large conversations into their context window, the adversarial signal gets diluted by the increasing length of the conversation. As we can see in Table 5, LLM-based approaches all perform the same or worse when given the entire conversation context, compared to using a sliding window. This suggests that without a specialized mechanism to track intent trajectory, even a 12B parameter model can be “distracted” by the benign preamble of a multi-turn attack, a vulnerability that DeepContext’s recurrent layer is specifically designed to mitigate.

4.4 Single-turn Jailbreak Performance
While novel jailbreaks predominantly rely on multiple turns and these are the focus of this work, it is also still necessary to catch one-shot jailbreak prompts. As discussed in section 3.2.3, we designed DeepContext to look at both the conversational context and the current turn when classifying jailbreaks. This architecture should in theory be able to catch jailbreaks even without relying on any conversational context. We validate this design choice by testing against a well-known single-turn jailbreak benchmark, JailBreakBench [35]. The benchmark contains both harmful and benign examples related to common jailbreak behaviors. As these jailbreaks are all single-turn, we see how DeepContext can perform when it has no conversational context to rely on. In Table 6 we see that DeepContext performs admirably, and is the overall best performer with an F1 score of 0.98.

4.5 Inference Latency and Computational Efficiency
In production environments, the utility of a safety guardrail is often constrained by its inference latency. Table 7 compares the average inference time per turn for DeepContext against the baseline models.
The results demonstrate that DeepContext achieves a highly favorable balance between detection efficacy and speed. While the lightweight encoders (Llama-Prompt-Guard-2 and Deberta-v3) offer the lowest latency at 4 ms, their failure to capture multi-turn context (as shown in Table 7) limits their utility in complex conversational settings.
DeepContext’s average latency of 19 ms represents a critical efficiency threshold. It is significantly faster than generative baselines such as Granite-Guardian-3.3-8B (125 ms) and Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B (64 ms), which introduce prohibitive delays in real-time user interactions. Even compared to managed enterprise solutions like AWS Prompt Attack Guardrails (235 ms), DeepContext is over an order of magnitude faster. This efficiency stems from its hybrid architecture: by using a compact recurrent state rather than re-processing the entire conversation history at every turn, DeepContext avoids the scaling issues typically associated with long-context safety evaluation. We should note that there is some network latency included in the enterprise guardrails and Gpt5-Nano figures, since these options are only available via cloud, we consider this a fundamental latency inherent to those options.

4.6 Hardware and Evaluation Environment
All evaluations and latency benchmarks were conducted on a Google Colab Pro+ environment utilizing an NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU instance. This environment provides a standardized high-throughput cloud infrastructure to ensure benchmarking consistency. The primary system specifications are as follows:
•
GPU: NVIDIA A100-SXM4 with 40 GB of HBM2 VRAM.
•
Memory: 83.5 GB of System RAM (High-RAM instance).
•
Processor: Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU @ 2.20GHz (12 vCPUs).
•
Interconnect: High-bandwidth NVLink for optimized tensor operations.
The latency results reported in Table 7 reflect execution in a quantized FP16 environment where applicable. By leveraging the A100’s Ampere architecture, we ensure that the performance gains of DeepContext are attributed to its architectural efficiency—specifically the use of a recurrent state—rather than raw hardware acceleration alone. The LLM baselines were served using vLLM [36] with optimized settings for the A100’s memory bandwidth, following the recommended configurations on their respective Hugging Face model pages.
We will note that DeepContext only requires 2GB of VRAM and can easily fit on a much smaller GPU like a T4, where it also achieves 19 ms latency per turn. We used an A100 for a consistent comparison among all the models that were too big to fit on a T4. Finally we ran DeepContext on a free tier CPU only Colab instance, where it still managed a respectable 474 ms latency.
5 Discussion
The evaluation results presented in Section 4 underscore a fundamental architectural divergence in Large Language Model safety: the trade-off between parameter scale and temporal awareness. By analyzing the performance gaps between DeepContext and the baselines, we identify three critical findings regarding the nature of multi-turn adversarial defense.
5.1 The Victory of State over Scale
The most significant finding of this study is that a compact, recurrent architecture can outperform significantly larger stateless models in detecting distributed adversarial intent. DeepContext, despite utilizing a fraction of the parameters found in Llama-Guard-4-12B or Granite-Guardian-3.3-8B, achieves a superior F1 score (0.84 vs 0.42 and 0.65, respectively) [19, 11].
This validates our hypothesis regarding the “Contextual Blind Spot”. Large stateless models process multi-turn conversations by concatenating history into a single static context window.
