5 Experiments
5.1 Experiment setups
Models and datasets.
We evaluate the performance of ZO optimization on fine-tuning tasks for both LLMs and ViTs. For LLMs, we primarily consider
LlaMA3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and OPT-1.3B/13B (Zhang et al., 2022a) on the SuperGLUE benchmark (Wang et al., 2019). For ViTs, we fine-tune ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/16 (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) on the CIFAR-10/100 datasets (Krizhevsky et al., 2009).
Baselines and evaluations.
We compare the proposed ZO-Muon with the following stateful ZO optimization baselines, which were primarily developed for large-model fine-tuning. These include MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), SparseMeZO (S-MeZO) (Liu et al., 2025b), HiZOO (Zhao et al., 2025b), LOZO (Chen et al., 2025a), SubZero (Yu et al., 2025), and Subspace-MeZO developed in Sec. 3 prior to integration with Muon.
We do not include other recent ZO optimization methods such as FZOO (Dang et al., 2025) and MUZO (Peng et al., 2025) in our comparison, as we were unable to find open-source and reproducible implementations for these approaches.
In addition to ZO approaches, we also include FO baselines, Adam (Kingma, 2014) and LoRA (with Adam) (Hu et al., 2022).
Implementation details.
For ZO-Muon, we perform a hyperparameter grid search over the projection rank r∈{64,128}r\in\{64,128\} across all settings, following the rank selection rationale in Sec. 4. We also search over Nq∈{4,8,16}N_{q}\in\{4,8,16\}, consistent with our justification for using Nq>1N_{q}>1 in Sec. 4. Empirically, we find that setting Nq=16N_{q}=16 and r=64r=64 for ViTs, and using Nq=4N_{q}=4 with r∈{64,128}r\in\{64,128\} for LLMs depending on the task, consistently yields strong performance.
To ensure a fair comparison with other baselines, we scale the number of training steps inversely with NqN_{q}, so that ZO-Muon does not incur additional query or runtime overhead. The projection matrix 𝐏\mathbf{P} is resampled via QR decomposition every 100 iterations (i.e., lazily updated);
For the msign\mathrm{msign} operation, we use the NS (Newton–Schulz) iteration unless otherwise specified.
We set the training batch size to 16 for all LLM experiments, and to 64 and 256 for ViT fine-tuning on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, respectively. The rationale for these choices is supported by the experimental results presented later.
Additional experiment setups, including settings of baselines, are detailed in Appendix F.

ZO-Muon achieves superior ZO fine-tuning accuracy for both LLMs and ViTs.
In Tab. 1, we compare the LLM and ViT fine-tuning performance of ZO-Muon with FO and ZO baselines (listed by rows) on LlaMA3-8B and OPT-13B, as well as ViT-B and ViT-L, where each column corresponds to a fine-tuning task.
As shown, ZO-Muon consistently improves fine-tuning accuracy over existing ZO baselines. In particular, for higher-complexity tasks such as RTE and WiC in LLM fine-tuning and CIFAR-100 in ViT fine-tuning, ZO-Muon achieves substantial gains, elevating ZO optimization to a new level, narrowing the gap to FO baselines. Notably, on ViT-L CIFAR-100 fine-tuning, ZO-Muon outperforms the second-best ZO method (Subspace-MeZO) by 7.7%7.7\% and MeZO by 25.1%25.1\% in accuracy.
In addition, we observe that Subspace-MeZO consistently ranks among the top-performing ZO baselines, and that ZO-Muon further improves upon it. This indicates that subspace ZO and Muon’s GO (gradient orthogonalization) provide complementary benefits for improving overall ZO optimization.
