Abstract
We identify a structural weakness in current large language model (LLM) alignment: modern refusal mechanisms are fail-open.
While existing approaches encode refusal behaviors across multiple latent features, suppressing a single dominant feature—via prompt-based jailbreaks—can cause alignment to collapse, leading to unsafe generation.
Motivated by this, we propose fail-closed alignment as a design principle for robust LLM safety: refusal mechanisms should remain effective even under partial failures via redundant, independent causal pathways.
We present a concrete instantiation of this principle: a progressive alignment framework that iteratively identifies and ablates previously learned refusal directions, forcing the model to reconstruct safety along new, independent subspaces.
Across four jailbreak attacks, we achieve the strongest overall robustness while mitigating over-refusal and preserving generation quality, with small computational overhead.
Our mechanistic analyses confirm that models trained with our method encode multiple, causally independent refusal directions that prompt-based jailbreaks cannot suppress simultaneously, providing empirical support for fail-closed alignment as a principled foundation for robust LLM safety.
Keywords: Machine Learning, ICML
1 Introduction
In traditional systems, safety mechanisms are explicitly designed with well-defined structure and failure modes, often ensuring that partial failures default to denial rather than continued operation.
In contrast, alignment in large language models (LLMs) is enforced implicitly through training objectives such as supervised fine-tuning and RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), without constraints on how refusal behavior is structured internally.
As a result, learned refusal mechanisms emerge in unconstrained forms, with no guarantees of redundancy or robustness under partial failure.
Recent work has shown that refusal behavior spans multiple features in representation space (Wollschläger et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025; Abbas et al., 2025; Piras et al., 2025), rather than a single direction as initially hypothesized (Arditi et al., 2024).
This apparent complexity might suggest that refusal is already robust to partial failure.
However, as we demonstrate in §2, the current representation of refusal does not confer robustness.
Under adversarial pressure from prompt-based jailbreaks (Zou et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024), refusal effectively collapses to a single dominant direction, and suppressing it is sufficient to bypass alignment entirely (Figure 1).
This reveals a structural weakness: current alignment mechanisms are fail-open—partial failure defaults to unsafe compliance.
Contributions.
In this paper, we ask: How should refusal mechanisms be structured so that safety remains effective under partial failure induced by prompt-based jailbreaks?
To this end, we propose fail-closed alignment as a design principle for robust LLM safety: refusal should be distributed across multiple independent, causally effective pathways, such that no single direction is a point of failure.
We present a concrete instantiation of this principle: a progressive alignment algorithm that explicitly prevents safety from collapsing into a single vulnerable direction.
At each iteration, our method identifies the dominant refusal direction, ablates it along with all previously discovered directions, and retrains the model. This forces the model to reconstruct refusal along new, independent, and redundant subspaces.
Figure 1:
Refusal in current LLMs is dependent on a single dominant feature that jailbreaks suppress.
(Left) ASR on 200 harmful prompts before and after ablating the DIM refusal feature across four models.
Ablation causes ASR to spike, showing that refusal depends on a single linear direction.
(Right) Average cosine similarity of the refusal feature in the activation space on the harmful prompts under different jailbreaks.
All attacks substantially reduce activation of the refusal feature, indicating they largely succeed by suppressing the same internal mechanism.
Details on the prompts, prompt-based jailbreaks, and ASR calculation are provided in §4.1.
We evaluate against four prompt-based jailbreaks across four models.
Our method achieves the strongest overall robustness, reducing attack success rates by 92–97% and outperforming all baselines on both white- and black-box attacks.
These gains come without sacrificing utility: we achieve the highest compliance rates on benign prompts among robust methods (86% on average) while preserving generation quality.
Fail-closed alignment also generalizes to parameter-efficient settings: LoRA fine-tuning with ∼\sim5% of parameters matches the performance of full fine-tuning.
