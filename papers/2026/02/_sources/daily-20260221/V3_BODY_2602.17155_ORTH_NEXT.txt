4 ZO-Muon: Boosting Subspace ZO via Gradient Orthogonalization
In this section, we show that gradient orthogonalization (GO), the core ingredient underlying the recently popular optimizer Muon (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a), can be used to further boost the subspace ZO method in Sec. 3.
Gradient orthogonalization in Muon: From FO to ZO, with insights from the failure of ZO-Muon-V0.
Before introducing ZO-Muon, we briefly recall the key idea behind FO Muon. Muon can be interpreted as a steepest-descent method under a spectral-norm constraint (Bernstein and Newhouse, 2024), or more broadly as a spectral optimization method that exploits the matrix-level spectral structure of descent directions rather than their entry-wise information.
Concretely, given the iterate 𝐗t\mathbf{X}_{t} of the optimization variable 𝐗\mathbf{X} at iteration tt and the learning rate ηt\eta_{t}, the basic Muon (without momentum) takes the form (Jordan et al., 2024):

As shown in (9), the msign\mathrm{msign} operation plays the role of GO by equalizing all active singular directions and producing a spectrally isotropic update. This leads to the key benefit in Muon, which enables the model update to fully explore and exploit the spectral descent directions. In addition, Muon provides an efficient numerical approximation of msign⁡(⋅)\mathrm{msign}(\cdot) via Newton–Schulz (NS) iterations (Bernstein and Newhouse, 2024), avoiding the need to compute a full SVD.
Inspired by Muon’s spectral advantage, one may naturally ask whether exploiting such spectral information in the ZO gradient estimator can also bring similar benefits as in FO optimization. Accordingly, by replacing 𝐆t\mathbf{G}_{t} with its standard RGE counterpart in (2), we obtain a vanilla ZO-Muon variant, which we denote as ZO-Muon-V0, i.e., applying GO directly to MeZO.

However, we find that ZO-Muon-V0 does not provide additional benefits over the GO-absent baseline, MeZO. As shown in Fig. 5, when fine-tuning OPT models on SST-2, ZO-Muon-V0 underperforms MeZO in both accuracy and runtime efficiency under the same query budget.
This accuracy underperformance is likely because directly applying GO to noisy ZO gradient estimates amplifies spurious directions during matrix whitening, thereby degrading optimization performance.
From a runtime perspective, performing the NS iterations for GO also introduces additional computational overhead compared to MeZO, i.e., RGE-based ZO-SGD. In addition to ZO-Muon-V0, we also evaluate the ineffectiveness of JAGUAR-Muon (Petrov et al., 2025), another direct attempt to integrate Muon into ZO optimization; see Fig. A1 in Appendix B.
ZO-Muon: How and why.
As motivated above, it is nontrivial to effectively leverage Muon’s spectral advantages in the ZO setting. In what follows, we show that the subspace ZO formulation in Sec. 3 is more suitable for integration with Muon.
Our rationale is that Subspace RGE (5) induces a low-rank structure by estimating gradients in a low-dimensional subspace. When combined with GO, this effectively acts as a low-rank spectral filter that suppresses non-informative noise in the RGE. From an efficiency standpoint, the cost of the msign\mathrm{msign} operation in (9) scales with the matrix rank (He et al., 2025), so integrating GO with subspace ZO also improves computational efficiency. As confirmed by ZO-Muon in Fig. 5, an accuracy-efficiency win-win emerges when GO meets subspace ZO.
We next formally develop ZO-Muon.
Recalling the projection-based view used to derive Subspace RGE in (6) from (4), we can integrate GO (i.e., the msign\mathrm{msign} operation) with the subspace RGE in (5) on the lower-dimensional variable 𝐙\mathbf{Z}, and then lift the resulting gradient-orthogonalized RGE back to the full space w.r.t. the original variable 𝐗\mathbf{X}.
