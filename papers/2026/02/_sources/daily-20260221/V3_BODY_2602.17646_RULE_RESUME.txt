4 Problem Formulation: Multi-Round Collaboration
In this section, we introduce an interaction protocol and a framework for user-defined requirements in multi-round human–AI collaboration.
4.1 Interaction Protocol
We propose a human–AI interaction protocol in an online setting, where problems arrive sequentially over time and are handled one at a time.
At each time step t=1,2,…t=1,2,\dots, a new problem instance PtP_{t} arrives, representing a task the human seeks to solve, together with an unknown ground-truth answer
yt∈𝒴y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}22

The label space 𝒴\mathcal{Y} could itself be a function of tt in our framework, but for simplicity we avoid introducing that notation.. The correct answer yty_{t} is unknown to both the human and the AI during the interaction.
For each problem PtP_{t}, the human and the AI engage in a multi-round dialogue. The interaction consists of Nt∈ℕN_{t}\in\mathbb{N} rounds, where the stopping time NtN_{t} is chosen by the human. At a high level, the human and the AI alternate between proposing candidate answer sets and exchanging textual messages, after which the true answer is eventually revealed.
Formally, for each day tt and round r=1,…,Ntr=1,\dots,N_{t}:
•
the human proposes a pair
(Ht,r,Ut,r),(H_{t,r},\,U_{t,r}),
where Ht,r⊆𝒴tH_{t,r}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t} is a set of candidate answers and Ut,rU_{t,r} is a textual message;
•
the AI responds with a pair
(Ct,r,At,r),(C_{t,r},\,A_{t,r}),
where Ct,r⊆𝒴tC_{t,r}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t} is a prediction set refining the human’s proposal and At,rA_{t,r} is a textual response.
At the end of day tt, after the interaction terminates, the ground-truth answer yty_{t} is revealed.
The interaction protocol consists of four components: human textual messages {Ut,r}\{U_{t,r}\}, AI textual messages {At,r}\{A_{t,r}\}, human prediction sets {Ht,r}\{H_{t,r}\}, and AI prediction sets {Ct,r}\{C_{t,r}\}. Among these, our algorithmic focus is exclusively on designing the AI prediction sets. The remaining components are treated as black boxes: we make no assumptions about how human or AI textual behavior evolves, nor about how the human updates their prediction sets in response to the AI. We briefly argue why this modeling choice is both natural and useful.
The textual components (Ut,r,At,r)(U_{t,r},A_{t,r}) may depend arbitrarily on the full interaction history and may convey any information available to the respective party, such as clarifications, questions, intermediate reasoning, or external context. In particular, the AI’s textual responses can be viewed as those of a fixed, pre-trained language model capable of engaging in a dialogue. Treating these textual components as black boxes allows us to decouple language generation from uncertainty quantification and to remain agnostic to how conversational behavior adapts to the interaction.
While the textual components facilitate the flow of information between the two parties, the prediction sets play a distinct role: they represent each party’s uncertainty about the ground-truth answer, which is ultimately the most consequential quantity for decision making and downstream action. Through principled communication of uncertainty via prediction sets, both parties can calibrate how much trust to place in each other’s inputs, determining whether the collaboration is both harmless and productive.
Accordingly, in the remainder of this paper we focus on how the dynamics between the human sets {Ht,r}\{H_{t,r}\} and the AI sets {Ct,r}\{C_{t,r}\} should be governed. From an algorithm-design standpoint, we have no direct control over human behavior, and we therefore treat the human prediction sets as black-box objects provided to the system. Therefore, our focus is exclusively on designing the AI’s prediction sets.
We now turn to the question of how the AI’s prediction sets should be constrained so that they are maximally beneficial to humans in a multi-round setting.
4.2 User-Defined Collaboration Requirements
In multi-round interactions, counterfactual harm and complementarity no longer admit a single canonical definition. Even when the high-level goal is clear, preserving correct human judgments and complementing incorrect ones, the precise operational meaning can vary across applications.
Let us first focus on counterfactual harm. To gain intuition, fix a day tt and a round r≥2r\geq 2. Consider the human transcript of sets up to this point,
Ht,1:r:=(Ht,1,…,Ht,r)H_{t,1:r}:=(H_{t,1},\dots,H_{t,r}), and the AI set at round rr, Ct,r⊆𝒴tC_{t,r}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t}.
From the perspective of defining a counterfactual harm requirement at round rr, the high level goal is to enforce that the AI includes the truth at this round, i.e., that 𝟏{yt∈Ct,r}\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}\in C_{t,r}\} is likely, conditional on an event indicating that the human “had” the truth in some meaningful sense. For instance, one might condition on:
- 𝟏{yt∈Ht,r}\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}\in H_{t,r}\}, that is human included the truth at round rr.
- 𝟏{yt∈Ht,r∩Ht,r−1}\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}\in H_{t,r}\cap H_{t,r-1}\}, that is human insisted on truth over the last two rounds so far.
- 𝟏​{∃r′≤r:yt∈Ht,r′}\mathbf{1}\{\exists\,r^{\prime}\leq r:\ y_{t}\in H_{t,r^{\prime}}\}, that is the human proposed the truth at least once.
Many other conditions are possible. Different choices can lead to qualitatively different collaboration dynamics, including different AI set sizes. This motivates a general framework in which counterfactual harm is defined with respect to a user-specified rule, depending on user’s preferences in different applications.
Rule-Based Formulation.
We formalize user-defined counterfactual-harm through a rule, a binary function that specifies when the counterfactual-harm constraint is applied. Concretely, a counterfactual-harm rule maps a label yy and a complete transcript prefix of human sets for the day, and a round index rr to {0,1}\{0,1\}:
RCH(y,Ht,1:Nt,r)∈{0,1},R^{\mathrm{CH}}\!\bigl(y,H_{t,1:N_{t}},r\bigr)\in\{0,1\},
where Ht,1:Nt:=(Ht,1,…,Ht,Nt)H_{t,1:N_{t}}:=(H_{t,1},\dots,H_{t,N_{t}}) denotes the full transcript prefix of human sets over all NtN_{t} round of day tt, and r∈{1,…,Nt}r\in\{1,\dots,N_{t}\} indexes the round under consideration. We interpret RCH(y,Ht,1:Nt,r)=1R^{\mathrm{CH}}(y,H_{t,1:N_{t}},r)=1 as the rule “triggering” at round rr of day tt if yy were the correct label. The examples above correspond to different choices of RCHR^{\mathrm{CH}}.
Allowing the rule to depend on the full transcript Ht,1:NtH_{t,1:N_{t}} allows to capture rules that involve global properties of the interaction, such as whether round rr is the terminal round (r=Ntr=N_{t}), or whether the human ever included a label across the entire dialogue. Since rules are evaluated post-hoc, after the interaction on day tt has concluded and the ground truth yty_{t} is revealed, the full transcript is available at evaluation time.
Given a rule, we say that counterfactual harm occurs on day tt if there exists a round in which the rule triggers but the AI fails to include the truth:
EtCH=maxr∈{1,…,Nt}RCH(yt,Ht,1:Nt,r)𝟏{yt∉Ct,r}E^{\mathrm{CH}}_{t}=\max_{r\in\{1,\dots,N_{t}\}}R^{\mathrm{CH}}(y_{t},H_{t,1:N_{t}},r)\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}\notin C_{t,r}\}
Accordingly, the cumulative number of counterfactual-harm violations up to time TT is
∑t=1TEtCH\sum_{t=1}^{T}E^{\mathrm{CH}}_{t}.
A similar construction applies to complementarity. We formalize user-defined complementarity requirements through a verifiable rule
RComp(y,Ht,1:Nt,r)∈{0,1},R^{\mathrm{Comp}}\!\bigl(y,H_{t,1:N_{t}},r\bigr)\in\{0,1\},
where RComp(y,Ht,1:Nt,r)=1R^{\mathrm{Comp}}(y,H_{t,1:N_{t}},r)=1 indicates that, if yy were the correct label, the AI is required to include it at round rr in order to complement the human’s proposal.
Given a complementarity rule, we say that a complementarity error occurs on day tt if there exists a round in which the rule triggers but the AI fails to include the truth:
EtComp=maxr∈{1,…,Nt}RComp(yt,Ht,1:Nt,r)𝟏{yt∉Ct,r}.E^{\mathrm{Comp}}_{t}=\max_{r\in\{1,\dots,N_{t}\}}R^{\mathrm{Comp}}(y_{t},H_{t,1:N_{t}},r)\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}\notin C_{t,r}\}.
The cumulative number of complementarity failures up to time TT is then given by
∑t=1TEtComp.\sum_{t=1}^{T}E^{\mathrm{Comp}}_{t}.
The goal of multi-round human–AI collaboration is to design the AI prediction sets {Ct,r}\{C_{t,r}\} so as to control counterfactual harm and complementarity errors over time. Concretely, for user-specified tolerances ε,δ∈(0,1)\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1), We seek to enforce the following constraints for all horizons T≥1T\geq 1:
1T​∑t=1TEtCH≤εand1T​∑t=1TEtComp≤δ.\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}E^{\mathrm{CH}}_{t}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}E^{\mathrm{Comp}}_{t}\leq\delta.
5 Algorithm and Guarantees
In this section, we present our online algorithm for multi-round human–AI collaboration. Throughout this section, we follow the interaction protocol and asssume two rule functions RCHR^{\mathrm{CH}} and RCompR^{\mathrm{Comp}} are pre-specified by the user, all of which discussed in Section 4.
Recall that the rules R(y,Ht,1:Nt,r)R(y,H_{t,1:N_{t}},r) depend on the full transcript of human sets, which is only available after the interaction on day tt has concluded. However, the AI must construct its prediction sets during the interaction, at each round rr, before NtN_{t} is known. To handle this, at round rr we evaluate each rule on the transcript prefix Ht,1:rH_{t,1:r}, treating the current round as if it were the terminal round:
