4 Evaluation
4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We focus on two fine-tuning datasets known to contain substantial amounts of PII: Enron (Klimt and Yang, 2004) and TREC (Wu et al., 2006). The Enron dataset, composed of internal company emails made public following investigation into the scandal, includes over 500,000 emails belonging to 150 individuals. The TREC dataset composes over 174,000 emails between World Wide Web Consortium members from 2005.
We consider three classes of PII: email addresses, phone numbers, and personal names.
Models.
We leverage a diverse set of language models with publicly available pretrained checkpoints, including GPT-Neo-125M (Black et al., 2021), PHI-2 (Javaheripi et al., 2023), and LLaMA-3-8B (Dubey and others, 2024). These models span a broad range of pretraining corpora (both open and proprietary) and parameter scales (125M–8B). We finetune each pretrained model on the two datasets described above and use the resulting models as our experimental targets.
Baseline attacks.
We evaluate UniLeak against three representative untargeted PII extraction attacks:
•
Carlini et al. (2021) (BOS) generate texts by initializing generation with the BOS token and randomly sampling new tokens from the top-kk predictions. We set kk to 40.
•
Lukas et al. (2023) generate texts beginning with the empty prompt, and similar to the attack by Carlini et al. (2021), applies top-kk sampling; we also set kk to 40.
•
Keum et al. (2025) (Private Investigator) optimize a set of diverse single-token prompts to maximize PII generation using a surrogate model (GPT-Neo-125M).
Following the evaluation protocol used in these prior works, we generate 200,000 256-token sequences per method.
4.2 Effectiveness of UniLeak
Methodology.
We evaluate UniLeak in extracting PII from language models finetuned on Enron and TREC. We compare the number of unique PII items produced by each attack, which highlights the complementary advantage of ours over existing baselines. Because UniLeak operates by steering model activations during generation, we apply it on top of two existing extraction attacks: Carlini et al. (2021) and Keum et al. (2025). For the attack of Carlini et al. (2021), we inject the universal activation during generation initialized from the BOS token. For the attack of Keum et al. (2025), we apply the activation to each optimized seed prompt (∼\sim20 per model). We did not apply UniLeak on Lukas et al. (2023), as their approach—top-kk sampling from the target model—is functionally similar to Carlini et al. (2021).
Results.
Table 1 summarizes the results of combining UniLeak with
baseline PII extraction attacks across models, datasets, and PII classes.
Because UniLeak operates via inference-time activation steering,
all results compare each baseline attack
and its UniLeak-augmented counterpart.
UniLeak amplifies baseline PII extraction.
For BOS-based extraction, augmenting baseline attacks with UniLeak increases the number of extracted PII records in 15 of 18 cases and yields up to 7,310 additional records. When combined with Private Investigator, UniLeak yields up to 13,399 additional PII records and improves leakage in 12 out of 18 cases.
While a few cases show no improvement, the overall trend indicates that inference-time activation steering consistently amplifies the effectiveness of prompt-level PII extraction.

4.3 Mechanistic Understanding
Next, we analyze the structural properties
of the model that UniLeak exploits
to induce increased PII leakage.
Specifically, we focus on where UniLeak has the strongest effect within the model and
identify the corresponding components responsible
for eliciting additional PII records.
Figure 3: Logit Lens analysis of UniLeak on Phi-2B.
Using 10k prefixes that cause the base model to generate a personal name, we report the average probability of generating the first PII token at each layer, measured with Logit Lens (Nostalgebraist, 2020).
Layer-wise attribution.
We use Logit Lens (Nostalgebraist, 2020) to identify the layers
at which UniLeak most strongly influences the generation of PII tokens.
Figure 3 shows results from Phi-2B fine-tuned on TREC for the personal names PII class, which corresponds to the most successful extraction in our experiments.
We find that UniLeak induces a subtle
but consistent shift toward PII generation in later layers,
while having negligible impact on earlier layers.
In both the base and steered models,
PII-related tokens do not begin to be promoted until layer ∼\sim20,
suggesting that PII information is primarily represented in later layers.
This indicates that
UniLeak amplifies existing signals
rather than introducing new PII-eliciting activations in early layers.
Figure 4: Direct logit attribution of UniLeak on Phi-2B. Using 10k prefixes that cause the base model to generate a personal name, we report the direct logit attribution (Elhage et al., 2021) for the outputs of the last ten self-attention and MLP layers.
Component-level attribution.
To better understand which model components
are responsible for the increased PII leakage,
we perform a direct logit attribution (DLA) analysis (Elhage et al., 2021).
We focus on the last ten layers,
as prior work has shown that contributions from earlier layers
can be effectively erased, leading to misleading attributions (Janiak et al., 2024).
Overall, UniLeak has little effect on the DLA scores of most components.
But we observe a substantial increase
in the attention component of layer ∼\sim31 in this setting.
Notably, the component most affected by UniLeak does not correspond to the component with the highest DLA score prior to steering,
suggesting that UniLeak exploits more subtle components
that are not strongly activated under standard prompting conditions.
Figure 5: Contextual similarity
between generated PII prefixes and training-data prefixes in Phi-2B.
Representation similarity.
Prior work suggests that extraction success is maximized
when the internal representations induced by PII-eliciting prompts
closely resemble those present in the training data (Keum et al., 2025).
Although UniLeak does not access the training data explicitly, it optimizes steering directions using model-generated text
that contains both training and non-training PIIs, and therefore may indirectly encode training-related signals.
To quantify this connection, we compare the internal representations of prefixes that lead to PIIs uniquely extracted by our method and by the BOS baseline with those of the corresponding training prefixes.
We focus on the last-layer activation at the final token position, which captures the model’s internal state immediately prior to PII generation.
Figure 5 shows the results for Phi-2B; see Appendix C for GPT-Neo.
Prefixes generated by UniLeak consistently show higher representational similarity to training prefixes than those generated by the baseline.
This effect is most pronounced for email and phone number PIIs (an 1–54% increase), while personal names show comparable similarity across methods (only differing by 1–10%).
The results indicate that UniLeak can recover states similar to those in the training data even without explicit access.

We study the impact of different configurations on UniLeak.
Figure 6 summarizes the results for
email extraction from the Phi-2B model fine-tuned on TREC.
Intervention layer.
We study the impact of intervention depth
by optimizing individual steering directions at different model layers, focusing on layers with the most distinct differences in training PIIs extracted.
The leftmost plot shows that steering at layer 0 achieves the highest attack success—comparable to steering at all layers (§4.2)—followed by a sharp drop in the subsequent layers.
This indicates that early-layer representations (e.g., embeddings) play an important role in enabling PII extraction.
Size of 𝒟gen(c)\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(c)}.
We examine whether UniLeak remains effective under query constraints by subsampling 𝒟gen(c)\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(c)}.
As shown in the middle plot of Figure 6, reducing its size (reported as a percentage of the original) leads to degradation in success.
The number of unique PII records extracted
drops from 846 to 820, worse than the performance of BOS prompting (835).
This suggests that recovering PII-sensitive directions
requires a self-generated dataset of comparable scale
to those used in prior PII extraction studies.
Percentage of ground-truth PII
in 𝒟gen(c)\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(c)}.
We vary the percentage of ground-truth training PIIs
in 𝒟gen(c)\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(c)}
while keeping the total size fixed.
We find no consistent relationship between
the fraction of training PIIs and extraction success;
in some cases, lower proportions yield higher extraction performance.
As shown in Figure 6, the use of 25% of training PIIs yields the strongest extraction results.
This indicates that the features exploited by UniLeak are shared across training and non-training in-distribution PIIs, rather than being specific to memorized training examples.
5 Discussion: Practical Implications
We discuss the practical implications of
UniLeak under realistic threat models, focusing on how representation-level PII leakage
can be systematically exploited or mitigated through minimal parameter/internal modifications.
5.1 Embedding Poisoning
Rather than applying our directions at inference-time, an attacker performs a one-time, minimal modification to increase the model’s susceptibility to PII leakage.
Here, UniLeak acts as a preparatory mechanism
that amplifies downstream prompt-level PII extraction by shaping representations.
Specifically, UniLeak applied at layer-0 operates directly on input embeddings.
Therefore, a learned direction can be embedded into a model
via a single-row update to the embedding matrix, either the embedding corresponding to the BOS token for BOS sampling or the single-token prompts from Private Investigator.
This modification reproduces a layer-0 UniLeak intervention—shown to be the most vulnerable layer in §4.4—without requiring inference-time steering.
As a result, implanting the direction into the embedding space constitutes a practical and minimal parameter modification, enabling a realistic embedding-poisoning attack.
