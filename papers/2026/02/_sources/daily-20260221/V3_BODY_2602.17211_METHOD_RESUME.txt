3.1 Moment Guided Diffusion
A stochastic interpolant SDE (9) produces XtX_{t} with the same distribution as ItI_{t}, thereby reproducing all moments. MGD uses the same interpolant ItI_{t}, but imposes only that a finite number of moments is preserved:
∀t∈[0,1]:𝔼[ϕ(Xt)]=𝔼[ϕ(It)]:⁣=defmt.\forall t\in[0,1]:\quad\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(X_{t})\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(I_{t})\big]\underset{\mathrm{def}}{:=}m_{t}.
(12)
The following theorem shows that this weaker constraint is satisfied by an SDE formally similar to the Langevin equation (6), but with a time-dependent drift analogous to (10).

Note that the existence of a solution needs to be assumed. Since GtG_{t}, ηt\eta_{t}, and θt\theta_{t} depend on the law of XtX_{t}, (13) is a nonlinear (McKean-Vlasov) SDE [33, 34] whose well-posedness is non-trivial. In particular, GtG_{t} may become singular, causing the drift to blow up. Sufficient conditions for existence are established in Appendix F: Theorem F.7 proves that for large enough σ\sigma, a version of MGD with an additional confining potential admits strong solutions XtX_{t} that converge to the maximum entropy distribution with moments mtm_{t}. The proof relies on the assumption that the Poincaré constant of a reference measure is finite.
If p0p_{0} is Gaussian and ϕ⁡(x)=(x,x​x⊤)\phi(x)=(x,xx^{\top}), then MGD solutions exist and are independent of σ\sigma. In particular, one may set σ=0\sigma=0, reducing the SDE (13) to an ODE. Appendix E shows that under these hypotheses, XtX_{t} is Gaussian with the same mean and covariance as ItI_{t}, so MGD exactly samples the maximum entropy distribution for all σ≥0\sigma\geq 0.
Remark 3.2 (Sampling vs. modelling error).
Throughout this paper, “exact sampling” refers to sampling from the maximum entropy distribution p∗p_{*} constrained by 𝔼p∗​[ϕ]=𝔼⁡[ϕ⁡(X)]\mathbb{E}_{p_{*}}[\phi]=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)], not from the true data distribution pp. The discrepancy DKL(p∥p∗)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|p_{*}) reflects model misspecification inherent to the choice of moment function ϕ\phi, and is distinct from the sampling error DKL(p1σ∥p∗)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{1}^{\sigma}\|p_{*}) that MGD controls (if p1σp_{1}^{\sigma} is the density of X1X_{1}).
For general ϕ\phi, the dynamics at σ=0\sigma=0 typically does not yield the distribution of X1X_{1} to be the maximum entropy distribution with moments 𝔼⁡[ϕ⁡(X)]\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)].
The volatility σ\sigma controls convergence to maximum entropy: Brownian noise increases entropy, while the guidance ηt\eta_{t} can reduce it. When σ\sigma is large, the noise dominates. We conjecture (Section 4) and verify numerically (Section 5) that p1σ→p∗p_{1}^{\sigma}\to p_{*} as σ→∞\sigma\to\infty. It means that MGD eliminates the sampling error while the modelling error remains a choice dictated by ϕ\phi.
Turning now to the structure of the MGD SDE, the drift in (13) has two components: ηt\eta_{t} steers the process to adjust the target moments mtm_{t} to mt+d​tm_{t+dt} , while σ2​θt\sigma^{2}\theta_{t} counterbalances the moment modification induced by the added white noise σ​d​Wt\sigma\,dW_{t}. Note that the MGD SDE (13) is structurally similar to the stochastic interpolant SDE (9): it has a transport term proportional to ηt\eta_{t} (analogous to btb_{t}) and a score-like term proportional to θt\theta_{t} (analogous to ∇log⁡pt\nabla\log p_{t}). In particular, θt\theta_{t} solves an equation of the same form as the score matching equation (7), but with expectations taken over the law of XtX_{t} rather than the data distribution pp.
As with stochastic interpolants (Section 2.2), MGD defines a non-equilibrium homotopic transport that reaches the target moments at t=1t=1, see Section 4.1 for more discussion.
We stress, however, that θt⊤∇ϕ(x)\theta_{t}^{\top}\nabla\phi(x) is not the score of the PDF of XtX_{t}.
Unlike the stochastic interpolant SDE, where the score term is exact, the MGD drift does not reproduce the full distribution of ItI_{t} but only its moments 𝔼⁡[ϕ⁡(It)]\mathbb{E}[\phi(I_{t})]. This is the key difference between MGD and stochastic interpolants.
Remark 3.3.
Observe that θ1\theta_{1} in (15) coincides with the score matching parameter in (7) computed for X1X_{1}. If the distribution of X1X_{1} converges to p∗p_{*} as σ→∞\sigma\to\infty, then θ1\theta_{1} converges to θ∗\theta_{*}. A finite sample estimator of θ1\theta_{1} is thus a nearly consistent estimator of θ∗\theta_{*} for large σ\sigma. However, as noted in Section 2, score matching estimators have high variance for non-convex energies.
Crucially, MGD’s sampling accuracy depends on the empirical estimation of mtm_{t}, not on the accuracy of θt\theta_{t} (see Section 5.2.)
3.2 Discretization of MGD
We solve numerically the MGD nonlinear (McKean-Vlasov) SDE (13) by iteratively updating a finite ensemble of interacting particles. To update the particles, we estimate ηt\eta_{t} in (14) and θt\theta_{t}
in (15) with empirical means over these particles. We also avoids computing 𝔼⁡[Δ​ϕ]\mathbb{E}[\Delta\phi], which is costly or ill-defined when ϕ\phi is not smooth. This is achieved with a two-step predictor-corrector scheme, which we first describe using exact expectations before discussing finite-particle estimations.

To turn this into a practical scheme, we need to choose the time step hh. Since the drift is proportional to σ2\sigma^{2} for large σ\sigma, so is its Lipschitz constant. We therefore set the number of steps to nσ=a​σ2+bn_{\sigma}=a\sigma^{2}+b, where bb ensures the limiting ODE (σ→0\sigma\to 0) is accurately solved. The computational cost of MGD thus scales as O⁡(σ2)O(\sigma^{2}).
The scheme is summarized in Algorithm 1. It iteratively evolves a population of nrepn_{\rm{rep}} particles (xki)1≤i≤nrep(x_{k}^{i})_{1\leq i\leq n_{\rm{rep}}} (replicas), whose empirical measure approximates the distribution of Xk/nσX_{k/n_{\sigma}}, using moments mtm_{t} estimated from training data. A key property is that the moment condition (20) remains valid when expectations are replaced by empirical averages over particles, since (21) holds for empirical distributions. As a result, the empirical mean of ϕ\phi over the particles converges to mt+hm_{t+h} as the step size h→0h\to 0. This exact moment tracking controls the dynamical stability of the algorithm: a divergence of particles would manifest as a moment mismatch (see Remark 3.3).
