# Our First Proof submissions

We’re sharing our proof attempts for First Proof, a math challenge testing if AI can produce checkable proofs on domain-specific problems.

View our set of proof attempts(opens in a new window)

We ran an internal model on all 10 First Proof⁠(opens in a new window) problems, a research-level math challenge designed to test whether AI systems can produce correct, checkable proof attempts. Unlike short-answer or competition-style math, these problems require building end-to-end arguments in specialized domains, and correctness is hard to establish without expert review. The authors of the First Proof problems are leading experts in their respective fields, and at least a couple of the problems were open for years before the authors found solutions. An academic department that has substantial overlap with the subject areas could conceivably solve many of the problems in one week.

We shared⁠(opens in a new window) our proof attempts on Saturday, February 14, 2026 at 12:00 AM PT. Based on feedback from experts, we believe at least five of the model’s proof attempts (problems 4, 5, 6, 9, and 10) have a high chance of being correct, and several others remain under review. We initially believed our attempt for problem 2 was likely correct. Based on the official First Proof commentary and further community analysis, we now believe it is incorrect. We’re grateful for the engagement and look forward to continued review. Our full set of proof attempts can be found here⁠(opens in a new window). The preprint includes all ten proof attempts, plus a newly added appendix with prompt patterns and examples that aim to simulate our manual interactions with the models during the process.

We believe novel frontier research is perhaps the most important way to evaluate capabilities of next generation AI models. Benchmarks are useful, but they can miss some of the hardest parts of research: sustaining long chains of reasoning, choosing the right abstractions, handling ambiguity in problem statements, and producing arguments that survive expert scrutiny. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Testarea reclamelor în ChatGPT (Free, Go)

Începem să lansăm un test pentru reclame în ChatGPT în SUA pentru adulții autentificați din nivelurile Free și Go. Planurile Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise și Education nu au reclame. Testul va include inițial doar un subset de utilizatori. Îl vom extinde în săptămânile următoare pe măsură ce învățăm din acest test.
Răspunsurile ChatGPT sunt generate independent de publicitate. Răspunsurile sunt optimizate pe baza a ceea ce îți este cel mai util. Când vezi o reclamă, aceasta este întotdeauna etichetată clar ca sponsorizată și separată vizual de răspunsul organic.
Reclamele sunt afișate folosind informații care rămân în ChatGPT și nu sunt partajate advertiserilor. Acestea includ ceea ce discuți, modul în care interacționezi cu reclamele (cum ar fi reclamele pe care le ascunzi sau cu care interacționezi) și chaturile și amintirile tale anterioare, dacă ai personalizarea reclamelor activată. Tu deții controlul asupra acestei experiențe: poți respinge reclamele, trimite feedback, afla cum și de ce îți este afișată o reclamă, șterge datele tale despre reclame cu o singură atingere și gestiona personalizarea reclamelor în orice moment.
Advertiserii nu văd niciodată chaturile sau detaliile tale personale și primesc doar informații agregate despre performanță, precum afișări și clicuri. În timpul testului, reclamele nu apar în chaturile despre subiecte sensibile sau reglementate, inclusiv sănătate, sănătate mintală sau politică, și nu sunt afișate utilizatorilor care ne spun sau despre care prezicem că au sub 18 ani.

# Simulacrum: Decision‑Theoretic Pretraining for Near‑Optimal Time‑Series Forecasting and Inference

Pablo Montero Manso

Marcel Scharth

Arxiv pre-print (2026) (to appear)

Download Google Scholar

## Abstract

We propose a neural network-based framework for time series forecasting and parameter estimation
that approximates optimal performance for a wide range of models and optimality criteria.
Trained on large-scale simulations of structural models with known ground truth,
the networks learn to map input time series to their corresponding forecasts and population parameters.
Once trained, they act as (neural) statistical estimators, producing forecasts and parameter
estimates for unseen data.
The training process can be adapted to target statistical decision-theory optimality
properties such as minimum forecast error,
unbiasedness, uniform calibration, model selection accuracy, and robustness.
These neural estimators outperform traditional methods
(e.g., Maximum Likelihood, selection by AIC) and achieve state-of-the-art accuracy on
major real dataset benchmarks, surpassing even complex AI models.
We demonstrate the framework by addressing two longstanding challenges:
the unbiased estimation of the AR(p) model class, and the forecast model combination puzzle.
The main advantage of the approach is its ability to approximate solutions to
analytically intractable or computationally prohibitive time series problems,
such as complex structural equations or optimality criteria.
Combined with inductive biases that can be expressed via neural architectures,
the framework offers a powerful tool for modernizing time series analysis and accelerating scientific research.--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## An Online Sequence-to-Sequence Model Using Partial Conditioning

Authors: Navdeep Jaitly, Quoc V. Le, Oriol Vinyals, Ilya Sutskever, David Sussillo, Samy Bengio

Image: Diagram of an online sequence-to-sequence model using partial conditioning, showing the interaction between the encoder and transducer layers.

Models which map from a sequence of observations to another sequence (sequence-to-sequence) have become extremely popular in the last two years due to their generality, achieving state-of-the-art results in a variety of tasks such as translation, captioning, or parsing. The main drawback of these models is that they need to read in the whole sequence of inputs “x” before starting producing the resulting output sequence “y”. In our paper we circumvent these limitations by allowing the model to emit output symbols before the whole input sequence has been read. Although this introduces some independence assumptions, making online decisions in certain domains such as speech recognition or machine translation makes these models much more desirable.
