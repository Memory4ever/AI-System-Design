2.2 Data Annotation
Different languages embody different cultures. Our benchmark includes QA pairs in 18 languages, and many of them touch on internationally sensitive issues, making it nontrivial to assess the values embedded in each sample. For example, when a QA pair concerns terrorism, the ‘Answer’ should explicitly condemn and resist such acts; when it concerns LGBTQ+ issues, the ‘Answer’ should reflect the diversity of legal frameworks and societal views across countries. To evaluate the values of each sample as fairly and objectively as possible, we adopt a cross-lingual, global perspective and propose a novel two-stage value-annotation framework, consisting of the following two steps:
Step 1: Determine the Type of Issue.
Because values vary across languages and cultural contexts, it is necessary to first identify whether the issue reflected by a QA pair is global consensus or pluralism. Specifically, we define consensus issues as those on which broad agreement can be reached across cultures and the international community (e.g.e.g., counterterrorism, safeguarding sovereignty, and protecting human rights), and pluralistic issues as those on which different views exist across countries, cultures, or social groups (e.g.e.g., political systems, cultural practices, and religious beliefs). The key criterion is whether the issue features multiple materially different positions worldwide that are well-grounded and broadly recognized or understood by the international community.
Step 2: Assess the Values of the ‘Answer’.
The assessment focus differs by issue type. If a QA pair involves a consensus issue, we assess whether the ‘Answer’ clearly adheres to that consensus and does not endorse, justify, or incite actions that violate it. If it involves a pluralistic issue, we assess whether the ‘Answer’ remains neutral and inclusive, reflects multiple mainstream perspectives, and avoids one-sided value imposition or polarizing expression. Particularly, we assign a binary label, values-appropriate or values-inappropriate, to each sample, because we consider ‘Answers’ that equivocate or exhibit an inconsistent value stance to be unacceptable.

To ensure rigor and accuracy, each sample is independently annotated by two trained native speakers. During annotation, annotators should only be allowed to consult information via search engines (e.g.e.g., Google) and are not allowed to utilize LLMs. The two annotations are then reviewed by a third native speaker, who adjudicates any disagreements to produce the final annotations. To further validate reliability, we compute agreement between the annotations for easy-level samples and the preliminary assessments of three LLMs; an agreement rate above 90% is considered acceptable. It is worth noting that we reclassified samples from the easy-level subset to the hard-level if the preliminary consensus among LLMs conflicted with human annotations. Detailed annotation guidelines are provided in Appendix C.
2.3 Benchmark Statistics
As shown in Table 2, X-Value covers 18 languages spanning diverse language families and representing over 75% of the world’s population, demonstrating broad global representativeness. As illustrated in Figure 2, each language in X-Value contains QA pairs from 5–7 issue domains, with approximately 300 samples per language. Note that the absence of certain domains in some languages is due to the near-zero number of samples collected for those categories in the corresponding language. Detailed statistics on the number of easy-level and hard-level samples per language, as well as the length distributions of questions and answers, are provided in Appendix D.
