4.1 Experimental Setup
Jailbreaking benchmarks.
We evaluate robustness to jailbreaks by randomly sampling 200 prompts from HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) and AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023).
Following recent work (Yu et al., 2025), we generate prompt-based jailbreaks using four attacks: GCG (Zou et al., 2023), AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2024), PAIR (Chao et al., 2024), and HumanJailbreaks (Shen et al., 2024).
See Appendix A.3 for a description of each attack and Appendix D for example prompts.
We include both white- and black-box algorithms to evaluate robustness under auditing-only settings and against practical adversaries.
We use the official HarmBench implementations and default hyperparameters for all attacks.
We also assess robustness to feature ablation by independently ablating the DIM (Arditi et al., 2024) and RDO (Wollschläger et al., 2025) refusal features, followed by generating responses to the unmodified harmful prompts.
For each attack (and the unmodified prompts), we report the attack success rate (ASR), defined as the fraction of model responses classified as harmful.
Following prior work (Yu et al., 2025; Dékány et al., 2025; Xhonneux et al., 2024; Sheshadri et al., 2025), we classify prompts using the HarmBench judge (Mazeika et al., 2024).
Utility and overrefusal benchmarks.
We measure utility using six standard datasets: BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), RTE (Wang et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), ARC-C (Clark et al., 2018), and OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018).
We report the average zero-shot accuracy (Acc.).
Refusal-trained models are also known to overrefuse benign prompts (Röttger et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2025).
To measure this, we generate responses to 50 XSTest prompts and 1000 Alpaca prompts (all disjoint from training), and report the compliance rate (CR)—the fraction of non-refusals determined via keyword matching (Röttger et al., 2024).
Models.
We consider four instruction-tuned, refusal-trained models from three families: Llama2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023), Llama3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Gemma2-2B and 9B (Riviere et al., 2024).
We intentionally select models with varying parameter scales (2–9B) and alignment techniques to assess the generalizability of our method.
Baselines.
We evaluate our method against four baseline training-based jailbreak defenses:
•
Augmented refusal training (RT) (Qi et al., 2025) fine-tunes models on safety recovery examples—harmful prefixes followed by a refusal—to deepen safety alignment.
•
CAT (Xhonneux et al., 2024) crafts adversarial examples in the embedding space, enabling much faster adversarial training that generalizes to discrete jailbreaks.
•
LAT (Chao et al., 2024) adversarially perturbs latent activations to elicit harmful target behaviors, and then fine-tunes the model to resist these perturbations.
•
DeepRefusal (Xie et al., 2025) ablates the model’s DIM refusal direction across random layers and token positions, then trains the model to continue refusing harmful prompts.
DeepRefusal also employs ablation, but only considers a single, statically computed direction.
4.2 Effectiveness of Fail-Closed Alignment
Methodology.
We fine-tune each model according to Algorithm 1 for 10 iterations with λ=1\lambda=1, selecting the checkpoint with the lowest ASR on 50 held-out harmful prompts (see Appendix A.2 for details).
For the baselines, we use official HuggingFace (Wolf et al., 2020) models when available; otherwise, we train them using the authors’ official implementations and recommended hyperparameters.
Results.
Table 1 presents the ASR, CR, and Acc. across all evaluation benchmarks and models; due to space limitations, we report feature ablation results in Appendix B.1.
Our fail-closed alignment method is the most robust to prompt-based jailbreaks, achieving ≤\leq 4% ASR in all cases and reducing average ASR by 92.3–97.2%, compared to 23.8–93.7% for the baselines.
Our method is particularly effective against the black-box attack PAIR, consistently yielding the lowest ASR and being the only defense for which PAIR fails to achieve an ASR ≥\geq 17.5%.
Furthermore, we attain the lowest average ASR and standard deviation across all models, indicating strong generalization across attack types, initial model alignment, and parameter scales.
These results indicate that enforcing structural redundancy rather than attack-specific training is a promising paradigm for improving robustness against prompt-based jailbreaks.
Our method incurs minimal degradation in overrefusal and utility.
For overrefusal, we achieve the best or second-best CR on all models, with the highest average (86.4%) compared to 68.8–75.7% for baselines, suggesting our models distinguish truly harmful from seemingly harmful inputs.
For utility, we achieve 61.6% average Acc., outperforming all baselines but CAT (62.0%)—a drop of just 0.8%.
Thus, increased robustness does not compromise output quality.
Finally, our method’s effectiveness does not come at a high computational cost, requiring only a small constant-factor overhead over standard fine-tuning (see Appendix C).

Our main evaluation uses full-scale fine-tuning, which may be infeasible at scale.
To address this, we evaluate the compatibility of our method with the parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique LoRA (Hu et al., 2022).
Following prior work (Yu et al., 2025), we insert LoRA modules into all linear layers and use a rank of r=128r=128, corresponding to ∼\sim5% of the total model parameters; full hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix A.2.
Figure 2 shows the impact of combining LoRA with our method on Gemma2-9B; we include additional results for Llama3-8B in Appendix B.2.
Our method remains effective with LoRA, achieving comparable ASR and Acc. to full-scale fine-tuning (within ∼\sim0.6% points).
Interestingly, LoRA also improves CR on Gemma2-9B from 86.1% to 94.4%.
It is possible that constraining the number of trainable parameters mitigates undesirable side effects.
Overall, these results demonstrate that fail-closed alignment can be achieved via only low-rank updates.
4.4 Mechanistic Understanding
To understand how our method works, we investigate how the directions identified during training operate, and whether the model uses them when refusing harmful prompts.
In Appendix B.3, we also analyze the role of individual neurons.
For this section, recall that each training direction 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_{i} is the it​hi^{th} refusal direction identified by Algorithm 1 (see §3.1).

Causal effects of identified refusal directions.
To understand how each direction 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_{i} operates in the residual stream, we empirically assess their causal effects using the interventions described in §2.
For addition (Eq. 2), we sample 200 benign prompts from Alpaca and report the CR when 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_{i} is added to the residual stream; a lower CR indicates that 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_{i} alone strongly mediates refusal.
For ablation (Eq. 3), we sample 200 harmful prompts from HarmBench and AdvBench and report the ASR when {𝐫1,…,𝐫i}\{\mathbf{r}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{r}_{i}\} are jointly ablated.
A low ASR indicates that the remaining directions {𝐫i+1,𝐫i+2,…}\{\mathbf{r}_{i+1},\mathbf{r}_{i+2},\dots\} can still enforce refusal, implying that refusal is encoded redundantly across multiple directions.
As we cannot extract such directions from the base model, we compare our method to a variant in which MFA is replaced with single-feature ablation (SFA): at each iteration ii, we ablate only direction 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_{i} and allow the model to use {𝐫1,…,𝐫i−1}\{\mathbf{r}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{r}_{i-1}\}.
The left plot in Figure 3 reports ASR under ablation and CR under addition for our method (Ours) and for SFA (Ours w/o MFA) on Llama3-8B, which we select for analysis because it achieves the lowest ASR (§4.2).
Our method learns multiple directions that mediate refusal.
Under the addition intervention, five directions reduce CR below 80%, and three reduce it below 10%—indicating that multiple learned directions can independently induce refusal.
Interestingly, CR varies substantially across iterations, suggesting the model does not weight these directions equally or in a consistent pattern.
The identified directions are comparatively effective under SFA: five reduce CR below 80%, and one below 10%, indicating that MFA is not strictly required to learn multiple refusal-mediating directions.
However, a stark contrast emerges under ablation.
The directions learned by our method redundantly mediate refusal: ASR increases from 0% to only 1% even when all 10 training directions are ablated.
In contrast, SFA exhibits a steady increase in ASR from 0% to 97% as directions are removed, indicating that although it learns multiple refusal-mediating directions, they do not operate independently.
Jailbreaks fail to suppress all training directions.
We next examine whether our models actively leverage the identified training directions when refusing jailbreaks, and whether the observed patterns exhibit the intended fail-closed behavior.
For each of the 200 harmful prompts and their corresponding jailbreaks used in §4.2, we compute the cosine similarity of each 𝐫i\mathbf{r}_{i} with the model’s activations, averaged across all layers.
The right plot in Figure 3 shows the mean cosine similarity, averaged across prompts in each jailbreak category, for the training directions identified in Llama3-8B.
Directions 𝐫1\mathbf{r}_{1}–𝐫5\mathbf{r}_{5} show consistent positive activation on harmful prompts, with cosine similarities ranging from 0.07–0.08—comparable to the base model’s DIM direction (0.10) shown in Figure 1.
For reference, the average cosine similarity across all 10 directions for 200 Alpaca prompts is ≤\leq 0.03, suggesting they activate selectively for harmful inputs.
Under prompt-based jailbreaks, directions 𝐫1\mathbf{r}_{1}–𝐫5\mathbf{r}_{5} are suppressed by 10–58%, consistent with prior findings that jailbreaks suppress refusal directions.
However, this suppression is substantially smaller than in the base model: while the base model’s DIM direction is suppressed by 43–71%, directions 𝐫1\mathbf{r}_{1}–𝐫5\mathbf{r}_{5} in our model are suppressed by only 10–58%, with most direction-attack combinations showing reductions below 40%.
Moreover, suppression varies by attack.
GCG reduces the directions by only 10–15%, whereas AutoDAN and PAIR suppress 𝐫1\mathbf{r}_{1}–𝐫2\mathbf{r}_{2} more strongly (53–58%) while leaving 𝐫3\mathbf{r}_{3}–𝐫5\mathbf{r}_{5} less affected (25–36%).
Thus, across attacks, no single direction is a point of failure.
Non-activating directions. 𝐫6\mathbf{r}_{6}–𝐫10\mathbf{r}_{10} show ∼\sim0 cosine similarity across prompts, suggesting they do not activate like earlier directions.
It is likely that later iterations encourage the model to encode refusal through different mechanisms—such as nonlinear operations—that are not captured by linear feature identification.
We leave the exploration of these mechanisms as
a potential direction for future work.

Multi- vs. single-feature ablation.
To validate the effectiveness of our core operator—multi-feature ablation (MFA)—we compare it with single-feature ablation (SFA), which ablates only the most recently identified direction.
The rightmost plot compares our method (Ours) to the SFA variant (w/o MFA).
MFA is significantly more effective than SFA, achieving an 80.2% lower ASR while maintaining comparable CR and Acc. (within ∼\sim3%).
We hypothesize that because SFA does not prevent the model from reusing previous directions, it concentrates refusal in fewer features, making it easier to bypass (see §4.4 for more analysis).
Activation- vs. gradient-based direction identification.
In §3.1, we adopt gradient-based direction identification, following prior work showing it outperforms activation-space methods (Wollschläger et al., 2025).
Here, we ablate this choice by replacing RDO with an activation-space method (DIM, Eq. 1) for direction extraction; the rightmost figure shows the effect of substituting RDO (Ours) with DIM (w/o RDO).
Using activation-space direction identification, our method still achieves a low ASR of 0.9.
However, while CR remains comparable (within ∼\sim1%), DIM incurs a substantial 5.5% drop in Acc., possibly due to activation-space directions overlapping with clean concepts that are disrupted by ablation.
Based on these results, we use gradient-based direction identification to achieve fail-closed alignment.
Model-specific vs. fixed training data.
We test whether fixed, model-agnostic data limits our method’s effectiveness.
For our adversarial training dataset and XSTest, we use pre-generated Llama3-8B generations from Zou et al. (2024).
For Alpaca, we use the dataset’s default responses.
The rightmost figure compares our method (Ours) with the variant using fixed training data (w/o Model-Specific Data).
Our model-specific data curation outperforms fixed data, achieving 46.8% lower ASR and 5.6% higher CR, with similar Acc.
As hypothesized, fixed data likely degrades performance by encouraging the model to learn distributional artifacts (e.g., style) rather than the intended objective.
5 Conclusion
This work challenges the assumption that LLM alignment can be robust without explicitly structuring refusal.
We find that standard alignment procedures encode refusal along a single activation direction, which can be easily suppressed via prompt-based jailbreaks.
This reveals a structural weakness: standard LLM alignment behaves as a fail-open system, where partial failures lead to unsafe compliance.
