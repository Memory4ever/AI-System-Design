3.3. Experimental Setup
In this section, we describe the experimental setup used to empirically evaluate the memorization capabilities of eight popular LLMs under ideal conditions, i.e. for well-known individuals with high web presence (Famous dataset). We contrast memorization with plausible inference using the names of 100 nonexistent individuals (Synthetic dataset). Finally, we define the metrics used to evaluate model outputs: precision, recall, and confidence.

We evaluate eight models across different access modalities and levels of transparency.

Prompting and formatting.
All models receive identical sets of canary paraphrases per property. For structured properties (e.g., dates, quantities, phone numbers, identifiers), we prepend short, property-specific output-format instructions to improve comparability across families. We also apply a minimal blacklist of function words and template echoes to the extracted spans prior to scoring.

3.3.3. Metrics
For each subject–property pair, we take the remaining top candidates from the association-strength procedure and apply an adaptive percentile cutoff that is stricter when we expect a single ground truth and more permissive when multiple ground truths are likely (0.80 when we expect one ground truth, 0.70 for two, down to a floor of 0.10). The candidates that pass this cutoff are our selected values.
For API models that do not expose token-level log-probabilities, we approximate association strength with unit-vote aggregation: each probe’s top-1 completion casts a vote for its value (set to zero if it matches the generic-subject baseline for the same probe), and we aggregate these votes across probes before applying the same cutoff and evaluation pipeline.
1.
Precision is the fraction of selected values that match at least one ground truth. A selected value counts as a match if either (a) the candidate’s informative content words (at least three characters long and not among the most frequent words in English) all appear in the ground truth, or (b) it is semantically very similar to the ground truth, based on cosine similarity between sentence embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2; we treat scores of 0.60 or higher as a match).
2.
Recall is the fraction of ground truths that are covered by at least one selected value.
3.
Confidence, as defined in §3.2.2, reflects how concentrated the association-strength mass is on the leading candidates. We combine a skewness-based unevenness measure with the leading candidate’s share of the total association strength (and take the higher of these two signals, clipped to [0,1][0,1]). It is close to 1 when one value clearly dominates the distribution and close to 0 when strength is spread more evenly.
Formal definitions and implementation details for association strength, selection, matching, and confidence, both in the evaluation and user-facing setting, are provided in Appendices A.5 and A.6.
3.4. Results and Discussion
In our empirical evaluation, we analyze two subject sets–Famous (n=100n=100 well-known individuals) and Synthetic (n=100n=100 non-existing individuals)–across eight different models. This allows us to (1) validate our black-box auditing approach by comparing individuals with high web presence (Famous) to those with no web presence (Synthetic), (2) compare the capability of retrieving memorized personal data and inferring protected attributes across popular LLMs, and (3) document differences in model confidence and accuracy between categories of features.
3.4.1. Overall Findings
Across models and subject sets, we identify four key patterns:
1.
Confidence separates memorization and inference from “guessing”. In this context, guessing refers to inconsistent outputs that behave like random or base-rate guesses and that do not show stable association with an individual across queries. As shown in Figure 5, confidence values are high for Famous and consistently lower for Synthetic, indicating that our method captures memorized or inferred personal data. This separation is least pronounced for Gemini Flash 2.0 and Llama 3.1 8B, but extreme for Ministral 8B Instruct, which assigns near-zero confidence to Synthetic while producing average confidence for Famous. This suggests built-in countermeasures against hallucinations, unique to this model.
2.
Property type matters. Low-cardinality or name-correlated properties (e.g., sex or gender, native language) show consistently higher confidence, whereas open-class or relational attributes (e.g., medical condition, godparent) yield lower confidence and weaker accuracy. Overall, core demographic and geographic facts (e.g., date of birth, country of citizenship) are reproduced with high precision and recall, while family ties, relational roles, and context-dependent facts (e.g., net worth) are unreliable.
3.
Confidence is not correctness. Models often default to high-confidence guesses even when wrong. For example, many models repeatedly output “ambidextrous” for handedness or “+1” for phone number, producing inflated confidence despite low precision. This illustrates systematic biases rather than faithful memorization.
4.
Smaller models underperform. All smaller open-source models are substantially less accurate in retrieving or inferring personal data. Grok-3 and GPT-5 achieve the highest mean f1f_{1} scores (0.54 and 0.47), while Ministral 8B Instruct and Qwen3 4B Instruct are lowest (0.16 and 0.19).
