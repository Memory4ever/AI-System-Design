5.4 LEACE Causal Erasure
The preceding analyses show that text representations emerge (logit lens) and can be sufficient (implicit cascade test).
LEACE erasure tests whether they are causally necessary: does surgically removing text information collapse task performance?
We apply LEACE to both Ultravox and Qwen2-Audio (Table 7, Figure 6).
Text erasure collapses both models to near-zero accuracy on every task (Ultravox 0.0–0.3%; Qwen2-Audio 0.0%), regardless of architecture.
Random erasure of matched dimensionality has negligible effect on text-sufficient tasks (Ultravox AG News −4.0-4.0%, SST-2 −1.5-1.5%), confirming the collapse is text-specific.
Text-predictive directions are causally necessary for task performance.
The more revealing finding concerns how each architecture encodes text.
CTC and BoC probes both target text but capture different structure: CTC relies on frame-aligned character sequences, BoC on unordered character frequencies.
Qwen2-Audio’s cross-attention preserves frame-aligned structure from the audio encoder, so CTC erasure is devastating (0.0% on four of five tasks) while BoC leaves residual function (AG News 20.1%, SST-2 35.5%).
Ultravox’s connector compresses audio into distributed representations that are not frame-aligned, so the pattern reverses: BoC is more destructive than CTC (AG News 5.9% vs. 9.0%; SST-2 19.4% vs. 63.8%).
In short, each probe erases text only insofar as the architecture makes that structure linearly accessible.
This maps onto the behavioral findings: Qwen2-Audio’s frame-aligned encoding is structurally distinct from ASR output, explaining its lower κ\kappa; Ultravox’s encoding crystallizes into transcript-like form by the final layers, explaining its higher κ\kappa.
Finally, acoustic erasure (2D) causes unexpected accuracy drops even on text-sufficient tasks.
A diagnostic reveals that in Qwen2-Audio, the acoustic eraser also damages text representations (TD drops by up to 0.250.25 at layer 8), confirming acoustic and text subspaces are entangled in cross-attention.
In Ultravox, acoustic erasure does not affect text decodability (TD change <0.03<0.03), suggesting it disrupts other processing rather than text itself.
MELD drops under both random and acoustic erasure in both models, indicating fragile representations where any perturbation degrades performance; MELD-specific LEACE effects should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

5.5 Synthesis: Four Levels of Evidence
The mechanistic evidence forms a convergent chain:
(i) Probing shows acoustic features are retained but compressed, while text decodability follows architecture-dependent trajectories (increasing for Ultravox, decreasing for Qwen2-Audio), answering what is encoded.
(ii) Logit lens visualizes this as literal text emergence through the model’s own vocabulary, answering how it emerges.
(iii) Implicit transcript testing shows the emergent text is functionally sufficient for topic classification (κ=0.94\kappa{=}0.94) but not for fine-grained tasks (SST-2 κ=0.14\kappa{=}0.14, CSQA κ=0.25\kappa{=}0.25), establishing partial sufficiency that depends on information threshold.
(iv) LEACE erasure confirms text representations are causally necessary in both architectures, with text erasure collapsing accuracy to near-zero; the architecture-dependent pattern of CTC and BoC erasure reveals that how text is encoded determines how cascade-like the model behaves, establishing necessity.
The different logit lens trajectories explain the behavioral spectrum: Ultravox’s progressive late-layer text emergence produces representations that closely resemble an explicit ASR transcript by the final layers, yielding higher cascade equivalence.
Qwen2-Audio’s cross-attention encoder already produces text-rich representations (CTC decodability ∼0.50{\sim}0.50 through layer 20) that the LLM sharply reorganizes in its final layers (0.29 at layer 31), yielding lower agreement even with matched backbones.
6 Discussion
When to use cascades vs. end-to-end models.
Our results suggest a decision framework.
For text-sufficient tasks in clean conditions, cascades are informationally equivalent to current speech LLMs, offering lower latency, cost, and engineering complexity, with independently upgradeable components.
For noisy environments, our Whisper-based cascades prove more robust, losing only 0.5–4.2% at 0 dB SNR vs. 5.9–12.7% for the E2E models tested, though this advantage has been demonstrated with Whisper’s noise-hardened front-end, it should generalize to most modern ASR systems given the widespread adoption of Whisper-adjacent modeling approaches.
The case for E2E architectures rests on tasks with genuine acoustic surplus (emotion, sarcasm, speaker intent), but even there, probing reveals a gap between retaining and exploiting acoustic features.
Breaking cascade equivalence: a path forward.
Our results identify the precise bottleneck: the models have the acoustic information but do not use it.
Pitch and energy are retained through the final layers (R2>0.29R^{2}>0.29), yet erasing these directions barely affects Ultravox on natural-speech tasks (MELD −3.1-3.1%, MUStARD −4.0-4.0%).
The problem is training incentive, not architectural capacity.
Qwen2-Audio shows a larger effect from acoustic erasure (MELD −18.0-18.0%), suggesting its cross-attention architecture partially exploits acoustic information, consistent with its lower cascade equivalence and higher behavioral divergence from its cascade.
Three directions could break this bottleneck.
First, paralinguistic auxiliary losses: a contrastive loss penalizing the model when acoustic features are predictable from text alone could encourage functionally independent acoustic channels.
Architectures like WavLLM [6], which explicitly separate semantic and paralinguistic pathways, provide a structural foundation.
Second, minimal-pair prosodic training: training on audio pairs that differ only in prosody (e.g., sincere vs. sarcastic readings of the same sentence) could force models to attend to paralinguistic cues rather than defaulting to textual shortcuts.
Building on contrastive objectives from self-supervised speech learning [32], a loss that pulls apart representations of prosodically distinct utterances with identical transcripts would directly target the textual bottleneck.
Third, architectural modality separation: cross-attention or dual-stream architectures that maintain a structurally independent acoustic pathway may be necessary for genuine multi-modal reasoning.
Implications for benchmarking.
Our findings show that comparing E2E speech LLMs against cascades requires controls that current evaluation practices lack: backbone matching to isolate architectural effects, per-example behavioral analysis beyond aggregate accuracy, and conditions that actually stress the audio pathway.
A benchmark suite designed to reveal genuine architectural advantages should include: (1) paralinguistic tasks where the label depends on how something is said, not just what is said; (2) noisy and reverberant conditions that stress the audio front-end; and (3) matched-backbone cascade baselines to control for LLM reasoning differences.
Without these controls, aggregate accuracy comparisons risk attributing backbone differences to architectural innovation.
Limitations.
TTS-synthesized speech may underestimate acoustic surplus due to less natural prosody; acoustic erasure results corroborate this, showing larger effects on TTS tasks (Ultravox SST-2 −15.7-15.7%) than on natural-speech tasks (Ultravox MELD −3.1-3.1%).
Both linear probes and LEACE operate in the linear subspace; nonlinearly encoded information would be invisible to both methods.
Mechanistic analysis covers two of the four E2E models; Gemini is API-only, and Phi-4-MM was not probed due
