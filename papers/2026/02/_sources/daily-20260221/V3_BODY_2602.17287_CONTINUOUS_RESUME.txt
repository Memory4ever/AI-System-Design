2.1 Next Token Prediction for Machine Translation
In NMT, given a source sequence 𝒙=(x1,…,xm)\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}), target sequence 𝒚=(y1,…,yn)\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}), and a finite vocabulary of type (sub)words V={t1,…,t|V|}V=\{t_{1},...,t_{|V|}\} such that xi,yi∈Vx_{i},y_{i}\in V, we aim to learn the mapping between the source and target sentences via the multi-class classification loss over VV which amounts to next-token prediction:
LNMT​(yi=t,𝒚<i,𝒙)=−log⁡p⁡(yi=t∣𝒚<i,𝒙).\begin{gathered}L_{\text{NMT}}(y_{i}=t;\bm{y}_{<i},\bm{x})\\
=-\log p(y_{i}=t\mid\bm{y}_{<i},\bm{x}).\end{gathered}
(1)
If we denote the latent representation produced by decoder as 𝑯=𝒉⁡(𝒙,𝒚<i)\bm{H}=\bm{h}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{<i}) and embeddings of the vocabulary token tt as 𝑬=𝒆⁡(t)\bm{E}=\bm{e}(t)
Equation 1 can be expressed via Euclidean dot products as follows:
−log⁡p⁡(yi=t∣𝒚<i,𝒙)=−⟨𝑬,𝑯⟩+log∑t′∈Vexp⟨𝑬′,𝑯⟩,\begin{gathered}-\log p(y_{i}=t\mid\bm{y}_{<i},\bm{x})\\
=-{{\langle}{\bm{E}},{\bm{H}}{\rangle}}+\log\sum_{t^{\prime}\in V}\exp{{\langle}{\bm{E^{\prime}}},{\bm{H}}{\rangle}},\end{gathered}
(2)
where tt is a token index, VV is the vocabulary, 𝒆:V→ℝd\bm{e}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an embedding lookup,
and 𝑯\bm{H} is a transformer hidden state calculated in terms of 𝒙\bm{x} and the output prefix 𝒚<i\bm{y}_{<i}.
The continuous alternative to next-token prediction, introduced by Kumar and Tsvetkov (2019),
replaces classification with regression and
can be expressed using cosine similarity, which replaces the more costly log-sum-exp:
LCoNMT​(yi=t,𝒚<i,𝒙)=1−cos⁡(𝑬,𝑯).L_{\text{CoNMT}}(y_{i}=t;\bm{y}_{<i},\bm{x})=1-\operatorname{cos}(\bm{E},\bm{H}).
(3)
CoNMT was proposed as an efficient way to eliminate the training cost
of the last classification layer and log-sum-exp, which scales linearly with vocabulary size.
Instead, the regression loss does not depend on vocabulary size, but is vulnerable to collapse.
All previous studies in this area use fixed embeddings, differing only in their specific configurations
(Kumar and Tsvetkov, 2019; Tokarchuk and Niculae, 2022; Tokarchuk and Niculae, 2024).
2.2 Measure of Representation Collapse
Previous works show that Transformer-based (Vaswani et al., 2017) models trained with next token prediction loss are prone to dimensional representation collapse, which manifests as a lack of diversity in the tokens’ representations (Arefin et al., 2025; Barbero et al., 2024). In the continuous case the matter gets even worse with complete collapse, i.e. trivial global optima of eq. 3, achieved by setting all
𝒆⁡(t)\bm{e}(t) to the same vector for all tt. Because of this, the target representations are typically fixed during training.
Representation collapse can be quantified using several methods, the most prominent of which we describe below.
Average cosine similarity.
Given a matrix of representations 𝒁(L)∈ℝN×d\bm{Z}^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}, where 𝒁i​jL\bm{Z}^{L}_{ij} is the activation of token ii at position jj of layer LL,
the average cosine similarity is: (Godey et al., 2024; Tokarchuk and Niculae, 2024).
Savg​(𝒁(L))≔2N⁡(N−1)​∑1≤i<j≤Ncos⁡(𝒁i(L),𝒁j(L))S_{\text{avg}}(\bm{Z}^{(L)})\coloneqq\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\operatorname{cos}(\bm{Z}^{(L)}_{i},\bm{Z}^{(L)}_{j})
(4)
Abnormally high cosine similarities that cannot be explained by semantic similarity may serve as a signal of complete representation collapse.
Matrix entropy.
Another common approach is to examine the covariance matrix (or Gram matrix) of the representations (Arefin et al., 2025; Gerasimov et al., 2025; Razdaibiedina et al., 2023; Skean et al., 2024), defined as
𝑮=𝒁(L)​(𝒁(L))⊤∈ℝd×d\bm{G}=\bm{Z}^{(L)}(\bm{Z}^{(L)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
The challenge is however to find a basis-invariant metric, since rotations of the space should lead to an identical metric value.
To this end, Skean et al. (2024) propose using the distribution of the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix.
Denote by λk​(𝑮)\lambda_{k}(\bm{G}) its kkth eigenvalue, which measures the variance of the data along the direction spanned by the kkth eigenvector, with 1≤k≤d1\leq k\leq d.
If the data is entirely constant in some dimension kk then λi​(𝑮)=0\lambda_{i}(\bm{G})=0, and implicitly the data lies exactly on a low-dimensional subspace.
More generally, since the eigenvalues are non-negative we may interpret the spectrum as a distribution by renormalizing λ~k​(𝑮)=λk​(𝑮)/∑k′λk′​(𝑮)\tilde{\lambda}_{k}(\bm{G})=\lambda_{k}(\bm{G})/\sum_{k^{\prime}}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{G}).
Ideally, the distribution would be close to uniform, with high entropy, so as the variance is spread evenly across all dimensions. In cases of partial collapse the entropy becomes low as the distribution peaks on a few dimensions.
This leads to the Rényi entropy of order α\alpha (Rényi, 1961) applied to the eigenvalue distribution, sometimes called the Matrix Rényi entropy (Sanchez Giraldo et al., 2015):
Sα​(𝒁(L))=11−α​log⁡[∑kλ~kα​(𝑮)].S_{\alpha}(\bm{Z}^{(L)})=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left[\sum_{k}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\alpha}(\bm{G})\right].
(5)
Note that in the limit of α→1\alpha\to 1, SαS_{\alpha} recovers the standard Shannon entropy of the eigenvalue distribution, and indeed in this work we keep α=1\alpha=1, despite the more general
definition given by Skean et al. (2024).
In a healthy model, SαS_{\alpha} is high, high, which indicates that the empirical covariance covers all dimensions more uniformly. In contrast, when the data only varies in a linear subspace of small dimensions (a form of dimensional collapse), SαS_{\alpha} will be low.
