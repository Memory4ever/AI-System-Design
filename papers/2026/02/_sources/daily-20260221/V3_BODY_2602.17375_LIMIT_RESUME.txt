7 Discussion
This work argues that classical MDP planning can be viewed as
posterior inference over policies without altering the underlying
optimality criterion. The key modeling move is to treat a policy as the
latent variable and to assign it an unnormalized probability of
optimality that is monotone in its expected return
(Eq. (7)). This yields a well-defined posterior
distribution over policies whose modes coincide with return-maximizing
solutions, while posterior dispersion provides an explicit and
interpretable notion of uncertainty about optimal behavior.
Why a Bayesian posterior over policies?
A posterior over policies is a natural object for planning because it
separates solution uncertainty from environment randomness.
In conventional approximate planning and RL, uncertainty about what to
do is often absorbed into heuristic choices (e.g., exploration bonuses,
entropy coefficients, or approximation artifacts), and is therefore hard
to interpret. Here, uncertainty is part of the model: policies with
similar expected returns plausibly coexist in the posterior, whereas
policies with substantially lower return are exponentially suppressed.
This framing makes it possible to use general-purpose inference tools
and to reason about decision-making as probabilistic selection
among coherent behaviors rather than as direct optimization of a single
parametric stochastic policy.
Three roles of uncertainty, made explicit.
Because the posterior is defined over deterministic policies but induces
stochastic action selection at execution time, the framework
disentangles three distinct sources of uncertainty that are commonly
conflated:
•
Aleatoric uncertainty due to stochastic environment
dynamics is handled by the forward simulator and appears as noise in the
Monte Carlo estimate of policy log-probability
(Eq. (8)). This randomness is not ‘inferred’; it is
sampled forward.
•
Epistemic uncertainty about optimal behavior is represented
by posterior dispersion over deterministic policies. Importantly, the
degree of dispersion depends on reward scale: when returns are sharply
separated, the posterior concentrates; when they are comparable, it
remains diffuse.
•
Action stochasticity for execution arises from
marginalizing over deterministic policies. Executing the posterior
predictive distribution (Section 4.2.3) corresponds to a
structured variant of Thompson sampling: uncertainty is expressed as
randomization across plausible deterministic policies, rather than as an
entropy term inside a single policy parameterization.
This separation clarifies what stochasticity means: it reflects
uncertainty about which deterministic behavior is optimal under the
specified preferences, not an intrinsic desire to behave randomly.
Reward scale as preference uncertainty.
A practical implication of the Bayesian framing is that reward magnitude
acquires an interpretation beyond shaping optimization. In the model,
rewards determine the relative posterior mass of policies: scaling
rewards up increases posterior concentration and yields near-deterministic
behavior; scaling them down makes multiple behaviors comparably plausible
and yields a more diffuse mixed policy. This supports the view that
reward scale encodes how sharply the agent distinguishes among outcomes,
i.e., how certain it is about its preferences. Under this
interpretation, the mixed policy induced by posterior predictive
sampling is an optimal stochastic policy under preference
uncertainty: it randomizes only to the extent that the preferences do
not decisively separate candidate behaviors. This differs from typical
entropy-regularized objectives, where stochasticity is introduced
directly as a design choice and tuned via a separate coefficient.
Inference mechanics and why VSMC fits the structure.
Once planning is cast as inference, the remaining question is how to
approximate the posterior when the target density is available only
through a stochastic estimator. The sequential factorization of returns
along trajectories makes sequential Monte Carlo a natural fit, and VSMC
provides a principled variational objective that leverages multiple
particles and resampling to avoid the collapse and under-dispersion
typical of single-trajectory variational objectives. However, planning
introduces additional structure not present in standard state-space
models, requiring two key adaptations for correctness and stability:
(i) enforcing determinism of particle policies by sampling each state’s
action only on first visit, and (ii) coupling transition randomness
across particles within a sweep so that particle weights reflect policy
differences rather than independent realizations of environment noise.
The grid-world study isolates both effects: degenerating VSMC to VIS or
single-particle VSA produces either high-variance solutions or inferior
modes, while removing shared dynamics yields ‘optimistic’ behavior
consistent with inferring both policy and dynamics rather than policy
alone.
