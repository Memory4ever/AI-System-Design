We implement our method using Python v3.10.19 and PyTorch v2.5.1 with CUDA 12.4.

We run all experiments on two machines: the first has an Intel Xeon Processor with 48 cores, 768GB of memory, and 8 Nvidia A40 GPUs.
The second has an Intel Xeon Processor with 112 cores, 2TB of memory, and 8 Nvidia H100 GPUs.

Model selection process.
Our method produces one model checkpoint per iteration. To select the final model, we evaluate each checkpoint on a held-out validation set.
Specifically, for each checkpoint, we compute its ASR on 50 randomly sampled prompts from HarmBench and AdvBench, disjoint from our main evaluation set.
We evaluate across all jailbreaking methods (including the no-attack setting) described in §4.1, excluding GCG. We omit GCG because it is the most computationally intensive attack and generally exhibits lower transferability than the other methods (Liu et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024).
Importantly, all jailbreak examples are crafted on the target model’s base model (e.g., for a Gemma2-2B checkpoint, attacks are generated on Gemma2-2B), ensuring that checkpoint selection does not overfit to the jailbreak optimization process.
After evaluating all checkpoints, we select the checkpoint with the lowest average ASR.
Utility objective for Llama2-7B.
Certain models exhibit poor compliance on benign prompts even before training.
Of the models we evaluate, this applies to Llama2-7B: Table 1 shows it achieves 23–25% points lower CR than the other models.
This poses a problem for KL-based utility preservation using the model’s own outputs, as we would effectively reinforce this poor behavior.
To address this, we use supervised fine-tuning for Llama2-7B, with XSTest completions generated by the higher-quality Llama3-8B; Alpaca completions are still generated from Llama2-7B to preserve in-distribution responses.
In preliminary experiments, this replacement yielded significantly better CR (as shown in Table 1) while achieving comparable ASR.
We note that this is the only modification to Llama2-7B’s training procedure; in particular, we still use λ=1\lambda=1.
A.3 Prompt-Based Jailbreaks
Here we provide a brief description of the prompt-based jailbreaks used in our work:
•
GCG (Zou et al., 2023), a white-box attack that generates adversarial suffixes by iteratively modifying tokens to maximize the probability of affirmative responses to harmful queries.
It formulates optimization over a discrete token space and uses gradient information to guide greedy token substitutions that increase the likelihood of the target behavior.
•
AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2024), a white-box attack that generates semantically meaningful adversarial prefixes to bypass safety alignment.
It initializes the search from handcrafted jailbreak templates and evolves them through a hierarchical genetic algorithm, applying sentence- and paragraph-level crossover along with LLM-based mutation.
This procedure produces fluent, stealthy inputs that can bypass model safeguards while preserving natural-language coherence.
•
PAIR (Chao et al., 2024), a black-box attack that uses a red-teaming LLM to iteratively refine harmful prompts. It establishes an automated system in which an attacker LLM proposes a candidate jailbreak prompt, queries the target LLM, and iteratively improves the prompt using scores from a LLM-as-a-Judge-based scoring function. This procedure enables PAIR to efficiently discover fluent, interpretable jailbreaks in a small number of queries.
•
HumanJailbreaks (Shen et al., 2024), a curated collection of in-the-wild, manually constructed jailbreaking templates. These were gathered from various online communities and prompt-sharing platforms from Dec. 2022 to Dec. 2023.
Appendix B Full Experimental Results
B.1 Jailbreaking Results for Feature Ablation
We next evaluate our method’s robustness to feature ablation—which, as shown in Figure 1, can entirely remove base LLM alignment.
Specifically, we compute the DIM (Arditi et al., 2024) and RDO (Wollschläger et al., 2025) refusal features, independently ablate each from model activations, and report the resulting ASRs for our method and all baselines in Table 3.
Our method exhibits the strongest overall robustness to feature ablation, achieving an average ASR of just 2.5, compared to 5.5–22.4 for the baselines.
Notably, although our method is trained using features identified by RDO, it is comparatively more robust to DIM ablation: we obtain the lowest ASR on 3/4 models under DIM removal, versus 2/4 under RDO.
This suggests that training on gradient-based features generalizes effectively to activation-space features.
Overall, these results further demonstrate that our fail-closed alignment approach addresses the core challenge identified in §2: it produces models that no longer rely on brittle, fail-open features that can be easily suppressed via activation-space or prompt-based jailbreaks.

B.3 Safety Neuron Analysis
Prior work has shown that safety behavior in LLMs is also brittle at the neuron level.
In particular, ablating as few as 0.2–3% of so-called safety neurons—by setting their activations to zero—can almost entirely eliminate safety alignment (Wei et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025).
Notably, Wu et al. (2025) demonstrate that selectively suppressing safety neurons suffices to achieve high jailbreak success in both white-box and black-box settings.
In this section, we examine whether our method—which improves robustness at the feature level—also increases robustness at the neuron level.
To identify and ablate safety neurons, we follow the procedure of Wei et al. (2024).
We first identify safety- and utility-relevant neurons using per-layer thresholds, denoted top-pp and top-qq, which specify the fraction of neurons deemed important for model behavior.
We measure neuron importance using the Wanda score (Sun et al., 2024), defined as the product of a weight’s magnitude and the norm of its corresponding input activation, averaged over a set of representative prompts.
Neurons with high importance under benign prompts form the Utility Set, while neurons with high importance under malicious prompts form the Safety Set.
Neurons that appear in the Safety Set but not in the Utility Set are designated as safety neurons and are subsequently ablated.
Although alternative methods for identifying safety neurons exist, we adopt this approach because it is widely used (Chen et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2024) and is directly compatible with our models.
We use this procedure to evaluate how our method affects the sparsity and distribution of safety-related behavior at the neuron level.
