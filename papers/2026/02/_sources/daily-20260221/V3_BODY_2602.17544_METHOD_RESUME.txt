2. Our Work
Consider a thinker model MTM_{T}. We want to evaluate reusability and verifiability of its CoT in the context of a specific test dataset QQ consisting of test questions and an executor model MEM_{E}. When the Thinker model MTM_{T} is prompted with a question qq, it generates an answer A​n​s​(MT,q)Ans(M_{T},q) on its own along with CoT. Given a question qq, the CoT of MTM_{T} is denoted as C​o​T​(MT,q)CoT(M_{T},q). A corrupted version of CoT is denoted as C​o​r​r​C​o​T​(MT,q)CorrCoT(M_{T},q). The corrupted CoT is generated in such a way that it still looks like a plausible CoT but a few steps are altered to change the final outcome. Example prompts to generate corrupted CoT are included in our code. A corrupted CoT is a modified version C​o​T​(MT,q)CoT(M_{T},q) such that it leads to wrong answer for question qq. In other words, when MTM_{T} is prompted with question appended with corrupted CoT, it leads to a wrong answer (A​n​s​(MT,q+C​o​r​r​C​o​T​(MT,q))Ans(M_{T},q+CorrCoT(M_{T},q)) is a wrong answer).
We partition QQ into two subsets:
•
Qc​o​r​r​e​c​t={q∈Q∣A​n​s​(MT,q)​ is the correct answer}Q_{correct}=\{q\in Q\mid Ans(M_{T},q)\text{ is the correct answer}\}
•
Qw​r​o​n​g={q∈Q∣A​n​s​(MT,q)​ is a wrong answer}Q_{wrong}=\{q\in Q\mid Ans(M_{T},q)\text{ is a wrong answer}\}
Please refer to Figure 1 for an overview of reusability computation procedure. We consider only the subset Qc​o​r​r​e​c​tQ_{correct} and discard Qw​r​o​n​gQ_{wrong}. We want to check if MTM_{T} is able to pursue MEM_{E} to flip its answers from correct to wrong and vice versa for question in the Qc​o​r​r​e​c​tQ_{correct} subset. For each question qq in Qc​o​r​r​e​c​tQ_{correct}, we first check if MEM_{E} is not able to answer it correctly on its own. If the answer A​n​s​(ME,q)Ans(M_{E},q) is wrong then we prompt MEM_{E} with q′=q+C​o​T​(MT,q)q^{\prime}=q+CoT(M_{T},q). If the answer A​n​s​(ME,q′)Ans(M_{E},q^{\prime}) is correct then we have the evidence that MEM_{E} reused the CoT from MTM_{T} to flip its answer to the correct answer. We denote the set of such questions as Qh​e​l​p​e​dQ_{helped}. On the other hand, if A​n​s​(ME,q)Ans(M_{E},q) is correct then we first generate C​o​r​r​C​o​T​(MT,q)CorrCoT(M_{T},q) and then prompt MEM_{E} with q′′=q+C​o​r​r​C​o​T​(MT,q)q^{\prime\prime}=q+CorrCoT(M_{T},q). If the answer A​n​s​(ME,q′′)Ans(M_{E},q^{\prime\prime}) is wrong then we have the evidence that MEM_{E} reused the corrupted CoT from MTM_{T} to flip its answer to a wrong answer. We denote the set of such questions as Qh​a​r​m​e​dQ_{harmed}. Reusability of MTM_{T} with respect to QQ and MEM_{E} is
(1)
R⁡(MT,Q,ME)=(|Qh​e​l​p​e​d|+|Qh​a​r​m​e​d|)×100|Qc​o​r​r​e​c​t|.R(M_{T},Q,M_{E})=\frac{\left(|Q_{helped}|+|Q_{harmed}|\right)\times 100}{|Q_{correct}|}.
The reusability score is in the range of 0 to 100. A high reusability score indicates that the MEM_{E} generates its answers by ignoring its own thinking process and blindly reusing the CoT provided by MTM_{T}. A high reusability score is a desirable property of CoT as it indicates that the generated CoT is not MTM_{T} specific, but it can be used across other models such as MEM_{E}. In the context of multi-agent systems, a high reusability CoT can be used to help other agents to understand how to arrive at the correct answer. At the same time, such a CoT can be used for effective adversarial attacks to intentionally lead an agent to a wrong answer. In other words, reusability measures persuasiveness of the CoT.

The verifiability of CoT measures its ability to be executed consistently across different executor models. We quantify this by measuring how often an executor model MEM_{E} produces the same final answer as the thinker model MTM_{T} when provided with C​o​T​(MT,q)CoT(M_{T},q). The verifiability is computed as
(2)
V=100|Q|​∑q∈Q𝕀⁡(A​n​s​(ME,q,C​o​TMT,q)=A​n​s​(MT,q,C​o​TMT,q))V=\frac{100}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbb{I}(Ans(M_{E},q,CoT_{M_{T},q})=Ans(M_{T},q,CoT_{M_{T},q}))
where 𝕀⁡(⋅)\mathbb{I}(\cdot) is the indicator function. A high verifiability score indicates that CoT is unambiguous and reliably leads different computational interpreters to the same conclusion.
The verifiability score is in the range of 0 to 100. A high verifiability score indicates that CoT from MTM_{T} is unambiguous and can be reliably executed by other computational interpreters such as MEM_{E}. A high verifiability score is a desirable property of CoT as it indicates that interpretation of CoT from MTM_{T} is not model dependent. In the context of multi-agent systems, a high verifiability CoT can be used to communicate terms of engagement or final consensus. Reusability and verifiability measure different aspects of CoT. Reusability is analogous with effective speeches from great orators who can persuade people to follow any ideology without thinking on their own. Whereas verifiability is analogous to legal documents where there is almost no scope for multiple interpretations.
3. Experiments
We performed experiments with four Thinker models. Two are general-purpose LLMs (Gemma3-27B and Llama3.1-8B). Two are specialized reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1-14B and Phi4-Reasoning-14B). For the Executor role, we employed a committee of ten models. Their sizes vary from 360M to 3B parameters. We accessed these models via the Ollama local API using default decoding strategies. We further divided the executor committee into two subcommittees based on size. The Strong committee consists of the five larger models (Gemma2-2B, Gemma-2B, Llama3.2-3B, OpenChat, and MistralLite). The Weak committee consists of the five smaller models (SmoLLM2-360M, TinyLlama, Qwen-0.5B, TinyDolphin, and Qwen2.5-0.5B). We evaluated all configurations across five benchmarks: GSM8K, SVAMP, ARC-Challenge, StrategyQA, and CommonsenseQA. All experiments were carried out on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (80 GB).
3.1. Results
Please refer to Tables 1 and 2. They show reusability and verifiability measurement for four Thinker models across five datasets with three versions of Executor committees. Reusability and verifiability score for a committee is calculated as the average of individual scores with each Executor model. The committee setting helps us to reduce the bias of a single Executor. First, we will perform analysis while varying the dataset and keeping the executor committee constant. We can observe that just like accuracy, the reusability scores for Thinkers vary depending on the target dataset. And there is not best Thinker for reusability or verifiability. For example, with Full committee Phi has highest reusability for the GSM8K dataset. But with the same evaluation committee, Llama has the best reusability when it comes to the SVAMP dataset.
The reusability and verifiability scores depend on the nature of Executor committee. Now, we will perform analysis while keeping the dataset constant and varying the committee. We can observe that the stronger the committee, the higher is the score for reusability and verifiability (Strong > Full > Weak). This change in scale for scores across committees is equivalent to multiple human judges assigning numeric scores to an artistic creation based on a suggested evaluation procedure. However, the evaluation across the judges is consistent if the relative ranking order remains consistent. Kendall’s τ\tau is used to compare consistency across any two rankings (Kendall, 1938). Please refer to Tables 3 and 4. They show the Kendall’s τ\tau correlation between the ranking of Thinkers created by three committees across the datasets. The correlation is exactly 1 for Strong vs Full committee for reusability. Similarly, the correlation is 0.8 for the verifiability ranking for Strong vs Full committee. This indicates that even though the scales of scores vary based on the executor committee, the relative ranking is stable across the committees that have at least a few strong Executors.
Please refer to Table 5 for comparison of our proposed measures reusability and verifiability with accuracy. Reusability and verifiability are computed using the full committee. We can observe that for a given dataset, highest accuracy does not guarantee highest reusability or verifiability. For example, on GSM8K dataset DeepSeek and Gemma3 have highest accuracy. However, when it comes to reusability and verifiability, Phi beats them comfortably by a large margin. Similarly, highest reusability does not guarantee high accuracy or verifiability. For example, Llama has highest reusability for the SVAMP dataset. However, it is the worst performing Thinker when it comes to accuracy and verifiability. This variation in the rankings of Thinkers using different evaluation measures is captured in the Table 6. It shows that across all datasets, Kendall’s τ\tau is quite low for the rankings created by any two evaluation measures. This low correlation shows that each evaluation measure is capturing different aspects of CoT behavior. A single leaderboard ranking based on accuracy fails to capture this nuance.
4. Related Work
The concept of CoT prompting was introduced by Wei et al. (Wei et al., 2022). They showed that eliciting step-by-step reasoning significantly boosts LLM performance. Many variants followed quickly. Self-Consistency samples multiple reasoning paths and selects the most frequent answer (Wang et al., 2023). Tree of Thoughts (ToT) generalizes this by searching over coherent "thoughts" like a game tree (Yao et al., 2023). It allows models to look ahead and backtrack. However, these methods for better CoT primarily focus on maximizing final accuracy. They do not evaluate the quality of the reasoning trace itself.
Generated reasoning is not always a true representation of the model’s process. Turpin et al. showed that CoT explanations can be plausible yet misleading (Turpin et al., 2023b). Models often generate explanations to rationalize biased answers. Lanham et al. measured this by testing if the answer changes when the reasoning is altered (Tutek et al., 2025). They found that larger models are often less faithful. Arcuschin et al. further showed that models can generate coherent arguments for contradictory answers (Arcuschin et al., 2025). This creates a "trust gap" in using CoT for explanation. Our work on verifiability adds an empirical check on causality between CoT and the generated answer.
To address these issues, research has moved toward separating reasoning from execution. Program of Thoughts (PoT) decouples computation from reasoning (Chen et al., 2023). In PoT, the LLM generates a program, and an external Python interpreter executes it. Similarly, Faithful CoT uses a "Translator" to generate a symbolic chain and a "Solver" to derive the answer (Lyu et al., 2023). Our Thinker-Executor framework generalizes the Python interpreter and the "Solver" to any Executor model. Han et al. proposed using "blueprints" where a larger model plans for a smaller one (Han et al., 2025). Our Thinker-Executor framework builds upon this modularity trend.
Current evaluation heavily relies on accuracy leaderboards. This overlooks the quality of the intermediate process. Process Reward Models (PRMs) attempt to fix this by scoring each step of the reasoning chain (Lightman et al., 2024). Math Shepherd extends this by verifying steps automatically without human annotations (Wang et al., 2024). However, these methods often require training specific verifiers. Other systems use an "LLM-as-a-Judge" approach (Zheng et al., 2024). These judges are subjective and inherit the biases of the evaluator model.
Our work bridges the gap between these paradigms. We leverage the modular architecture to test the utility of the reasoning. We move beyond the supervision required by Process Reward Models.
