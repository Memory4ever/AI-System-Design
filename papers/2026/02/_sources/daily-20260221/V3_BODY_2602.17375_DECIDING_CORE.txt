3 Probabilistic Model
A widespread setting for posterior Bayesian inference is a
graphical model involving a latent variable xx, an observable
variable yy, and a joint distribution p⁡(x,y)p(x,y). By Bayes’
rule,
p⁡(x∣y)=p⁡(x,y)p⁡(y),p(x\mid y)=\frac{p(x,y)}{p(y)},

Posterior inference methods then aim to characterize this
distribution, exactly or approximately, by producing samples or
expectations consistent with the corresponding normalized
distribution. Although most practical algorithms do not evaluate the
normalizing constant Z=p⁡(y∗)Z=p(y^{*}) explicitly, they can be viewed as
implicitly solving the normalization problem, in the sense that
their outputs behave as if such a normalization were available.
However, the presence of an explicit observable variable yy is
not essential. Rather, yy serves as a convenient way to parameterize
an unnormalized target distribution. From a more general perspective,
posterior inference only requires access to an unnormalized
probability (more precisely, a finite measure) p~​(x)\tilde{p}(x). The objective is then to work with the corresponding normalized
distribution

This formulation naturally accommodates cases in which p~​(x)\tilde{p}(x)—or, more typically, log⁡p~​(x)\log\tilde{p}(x)—is intractable and
available only through unbiased stochastic estimators. A range of

inference (Hoffman et al., 2013),
and stochastic gradient Markov chain Monte Carlo (Nemeth and Fearnhead, 2020).
We cast policy inference within this general framework.
Since we are interested in inferring a policy, the policy π\pi
is the latent random variable. The objective of MDP planning is to identify
policies that maximize expected return. To align the
probabilistic model with this objective, we assign to each policy an
unnormalized probability of optimality that is monotone in its
expected return.
Specifically, we define the unnormalized log probability of a policy
as the expected return obtained by the agent following

stochastic policies are considered) induces a stochastic action

their respective distributions rather than conditioned for the purpose
of posterior inference. The randomness they induce propagates into

4 Inference
We begin by specifying the object of inference. In this work, we
perform posterior inference over the space of deterministic
policies, equipped with a uniform prior 11
1
The proposed
formulation also admits posterior inference over stochastic policies.
We restrict attention to deterministic policies in this work to avoid
an additional layer of action-level randomness and to keep the focus
on policy-level uncertainty.. While one may place a posterior over
stochastic policies, doing so introduces an additional and largely
redundant level of randomness. A posterior over stochastic policies
combines epistemic uncertainty over policies with intrinsic
action-level stochasticity, leading to mixtures of mixtures whose
behavioral interpretation is unclear and whose posterior geometry is
highly non-identifiable. In contrast, a posterior over deterministic
policies represents uncertainty solely at the level of coherent
behaviors, admits a clear decision semantics, and ensures that
posterior concentration corresponds directly to behavioral certainty.
Action stochasticity then arises naturally through marginalization
over deterministic policies (Section 4.2.3), rather than
being embedded inside each policy.
4.1 Algorithm
A natural baseline for posterior inference over the policy
distribution specified by (8) is structured
variational approximation (Hoffman et al., 2013). However, single-trajectory
variational inference tends to produce underdispersed approximations,
collapse onto a small subset of trajectory modes, and requires
problem-specific heuristics to remain stable under multimodal dynamics
or sparse rewards. The sequential structure
of (8) encourages application of variational
SMC, which multi-particle objective incorporates information from
diverse trajectory proposals, yielding a tighter and more robust
approximation to the policy posterior. These advantages, however,
require adaptation of VSMC to the policy inference setting, which is
the subject of this section.
VSMC optimizes a stochastic lower bound on the log marginal
likelihood, where the stochasticity arises from Monte Carlo sampling
of particle trajectories from the variational proposal. In the policy
inference setting considered here, the objective is additionally noisy
because the unnormalized log density of a policy is intractable and
can only be evaluated through an unbiased Monte Carlo
estimator (8). This second source of
randomness is independent of the variational proposal and therefore
does not alter the variational interpretation of the VSMC objective.
It can be treated as exogenous estimator noise on the target density,
analogous to the pseudo-marginal setting (Andrieu and Roberts, 2009), and incorporated
directly into the optimization without modification of the underlying
algorithm.
