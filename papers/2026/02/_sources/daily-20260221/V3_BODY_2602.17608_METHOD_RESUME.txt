3 Problem Formulation
In this section, we formulate the problem of Anchored E-Watermarking. Similar to statistical watermarking, the goal is to embed statistical signals into samples from a target distribution to enable detection. However, in Anchored E-Watermarking, the generator and the detector share access to an anchor distribution that serves as a robust a priori estimate of the target distribution. This setting is common in practice; for example, when watermarking Large Language Models or image generators, the target distribution is known to be human language or natural images, respectively. Consequently, watermarking efficacy can be improved by leveraging this structure. Besides, Anchored E-Watermarking uses e-values for detection, thus enjoying improved efficiency in sequential testing. In the following, we introduce the specific components of Anchored E-Watermarking.
Anchor distribution.
In the Anchored E-Watermarking framework, we assume that both the generator and the detector share access to an anchor distribution p0∈Δ⁡(𝒱)p_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}), which is in the same probability simplex as the target distribution qq. This p0p_{0} serves as the best a priori estimate of the target distribution. For instance, in the context of watermarking Large Language Models (LLMs), p0p_{0} could be an open-source, smaller-scale LLM such as Qwen3-8B (Team, 2025). Leveraging its role as a known reference, we designate p0p_{0} as the marginal distribution for the watermark signal (or random seed), denoted as ss. Therefore, the seed space 𝒮\mathcal{S} is equal to 𝒱\mathcal{V} in our framework. Throughout the paper, we assume p0p_{0} satisfies the condition infv∈𝒱p0​(v)>δ\inf_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{0}(v)>\delta for a positive robustness tolerance parameter δ\delta. This condition ensures p0p_{0} has enough randomness as required for watermarking (Aaronson, 2022a) and that the δ\delta-neighborhood defined below is a strict subset of the probability simplex.
Target distribution.
The target distribution q∈Δ⁡(𝒱)q\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}) represents the desired distribution from which (watermarked) output samples vv are generated. Consistent with the definition of the anchor, we premise that qq remains sufficiently proximal to p0p_{0}. Formally, we assume qq resides within the δ\delta-neighborhood of the anchor:
𝒬⁡(p0,δ)={q∈Δ⁡(𝒱):‖q−p0‖1≤δ}.\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta)=\left\{q\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}):\|q-p_{0}\|_{1}\leq\delta\right\}.
Here ∥⋅∥1\|\cdot\|_{1} is the ℓ1\ell_{1} distance and the radius δ>0\delta>0 represents a robustness tolerance parameter that controls how the true target distribution qq can deviate from the anchor.
This assumption holds for most practical statistical watermarking scenarios; for example, in the LLM domain, high-performing models tend to converge towards the same underlying distribution of natural language, resulting in low statistical divergence between them.
Generator.
The objective of the generator is to sample an output vv from the target distribution qq while embedding a statistical signal ss. To achieve this, the generator constructs a joint distribution (or coupling) ww over vv and ss, subject to the constraint that the marginals recover the target and anchor distributions, respectively. The feasible set of valid couplings is defined as:
𝒫(p0,q)={w∈Δ(𝒱×𝒱):∑s∈𝒱w(v,s)=q(v),∑v∈𝒱w(v,s)=p0(s)}.\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(p_{0},q)=\bigg\{w\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}):\sum_{s\in\mathcal{V}}w(v,s)=q(v),\quad\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}w(v,s)=p_{0}(s)\bigg\}.
Note that the generator has access to qq, as is common in practice (e.g., when the generator is a service provider). During generation, the system samples the outcome vv and the signal ss jointly from ww. This procedure ensures that the marginal distribution of the outcome vv is unbiased (satisfying Definition 2.1), while the watermark signal is embedded within the statistical dependency between vv and ss.
E-value.
In the detection phase, the detector receives an outcome-signal pair (v,s)(v,s) and computes an e-value e⁡(v,s)≥0e(v,s)\geq 0. The objective is to distinguish between the following hypotheses:
H0:\displaystyle\textbf{H}_{0}:
v​ and ​s​ are sampled independently,\displaystyle\penalty\ v\text{ and }s\text{ are sampled independently},
H1:\displaystyle\textbf{H}_{1}:
(v,s)​ are sampled from the joint coupling ​w.\displaystyle\penalty\ (v,s)\text{ are sampled from the joint coupling }w.
The detector rejects the null hypothesis if e⁡(v,s)>1/αe(v,s)>1/\alpha. To guarantee a Type-I error rate of at most α\alpha, the expectation of the e-value under the null hypothesis must be bounded by 11. Adhering to the model-agnostic constraint (Definition 2.2), the scoring function e⁡(⋅,⋅)e(\cdot,\cdot) must be defined prior to observing the specific target distribution qq. The design of the e-value relies solely on the constraint that the target distribution lies within the anchor’s neighborhood, i.e., q∈𝒬⁡(p0,δ)q\in\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta). Consequently, to ensure validity, the e-value must satisfy the expectation bound uniformly across the entire uncertainty set 𝒬\mathcal{Q}:
supq∈𝒬⁡(p0,δ)𝔼v∼q,s∼p0​[e⁡(v,s)]≤1.\displaystyle\sup_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta)}\mathbb{E}_{v\sim q,s\sim p_{0}}[e(v,s)]\leq 1.
(1)
We let ℰ\mathcal{E} denote the set of all nonnegative functions e:𝒱×𝒱→ℝe:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R} satisfying Eq. (1).
3.1 Robust log-optimality
While the definition of an e-value ensures validity (safety) under the null, it does not guarantee power (growth) under the alternative. To reject H0\textbf{H}_{0} effectively, we desire the e-value to be large when the data is generated from the alternative distribution H1\textbf{H}_{1}. The standard criterion for selecting an e-value is log-optimality, often referred to as the “Kelly criterion” in the finance literature (Kelly, 1956). This approach seeks to maximize the expected logarithmic growth rate of evidence against the null, under the alternative. For simple hypotheses where 𝒫0={P}\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{P\} and 𝒫1={Q}\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{Q\}, the likelihood ratio e∗=d​Qd​Pe^{*}=\frac{dQ}{dP} is log-optimal, such that
𝔼Q​[log⁡e∗]≥𝔼Q​[log⁡e]\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log e^{*}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log e]
for any valid e-value ee.
In Anchored E-Watermarking, the alternative is defined by the joint coupling ww constructed by the generator. However, since the target distribution qq lies in a set 𝒬⁡(p0,δ)\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta) that is unknown to the detector (i.e., the designer of the e-value), we must optimize the worst-case log-growth rate over arbitrary q∈𝒬⁡(p0,δ)q\in\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta). This gives rise to the following robust log-optimality problem.
Problem 3.1 (Robust log-optimality).
An e-value ee is robust log-optimal in Anchored E-Watermarking if it optimizes the following objective:
supe∈ℰinfq∈𝒬⁡(p0,δ)supw∈𝒫⁡(p0,q)\displaystyle\sup_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta)}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{P}(p_{0},q)}
∑v,s∈𝒱w⁡(v,s)⋅log⁡e⁡(v,s).\displaystyle\penalty\ \sum_{v,s\in\mathcal{V}}w(v,s)\cdot\log e(v,s).
(2)
In this formulation, the objective ∑w⁡(v,s)​log⁡e​(v,s)\sum w(v,s)\log e(v,s) represents the log-growth rate under the alternative. The inner maximization supw∈𝒫⁡(p0,q)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{P}(p_{0},q)} reflects the generator’s optimization of the coupling ww given knowledge of qq; the minimization infq∈𝒬⁡(p0,δ)\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta)} enforces robustness against the worst-case target distribution in the family; and the outer maximization supe∈ℰ\sup_{e\in\mathcal{E}} seeks the optimal detector without access to qq subject to model-agnosticy (Definition 2.2).
Remark 3.2 (Relationship to growth rate optimality in the worst case (GROW) (Grünwald et al., 2020)).
GROW studies the worst-case optimal expected capital growth rate under composite null 𝒫0\mathcal{P}_{0} and alternative 𝒫1\mathcal{P}_{1}:
GROW⁡(𝒫1)=supE∈ℰ⁡(𝒫0)infθ∈𝒫1𝔼Pθ​[l​o​g​E],\displaystyle\mathrm{GROW}(\mathcal{P}_{1})=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P}_{0})}\inf_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\theta}}[logE],
where ℰ⁡(𝒫0)\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P}_{0}) is the set of all valid e-values for the null 𝒫0\mathcal{P}_{0}.
Therefore, our problem in Eq. (2) can be seen as generalizing GROW into an ‘active’ scenario where a generator (corresponding to supw∈𝒫⁡(p0,q)\sup_{w\in\mathcal{P}(p_{0},q)}) seeks to maximizes the power after the worst-case hypothesis selection infθ∈𝒫1\inf_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}. Note that by designing the coupling ww, the generator essentially alters the alternative, so our problem can not be reduced to GROW even if the watermark generator is fixed.
3.2 Stopping time
While Problem 3.1 addresses the one-step growth rate, it remains to analyze the sample complexity of the framework. In the context of sequential hypothesis testing, sample efficiency is characterized by the stopping time τα=inf{n∈ℤ+:Wn≥log⁡(1/α)}\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:W_{n}\geq\log(1/\alpha)\right\} under the alternative, where
Wn=∑t=1nlog⁡e⁡(vt,st),W_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log e(v^{t},s^{t}),
is the accumulated wealth process. This quantity represents the number of samples required to reject the null hypothesis. We consider a stochastic process μ⁡(𝒜,𝒢)\mu(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G}) governed by the interaction between an adversary 𝒜\mathcal{A} and a generator 𝒢\mathcal{G}. At each step t∈ℤ+t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:
•
The adversary 𝒜\mathcal{A} selects qt∈𝒬⁡(p0,δ)q^{t}\in\mathcal{Q}(p_{0},\delta) based on the e-value ee and the history (v1,s1),…,(vt−1,st−1)(v^{1},s^{1}),\dots,(v^{t-1},s^{t-1});
•
The generator 𝒢\mathcal{G} specifies the joint coupling wtw^{t} given qtq^{t} and p0p_{0}, and draws the sample (vt,st)∼wt(v^{t},s^{t})\sim w^{t}.
Due to the adaptive nature of the adversary, the outcome sequence v1,v2,…v^{1},v^{2},\dots may be generated autoregressively, conditional on previous outcomes. This formulation extends the i.i.d. setting of Huang et al. (2023) to arbitrary dependent distributions. In this setting, we write the stopping time as τα​(e)\tau_{\alpha}(e) to highlight the dependence on the e-value ee. In the context of sequential testing (Breiman, 1961; Chugg et al., 2023; Shekhar and Ramdas, 2023; Kaufmann and Koolen, 2021; Waudby-Smith et al., 2025), the expected stopping time 𝔼⁡[τα]\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\alpha}] typically scales linearly with log⁡(1/α)\log(1/\alpha), modulated by an information-theoretic quantity capturing the complexity of the testing problem. Therefore, we are explicitly interested in the following question:
Problem 3.3 (Sample efficiency).
For any e-value ee satisfying the constraint in Eq. (1), define its sample complexity by
SC⁡(e)=sup𝒜inf𝒢lim infα→0𝔼μ⁡(𝒜,𝒢)​[τα​(e)]log⁡(1/α).\displaystyle\mathrm{SC}(e)=\sup_{\mathcal{A}}\inf_{\mathcal{G}}\liminf_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G})}[\tau_{\alpha}(e)]}{\log(1/\alpha)}.
We are interested in identifying the e-value that minimizes this sample complexity.
Note that this formulation is stronger than the classical expected stopping time because we allow the adversary to alter the distribution at each time step. This implies that samples are not generated in an i.i.d. fashion, a distinction crucial for applications such as the watermarking of autoregressive language models.
4 Theoretical Results
In this section, we answer the questions posed in the previous section. We first derive the optimal detector for the single-step decision problem and then extend this analysis to the sequential setting to characterize the fundamental limit of sample efficiency. Let nn denote the cardinality of the sample space 𝒱\mathcal{V} and 𝐞v\mathbf{e}_{v} denote the one-hot vector (i.e., Dirac measure) that assigns mass 11 to v∈𝒱v\in\mathcal{V}.
