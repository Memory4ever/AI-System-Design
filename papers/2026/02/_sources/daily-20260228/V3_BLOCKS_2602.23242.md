[2] h1: A Model-Free Universal AI

[3] h6: Abstract

[4] p: In general reinforcement learning, all established optimal agents, including AIXI, are model-based, explicitly maintaining and using environment models. This paper introduces Universal AI with Q-Induction (AIQI), the first model-free agent proven to be asymptotically ε \varepsilon -optimal in general RL. AIQI performs universal induction over distributional action-value functions, instead of policies or environments like previous works. Under a grain of truth condition, we prove that AIQI is strong asymptotically ε \varepsilon -optimal and asymptotically ε \varepsilon -Bayes-optimal. Our results significantly expand the diversity of known universal agents.

[5] h2: 1 Introduction

[6] p: The theory of general reinforcement learning [ Lattimore, 2014 , GRL;] provides a framework for studying agents in the most general class of environments, those that satisfy only minimal structural assumptions. In this setting, AIXI [ Hutter, 2000 ] is a foundational theoretical model: it combines universal induction [ Solomonoff, 1964a , Solomonoff, 1964b ] with sequential decision theory [ Von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944 , Bellman, 1957 ] to define a Bayes-optimal agent. Although AIXI is uncomputable, it serves as a useful theoretical model of powerful goal-driven AIs [ Orseau and Ring, 2012 , Everitt et al., 2016 ] , and has also been used to define a universal measure of intelligence, called the Legg-Hutter intelligence [ Legg and Hutter, 2007 ] .

[7] p: Interestingly, AIXI and all other established optimal agents in GRL [ Hutter, 2000 , Lattimore and Hutter, 2014 , Leike et al., 2017 , Cohen et al., 2019 , Catt et al., 2023 ] , otherwise known as universal agents or universal AI, are model-based : they explicitly infer and make use of a model of the environment (a world model). In contrast, the dominant paradigm in practical RL is model-free , learning value functions or policies directly from experience [ Watkins, 1989 , Williams, 1992 , Rummery and Niranjan, 1994 , Konda and Tsitsiklis, 1999 ] . Showing that a model-free algorithm is optimal in GRL has therefore remained elusive and has been repeatedly highlighted as an open problem [ Everitt and Hutter, 2018 , Catt, 2022 , Hutter et al., 2024 ] .

[8] p: In this paper, we present Universal AI with Q - I nduction (AIQI), a model-free agent that performs universal induction over return-predictors , objects similar to distributional Q-values [ Bellemare et al., 2017 ] . AIQI is essentially an ε \varepsilon -greedy, on-policy distributional Monte Carlo control algorithm. Under a grain of truth condition [ Kalai and Lehrer, 1993 ] , we prove that AIQI achieves strong asymptotic ε \varepsilon -optimality and asymptotic ε \varepsilon -Bayes-optimality in GRL. Our results significantly expand the class of known universal agents, and provide a blueprint for the analysis of other policy iteration algorithms in general environments.

Below we discuss in detail several lines of work that are related to ours. Compress and Control [veness2015compress, CNC;] is an algorithm that shares many similarities to AIQI such as return prediction, but has only been analyzed for fully observable MDPs. Both CNC and AIQI are on-policy Monte Carlo control methods with a distributional flavour [Bellemare et al., 2017], since they predict the return distribution instead of the average return. CNC uses a model-based approach for MC evaluation, unlike AIQI. Feature reinforcement learning [daswani2015generic] converts a general environment to a finite state MDP through a learned feature map, and then performs planning [hutter2009feature] or model-free RL [nguyen2011feature, daswani2013q] inside the MDP. hutter2014extreme shows that any general environment can be mapped to an MDP where the optimal policy is still ε -optimal in the general environment. majeed2018q prove that Q-learning is optimal when the mapping satisfies “state-uniformity”. However, these theoretical results do not tell us about the learning of such mappings, and results that are concerned with the learning [sunehag2010consistency] do not concern themselves with control. AIXItl [Hutter, 2000] is an approximation of AIXI that considers a set of candidate policies that output an estimate of its value under ξ . It keeps only the policies that can be proven to output conservative estimates, and chooses the policy with the highest value estimate. Conceptually, the search for a proof that the value estimate under ξ is conservative, can be considered a generalization of planning under ξ . AIXItl doesn’t have an analogue in practical RL. Self-AIXI [Catt et al., 2023] is a variant of AIXI that learns both a model of the environment and a model of itself. At every step, it uses both models to estimate its own action-values, by simulating the model of itself interacting with the model of the environment. It then chooses the action with the highest estimated value. Although Self-AIXI gets rid of planning, it is clearly model-based in that it learns and uses an environment model. Our work was inspired by Self-AIXI, and can be seen as a modification that further gets rid of environment models by directly predicting action-values. 6 section 6 § Section §§ Sections 6 §6 6Discussion 6.1 subsection 6.1 § Section §§ Sections 6.1 §6.1 6.1Proof Technique To prove that AIQI is strong asymptotically ε -optimal, we first showed that the action-value estimates converge to the true ν π action-values (), and that choosing the action with the highest (estimated) ν π value makes the policy converge to optimal actions (). There are two key novelties in our proofs compared to existing work in universal AI. First, we had to deal with the problem of delayed feedback using periodic augmentations. This was not needed in previous works which model policies or environments, since actions and percepts are never delayed, unlike H -step returns. Second, we introduced a chain of inequalities using , to make the δ ∞ term in the upper bound vanish with γ L . This allowed us to bypass ad-hoc assumptions such as off-policy sensibility [Catt et al., 2023, Theorem 16]. Overall, our work provides a blueprint for the analysis of policy iteration algorithms in general environments. 6.2 subsection 6.2 § Section §§ Sections 6.2 §6.2 6.2Partial Observability Real-world RL tasks are almost never plain MDPs, due to partial observability [aastrom1965optimal]. The most straightforward way to extend MDP methods to partially observable MDPs (POMDPs) is by viewing the whole history as a state, and using function approximators, sequence models in particular, to handle this exponentially larger state space. In fact, this is exactly the dominant approach in modern RL, where sequence models such as LSTMs [hochreiter1997long] or transformers [vaswani2017attention] are trained to model value functions [hausknecht2015deep], policies [mnih2016asynchronous], or environments [ha2018world]. The theory of universal AI provides a particularly compelling account that is in line with practice: it regards the sequence model as a flexible learner with some broad prior over mappings whose domain consists of arbitrary length sequences. In particular, it abstracts away the idea of a hidden MDP or a hidden state space. Our work is the first to analyze a model-free algorithm under this account. 6.3 subsection 6.3 § Section §§ Sections 6.3 §6.3 6.3Continual Reinforcement Learning The class of general environments is equivalent to the class of infinite-state POMDPs, which might seem unnecessarily large. However, there are nontrivial settings that cannot be captured by finite-state POMDPs, one such example being continual RL [ring1994continual]. Continual RL studies environments that never stop changing [khetarpal2022towards], and where agents must keep learning in order to act optimally [abel2023definition]. The theory of universal AI sets aside problems with continual learning, e.g., catastrophic forgetting, by assuming a perfect Bayesian learner with a broad prior. This lets it focus on a different question: how to design principled objectives for continual RL, assuming continual learning is approximately solved. Our work shows that learning distributional action-values is one such objective. 6.4 subsection 6.4 § Section §§ Sections 6.4 §6.4 6.4Temporal Difference Learning Most practical methods in model-free RL involve temporal difference (TD) learning [sutton1988learning], a form of stochastic approximation [robbins1951stochastic]. As has been noted by engel2003bayes, engel2005reinforcement, a simple Bayesian version of TD learning does not model the correct generative process of a value function, except in deterministic environments. A possible workaround is to perform full expectation updates by learning an environment model. This is analogous to Dyna [sutton1990integrated] with one-step rollouts. We hypothesize that distributional, one-step Dyna-SARSA and Dyna-Q are, respectively, asymptotically optimal and self-optimizing. 7 section 7 § Section §§ Sections 7 §7 7Conclusion We introduced Universal AI with Q-Induction (AIQI), the first model-free universal agent. AIQI performs universal induction over distributional action-value functions—rather than over environments or policies as in prior work. It is essentially a Monte Carlo control algorithm, the most primitive form of model-free RL. We prove that AIQI is strong asymptotically ε -optimal, asymptotically ε -Bayes-optimal, and, as expected from an on-policy MC control algorithm, not self-optimizing. Remarkably, the only assumption we make is grain of truth, which is standard in universal AI and has a known resolution via reflective oracles. Our findings significantly broaden the known landscape of universal agents. We hope this provides both a theoretical foundation for understanding MC control with sequence models in partially observable or continual settings, and a blueprint for the analysis of other policy iteration algorithms in general environments. Promising directions for future work include exploring whether a single unified predictor can replace the phase-indexed family of return-predictors, investigating whether a Bayesian version of temporal difference learning can be used to construct asymptotically optimal or self-optimizing policies, and analyzing the properties of AIQI with exploration strategies that are more sophisticated than ε -greedy, such as Thompson sampling. {contributions} Briefly list author contributions. Below we discuss in detail several lines of work that are related to ours. \par Compress and Control \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{veness2015compress}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, CNC;]} is an algorithm that shares many similarities to AIQI such as return prediction, but has only been analyzed for fully observable MDPs. Both CNC and AIQI are on-policy Monte Carlo control methods with a distributional flavour \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{bellemare2017distributional}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}, since they predict the return distribution instead of the average return. CNC uses a model-based approach for MC evaluation, unlike AIQI. \par\par Feature reinforcement learning \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{daswani2015generic}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} converts a general environment to a finite state MDP through a learned feature map, and then performs planning \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{hutter2009feature}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} or model-free RL \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{nguyen2011feature,daswani2013q}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} inside the MDP. \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{hutter2014extreme}{\@@citephrase{[}}{\@@citephrase{]}}} shows that any general environment can be mapped to an MDP where the optimal policy is still $\varepsilon$-optimal in the general environment. \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{majeed2018q}{\@@citephrase{[}}{\@@citephrase{]}}} prove that Q-learning is optimal when the mapping satisfies ``state-uniformity''. However, these theoretical results do not tell us about the learning of such mappings, and results that are concerned with the learning \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{sunehag2010consistency}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} do not concern themselves with control. \par AIXI{tl} \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{hutter2000theory}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} is an approximation of AIXI that considers a set of candidate policies that output an estimate of its value under $\xi$. It keeps only the policies that can be proven to output conservative estimates, and chooses the policy with the highest value estimate. Conceptually, the search for a proof that the value estimate under $\xi$ is conservative, can be considered a generalization of planning under $\xi$. AIXI{tl} doesn't have an analogue in practical RL. \par Self-AIXI \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{catt2023self}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} is a variant of AIXI that learns both a model of the environment and a model of itself. At every step, it uses both models to estimate its own action-values, by simulating the model of itself interacting with the model of the environment. It then chooses the action with the highest estimated value. Although Self-AIXI gets rid of planning, it is clearly model-based in that it learns and uses an environment model. Our work was inspired by Self-AIXI, and can be seen as a modification that further gets rid of environment models by directly predicting action-values. \par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Discussion} \par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Proof Technique} \par To prove that AIQI is strong asymptotically $\varepsilon$-optimal, we first showed that the action-value estimates converge to the true $\nu^{\pi}$ action-values (\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{thm:error-q-estimation}), and that choosing the action with the highest (estimated) $\nu^{\pi}$ value makes the policy converge to optimal actions (\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{thm:aiqi-eps-optimal}). There are two key novelties in our proofs compared to existing work in universal AI. First, we had to deal with the problem of delayed feedback using periodic augmentations. This was not needed in previous works which model policies or environments, since actions and percepts are never delayed, unlike $H$-step returns. Second, we introduced a chain of inequalities using \lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{thm:one-step-and-global}, to make the $\delta_{\infty}$ term in the upper bound vanish with $\gamma^{L}$. This allowed us to bypass ad-hoc assumptions such as off-policy sensibility \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{catt2023self}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Theorem\penalty\ 16]}. Overall, our work provides a blueprint for the analysis of policy iteration algorithms in general environments. \par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Partial Observability} \par Real-world RL tasks are almost never plain MDPs, due to partial observability \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{aastrom1965optimal}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}. The most straightforward way to extend MDP methods to partially observable MDPs (POMDPs) is by viewing the whole history as a state, and using function approximators, sequence models in particular, to handle this exponentially larger state space. In fact, this is exactly the dominant approach in modern RL, where sequence models such as LSTMs \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{hochreiter1997long}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} or transformers \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{vaswani2017attention}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} are trained to model value functions \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{hausknecht2015deep}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}, policies \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{mnih2016asynchronous}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}, or environments \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{ha2018world}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}. \par The theory of universal AI provides a particularly compelling account that is in line with practice: it regards the sequence model as a flexible learner with some broad prior over mappings whose domain consists of arbitrary length sequences. In particular, it abstracts away the idea of a hidden MDP or a hidden state space. Our work is the first to analyze a model-free algorithm under this account. \par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Continual Reinforcement Learning} \par The class of general environments is equivalent to the class of infinite-state POMDPs, which might seem unnecessarily large. However, there are nontrivial settings that cannot be captured by finite-state POMDPs, one such example being continual RL \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{ring1994continual}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}. Continual RL studies environments that never stop changing \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{khetarpal2022towards}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}, and where agents must keep learning in order to act optimally \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{abel2023definition}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}. The theory of universal AI sets aside problems with continual \emph{learning}, e.g., catastrophic forgetting, by assuming a perfect Bayesian learner with a broad prior. This lets it focus on a different question: how to design principled \emph{objectives} for continual RL, assuming continual learning is approximately solved. Our work shows that learning distributional action-values is one such objective. \par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Temporal Difference Learning} \par Most practical methods in model-free RL involve temporal difference (TD) learning \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{sutton1988learning}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}, a form of stochastic approximation \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{robbins1951stochastic}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}. As has been noted by \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{engel2003bayes,engel2005reinforcement}{\@@citephrase{[}}{\@@citephrase{]}}}, a simple Bayesian version of TD learning does not model the correct generative process of a value function, except in deterministic environments. A possible workaround is to perform full expectation updates by learning an environment model. This is analogous to Dyna \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{sutton1990integrated}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]} with one-step rollouts. We hypothesize that distributional, one-step Dyna-SARSA and Dyna-Q are, respectively, asymptotically optimal and self-optimizing. \par\par \par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Conclusion} \par We introduced Universal AI with Q-Induction (AIQI), the first model-free universal agent. AIQI performs universal induction over distributional action-value functions---rather than over environments or policies as in prior work. It is essentially a Monte Carlo control algorithm, the most primitive form of model-free RL. We prove that AIQI is strong asymptotically $\varepsilon$-optimal, asymptotically $\varepsilon$-Bayes-optimal, and, as expected from an on-policy MC control algorithm, not self-optimizing. Remarkably, the only assumption we make is grain of truth, which is standard in universal AI and has a known resolution via reflective oracles. \par Our findings significantly broaden the known landscape of universal agents. We hope this provides both a theoretical foundation for understanding MC control with sequence models in partially observable or continual settings, and a blueprint for the analysis of other policy iteration algorithms in general environments. Promising directions for future work include exploring whether a single unified predictor can replace the phase-indexed family of return-predictors, investigating whether a Bayesian version of temporal difference learning can be used to construct asymptotically optimal or self-optimizing policies, and analyzing the properties of AIQI with exploration strategies that are more sophisticated than $\varepsilon$-greedy, such as Thompson sampling. \par\par\contributions Briefly list author contributions.
