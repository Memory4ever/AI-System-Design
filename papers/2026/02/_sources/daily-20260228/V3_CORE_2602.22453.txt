Abstract
Recent work has identified a subset of attention heads in Transformer as retrieval heads, which are responsible for retrieving information from the context. In this work, we first investigate retrieval heads in multilingual contexts. In multilingual language models, we find that retrieval heads are often shared across multiple languages. Expanding the study to cross-lingual setting, we identify
Retrieval-Transition heads (RTH)
, which govern the transition to specific target-language output.
Our experiments reveal that RTHs are distinct from retrieval heads and more vital for Chain-of-Thought reasoning in multilingual LLMs. Across four multilingual benchmarks (MMLU-ProX, MGSM, MLQA, and XQuaD) and two model families (Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.1), we demonstrate that masking RTH induces bigger performance drop than masking Retrieval Heads (RH).
Our work advances understanding of multilingual LMs by isolating the attention heads responsible for mapping to target languages.
1
Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved state-of-the-art performance in reasoning and knowledge-intensive tasks
DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025)
;
Qwen et al. (2025)
. As LLMs are deployed globally, their ability to retrieve, reason, and respond across multiple languages became crucial. Recent research has linked specific behaviors to internal circuits
Zhou et al. (2024)
;
Ferrando et al. (2024)
, notably identifying
retrieval heads
(RH) as the sparse subset of attention heads responsible for extracting facts from long contexts
Wu et al. (2024)
.
In this work, we investigate multilingual retrieval by extending standard Needle-In-A-Haystack (NIAH) analysis to multiple languages. Evaluating three model families across English, German, Chinese, and Swahili, we find a consistent mixture of language-shared and language-exclusive retrieval heads. For instance, Qwen2.5-7B exhibits 59% overlap across all four languages, while Phi-3.5 shows significantly higher language-specific specialization with 21%
en
-only heads (§
3
). We observe a rough correlation between model pretraining corpus and the shared
linguistic
nature of its retrieval head, similar to analogous findings for MLP heads
Chen et al. (2025)
.
Multilingual proficiency requires more than "copy-paste" retrieval; models must translate retrieved evidence into target-language output.
Multilingual models are observed to reason within a English-centric latent space, independent of the specific language used for final generation.
Wendler et al. (2024a)
;
Wang et al. (2025)
.
This raises a critical question:
what enables the transition from language-agnostic reasoning to language-specific token generation?
We define Retrieval-Transition Heads (RTH), by measuring whether a head’s attention aligns target-language output tokens with their corresponding source-language "needle" counterparts (§
4
).
Finally, through causal interventions across two model families (
Qwen-2.5
and
Llama-3.1
) and four benchmarks, we establish that RTHs are functionally indispensable for multilingual reasoning. We find that masking a sparse set of RTHs triggers a catastrophic performance collapse—most notably a 54-point drop in MGSM for Llama-3.1—over 3
×
\times
the drop seen in standard RH (§
5
)
. Qualitatively, RTH masking spikes loss of language coherence when compared to RH masking which predominately causes retrieval failures (Table
3
). These results characterize RTHs as a critical, language-agnostic bottleneck for the internal reasoning mechanisms of LLMs.
Our main contributions are as follows:
•
We analyze retrieval heads in multilingual LLMs, finding that they decompose into language-shared and language-exclusive subsets, with significant variance across model families.
•
We propose the Retrieval-Transition Score to measure cross-lingual correspondence at the head level and characterize the Retrieval-Transition Heads responsible for mapping retrieved evidence to target-language synthesis.
•
Through masking experiments on several multilingual reasoning benchmarks we causally prove the role of RTH for CoT reasoning in multilingual LLMs.

Related Work
Role of attention heads
Various studies have proposed techniques to explain Multi-Head Attention (MHA) in Transformer architectures and discovered distinct functions performed by a certain set of attention heads
Zheng et al. (2024)
;
Gould et al. (2024)
. For example,
McDougall et al. (2024)
studies copy suppression, the functionality of the attention head to reduce the prediction of a token that has appeared previously in the context.
Olsson et al. (2022)
discovered
Induction heads
, which implement a "match and copy" mechanism to complete patterns found in the preceding context. They observe that emergence of Induction Heads coincides with sudden improvement in in-context learning abilities of the models. Most relevant to our work,
Wu et al. (2024)
identify
Retrieval Heads
as the primary drivers of long-form in-context learning and retrieval. They note that masking the 50 most influential retrieval heads leads up to 50% drop in performance tasks requiring Chain-of-Thought reasoning. This discovery has enabled practical applications, such as head-level KV cache compression
Fu et al. (2025)
and improving contextual faithfulness in long-horizon reasoning
Huang et al. (2025)
.
Understanding Multilingual Language Models
Despite a English-centric bias in training data, MLMs demonstrate surprisingly good multilingual proficiency
Zhao et al. (2025)
.
Wendler et al. (2024a)
investigated this via logit lens and causal interventions. They show that while the initial and final layers operate in the input/output language, the middle layers transition into an English-centric but latent concept space.
Following this study,
Wang et al. (2025)
explored cross-lingual inconsistencies, where models successfully retrieve parametric facts when prompted in English but fail in other languages. They attribute failure in mapping language-agnostic concepts back into the specific non-English token space. Recent work by
Chen et al. (2025)
reports that as models scale and are trained more multi-lingually, they move away from English-centric behaviour and develop a core language-agnostic parameter space composed of MLP heads termed as "shared neurons". Further,
Zhang et al. (2025a)
reported that MLMs share circuits for identical syntactic processes while employing distinct attention heads and feed-forward layers for language specific linguistic processes. In our work we investigate whether retrieval heads, the backbone of complex reasoning, are similarly partitioned and compare how this attribute varies with model families.
Attention heads in multilingual LLMs
Recent studies have localized language-specific heads and demonstrated how causal interventions can shift a model’s language focus
Liu et al. (2025)
or trigger direct translation
Anonymous (2025)
. While we share the goal of identifying heads responsible for multilingual performance, prior efforts focus largely on translation or short-context QA. These approaches offer limited insight into modern workflows requiring sustained coherence across long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) sequences. We introduce Retrieval-Transition Heads (RTH), which are analogous to standard retrieval heads
Wu et al. (2024)
but specialized for multilingual contexts. Crucially, whereas previous work attributes performance drops to incorrect output language (language-shifting), we demonstrate that RTH masking causes failures in linguistic coherence and context grounding even when the model attempts to answer in the correct target language.

3.3
Analyzing distribution of retrieval heads across different languages
We find that retrieval heads also exhibit a mixture of language-shared and language-exclusive heads, consistent with patterns discovered in recent multilingual studies
Liu et al. (2025)
;
Chen et al. (2025)
. However, from Figure

For instance, 118 of 200 (59.0%) heads in Qwen2.5-7B are shared among all four languages while 94 of 211 heads (44.5%) in Phi3.5-3B are shared. Moreover, the proportion of bilingual
en-zh

Retrieval-Transition heads
While retrieval heads facilitate reasoning by copying information from the previous context
Wu et al. (2024)
, this mechanism alone is insufficient for LLMs trained on multiple languages.
Because MLMs operate within a target-language-agnostic concept space
Wendler et al. (2024b)
;
Wang et al. (2025)
;
Chen et al. (2025)
, they must do more than just copy
; they must translate latent, abstract thoughts into specific target-language tokens. Modifying experimental setup from standard retrieval head detection, we identify distinct heads, which we name as
Retrieval-Transition heads (RTH)
that perform this mapping behavior.
Consequently, we show that masking these heads causes a more severe drop in CoT performance than standard retrieval heads (RH), as the model is impaired from its ability to verbalize latent thoughts.

Illustration of the retrieval-transition score (RTS). Needle tokens in the source language are first aligned to their target-language equivalents using a LM. The degree of overlap between the model’s generated tokens and these aligned target needles quantifies each attention head’s contribution to cross-lingual retrieval.

5.3
Qualitative Analysis
We conduct a qualitative error analysis to better understand the failures induced by masking, identifying two dominant failure modes: loss of coherence (LoC) and failure to retrieve (FR). In the LoC mode, masking RTHs fundamentally disrupts generation, causing the model to frequently collapse into nonsensical outputs, repetition, or looping behaviors. In contrast, in the FR mode, the model maintains fluency but does not retrieve the key information required to answer correctly—even when that information resides within the provided context or the model’s own chain-of-thought.
To quantify these failure modes, we evaluate Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct at
k
=
25
k=25
using Qwen3-235B-A22B
Yang et al. (2025)
as an LLM-as-a-judge (see Figure

As detailed in Table

, the proportion of LoC failures is notably higher when masking RTHs compared to masking RHs. Furthermore, we observe distinct cross-lingual and cross-dataset variations. On MMLU-ProX, Swahili exhibits a markedly different error profile under RH-Mask compared to English, German, and Chinese, with generation failures occurring substantially more frequently than retrieval failures. Under RTH-Mask, however, lack of coherence failures mode increases substantially across all evaluated languages. We observe a similar trend on MGSM, where masking RTHs consistently induces higher rates of LoC as compared to masking RH. The primary cross-dataset discrepancy lies in how Swahili responds to RH-Mask: while RH masking triggers a high volume of generation failures on MMLU-ProX, this degradation is significantly less pronounced on MGSM. This suggests that when evaluating low-resource languages on tasks requiring generative synthesis with minimal fact retrieval (such as MGSM), RTHs are likely more critical than RHs, especially in comparison to tasks that demand precise information recovery from either the local context or parametric memory (such as MMLU-ProX). 6
Conclusion and Future Work
This paper identifies Retrieval-Transition Heads (RTHs), a specialized sparse set of attention heads within multilingual LLMs responsible for bridging the gap between latent reasoning and target-language generation. Unlike standard retrieval heads that implement a copy-paste mechanism, RTHs bridge the target-language independent latent space to the final output. We demonstrate that RTHs are functionally indispensable for complex tasks: masking them triggers a catastrophic performance collapse on multilingual reasoning benchmarks. These findings have immediate, corrective implications for state-of-the-art efficiency methods. Recent frameworks for KV-cache compression
Fu et al. (2025)
;
Tang et al. (2024)
, efficient inference
Xiao et al. (2024)
, and context re-ranking
Zhang et al. (2025b)
rely on heuristics that prioritize standard retrieval behavior. Our results suggest these methods risk pruning the critical "transition" nodes required for multilingual coherence. We propose that future "transition-aware" strategies must incorporate RTH identification to preserve the specific heads responsible for translating latent context into linguistic fluency, thereby preventing the reasoning degradation that standard pruning would otherwise cause in multilingual settings.
Limitations
In spite of strong causal results on downstream tasks, our methodology faces the following constraints and limitations. First, the Retrieval Transition Score (RTS) approximates the language-agnostic concept space using English as a proxy; while effective due to inherent model biases, this results in RTHs that are less sharply defined than standard retrieval heads.
Another limitation of this study is its exclusive focus on attention heads. While heads are accessible units for causal masking, they represent only one part of the model’s reasoning circuitry. Future work should explore the interplay between these heads, MLP ’shared neurons’, and residual stream activations to provide a more holistic understanding of the latent-to-target language transition.
Finally, as models are trained with more balanced multilingual data, the single-language approximation (e.g. English) becomes typologically distant from the model’s latent space reducing the discriminative signal of the method. Moreover, future research should transcend this reliance on pivot languages by developing systems that learn to mimic true latent tokens. By generating a model-specific latent vocabulary, researchers can move toward identifying transition mechanisms that reflect the model’s internal conceptual structures rather than cross-lingual projections.

We observe that the degree of cross-lingual head intersection is strongly correlated with pre-training data composition and scale. In Qwen-2.5 7B, the massive 18T token corpus and high-density non-English data promote "universal" retrieval mechanisms, with 59.0% of heads shared across all four studied languages. Conversely, Phi-3.5 3B exhibits greater linguistic specialization, with the lowest 4-language intersection (44.5%) and a bias toward English-specific heads; this likely reflects its reliance on high-quality, primarily English synthetic data. These findings suggest that while scale drives language-agnostic representations, the specific data mixture determines whether a model develops universal or language-specific retrieval pathways.

Masking RTHs disrupts the mapping from latent reasoning to target-language syntax. While the model may grasp the underlying fact, it fails to synthesize grammatical output, resulting in structural gibberish or character-level hallucinations.
