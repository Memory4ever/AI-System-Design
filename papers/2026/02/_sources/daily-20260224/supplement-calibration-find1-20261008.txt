Building on the Folk Theorem, prior work shows that cooperation can be sustained when algorithms embed credible punishment for deviations [cite71†51 , cite72†28 , cite73†36 , cite74†2 ].\nL119: More recent studies find that collusive equilibria may also emerge without explicit threat mechanisms, as shown in a Stackelberg framework [cite75†6 ], and examine how algorithmic (non-)collusion can be detected and regulated through data audits [cite76†29 , cite77†30 ].\nL120: Collusive behavior has been documented in e-commerce and dynamic pricing settings [cite78†22 , cite79†13 , cite80†43 , cite81†7 , cite82†12 ]. Following cite68†Calvano et al. [14] , a growing body of literature investigates algorithmic collusion in laboratory across settings ranging from stylized games, such as the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma [cite83†42 , cite84†10 ], to more realistic auctions [cite85†9 ].\nL121: These studies also consider different algorithms, including reinforcement learning [cite86†35 , cite87†31 ], bounded-regret methods [cite72†28 ], and LLMs [cite69†23 , cite88†5 ]. Several follow-up studies suggest that prior findings may overstate the threat of algorithmic collusion, as they rely on restrictive modeling assumptions [cite89†1 ].\nL122: Collusion typically emerges after long training horizons—on the order of millions of repeated-game rounds—and tends to depend on symmetric algorithmic configurations, suggesting implicit coordination or prior communication [cite90†37 ]. Moreover, the algorithmic configurations that give rise to collusion may be irrational in practice, being outperformed by more robust or commonly used alternatives [cite91†3 , cite92†11 ].\nL123: Thus, the emergence of algorithmic tacit collusion among rational agents under realistic conditions and finite interaction horizons remains an open question.\nL124: ##### Strategy selection, adaptation, and evaluation.\nL125: Our work focuses on strategy performance at deployment, resembling a test-time or tournament environment. Prior research has examined strategy selection and evaluation through meta-game frameworks [cite93†34 , cite94†38 ] and empirical game-theoretic analysis (EGTA) [cite95†58 ], applied to domains such as trading agent competitions [cite96†47 , cite97†21 , cite98†45 ], financial markets [cite99†53 , cite100†57 , cite101†56 , cite102†55 ], supply chain management [cite103†33 ], negotiation [cite94†38 ], and auctions [cite104†52 ].\nL126: Related to our setting is cite105†Carissimo et al. [15] , who model collusion as coordination in Q-learning hyperparameters (e.g., discount and exploration factors) during training. Our study differs by focusing on test-time adaptation among pretrained agents with heterogeneous initializations, distinguishing deployment behavior from training dynamics.\nL127: Related online learning literature also examines adaptability and non-exploitability in repeated games [cite106†17 , cite107†18 , cite108†19 ], and develops approximate best-response methods for equilibrium analysis in complex games such as poker and Go [cite109†39 , cite110†50 , cite111†40 ]. These insights inform our design of metrics for characterizing pretrained policies and their adaptation strategies.\nL128: ## 2 Preliminaries\nL129: \nL130: We consider an $n$-player simultaneous-move repeated game progressing from time $0$ to an end time unknown to the players. At each round $t$, a player $j$ commits to an action (e.g., price) $p_{j,t}\\in\\mathcal{P}$, where $\\mathcal{P}$ denotes a discrete action space.\nL131: The game admits a Stage-Game Nash Equilibrium (SGNE) in which no player can profitably deviate in a single round, given that stage games are independent across periods. Let $r^{N}_{j}$ denote player $j$’s competitive payoff, $\\bar{r}^{N}$ the average competitive payoff, and $p^{N}_{j}$ the competitive action of player $j$ under the SGNE. In pricing settings with homogeneous goods and symmetric costs, this corresponds to the Bertrand Nash equilibrium.\nL132: Players may nonetheless achieve higher payoffs through coordinated behavior that avoids the SGNE. The optimal joint outcome for all players in a stage game can be derived by maximizing the joint payoffs; we refer to this outcome as the monopoly which characterizes full collusion. In pricing contexts, such outcomes are detrimental to consumers. Let $r^{M}_{j}$ denote player $j$’s monopoly payoff, $\\bar{r}^{M}$ the average monopoly payoff, and $p^{M}_{j}$ the associated monopoly action of player $j$.\nL133: The repeated pricing game can be modeled as an extensive-form game whose central solution concept is the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE)—a strategy profile that constitutes a Nash equilibrium in every subgame. According to the Folk Theorem [cite112†24 ], many SPEs exist, with each supported by credible threats, i.e., responses that are rational for the player making them [cite113†48 ]. By contrast, non-credible threats cannot sustain an SPE, and monopoly outcomes often depend on such threats.\nL134: Indeed, in our experiments, we find no evidence of credible threats among pretrained policy pairs, suggesting that realistic collusion may not rely on them.\nL135: Following cite68†Calvano et al. [14] , we define the Collusion Index (CoI) as a measure of collusion: when CoI $=0\\%$, players are fully competitive by playing SGNE, whereas CoI $=100\\%$ corresponds to full collusion at the monopoly outcome.\nL136: ###### Definition 2.1 (Collusion Index (CoI)).\nL137: \nL138:  | $$CoI\\coloneqq\\frac{\\bar{r}-\\bar{r}^{N}}{\\bar{r}^{M}-\\bar{r}^{N}}\\text{, where $\\bar{r}$ is the average per-player payoff.}$$  |\nL139: We define a state of the game $s\\in\\mathcal{S}$ as actions (i.e., prices) of all players in one round, ordered by the player IDs. A policy $\\pi_{j,t}\\in\\Pi$ for player $j$ at time $t$ maps the state $S_{t}=s$ to a probability distribution over actions.\nL140: Given a discount factor $\\gamma$ and a fixed policy profile $(\\pi_{j},\\pi_{-j})$, let $V^{\\pi_{j}|\\pi_{-j}}(s)$ denote the expected utility of player $j$ starting from state $s$.\nL141: ###### Definition 2.2 (State-Value Function).\nL142: \nL143: Suppose player $j$ interacts with an opponent following a fixed policy $\\pi_{-j}$. We model this by applying a learning algorithm $\\mathcal{A}_{\\theta}$ and a set of random seeds $\\mathcal{K}$ to a game of interest $\\mathcal{G}$ to generate a set of initial policy profiles $\\Pi$.\nL154: We assume each pretraining process, i.e., a stochastic procedure that produces a policy profile $\\pi_{\\kappa}=\\{\\pi^{\\kappa}_{1},...,\\pi^{\\kappa}_{N}\\}=\\mathcal{A}_{\\theta}(\\mathcal{G},\\kappa)$, is subject to no time constraint or cost of learning.\nL155: #### 3.1.2 Strategy\nL156: Without adaptation, however, pretrained policies can fail against unfamiliar opponents, resulting in unexpected state transitions and suboptimal outcomes at test time.^{1}^{1} 1 Indeed, in experiments, we find that two independently pretrained Q-learning agents, when paired against each other without adaptation, can yield payoffs lower than competitive pricing (see Fig. cite114†4 in the appendix).\nL157: Achieving strong performance thus requires an update procedure to adapt the initial policy to the specific opponent during play. We define an agent’s strategy for the game at test time $\\mathcal{G^{*}}$ as the combination of a (pretrained) initial policy and an update procedure. This strategy is evaluated over a shorter interaction horizon, where learning incurs costs and performance at each round counts.\nL158: Rather than directly modifying the policy, we consider strategies that operate on an underlying internal representation that encodes the policy. We denote this representation by $Z_{j,t}$ for player $j$ at time $t$, and it is updated after each round. For example, in Q-learning, the internal representation consists of Q-values, from which the agent selects the action with the highest value.\nL159: Formally, a strategy is specified by two functions, typically determined by the learning algorithm and its hyperparameters:\nL160: \nL161:   * •\nL162: \nL163: A decoding function $\\phi$ which maps the internal representation to a policy, $\\phi(Z_{j,t})=\\pi_{j,t}$, and\nL164: \nL165:   * •\nL166: \nL167: An update function $\\omega$ which updates the representation based on the latest experience (i.e., the state), $\\omega(Z_{j,t},S_{j,t})=Z_{j,t+1}$.\nL168: At any time $t$, a player’s strategy is fully specified by its current representation $Z_{j,t}$ and its decoding and update functions $(\\phi_{j},\\omega_{j})$. In Section cite20†4 , we will illustrate how different algorithms, including Q-learning, UCB, and LLMs, instantiate these components.\nL169: #### 3.1.3 Meta-strategy\nL170: Pretraining can generate a large set of initial policies, each of which may be paired with infinitely many update procedures (e.g., varying learning rates). As a result, the induced strategy space becomes prohibitively large, rendering direct and systematic analysis intractable.\nL171: To manage this complexity, we group initial policies according to their performance along two key strategic dimensions: their ability to achieve cooperation (i.e., how effectively a policy learns to cooperate with a specific partner) and their ability to avoid exploitation (i.e., how robustly the policy performs against a best-response opponent). Potential Impact\nL248: \nL249: The emergence of agentic search systems necessitates a critical re-examination of the assumptions underlying modern IR infrastructures, which were developed around the characteristics of organic query streams. A preliminary analysis of a sample of ASQ’s traces reveals several systematic differences between synthetic and organic queries with direct implications for search system design.\nL250: Query Pre-processing. Spelling correction and query normalisation techniques may add unnecessary latency when applied indiscriminately to synthetic query streams. Our analysis shows that while approximately 16% of organic queries contain spelling errors,^{9}^{9} 9 This is based on the English spellchecker built into MacOS 26.2. consistent with prior query log studies (cite89†Sun et al., 2012 ), only 5% of synthetic queries exhibit spelling mistakes.\nL251: Query Understanding. Intent classification and query understanding components trained on organic query distributions may degrade on synthetic queries due to structural shifts. We observe a 25--50% reduction in WH-word^{10}^{10} 10 WH-words are interrogative words that typically begin with “wh” in English: what, why, how, when, where, who, which, and whose. frequency between organic and synthetic queries across all three benchmarks.\nL252: Additionally, the hapax legomena^{11}^{11} 11 Hapax legomena are words occurring exactly once in a given text. ratio decreases by 25–40%, indicating greater term repetition in synthetic queries.\nL253: Query Processing. The concentrated term distribution in synthetic queries may improve posting list cache hit rates, but could affect BM25 scoring as repeated terms receive lower IDF weights. This distributional shift may also impact the training of learned sparse models and their query expansion abilities at inference time. Our measurements show synthetic queries exhibit hapax ratios of only 21–43% compared to 62–78% in organic streams.\nL254: Result Caching. Traditional exact-match caching strategies become less effective for synthetic query streams due to reduced query repetition. Within a single session, human users frequently repeat exact queries (12% in RQ), whereas agents exhibit lower repetition rates (5% in RQ).\nL255: Semantic Caching. Semantic caching, where results are shared across semantically similar queries rather than requiring exact matches, presents significant opportunities for synthetic query streams. Queries generated from the same original query share 35% (HQA) to 83% (RQ) Jaccard similarity, despite low exact-match overlap.\nL256: \nL257: ASQ enables the community to further investigate these differences and to develop retrieval systems robust to mixed organic-synthetic query distributions.\nL258: ## 8. Challenges & Limitations\nL259: Our approach faces several challenges and limitations. Frequent releases of new agents make ASQ susceptible to inherent staleness. Moreover, inference with agentic RAG systems is computationally intensive and requires substantial hardware resources, limiting us to only use a limited number of configurations over a restricted number of query sets. Since organic, full-scale, production logs are publicly unavailable, we ground ASQ on query sets sampled from existing benchmarks.\nL260: While this prevents the study query frequency and long-tail distributions, it does not hinder the study of how agents transform queries during search. Moreover, the controlled nature of benchmark queries enables reproducible experiments and fair comparisons across agent configurations. Finally, we do not provide relevance judgments for sub-queries, limiting the ability to evaluate intermediate retrievals.\nL261: ## 9.
