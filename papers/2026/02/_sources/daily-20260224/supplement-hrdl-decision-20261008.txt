2026\nL82: ###### Abstract.\nL83: When training artificial intelligence (AI) to perform tasks, humans often care not only about whether a task is completed but also how it is performed. As AI agents tackle increasingly complex tasks, aligning their behavior with human-provided specifications becomes critical for responsible AI deployment. Reward design provides a direct channel for such alignment by translating human expectations into reward functions that guide reinforcement learning (RL).\nL84: However, existing methods are often too limited to capture nuanced human preferences that arise in long-horizon tasks. Hence, we introduce Hierarchical Reward Design from Language (HRDL): a problem formulation that extends classical reward design to encode richer behavioral specifications for hierarchical RL agents. We further propose Language to Hierarchical Rewards (L2HR) as a solution to HRDL.\nL85: Experiments show that AI agents trained with rewards designed via L2HR not only complete tasks effectively but also better adhere to human specifications. Together, HRDL and L2HR advance the research on human-aligned AI agents.\nL86: ###### Keywords:\nL87: \nL88: Reward Design; Human-Centered AI; Hierarchical RL\nL89: ### 1. Introduction\nL90: cite60†Image: Refer to caption Figure 1. This work introduces the Hierarchical Reward Design from Language (HRDL) problem. Unlike prior work on reward design, HRDL decomposes reward design into low- and high-level components $(\\tilde{r}_{L},\\tilde{r}_{H})$. Language to Hierarchical Rewards (L2HR), our proposed solution to HRDL, leverages language models to guide the synthesis of these hierarchical rewards, enabling existing RL algorithms to train agents that are better aligned with human specifications.\nL91: Robots and other AI agents are increasingly being deployed in human-centric environments, such as homes, hospitals, and disaster zones (cite61†Scopelliti et al., 2004 ; cite62†Kidd and Breazeal, 2008 ; cite63†Pantofaru et al., 2012 ; cite64†Unhelkar et al., 2018 ; cite65†Unhelkar et al., 2019 ; cite66†Gonzalez-Aguirre et al., 2021 ; cite67†Wang et al., 2024 ; cite68†Qian et al., 2025 ; cite69†Seo et al., 2025 ).\nL92: Their usefulness depends not only on task completion, but also on respecting human intentions, operational rules, and safety requirements (collectively referred to as behavior specifications).\nL93: Aligning agent behavior with these specifications is central to their responsible deployment, with various paradigms being actively explored for conveying such specifications (cite70†Chernova and Thomaz, 2022 ). This work focuses on reward design, a paradigm where humans convey specifications through reward functions that guide reinforcement learning (RL), typically before AI deployment.\nL94: This paradigm is particularly suitable for use cases where specifications are relatively stable and the costs of reward design can be amortized, such as during the repeated use of AI agents by humans in domain-specific contexts. ^{†}^{†} This article is an extended version of an identically-titled paper accepted at the International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2026).\nL95: As AI agents take on increasingly complex tasks, more advanced reward design methods are needed to capture equally complex specifications. Humans rarely reason about tasks and associated specifications as monolithic goals (cite71†Miller et al., 2017 ; cite72†Botvinick et al., 2009 ; cite73†Correa et al., 2023 ; cite74†Catrambone, 1998 ; cite75†Ramaraj, 2023 ; cite76†Kim et al., 2013 ). Instead, we naturally break them into subtasks.\nL96: Hierarchical frameworks in RL mirror this structure by decomposing tasks into subtasks and organizing them over long horizons (cite77†Dayan and Hinton, 1992 ; cite78†Parr and Russell, 1997 ; cite79†Sutton et al., 1999 ; cite80†Dietterich, 2000 ). This hierarchical approach to policy learning has enabled agents to complete tasks of increasingly longer horizons. However, the reward design for these hierarchical RL agents remains largely unexplored, thereby limiting human-AI alignment.\nL97: As illustrated in cite81†Fig. 1 , specifications for long-horizon tasks often include details on what subtasks to perform, in which order, and how they are executed. Existing reward design methods encode these specifications via a flat reward function of the form $\\tilde{r}_{flat}(s,a)$. We show theoretically and empirically that flat rewards are fundamentally limited in capturing specifications for long-horizon tasks. To address this limitation:\nL98: \nL99:   * •\nL100: We introduce the Hierarchical Reward Design (HRD) problem, which enables designers to express behavioral specifications inspired by the same structured way people naturally think and teach. Unlike the classical (flat) reward design problem (cite82†Singh et al., 2009 ), HRD admits reward solutions that enable encoding of complex specifications for long-horizon tasks, capturing both what subtasks to perform and how to execute them.\nL101: HRD is a general formulation that can be instantiated with multiple input modalities, analogous to how flat reward design has been realized via proxy signals or language (cite83†Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017 ; cite84†Ma et al., 2024 ; cite85†Kwon et al., 2023 ). Because natural language is an intuitive medium for specifying layered instructions, we then provide a language-based instantiation called Hierarchical Reward Design from Language (HRDL, pronounced “hurdle”).\nL102:   * •\nL103: \nL104: We prove that hierarchical rewards of HRDL are strictly more expressive than flat rewards used by prior works, while remaining compatible with standard decision-making frameworks (i.e. Markov and semi-Markov Decision Processes) and RL algorithms.\nL105: \nL106:   * •\nL107: We present Language to Hierarchical Rewards (L2HR), an initial solution to HRDL that generates hierarchical rewards directly from natural language specifications, making reward design more accessible while leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models (cite86†Achiam et al., 2023 ; cite87†Huang and Chang, 2023 ; cite88†Li et al., 2022 ). L2HR produces reward structures that guide both high-level subtask selection and low-level execution.\nL108: Human subject and numerical experiments demonstrate hierarchical reward design’s advantages over flat reward design. Hierarchical reward design (coupled with hierarchical RL) enables AI agents to successfully complete tasks and better align their behavior with language specifications. We view this work as an initial but important step toward aligning AI systems with human expectations through the lens of HRD.\nL109: Through theoretical analysis and empirical findings, this paper lays the groundwork for future research on designing human-aligned reward structures that employ hierarchies and human input.\nL110: ### 2. Background and Related Work\nL111: This work focuses on AI agents tasked with sequential decision-making tasks, which are commonly modeled using Markov Decision Processes (MDP) or its variants (cite89†Puterman, 2014 ; cite90†Sutton and Barto, 2018 ). An MDP is defined by the tuple $\\mathcal{M}\\doteq(\\mathcal{S},\\mathcal{A},T$, $r,\\gamma,h)$, where $\\mathcal{S}$ and $\\mathcal{A}$ are the state and action spaces, $T(s^{\\prime}|s,a)$ the transition dynamics, $r(s,a)$ the reward function, $\\gamma\\in[0,1]$ the discount factor, and $h$ the horizon.\nL112: MDPs can be solved using RL, which aims to find a policy $\\pi(a|s)$ that maximizes the expected discounted return $\\mathbb{E}[\\sum_{t=0}^{h}\\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})]$.\nL113: #### 2.1. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning\nL114: While capable, standard RL algorithms struggle with long-horizon tasks. Hierarchical RL (HRL) seeks to solve MDPs with long horizons by decomposing them into simpler subtasks  (cite77†Dayan and Hinton, 1992 ; cite78†Parr and Russell, 1997 ; cite79†Sutton et al., 1999 ; cite80†Dietterich, 2000 ; cite91†Seo and Unhelkar, 2024 ; cite92†Seo and Unhelkar, 2025 ). A widely used HRL paradigm is the options framework (cite79†Sutton et al., 1999 ; cite93†Bacon et al., 2017 ; cite94†Zhang and Whiteson, 2019 ).\nL115: Here, the agent has access to a discrete set of temporally-extended behaviors called options $\\mathcal{O}$. Each $o\\in\\mathcal{O}$ is associated with an intra-option policy $\\pi_{L}(a|s,o)$ and a termination condition $\\beta(o^{-},s)$. A high-level policy $\\pi_{H}(o|o^{-},s)$ selects options, while the low-level policy executes primitive actions until the selected option terminates. The reward model for options computes the expected cumulative reward until option termination.\nL116: Following (cite79†Sutton et al., 1999 ), we let $\\mathcal{E}(o,s,t)$ denote the event where option $o$ is initiated in state $s$ at timestep $t$, and define the option-level reward as:\nL117: (1)  |  | $$\\displaystyle r_{opt}(s,o)\\doteq\\mathbb{E}[\\Sigma_{i=1}^{k}\\gamma^{i-1}r_{t+i}\\mid\\mathcal{E}(o,s,t)]$$  |\nL118: \nL119: where $k$ denotes the number of steps after which the initiated option $o$ terminates, determined by its termination condition $\\beta$.\nL120: Another line of HRL research follows the feudal/goal-conditioned framework (cite77†Dayan and Hinton, 1992 ; cite95†Vezhnevets et al., 2017 ; cite96†Kulkarni et al., 2016 ; cite97†Nachum et al., 2018 ; cite98†Jiang et al., 2019 ), which also decomposes a task into subtasks but differs in how the hierarchical policies are trained and how reward signals are assigned. In this framework, the high-level manager selects subgoals and receives a task reward, as in the options framework.\nL121: However, unlike the options framework, the low-level worker receives a separate pseudo-reward $r_{p}(s,o,a)$ that measures progress toward achieving the current subgoal.\nL122: #### 2.2. Reward Design\nL123: \nL124: A core challenge in using MDPs and RL is reward design (cite82†Singh et al., 2009 ; cite90†Sutton and Barto, 2018 ). Given a well-designed reward function, agents can use RL algorithms to solve the MDP. However, in practice, the design of a reward function is non-trivial and can lead to a host of problems, including poor alignment between humans and agents (cite99†Amodei et al., 2016 ).\nL125: ##### 2.2.1. Reward Design Problem.\nL126: \nL127: To formally study reward design, cite82†Singh et al. introduce the (flat) Reward Design Problem (RDP) (cite82†Singh et al., 2009 ). RDP is formalized as a tuple $P=(\\mathcal{M}_{p},\\mathcal{R},\\mathcal{A}_{\\mathcal{M}_{p}},F)$, where\nL128: \nL129:   * •\nL130: \nL131: $\\mathcal{M}_{p}=(\\mathcal{S},\\mathcal{A},T,\\gamma,h)$ is the world model;\nL132: \nL133:   * •\nL134: \nL135: $\\mathcal{R}$ is the space of reward functions;\nL136: \nL137:   * •\nL138: $\\mathcal{A}_{\\mathcal{M}_{p}}(r):\\mathcal{R}\\rightarrow\\Pi$ is an algorithm to compute policy $\\pi:\\mathcal{S}\\rightarrow\\Delta(\\mathcal{A})$ that optimizes reward $r\\in\\mathcal{R}$ in the MDP $(\\mathcal{M}_{p},r)$; and\nL139: \nL140:   * •\nL141: \nL142: $F:\\Pi\\rightarrow\\mathbb{R}$ is the fitness function that produces a scalar evaluation of a policy, only accessible via policy queries.\nL143: The RDP seeks a reward function $r\\in\\mathcal{R}$ such that the policy $\\pi:=\\mathcal{A}_{\\mathcal{M}_{p}}(r)$ that optimizes $r$ achieves the highest Fitness score $F(\\pi)$. Rather than treating the reward as fixed and exogenous, RDP reframes reward design as a search problem, a perspective that has profoundly shaped subsequent research.\nL144: It inspired methods that optimize or evolve reward functions directly (cite100†Sorg et al., 2010a ; cite101†Niekum et al., 2010 ) as well as formulations that infer or generate them from indirect signals, such as Inverse Reward Design (cite83†Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017 ), which recovers true rewards from proxy rewards, and Eureka (cite84†Ma et al., 2024 ), which synthesizes executable rewards from natural language.\nL145: Many other paradigms can be viewed as RDP instantiations: inverse RL (cite102†Abbeel and Ng, 2004 ; cite103†Ziebart et al., 2008 ; cite104†Ho and Ermon, 2016 ; cite105†Fu et al., 2018 ) treats expert behavior as evidence for the reward search, while preference-based learning (cite106†Sadigh et al., 2017 ; cite107†Basu et al., 2018 ) and RLHF (cite108†Ouyang et al., 2022 ; cite109†Bai et al., 2022 ) extend this to human feedback.\nL146: Figure 2. Although prior works have utilized multi-level rewards to train hierarchical agents, the design of such rewards remains underexplored and lacks a concrete problem formulation. This work bridges this gap by introducing HRDL.\nL147: ##### 2.2.2. Rewards Design for Hierarchical RL.\nL148: While RDP provides a unifying framework that has catalyzed advances in both RL and human-AI alignment, it does not explicitly consider hierarchical RL. Many prior works have explored the use of hierarchical rewards, from early studies in feudal reinforcement learning (cite77†Dayan and Hinton, 1992 ) and precursors to the options framework (cite110†Singh, 1992 ) to more recent advances in deep HRL (cite95†Vezhnevets et al., 2017 ; cite96†Kulkarni et al., 2016 ; cite97†Nachum et al., 2018 ).\nL149: While these works often assume access to hierarchical rewards for training agents to complete tasks, the problem of designing such hierarchical rewards has received little attention and, to our knowledge, has yet to be formally defined as a concrete research problem.\nL150: A literature search using the keywords “hierarchical reward design” primarily returns domain-specific studies that discuss how using hierarchical rewards enables solving application-level problems (cite111†Huang et al., 2023 ; cite112†Newton et al., 2022 ; cite113†Chen et al., 2023 ; cite114†Naito et al., 2025 ).\nL151: Other works either employ the term hierarchy in different contexts (cite115†Sun, 2019 ; cite116†Li et al., 2024b ; cite117†Lai et al., 2024 ; cite118†Jin et al., 2024 ), such as to express the relative importance of multiple flat reward signals (cite116†Li et al., 2024b ). A related but conceptually distinct line of work utilizes Reward Machines (RMs) (cite119†Icarte et al., 2022 ; cite120†Furelos-Blanco et al., 2023 ), which represent rewards using automata or temporal logic.\nL152: RM-based approaches focus on exploiting known reward structures to improve learning efficiency, rather than on studying how such structures can be specified from human input.\nL153: Since no previous work formally defines the design of hierarchical rewards as a general problem, there is a lack of consistent language and theoretical foundation for studying it.\nL154: This work addresses this gap at the intersection of hierarchical rewards and reward design (Figure cite121†2 ) by formalizing the Hierarchical Reward Design problem and introducing a reward structure that is hierarchical, compact, and capable of capturing nuanced behavioral specifications for both what subtasks to select and how to execute them.\nL155: Just as RDP lays the groundwork for studying algorithmic reward design in flat settings, we posit that HRD will provide a principled foundation for reasoning about hierarchical reward structures. In line with the research that originated from RDP, we anticipate that HRD will enable a broad range of problem instantiations (of reward design with different types of human inputs) and solution methods for designing hierarchical rewards.\nL156: ##### 2.2.3. Reward Design from Language.\nL157: Early work addressing the RDP focused on designing rewards for intrinsic motivation and reward shaping (cite122†Singh et al., 2010 ; cite123†Sorg et al., 2010b ; cite100†Sorg et al., 2010a ). More recently, research in this area has explored aligning agent behavior more closely with human-provided specifications, using learning or large language models (LLMs) to infer and generate reward functions (cite83†Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017 ; cite85†Kwon et al., 2023 ).\nL158: In these cases, the human or an oracle, either implicitly or explicitly, serves as the fitness function by evaluating the policy.\nL159: However, most existing work on reward design or generation focuses exclusively on non-hierarchical (flat) RL settings, producing reward functions of the form $r_{flat}(s,a)$ or $r_{flat}(s)$ (cite122†Singh et al., 2010 ; cite123†Sorg et al., 2010b ; cite100†Sorg et al., 2010a ; cite83†Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017 ; cite85†Kwon et al., 2023 ; cite124†Du et al., 2023 ; cite125†Han et al., 2024 ; cite84†Ma et al., 2024 ; cite126†Li et al., 2024a ; cite127†Xie et al., 2024 ; cite128†Bhambri et al., 2024 ; cite129†Yu et al., 2023 ).\nL160: While sufficient for certain behaviors, flat reward functions are fundamentally limited when specifying complex preferences, such as desired subtask sequences or option-conditioned execution strategies, that naturally arise in long-horizon tasks.\nL161: To our knowledge, the only prior work that explicitly considers a hierarchical setting is (cite130†Masadome and Harada, 2025 ), though its focus differs substantially from ours. Their approach does not formalize the hierarchical reward design problem or analyze the expressivity gap between flat and hierarchical formulations. Moreover, the rewards generated by their LLMs are limited to task completion objectives and do not capture behavioral specifications.\nL162: In contrast, L2HR generates both high- and low-level rewards that encode natural language behavioral preferences while preserving task feasibility.^{1}^{1} 1 Please see the Appendix for additional details on related works.\nL163: ### 3. Hierarchical Reward Design\nL164: This section formally introduces the Hierarchical Reward Design problem. We begin by defining the low- and high-level reward functions in HRD and proceed to show that they naturally induce a family of MDPs at the low-level and a semi-MDP (SMDP) at the high-level. Low-level and High-level Reward Models\nL166: \nL167: ###### Definition 0 (Low-level Reward).\nL168: \nL169: The low-level reward is a function $r_{L}:\\mathcal{S}\\times\\mathcal{O}\\times\\mathcal{A}\\rightarrow\\mathbb{R}$, which provides feedback for selecting a low-level action $a\\in\\mathcal{A}$ in state $s\\in\\mathcal{S}$ while pursuing option $o\\in\\mathcal{O}$.\nL170: \nL171: Intuitively, $r_{L}(s,o,a)$ encodes specifications for how the agent should execute the subtask associated with option $o$ in state $s$.\nL172: ###### Proposition 0 (Low-level MDP Models).\nL173: \nL174: Let $\\mathcal{M}_{p}=(\\mathcal{S},\\mathcal{A},T,\\gamma)$ be a world model, $\\mathcal{O}$ a set of options, and $r_{L}:\\mathcal{S}\\times\\mathcal{O}\\times\\mathcal{A}\\rightarrow\\mathbb{R}$ a low-level reward. Hierarchical Reward Design from Language\nL211: As discussed in Sec. cite6†1 , real-world deployments often require agents to satisfy additional behavioral specifications beyond task completion. In these cases, the task reward can typically be defined once and reused across different behavioral contexts. In contrast, additional rewards must be redesigned for each new behavior specification.\nL212: While the cost of task reward design is amortized, the cost of designing rewards that match human specifications grows linearly with the number of distinct behaviors desired. This motivates the need for an automated approach to generate rewards to encode behavioral specifications while reusing the existing domain dynamics and task objectives.\n"
