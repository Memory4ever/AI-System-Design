B73: 3 Spectral-Decoupled MoE
B74: This section introduces Spectral-Decoupled Mixture of Experts (SD-MoE), a spectral decomposition-based approach that directly addresses the considerable overlap among experts in parameter and gradient spaces. As demonstrated in Section 4 , this design reduces expert overlap by 70%, improves training efficiency by 30% on MoE architectures based on DeepSeek and Qwen, indicating its broad applicability across diverse existing MoE architectures.
B75: 3.1 Expert Spectral Decomposition
B76: SD-MoE optimizes expert specialization though (i) spectrally decouples each expert’s parameter matrix into a shared low-rank component and an expert-specific component within its orthogonal complement; and (ii) decouples the gradient updates for these two components to enable independent optimization. This design reduces inter-expert gradient interference and variance, relaxes learning rate constraints, and accelerates convergence.
B77: Figure 7 uses a two-layer MLP as an example to illustrate how to apply SD-MoE to the standard linear-gate MoE paradigm. Given an input token 𝐱 \mathbf{x} , routing scores are computed as 𝐖 gate ​ 𝐱 \mathbf{W}_{\text{gate}}\mathbf{x} , where 𝐖 gate \mathbf{W}_{\text{gate}} is a learnable gating matrix. The top-n experts with the highest scores are selected for activation. Each expert i i consists of two weight matrix: an up-projection 𝐖 U ( i ) \mathbf{W}_{U}^{(i)} and a down-projection 𝐖 D ( i ) \mathbf{W}_{D}^{(i)} . The output is computed as 𝐖 D ( i ) ​ σ ​ ( 𝐖 U ( i ) ​ 𝐱 ) , \mathbf{W}_{D}^{(i)}\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{U}^{(i)}\mathbf{x}), where σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) denotes the activation function.
B78: However, architectural decoupling alone is insufficient to ensure sustained specialization during training. Section 3.2 addresses this by spectrally decoupling expert parameters at initialization, preventing dominant shared components from biasing early optimization, while Section 3.3 further decomposes gradients during training to prevent cross-expert interference from re-emerging. Together, these steps ensure that spectral decoupling is preserved throughout training.

B86: Before detailing our gradient decomposition strategy, we first outline the forward pass, which determines how gradients are subsequently computed.
B87: Forward Pass The routing mechanism in SD-MoE follows the standard linear MoE router. If a token 𝐱 \mathbf{x} is routed to expert i i , SD-MoE computes the forward pass of each linear matrix in the expert as

The same operation is applied to all linear matrices, and thus the output is 𝐖 D ( i ) ​ σ ​ ( 𝐖 U ( i ) ​ 𝐱 ) \mathbf{W}_{D}^{(i)}\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{U}^{(i)}\mathbf{x})\, where 𝐖 U ( i ) = 𝐖 U , c + 𝐖 U , u ( i ) , 𝐖 D ( i ) = 𝐖 D , c + 𝐖 D , u ( i ) \mathbf{W}_{U}^{(i)}=\mathbf{W}_{U,c}+\mathbf{W}_{U,u}^{(i)},\quad\mathbf{W}_{D}^{(i)}=\mathbf{W}_{D,c}+\mathbf{W}_{D,u}^{(i)} .

Let 𝐏 U = 𝐔 k ​ 𝐔 k ⊤ \mathbf{P}_{U}=\mathbf{U}_{k}\mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top} and 𝐏 V = 𝐕 k ​ 𝐕 k ⊤ \mathbf{P}_{V}=\mathbf{V}_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top} . The gradient is decomposed as

In the spectral space spanned by the left and right singular subspaces 𝐔 \mathbf{U} and 𝐕 \mathbf{V} , gradient components involving the dominant low-rank subspaces 𝐔 k ​ 𝐕 k ⊤ \mathbf{U}_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top} are absorbed into 𝐆 c ( i ) \mathbf{G}_{c}^{(i)} , while the remaining components in the double orthogonal complement are assigned to 𝐆 u ( i ) \mathbf{G}_{u}^{(i)} . Since the basis for the common expert may change after update, we perform SVD on 𝐖 c \mathbf{W}_{c} periodically to obtain correct 𝐔 k , 𝐕 k \mathbf{U}_{k},\mathbf{V}_{k} .

B107: 4.1 Experiment Setup
B108: Our experiments are based on widely available open-source MoEs DeepSeek ( Dai et al., 2024 ) and Qwen ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , which are explicitly designed to encourage expert specialization. We conduct comparisons at two scales: (2B/0.8B) and (7B/1.5B). All models are trained on a 100B subset of the DCLM corpus ( Li et al., 2024 ) . The shared expert subspace rank is set to 8, and we empirically fix the periodic SVD update interval to every 16 steps. Full details on hyperparameters are provided in Appendix D .
B109: 4.2 Main Results
B110: On downstream tasks (Table 1 ), SD-MoE delivers better performance across nearly all benchmarks compared to baselines. Notably, spectral sharing and decomposition further enable training with larger learning rates with out instability, delivering a 30% training efficiency. As is shown in Figure 8 , while baseline Qwen model diverges at learning rates beyond 1 × 10 − 4 1\times 10^{-4} , SD-MoE remains stable up to a 4× increase 4 × 10 − 4 4\times 10^{-4} . More details for the larger learning rate experiments can be referred to in Appendix E .
B111: Spectral conditioning and training stability.
B112: Prior work has shown that training stability and convergence speed are closely tied to the spectral geometry of network mappings: well-conditioned singular spectra, often described through dynamical isometry , permit larger stable learning rates and faster optimization ( Pennington et al., 2017 ; Xiao et al., 2018 ) . Related approaches further demonstrate that explicitly regulating dominant singular components, such as through spectral normalization, stabilizes training by controlling the effective Lipschitz constants of learned transformations ( Miyato et al., 2018 ; Chen et al., 2023b ; Shi et al., 2023 ) .
B113: In MoE models, orthogonality- and decoupling-based methods reduce cross-expert redundancy and gradient interference, improving specialization and optimization ( Liu et al., 2023 ) . Building on this insight, our spectral decomposition separates shared low-rank components from expert-specific subspaces, removes common spectral “spikes,” yields more benign conditioning across experts, and enables larger stable learning rates in practice.

B115: 4.3 Analysis
B116: Expert & Gating Specialization.
B117: To verify whether our model achieves effective specialization across experts as expected, we compute the principal subspace similarity among all experts as defined in Section 2 . As shown in Figure 9 , the pairwise principal subspace similarities are lower than 0.1 at convergence, indicating minimal overlap in their parameter directions. The gating mechanism also exhibits improved specialization. Compared to Qwen model in Figure 10 , the row vectors of the gating matrix in SD-MoE in Figure 10 no longer exhibit strong alignment with the top singular directions of the expert parameters. Instead, they distribute their influence across a broader set of singular vectors, reflecting the use of richer, more diverse semantic cues during routing. This confirms that SD-MoE successfully promotes expert specialization and reduces parameter redundancy.

B196: Appendix C Method Details
B197: Here we detail the initialization of unique experts in the orthogonal complement subspace, and the pseudo code is given in Algorithm 1 . Given a weight matrix 𝐖 ∈ ℝ m × n \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} and its rank- r r shared component obtained via SVD, 𝐖 c = 𝐔 k ​ 𝚺 k ​ 𝐕 k ⊤ \mathbf{W}_{c}=\mathbf{U}_{k}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top} , where 𝐔 k ∈ ℝ m × r \mathbf{U}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r} and 𝐕 k ∈ ℝ n × r \mathbf{V}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r} have orthonormal columns, we aim to initialize each unique expert with a matrix supported entirely in the orthogonal complement of the shared subspace.
B198: Specifically, for the row space, we seek a random matrix 𝐔 ~ ∈ ℝ m × ( m − r ) \widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m-r)} such that:
B199: 1.
B200: 𝐔 ~ ⊤ ​ 𝐔 ~ = 𝐈 m − r \widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{I}_{m-r} (columns are orthonormal);
B201: 2.
B202: 𝐔 k ⊤ ​ 𝐔 ~ = 𝟎 \mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{0} (orthogonal to the shared row subspace).
B203: An analogous condition holds for 𝐕 ~ ∈ ℝ n × ( n − r ) \widetilde{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-r)} in the column space.
B204: To achieve this, we first sample a Gaussian random matrix 𝐙 ∈ ℝ m × ( m − r ) \mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m-r)} . We then project it onto the orthogonal complement of col ⁡ ( 𝐔 k ) \mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}_{k}) using the projector 𝐏 ⟂ = 𝐈 − 𝐔 k ​ 𝐔 k ⊤ \mathbf{P}_{\perp}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{k}\mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top} :
B205: 𝐙 ⟂ = ( 𝐈 − 𝐔 k ​ 𝐔 k ⊤ ) ​ 𝐙 . \mathbf{Z}_{\perp}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{k}\mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top})\mathbf{Z}.
B206: Since 𝐙 ⟂ \mathbf{Z}_{\perp} lies in the desired subspace but its columns are not necessarily orthogonal, we apply QR decomposition to obtain an orthonormal basis:
B207: 𝐔 ~ = QR ⁡ ( 𝐙 ⟂ ) . \widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=\mathrm{QR}(\mathbf{Z}_{\perp}).
B208: The same procedure is applied to the column space using 𝐕 k \mathbf{V}_{k} . Finally, the unique weight is constructed as 𝐖 u = 𝐔 ~ ​ 𝚺 ~ ​ 𝐕 ~ ⊤ \mathbf{W}_{u}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\,\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\top} , where 𝚺 ~ \widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}} is a diagonal matrix populated with the long-tail singular values of 𝐖 \mathbf{W} (i.e., σ r + 1 , … , σ min ⁡ ( m , n ) \sigma_{r+1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(m,n)} ).
B209: This procedure ensures that each unique expert starts in a direction fully orthogonal to the common subspace while maintaining proper scale and diversity across experts.
