B28: 2.3 Numerical Integration and Scheduling
B29: Solving Equation 1 requires discretizing the time interval [ 0 , T ] [0,T] into a sequence { t i } i = 0 N \{t_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N} . The sample quality is governed by two coupled design choices: numerical solver and timestep schedules.
B30: Numerical Solvers. Solvers navigate the trade-off between local precision and computational cost (NFE). First-order methods (e.g., Euler) are computationally cheap (1 NFE/step) but incur a local truncation error of O ⁡ ( h 2 ) O(h^{2}) . To improve precision without increasing steps, high-order solvers such as DPM-Solver++ ( Lu et al., 2025 ) , UniPC ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) , and DEIS ( Zhang and Chen, 2022 ) employ higher-order Taylor approximations or predictor-corrector schemes. While these methods significantly reduce discretization error ( O ⁡ ( h k ) O(h^{k}) for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 ), they typically require complex multi-step history or additional evaluations per step, making their uniform application across the entire trajectory potentially inefficient.
B31: Timestep Scheduling. Diffusion timesteps { t i } i = 0 N \{t_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N} determines the step size h i h_{i} and the noise levels σ ⁡ ( t i ) \sigma(t_{i}) . Early works employed heuristic time-based schedules, such as linear ( Ho et al., 2020 ) or cosine schedules ( Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021 ) . More recently, Karras et al. ( Karras et al., 2022 ) proposed a noise-level based polynomial schedule (EDM), σ i = ( σ max 1 ρ + i N − 1 ​ ( σ min 1 ρ − σ max 1 ρ ) ) ρ \sigma_{i}=\left(\sigma_{\text{max}}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}+\frac{i}{N-1}\left(\sigma_{\text{min}}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}-\sigma_{\text{max}}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}\right)\right)^{\rho} , which has become a standard baseline due to its empirical robustness. Subsequent studies have explored timestep optimization based on various criteria including score-based measures ( Williams et al., 2024 ; Xue et al., 2024 ) , KL divergence ( Sabour et al., 2024 ; Park et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2023 ) , and Wasserstein-based error estimates ( Hu et al., 2024 ) . However, there does not exist a unified framework that integrates solver selection and timestep allocation based on the intrinsic geometry of the diffusion trajectory. Motivated by this gap, we propose a unified sampling design space that characterizes both of these dimensions. Both components can be optimized independently for pre-trained diffusion models without additional training, while their combination yields further improvements in sampling efficiency and sample quality.
B32: 3 SDM: Sampling via Adaptive Solvers and Timestep Schedulers
B33: We investigate the sampling design space of diffusion-based generative models by rigorously formalizing the trade-off between numerical precision and computational cost. While prior works often rely on static heuristics, we propose a geometric framework, SDM , which aligns the numerical solver with the intrinsic properties of the diffusion trajectory.
B34: In Section 3.1 , we analyze the local stiffness of the probability flow ODE. While state-of-the-art exponential integrators (e.g., DPM-Solver++ ( Lu et al., 2025 ) ) achieve efficiency by analytically solving the linear component of the diffusion ODE, they fundamentally rely on the assumption that the non-linear residual (the effective score term) varies smoothly. Our analysis generalizes this by explicitly quantifying the curvature of this residual, demonstrating that low-order solvers, such as Euler method, are sufficient for high-noise regimes, while higher-order corrections become necessary only in later stages. In Section 3.2 , we introduce a Wasserstein-bounded adaptive strategy that optimizes the timestep schedule to minimize discretization error under a fixed computational budget. We empirically show that this leads to consistent improvements in sample quality.
B35: 3.1 Adaptive ODE Solvers
B36: 3.1.1 Analysis of ODE curvature
B37: To design an efficient adaptive sampling strategy, we must first quantify the geometric properties of the diffusion trajectory that govern numerical stability. The local truncation error (LTE) of any numerical solver is strictly bounded by the magnitude of the trajectory’s higher-order derivatives, specifically the acceleration ‖ x ¨ ‖ \|\ddot{x}\| . This quantity serves as a direct proxy for the local stiffness of the probability flow ODE ( Krämer and Hennig, 2024 ) .
B38: Exponential integrators mitigate stiffness by explicitly solving the linear drift term. However, the discretization error is then dominated by the higher-order derivatives of the neural network prediction itself (the non-linear noise residual). Our curvature analysis focuses on this residual complexity, providing a solver-agnostic measure of difficulty that applies regardless of the integrator’s baseline order.

B123: 4.2 Results
B124: Unconditional Generation. Quantitative results for unconditional generation are reported in Table 1 . Across all datasets, solvers, and pre-trained model configurations, SDM consistently improves sample quality over prior baselines, demonstrating robust performance under both VP and VE parameterizations.
B125: Under the Euler solver, SDM with adaptive scheduling yields substantial gains, achieving FID 6.18 on CIFAR-10, 4.16 on FFHQ, and 2.71 on AFHQv2 for VP models, compared to 7.61, 4.59, and 2.93 obtained by the EDM baseline, respectively.
