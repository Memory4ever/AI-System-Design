B23: 2 Related work
B24: Advances in Diffusion-Based Visual Synthesis.
B25: Diffusion models have advanced the field of image generation by producing images of remarkable quality [ 12 , 29 , 5 ] . Latent diffusion models [ 24 ] built on a U‑Net backbone improve efficiency by operating in a compressed latent space, enabling complex tasks such as text‑to‑image synthesis (e.g., Stable Diffusion, SDXL) [ 22 ] . While U‑Net architectures dominated early diffusion work, recent studies show that diffusion transformers scale more effectively [ 21 , 18 ] , and current state‑of‑the‑art diffusion systems increasingly adopt transformer architectures [ 3 , 36 , 8 ] . Building on these foundation models, our work focuses on intervening in the inference process of pretrained models such as SDXL [ 22 ] to enhance their ability to generate high‑resolution images.
B26: A key drawback of diffusion is its reliance on slow, multi‑step inference. This cost can be mitigated by applying distillation methods [ 33 , 20 , 28 , 38 ] to distill a multi‑step diffusion teacher into a one‑step or few‑step student without degrading sample quality. We leverage the efficiency of few‑step models to accelerate our refinement pipeline while preserving high‑fidelity generation.
B27: Tuning-free High-resolution Synthesis.
B28: MultiDiffusion [ 1 ] and DemoFusion [ 7 ] partition high‑resolution latents into patches sized to the pretrained model’s native resolution, process patches independently, and stitch them using average blending technique. While reducing the memory footprint, these pipelines frequently exhibit object repetition. FouriScale [ 13 ] attributes this failure to frequency‑domain misalignment across resolutions, and both FouriScale [ 13 ] and FreeScale [ 23 ] introduce frequency interventions to counteract it. Although repetition is reduced, direct manipulation in the frequency domain often yields unnatural textures and additional high‑frequency artifacts. All these methods typically initiate the upsampling stage by perturbing the coarse, target‑resolution latent with Gaussian random noise. In contrast, HiWave [ 32 ] injects DDIM‑inverted [ 27 ] noise, which preserves structural information and also helps mitigate object repetition.
B29: Another major limitation of these methods is their slow inference: all rely on the multi‑step reverse process [ 7 , 1 , 13 , 23 , 10 , 32 ] of pretrained diffusion models. CutDiffusion [ 16 ] attempts to reduce runtime by processing fewer patches, but the improvement is only incremental. In contrast, our method addresses both limitations with a novel partial inversion pipeline that leverages a few‑step diffusion model. By initiating the upsampling stage from a structure‑preserving latent, it inherently mitigates small object repetition. This design also enables generation at resolutions of 8 ​ K 8K and beyond on a single GPU, while dramatically shortening inference time compared to prior patch‑based methods.
B30: Figure 2 : An overview of two-stage system with for high-resolution generation with Cascade Upsampling. (a) Two-Stage System. A base diffusion model generates a low-resolution, base image. This image then goes into a cascade upsampling process to progressively upscale to target resolution. (b) The Cascade Step. Each cascade step doubles the height and width of an input image. First, the initial image at resolution R R is upscaled to 4 ​ R 4R via interpolation in pixel space, creating a coarse image. Finally, this refined latent is decoded back to the pixel space, producing a sharp, high-fidelity image at resolution 4 ​ R 4R .
B31: Super-Resolution.
B32: Image Super-Resolution (SR) is a classical computer vision task focused on reconstructing a high-resolution (HR) image from a given, authentic low-resolution (LR) input. Studies on image super-resolution have a long history, from classical interpolation [ 39 , 31 ] to modern deep learning approaches using convolutional networks, generative adversarial networks [ 4 , 6 , 34 ] , and more recently, diffusion models [ 15 , 35 ] trained specifically for the SR task. It is important to distinguish our high‑resolution generation task from classical super‑resolution. In super‑resolution, the low‑resolution image is a fixed, real‑world input to be faithfully restored. In our pipeline, the low‑resolution image is an intermediate product that provides a structural foundation. Consequently, our aim is not pixel‑perfect reconstruction but coherent novel synthesis: produce a high‑resolution image that preserves the intermediate result’s global layout and semantics while introducing plausible high‑frequency details consistent with the original text prompt.
B33: Figure 3 : The PixelRush Refinement Stage. Our refinement stage takes a coarse latent as input and first divides it into overlapping patches. These patches pass through proposed partial inversion few-step pipeline (Sec. 4.3 ), where DDIM inversion maps each patch to an intermediate noisy latent. A few-step diffusion model then refines these latents, synthesizing high-frequency details. Finally, the refined patches are processed by our Gaussian Filter Patches Blending & Noise Injection module (Sec. 4.4 + Sec. 4.5 ) to produce a seamless, high-quality latent.

B41: 3.2 DDIM Inversion
B42: Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model (DDIM [ 27 ] ) provides a deterministic sampling path that allows for much faster sampling with fewer steps and enables a consistent mapping between the Gaussian noise space and the clean latent space. The goal of DDIM inversion is to find a latent noise vector 𝐳 T \mathbf{z}_{T} that, when used as the starting point for the standard DDIM sampling process, reconstructs a given source image 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{0} . The deterministic DDIM inversion mapping from 𝐳 t − 1 \mathbf{z}_{t-1} to 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} is defined as:
B43: 𝐳 t = α ¯ t ​ ( 𝐳 t − 1 − 1 − α ¯ t − 1 ​ ϵ θ ​ ( 𝐳 t − 1 , t ) α ¯ t − 1 ) + 1 − α ¯ t ​ ϵ θ ​ ( 𝐳 t − 1 , t ) , \begin{split}\mathbf{z}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_{t-1}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t-1},t)}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}\right)\\ \quad+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t-1},t),\end{split} (3)

This process can be stopped at any intermediate timestep t t to find a noisy latent 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} that lies on the trajectory leading back to the original latent 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{0} .
B45: 4 Method
B46: We present the PixelRush pipeline for high-resolution image generation using pretrained diffusion models. We first provide an overview of the system architecture (Sec. 4.1 ), then describe our motivation and design choices to enable patch-based inference in the few-step diffusion regime (Sec. 4.2 and 4.3 ), introduce our patch blending technique inspired by image feathering that eliminates artifacts (Sec. 4.4 ) and finally introduce our noise injection technique to prevent oversmoothing in few-step setup (Sec. 4.5 ).
B47: 4.1 Overall
B48: The overall architecture of training‑free patch-based high‑resolution synthesis systems can be viewed as a two‑stage pipeline (Fig. 2 ): a base generation stage followed by a cascade upsampling stage.

Crucially, all such refinement pipelines depend on a full multi‑step reverse diffusion which is the dominant source of their computational cost and the bottleneck we address in the next section.
B54: 4.2 Is fully Gaussian noise needed?
B55: It is well established that the reverse diffusion process reconstructs images hierarchically in the frequency domain, producing low‑frequency, global structure first and adding high‑frequency, fine‑grained details later [ 19 ] . We find the same temporal ordering in high‑resolution generation. As illustrated in Fig 4 , DemoFusion [ 7 ] follows this frequency‑wise reconstruction. This observation motivates the question: Is perturbing the latent all the way to a full Gaussian noise, i.e., t = T t=T , truly necessary?
B56: Figure 4 : Training-free high-resolution pipeline synthesize images hierarchically.
B57: We argue that perturbing to full Gaussian noise is suboptimal because denoising steps at large timesteps are largely redundant: they mainly restore global structure, which is already present in the coarse latent ( 𝐳 ¯ 0 r \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{r} ), and they substantially increase inference time. Therefore, we propose truncating the forward perturbation and instead mapping ( 𝐳 ¯ 0 r \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{r} ) to an intermediate noise level ( 𝐳 ¯ K r \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{K}^{r} ) with ( K < T ) (K<T) . For example, in Fig. 4 , we can perturb the coarse image to timestep t = 259 t=259 (instead of 999), and denoise it back, saving 75% computation time. Furthermore, the quantitative justification for this design choice is presented in our component ablation study (Table 3 ). By replacing the full 50-step reverse process with our 15-step partial inversion scheme, we achieve a 3.7 × 3.7\times acceleration and this dramatic speedup does not come at the cost of quality. This result provides strong empirical evidence that our partial inversion strategy is both more efficient and effective for high-fidelity refinement.
B58: This truncated strategy naturally complements few‑step diffusion models, which can make large, high‑impact updates and synthesize the required high‑fidelity details within a shortened reverse trajectory. This is the core idea behind our approach to speed up the refinement pipeline by leveraging few‑step diffusion models.
B59: 4.3 Speed-up with few-step model
B60: In the refinement stage, instead of using the same multi-step diffusion model as in previous works, we can employ a few‑step sampling diffusion model ϵ γ refine \epsilon_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{refine}} to further boost inference speed, as illustrated in Fig. 3 . Motivated by the observations in Sec. 4.2 , our perturbation process during refinement maps the coarse latent 𝐳 ¯ 0 r \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{r} only to an intermediate noise level 𝐳 ¯ K r \bar{\mathbf{z}}_{K}^{r} rather than to full Gaussian noise. To maximize pipeline speedup, we perform both the forward perturbation and the reverse refinement using a single step, choosing the corresponding intermediate timestep K K of ϵ γ refine \epsilon_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{refine}} . For instance, if ϵ γ refine \epsilon_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{refine}} samples over 4 equally distributed time-steps, we can select K = 249 K=249 , enabling natural one-step inversion and one-step reverse diffusion.
B61: For the initial refinement stage, we employ deterministic DDIM inversion. While stochastic q q -sampling is also a feasible option, we prefer this deterministic inversion to preserves the base image’s structural information. The DDIM-inverted noise retains the crucial low-frequency content from the coarse image, enabling the few-step denoiser to focus its capacity on synthesizing high-frequency details, a principle supported by prior work [ 32 , 27 ] .

However, this efficiency does not come without cost, as this baseline approach introduces noticeable checkerboard artifacts and a degree of oversmoothing in the generated images, as illustrated in Fig. 5 . B64: 4.4 Image feathering blending technique
B65: Although efficient, naively equipping a patch‑based pipeline with a few‑step diffusion model produces severe artifacts at patch boundaries (see Fig. 5 ). We trace this failure to the simple averaging blending (MultiDiffusion [ 1 ] ) when applied in few‑step or one‑step regimes. Few‑step reverse processes produce large, sharp updates inside individual patches, and naively averaging overlapping regions only smooths those differences without reconciling them, yielding visible seams.
B66: Our idea is that when combining two overlapping patches P 1 P_{1} and P 2 P_{2} , pixels nearer to one patch center should follow that patch more strongly, rather than applying a uniform average across the overlap. Concretely, we convolve the hard binary overlap mask with a Gaussian blur kernel to produce a smooth mask (see Fig. 6 for a visualization). Blending the two patches using this smooth mask, even in a single step, removes visible boundary artifacts.


B68: 4.5 Noise injection
B69: A further challenge arises when applying few-step models within our training-free, patch-based high-resolution generation framework: the resulting images often exhibit oversmoothing artifacts and a lack of fine detail, as shown in Fig. 5 . We hypothesize that multi‑step models refine latents through many small, incremental updates that gradually synthesize high‑frequency detail. In contrast, few‑step models take larger denoising steps that may not fully recover fine‑grained details, yielding overly smooth outputs. Prior work [ 37 , 27 ] demonstrates that flattening the data distribution p γ ​ ( 𝐱 ) p_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}) encourages the synthesis of higher-frequency components. Inspired by this principle, we propose a simple yet effective noise injection technique to counteract oversmoothing. We slightly augment the predicted noise ϵ γ ​ ( 𝐱 , t ) \epsilon_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},t) at reverse step by interpolating it with a random noise, This injects randomness which helps flatten the distribution p γ ​ ( 𝐱 ) p_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}) and improve high-frequency details. Since the interpolation is done in latent space, as suggested by [ 20 ] , we employ spherical interpolation (slerp) instead of normal linear interpolation (lerp):
B70: ϵ γ ′ ​ ( 𝐱 , t ) = slerp ⁡ ( ϵ γ ​ ( 𝐱 , t ) , ϵ rand , λ ) . \epsilon^{\prime}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},t)=\operatorname{slerp}(\epsilon_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},t),\epsilon_{\mathrm{rand}},\lambda). (4)
B71: where ϵ rand ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 𝕀 ) \epsilon_{\mathrm{rand}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}) and λ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \lambda\in[0,1) is an interpolation coefficient. For all our experiments, we use a fixed λ = 0.95 \lambda=0.95 . As demonstrated in Figure 7 , this technique effectively mitigates the oversmoothing issue.
B72: It is crucial to note that this technique is specifically tailored to counteract the oversmoothing inherent in our few-step, patch-based pipeline. When applied to standard multi-step pipelines, it can degrade performance because repeatedly steering the distribution over many steps can lead to significant error accumulation, which introduce unwanted artifacts, as shown in Figure 7 .
