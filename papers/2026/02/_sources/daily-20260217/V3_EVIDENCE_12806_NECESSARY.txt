B33: Motivation. To properly evaluate text anonymization tools, measure progress and align with legal standards, benchmarks must assess residual re-identification risk. This requires accounting for a broad range of direct and indirect identifiers, varying the difficulty by which they appear in the text, across domains and languages, and supporting the evaluation of strong, adaptive anonymizers. While prior benchmarks for text anonymization exist ( Sang and Meulder, 2003 ; Walker et al., 2006 ; Weischedel et al., 2013 ; Pilán et al., 2022 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Yukhymenko et al., 2024 ; Loiseau et al., 2025 ; Singh and Narayanan, 2025 ) , they exhibit several important limitations.
B34: First, some benchmarks ( Pilán et al., 2022 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ) rely on manually annotated, real-world text. While this captures realistic language and identifiers, these benchmarks are limited in the range of identifiers they consider due to privacy constraints when releasing. They are moreover extremely costly to extend to other identifiers, domains or languages and not allow for varying levels of difficulty.
B35: Alternatively, Yukhymenko et al. (2024) ; Loiseau et al. (2025) ; Singh and Narayanan (2025) use fully synthetic identifiers and text, enabling controlled variation in difficulty and context. However, these benchmarks often focus on indirect identifiers only, are limited to English, and – importantly – do not reflect real-world demographic distributions. As a result, they cannot translate any identifiers that remain in the text after anonymization into a meaningful re-identification risk. Indeed, in practice, an individual’s re-identification risk depends on attribute types, their values, and combinations relative to a broader population: high-entropy attributes (e.g., exact birth dates) pose far greater risk than low-entropy ones (e.g., gender), rare values (e.g., residence in Rhode Island) are more identifying than common ones (e.g., California), and certain combinations can be highly identifying (e.g., a 50-year-old male CEO (unfortunately) has a lower re-identification probability compared to a 30-year-old female one). Benchmarks based solely on synthetic identifiers lack these ground-truth, joint distributions and therefore can not accurately measure risk.
B36: Instead, lacking realistic demographics, these benchmarks evaluate anonymization performance by recall – the fraction of identifiers in the text that are successfully masked. However, recall assigns equal weight to all attributes and values. As a result, a method may achieve high recall by consistently removing generic attributes or common values, while still leaving substantial re-identification risk by missing high-entropy attributes, rare values, or identifying combinations.
B37: To address this, we present an end-to-end benchmark grounded in real-world demographics enabling the evaluation of re-identification risk in anonymized texts. We use our extensible framework to consider a diverse set of direct and indirect identifiers while properly accounting for their disparate impact on the risk, vary levels of difficulty to measure progress, consider multiple languages and vary the set of indirect identifiers known to strong, adaptive anonymizers to stress-test how they generalize to new settings.

Next, we carefully produce synthetic yet realistic direct identifiers linked to the record’s indirect identifiers 𝒜 indirect ​ ( r ) \mathcal{A}_{\text{indirect}}(r) (more details see App. A ). The indirect and synthetic direct identifiers are then combined into a complete profile x full x_{\text{full}} . B40: Next, we query language model LLM gen \text{LLM}_{\text{gen}} with a prompt P = BuildPrompt ​ ( x , 𝒜 , ℓ , s , g ) P=\textsc{BuildPrompt}(x,\mathcal{A},\ell,s,g) (Alg. 3 ) to generate text t t . The prompt is constructed such that the generated text t t (i) conveys information about the target attributes 𝒜 ⁡ ( r ) \mathcal{A}(r) ; (ii) does so at a specified difficulty level ℓ \ell and is situated within a specified scenario s s (e.g., a patient–doctor transcript), and (iii) is in language g g . This design allows us to systematically vary attributes, difficulty and scenarios while retaining control over the ground-truth.
B41: Our goal is to design a benchmark that allows for stress-testing and comparing anonymization tools, and measuring their progress. For this, we consider three difficulty levels: attributes may be (1) stated explicitly in a clean, standard form ( explicit, easy ), (2) explicitly present but in a non-standard form (e.g., slang, nonstandard formatting, partial masking) ( explicit, hard ), or (3) only implied through context or indirect cues, never directly stated ( implicit ). Illustrative examples are shown in App. F . For direct identifiers, we restrict to levels 1 and 2, as values such as phone numbers are not realistically implied through contextual cues.

B43: 4 Experimental setup
B44: RAT-Bench construction. As tabular dataset D D with real-world demographics, we use indirect identifiers collected during the American Community Survey and made available as the 5% Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS) ( USCensusBureau, 2010 ) . We select a subset of 9 9 PUMS attributes also considered by Rocher et al. (2019) as indirect identifiers Q ⁡ ( r ) Q(r) : state of residence, gender, date of birth, race, marital status, highest level of education obtained, employment status, occupation and citizenship status. To construct a single benchmark entry, we first sample N q = 5 N_{q}=5 indirect identifiers 𝒜 indirect \mathcal{A}_{\text{indirect}} . We then sample record r r from D D , and include it in our benchmark if its baseline correctness calculated using 𝒜 indirect \mathcal{A}_{\text{indirect}} is higher than θ 0 = 0.9 \theta_{0}=0.9 . Next, we generate the following 6 6 direct identifiers I ⁡ ( r ) I(r) : name, social security number (SSN), credit card number, phone number, address, and email address (details in App. A ) and sample N i = 1 N_{i}=1 to be included in the target attributes (see App. E for detailed identifier distribution).
B45: For each benchmark entry, we randomly sample one of three possible scenarios s s : medical appointment transcripts, AI chatbot interactions and meeting transcripts, representing diverse and realistic targets for anonymization ( Johnson et al., 2020 ; Liu et al., 2023 ; King et al., 2025 ; OpenAI, 2025a ) (see Table 4 for details). We query LLM gen \text{LLM}_{\text{gen}} using the prompt in Alg. 3 , to generate text in which attributes are mentioned explicitly in their standard (easy) and non-standard (hard) form or only mentioned implicitly, providing a detailed description and examples. We use GPT-4.1 ( OpenAI, 2023 ) as LLM gen \text{LLM}_{\text{gen}} . For our main experiments, we sample 100 100 records r r , generate benchmark entries in English, and limit the length of the text to 750-1000 words for explicitly mentioned identifiers, and 1500-2000 for implicitly mentioned ones. We provide results per scenario in App. E and provide example benchmark entries in App. L . We also generate 50 50 benchmark entries each in Spanish and Simplified Chinese, based on a random subset of the profiles used for our main experiments and consider easy, explicitly mentioned identifiers. Exact generation, anonymization, and attack details are provided in App. D .
B46: Anonymization tools. We evaluate the effectiveness of anonymization tools 𝒯 \mathcal{T} , broadly distinguishing between tools relying on NER, perturbations and LLMs. (i) NER-based approaches rely on NLP models (e.g., BERT ( Devlin et al., 2019 ) ) and heuristics such as regular expression matching to detect patterns corresponding to predefined attributes. In this category, we evaluate Azure Language Studio ( Azure, 2023 ) , Presidio ( Microsoft, 2021 ) , Scrubadub ( LeapBeyond, 2023 ) , GliNER ( Zaratiana et al., 2024 ) , UniNER ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) and TextWash ( Kleinberg et al., 2022 ) . (ii) Perturbation-based methods TEM ( Carvalho et al., 2023 ) with ϵ = 11 \epsilon=11 (results for Madlib ( Feyisetan et al., 2020 ) and other values of ϵ \epsilon can be found in App. K ). (iii) LLM-based approaches use one- or few-shot prompting for anonymization, without task-specific fine-tuning. We use the PII purifier prompt as proposed by Anthropic (2024) and the Rescriber prompt ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) to initialize GPT-4.1 ( OpenAI, 2023 ) , Gemini 2.5 Flash ( Comanici et al., 2025 ) and Llama 3.1–8B AI@Meta (2024) as LLM-based anonymizers. We provide the exact prompts, alongside ablation results for variations of PII purifier and analyses of privacy-preserving summarization prompt Clio ( Tamkin et al., 2024 ) in App. B . For text in Spanish and Simplified Chinese, we evaluate a representative subset of anonymizers: Azure, Presidio, and GPT-4.1 (Anthropic), adapted to the target language.
B47: We additionally evaluate 4 variants of the iterative LLM-based anonymizer from Staab et al. (2025) , differing only in the provided attribute list: (1) Ideal , the full list of 6 direct and 9 indirect identifiers, (2) Ideal-extended , the same list extended with 10 additional attribute names from the PUMS dataset, (3) Out-of-the-box (Presidio) , the general and US-specific attribute lists used by Presidio and (4) Generalization , a random subset of 5 of the Ideal attributes. We use GPT-4.1 as both attacker and anonymizer.
B48: Evaluation metrics. We instantiate a state-of-the-art attacker to infer attributes from anonymized text t a t^{a} ; we use GPT 4.1 ( OpenAI, 2023 ) as LLM att \text{LLM}_{\text{att}} , prompted with the attacker template from Staab et al. (2024) . Notably, we also experimented with later generations of GPT models, but found that, for instance, GPT-5 often refuses to guess an individual’s attributes. For each target attribute a ∈ 𝒜 a\in\mathcal{A} present in the original text t t , the attacker generates a guess, which is compared against the ground truth for x d ​ i ​ r ​ e ​ c ​ t ∗ {x}^{*}_{direct} and x i ​ n ​ d ​ i ​ r ​ e ​ c ​ t ∗ {x}^{*}_{indirect} (details see App. C ). Given the matches, we compute the re-identification risk of record x x as ℛ ⁡ ( x ) = 1 \mathcal{R}(x)=1 if x d ​ i ​ r ​ e ​ c ​ t ∗ ≠ 0 {x}^{*}_{direct}\neq 0 , and ℛ ⁡ ( x ) = κ x \mathcal{R}(x)=\kappa_{x} otherwise. Consistent with privacy advisories that recommend k k -anonymity with k = 5 k=5 for external data releases ( NHS England, 2013 ; O’Keefe et al., 2017 ; PDPC, 2024 ; UK ICO, 2025 ) , we select θ = 0.2 \theta=0.2 , which corresponds to the equivalent re-identification risk ( 1 / 5 1/5 ). We note that this is a comparatively lenient threshold for θ \theta , as for instance ( OCHA, 2019 ) states that an acceptable re-identification risk level of personal data is 5% or lower. B49: Furthermore, to evaluate how precisely anonymization tools remove identifying information while preserving text integrity, we also compute the BLEU score ( Papineni et al., 2002 ) between each anonymized text t a t^{a} and the original version t t . A high BLEU score paired with low re-identification risk implies high anonymizer precision: the tool effectively removes identifying information while leaving the surrounding text largely intact. We only measure precision on texts containing explicit identifiers as many tools were unable to detect implicit identifiers and thus made minimal redactions. We study additional metrics in Appendix J .

In the hard setting, direct identifiers become significantly more difficult to redact, and are the majority source of re-identification for NER-based methods (e.g., Scrubadub, Presidio) while LLMs remain more robust. When identifiers are implicit , the anonymization tools only marginally reduce the risk, and none perform well. Together, our benchmark demonstrates that there is significant room for improvement for existing anonymization tools at removing identifiers that are represented in non-standard forms or only appear implicitly.

B97: 6 Discussion and conclusion
B98: We find that evaluating anonymization tools solely by recall in removing specific identifiers is insufficient. Even when some identifiers are removed, re-identification risk for an individual may remain high. Our benchmark addresses this by directly measuring re-identification risk through what the best attacker can infer, in line with the legal standard ( GDPR, 2016 ; CCPA, 2018 ) .
B99: Our benchmark further provides useful insights for users and developers. Ultimately, for users, choosing the right anonymizer for a given use-case requires balancing trade-offs in privacy, application-specific utility and computational cost. While our results generally agree with prior work ( Staab et al., 2025 ) that LLMs are strong anonymizers, allowing methods such as GPT-4.1 (Anthropic) to substantially reduce re-identification risk while maintaining data integrity, they also highlight the difficulty of specifying attributes for methods such as Rescriber and Staab et al. (2025) , as they may not generalize well to other identifiers.
B100: For developers, our results point to several areas for future work. First, we believe more emphasis should be placed on indirect identifiers. A significant proportion of the re-identification risk in our benchmark comes from indirect identifiers (Figure 3 ), which are often missed by all tested anonymizers (Figure 18 ). Second, NER-based anonymization tools should be more robust to unusual representations of identifiers and languages beyond English, likely requiring new annotated datasets that capture such variability. Other failure cases similarly revealed by our benchmark can also inform the development of better anonymization tools. Third, more focus should be placed on identifier values and combinations with high impact on re-identification risk, even if the features themselves are low risk overall. Further, we are excited for our benchmark to enable future work on prompt design for (one-shot) LLM-based anonymizers, or to develop more lightweight alternatives, potentially through targeted finetuning – while balancing the risk of overfitting (Section 5 ). Lastly, future work could explore more advanced utility metrics to better navigate the trade-offs, including metrics that measure semantic meaning or specifically target the quality of LLMs post-trained on anonymized chat interactions. We further elaborate on failure cases and lessons for users and developers in App. H .

B10: 1 Introduction
B11: Domain-specific text is essential for advancing today’s AI models and systems. Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ; OpenAI, 2025c ) are not only trained on vast amounts of publicly available data ( Brown et al., 2020 ; Touvron et al., 2023 ) but are also frequently fine-tuned on specialized datasets to perform specific tasks. For example, AI companies, including OpenAI ( OpenAI, 2025a ) and Anthropic ( TechCrunch, 2025 ) are leveraging user–chatbot interactions to improve their models ( King et al., 2025 ) , a practice recognized as key to generating a sustainable competitive advantage ( Huang and Grady, 2023 ) while specialized models have been developed for high-stakes domains such as medicine ( Kraljevic et al., 2022 ) and law ( FinancialTimes, 2025 ) .
B12: LLMs have, however, been shown to memorize portions of their training data. This makes them susceptible to membership inference attacks ( Meeus et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2024 ; Hayes et al., 2025 ) and to reproducing training sequences ( Carlini et al., 2021 ; Nasr et al., 2025 ; Cooper et al., 2025 ) . While several mitigation strategies exist, they entail trade-offs. Deduplicating training data can reduce memorization ( Kandpal et al., 2022 ; Lee et al., 2022 ) . Aggressive deduplication, in particular sequence-level deduplication, can however impact utility and models have recently been shown to still memorize across quite dissimilar sequences ( Shilov et al., 2024 ) . Training with formal privacy guarantees often incurs significant utility costs ( McKenna et al., 2025 ) . It is thus currently impossible to guarantee that sensitive sequences, including personal or confidential information, when incorporated into training, will not be memorized and, possibly, reproduced. Beyond training, sensitive data may also be exposed during inference, with e.g., information retrieved from private databases, user-data provided for few-shot examples ( Priyanshu et al., 2023 ; OpenAI, 2025b ; Mireshghallah and Li, 2025 ) , with recent work showing that such in-context data can be susceptible to leakage and active extraction attacks ( Zeng et al., 2024 ; Mireshghallah et al., 2024 ) .

B266: Appendix C Details for attack evaluation
B267: As described in Section 4 , evaluating the anonymized text requires matching the ground-truth attributes with the guesses produced by the LLM-based attacker. For each target attribute a ∈ 𝒜 a\in\mathcal{A} present in the original text t t , the attacker outputs a guess, which we compare to the ground truth.
B268: Both the ground-truth attributes and the LLM-generated guesses are unstructured and may not match exactly. For numerical attributes (e.g., age, SSN), we require exact matches. For text-based attributes (e.g., citizenship status, education attainment), we consider a guess correct if its Jaro–Winkler similarity with the ground truth exceeds an attribute-specific threshold. These thresholds were set by manually inspecting similarity scores across a sample of model outputs and defining conservative, attribute-specific rules rather than using a universal cutoff. For example, for ‘education attainment’, we require the grade level to match exactly—treating ‘Grade 8’ as incorrect when the ground truth is ‘Grade 9’, even if the string similarity is high. More broadly, when strings are similar but differ semantically (e.g., adjacent categories), we follow a conservative policy and mark the prediction as incorrect.
