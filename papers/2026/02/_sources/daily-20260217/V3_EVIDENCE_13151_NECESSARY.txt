B46: III Unlearning Failure via Quantization
B47: Recent empirical observations indicate that while unlearning methods appear successful in full precision, unlearning effects are frequently erased upon quantization. This section provides a theoretical explanation of this phenomenon, adhering to the framework established by [ 4 ] , identifying the conflict between the minimal weight updates characteristic of current unlearning algorithms and the resolution limits of low-precision quantization.
B48: Minimal Weight Change Constraint. In Full-FT, the optimizer must balance the forgetting of specific samples against the preservation of the entire parameter distribution. To avoid catastrophic forgetting of the retain set 𝒟 r ​ e ​ t ​ a ​ i ​ n \mathcal{D}_{retain} , unlearning benchmarks such as MUSE [ 7 ] and TOFU [ 10 ] typically require small learning rates (e.g., η ≈ 10 − 5 \eta\approx 10^{-5} to 10 − 7 10^{-7} ). This results in diffuse, low-magnitude updates spread across all parameters. Consequently, the unlearned weights W u W_{u} remains proximate to the original weights W 0 W_{0} and, therefore, the update Δ ​ W = W u − W 0 \Delta W=W_{u}-W_{0} is minute.
B49: Quantization Masking. This minimal deviation becomes critical during PTQ. Considering a group or block of weights, the quantization function Q ⁡ ( ⋅ ) Q(\cdot) maps continuous weights into a discrete set of indices within the range [ − 2 N − 1 , 2 N − 1 − 1 ] \left[-2^{N-1},\,2^{N-1}-1\right] , using a step size s s . A weight W W is mapped to a quantized value q i = i ​ s q_{i}=is if it falls within the interval:

B51: For the unlearning effect to persist in the quantized model, the update Δ ​ W \Delta W must shift the weight from its original interval ℐ i \mathcal{I}_{i} to a different interval. However, if the weight update does not cross a quantization bin boundary, i.e., W 0 W_{0} and W u = W 0 + Δ ​ W W_{u}=W_{0}+\Delta W lie in the same interval ℐ i \mathcal{I}_{i} , then the quantized index remains unchanged, so Q ⁡ ( W u ) = Q ⁡ ( W 0 ) Q(W_{u})=Q(W_{0}) . When this equality holds for the majority of parameters, the quantized unlearned model becomes the same as the quantized original model, resulting in the recovery of the forgotten knowledge [ 4 ] .
B52: Impact of Bit-Width. The likelihood of this failure is dictated by the bit-width N N , which defines the step size s = max ⁡ ( | W | ) 2 N − 1 s=\frac{\max(\left\lvert W\right\rvert)}{2^{N-1}} .

B54: 8-bit Quantization: With 2 7 = 128 2^{7}=128 intervals, s int8 s_{\text{int8}} is small, providing a resolution that can often capture the subtle shifts Δ ​ W \Delta W induced by unlearning. Thus, it maintains comparable performance to full-precision models [ 4 ] .

B57: Since the unlearning updates Δ ​ W \Delta W generated by regularized GA or NPO are typically smaller than the coarse s int4 s_{\text{int4}} , 4-bit quantization aggressively masks these changes. This theoretical threshold explains the empirical evidence in [ 4 ] , where 4-bit quantization is observed to be catastrophic for unlearning, effectively reverting the model to its pre-unlearning state.
B58: IV Robust Unlearning via LoRA
B59: To address the failure of unlearning under quantization described in section III , we propose Quantization-Robust Unlearning via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) . While standard unlearning methods typically operate on the full parameter space, often resulting in minute weight updates that are erased by quantization, we hypothesize that restricting the unlearning optimization to a low-rank subspace concentrates the gradient signal, producing structural updates robust to the discretization noise of low-precision formats.
B60: Unlearning Formulation with LoRA. Let f θ f_{\theta} be the target LLM with pre-trained weights W 0 ∈ ℝ d × k W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k} . In the standard unlearning setting described in eq. 1 , the optimization is performed over the full set of parameters θ = { W 0 } \theta=\{W_{0}\} . In our proposed method, we freeze the pre-trained weights W 0 W_{0} and introduce trainable low-rank matrices B ∈ ℝ d × r B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r} and A ∈ ℝ r × k A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k} , where r ≪ min ⁡ ( d , k ) r\ll\min(d,k) [ 5 ] . The forward pass for a layer becomes h = W 0 ​ x + α r ​ B ​ A ​ x h=W_{0}x+\frac{\alpha}{r}BAx , where α \alpha is a scaling hyperparameter constant in r r . The unlearning objective function ℒ t ​ o ​ t ​ a ​ l \mathcal{L}_{total} is minimized solely with respect to the adapter parameters Φ = { A , B } \Phi=\{A,B\} . By freezing W 0 W_{0} , we ensure that the base knowledge of the model is structurally preserved, shifting the unlearning burden entirely to the additive term Δ ​ W = α r ​ B ​ A \Delta W=\frac{\alpha}{r}BA [ 5 ] .
B61: As discussed in section III , the primary cause of unlearning failure in quantized models is the “Minimal Weight Change Constraint” [ 4 ] , where the unlearning update Δ ​ W \Delta W is smaller than the quantization step size s s . We argue that LoRA overcomes this through two mechanisms: Optimization Dynamics and Step Size and Magnitude Control via Scaling and Architecture .
B62: Optimization Dynamics and Step Size. As prior research has pointed out [ 6 ] , LoRA imposes a low-rank constraint that serves as an implicit regularizer. Because the optimization is restricted to a subspace of rank r r , the risk of distorting the model’s general features is significantly reduced compared to Full-FT. This structural stability allows us to employ significantly larger learning rates (e.g., η ≈ 10 − 4 \eta\approx 10^{-4} ) [ 5 ] , resulting in larger numerical updates within the targeted subspace.
B63: Crucially, this higher learning rate translates into a larger effective step size for the weight updates. By taking larger optimization steps, the accumulated values in matrices A A and B B rapidly grow large enough to push the effective weight update Δ ​ W \Delta W across the quantization boundary. The higher learning rate ensures that the unlearning signal is not just a theoretical gradient direction, but a numerical displacement large enough to survive the quantization process.
B64: Magnitude Control via Scaling and Architecture. Beyond the optimizer step size, LoRA makes unlearning robust to quantization in regards of the scaling factor α \alpha and layer selection. The scaling factor α \alpha acts as a direct amplifier of this signal. By tuning α \alpha , we linearly scale the magnitude of the updates independent of the learning rate. This allows us to enforce the quantization threshold condition.
B65: While increasing the learning rate in Full-FT might generate weight updates large enough to cross quantization boundaries, applying such large rates to the entire parameter set is risky. It can introduce a bias toward the retain data, skewing the model’s behavior and degrading performance on disjoint tasks [ 4 ] . To mitigate these side effects, we adopt a targeted strategy, akin to localized unlearning approaches [ 11 ] by utilizing LoRA’s capacity for explicit layer selection. Rather than distributing the unlearning budget across all layers, we target specific modules (e.g., MLP layers, attention projections or both) where knowledge is localized. This concentration of the unlearning objective not only preserves utility by limiting the scope of updates but also forces the update magnitude in those specific layers to be significantly higher to minimize the loss.
B66: Consequently, the magnitude of the LoRA unlearning matrix updates is enough to persist after quantization, minimizing the masking effect common in full-FT methods.

B99: V-A Evaluation Metrics
B100: Following the MUSE protocol [ 7 ] , we assess performance using four key metrics that balance the trade-off between forgetting, utility, and privacy:
B101: Verbatim Memorization (VerMem). Measures the model’s tendency to reproduce the forget set verbatim. The model is prompted with the first l l tokens from a sequence x [ : l ] x[:l] from the forget set 𝒟 f \mathcal{D}_{f} , and the generated continuation is compared to the ground truth x [ l + 1 : ] x[l+1:] using the ROUGE-L F1 score, calculated as VerMem ( f , 𝒟 f ) = 1 | 𝒟 f | ∑ x ∈ 𝒟 f ROUGE ( f ( x [ : l ] ) , x [ l + 1 : ] ) \text{VerMem}(f,\mathcal{D}_{f})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{f}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{f}}\text{ROUGE}(f(x[:l]),x[l+1:]) . Lower scores indicate better unlearning.
B102: Knowledge Memorization (KnowMem). Evaluates if the model retains semantic knowledge of the forgotten data. It computes the ROUGE-L score between the model’s answer f ⁡ ( q ) f(q) and the ground truth answer a a for QA pairs in the forget set 𝒟 f \mathcal{D}_{f} , defined as KnowMem ​ ( f , 𝒟 f ) = 1 | 𝒟 f | ​ ∑ ( q , a ) ∈ 𝒟 f ROUGE ​ ( f ⁡ ( q ) , a ) \text{KnowMem}(f,\mathcal{D}_{f})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{f}|}\sum_{(q,a)\in\mathcal{D}_{f}}\text{ROUGE}(f(q),a) . Lower scores indicate effective knowledge erasure.
B103: Privacy Leakage (PrivLeak). Assesses the indistinguishability between the unlearned model and a retrained model using Membership Inference Attacks (MIA). It uses the Min-K % Prob method to compute the AUC-ROC of discriminating between 𝒟 f \mathcal{D}_{f} and 𝒟 r \mathcal{D}_{r} . The metric is defined as the relative degradation compared to a model retrained from scratch ( f retrain f_{\mathrm{retrain}} ): PrivLeak = ( AUC ⁡ ( f unlearn , 𝒟 f , 𝒟 r ) − AUC ⁡ ( f retrain , 𝒟 f , 𝒟 r ) ) / AUC ⁡ ( f retrain , 𝒟 f , 𝒟 r ) \text{PrivLeak}=(\mathrm{AUC}(f_{\mathrm{unlearn}}\allowbreak;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}})-\mathrm{AUC}(f_{\mathrm{retrain}}\allowbreak;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}))\allowbreak\;/\;\allowbreak\mathrm{AUC}(f_{\mathrm{retrain}}\allowbreak;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{r}}) . Optimal scores are near zero, indicating the unlearned model leaks no more information than a model that never saw the data.
B104: Utility Preservation (UtilityPres). Ensures general capabilities are maintained. We measure this by computing the Knowledge Memorization score (ROUGE-L) on the retain set 𝒟 r \mathcal{D}_{r} . Higher scores indicate better preservation of general knowledge.
B105: V-B Implementation Details
B106: We employ Llama-2-7B as the base model for all experiments. We evaluate two primary families of unlearning algorithms: GA and NPO. To ensure fair comparison and utility preservation, both methods are coupled with regularization strategies: GDR and KLR. This results in six baseline configurations: GA, NPO, GA+GDR, GA+KLR, NPO+GDR, and NPO+KLR. Additionally, we pair these baselines with LoRA and compare them against their full-parameter fine-tuned counterparts.
B107: To evaluate unlearning with LoRA , updates are maintained after PTQ. We freeze the pre-trained weights W 0 W_{0} and inject trainable LoRA adapters into all linear layers, including MLP modules and Attention projections.
B108: We performed a grid search over quantization-robustness hyperparameters, sweeping LoRA ranks r ∈ { 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 } r\in\{16,32,64,128\} , coupling the scaling factor to rank with α ∈ { 0.5 ​ r , r , 2 ​ r } \alpha\in\{0.5r,\,r,\,2r\} , and tuning optimization settings via learning rates η ∈ { 10 − 4 , 7 × 10 − 4 } \eta\in\{10^{-4},7\times 10^{-4}\} and training durations of { 5 , 10 } \{5,10\} epochs. For unlearning methods with KLR and GDR, we searched for the optimal regularization weight λ ∈ { 0.1 , 1 , 2 , 10 , 50 , 100 , 200 , 300 } \lambda\in\{0.1,1,2,10,50,100,200,300\} , and these weights were fixed for LoRA experiments to ensure that performance improvements are attributable solely to LoRA. We set the NPO β = 0.1 \beta=0.1 as done in [ 2 ] .
B109: Crucially, for all LoRA-based experiments, we explicitly merge the trained low-rank adapters into the base model parameters before quantization. This ensures that the quantization step is applied to the final unlearned weights ( W u ​ n ​ l ​ e ​ a ​ r ​ n = W 0 + Δ ​ W W_{unlearn}=W_{0}+\Delta W ), thereby subjecting the unlearning updates to the potential masking effects described in section III .
B110: We employ Round-to-Nearest (RTN) as our primary post-training quantization method. We note that recent studies have demonstrated that advanced calibration-based methods, such as GPTQ and AWQ, exhibit similar failure modes at 4-bit precision due to the resolution limits discussed in [ 4 ] . We report the degradation in unlearning metrics as the bit-width decreases across three settings: BF16 (original bfloat16 precision), Int8 (8-bit post-training quantization), and Int4 (4-bit post-training quantization).

B144: VII Conclusion
B145: This paper studied the failure of LLM Unlearning with PTQ, especially under aggressive 4-bit quantization. To mitigate this failure, we proposed quantization-robust unlearning via LoRA , which freezes the base model and concentrates unlearning into trainable low-rank adapters.
B146: We found that merging LoRA adapters before PTQ substantially improves robustness at 4-bit. When compared to Full-FT, LoRA-based unlearning preserves the forgetting/privacy signal after quantization and, in many settings, reduces the quantization-induced drop in utility. Our findings suggest that parameter-efficient, structurally constrained updates offer a principled path toward deployable unlearning in resource-constrained, low-precision regimes.
