B47: 3 Problem Formulation
B48: Standard RLVR employs static or heuristic temperature schedules that cannot adapt to task heterogeneity. Empirical studies reveal three key limitations: (i) different reasoning steps require different exploration levels—reasoning pivots benefit from high temperature while factual tokens require low temperature ( Cui et al., 2025b ; Wang et al., 2025b ) ; (ii) optimal temperature varies across prompts and difficulty levels; (iii) exploration requirements evolve during training as model capabilities improve ( An et al., 2025 ) .
B49: We address these limitations by formulating temperature selection as a learnable sequential decision process. At each step t t , a temperature policy π ϕ ​ ( τ t ∣ h t , τ t − 1 ) \pi_{\phi}(\tau_{t}\mid h_{t},\tau_{t-1}) selects sampling temperature τ t \tau_{t} based on hidden state h t h_{t} , while a token policy π θ ​ ( y t ∣ h t , τ t ) \pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid h_{t},\tau_{t}) generates the next token. The joint process factorizes as:
B50: π ⁡ ( y , τ ∣ x ) = ∏ t = 1 T π ϕ ​ ( τ t ∣ h t , τ t − 1 ) ​ π θ ​ ( y t ∣ h t , τ t ) , \textstyle\pi(y,\tau\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\phi}(\tau_{t}\mid h_{t},\tau_{t-1})\,\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid h_{t},\tau_{t}), (6)
B51: where y = ( y 1 , … , y T ) y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T}) and τ = ( τ 1 , … , τ T ) \tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{T}) are the token and temperature sequences. The objective is to maximize the expected reward:
B52: J ( θ , ϕ ) = 𝔼 x ∼ 𝒟 𝔼 τ , y ∼ π ( ⋅ ∣ x ) [ R ( x , y ) ] . J(\theta,\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\tau,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[R(x,y)\big]. (7)
B53: This hierarchical formulation enables: (i) reward-driven exploration optimized from task feedback, (ii) context-aware adaptation via hidden state conditioning, and (iii) principled separation of content generation and exploration control. In the following section, we expound on the architectural design and joint optimization procedure for this framework.
B54: 4 Introspective LLM
B55: As illustrated in Figure 1 , IntroLLM instantiate the hierarchical formulation through two coupled policies: (i) a temperature policy π ϕ \pi_{\phi} that observes hidden state h t h_{t} and selects sampling temperature τ t \tau_{t} , and (ii) a token policy π θ \pi_{\theta} that generates the next token y t y_{t} from a τ t \tau_{t} -modulated distribution. These operate sequentially at each decoding step. Training follows a standard RL pipeline: sample trajectories with both temperature and token decisions, evaluate via verifiable rewards, and update both policies jointly using policy gradients. The following subsections detail the token policy conditioning (§ 4.1 ), temperature policy design (§ 4.2 ), and joint optimization procedure (§ 4.3 ).
B56: 4.1 Token Policy with Temperature Conditioning
B57: The token policy generates the next token from a distribution modulated by the temperature policy. Specifically, given hidden state h t h_{t} , the model produces pre-softmax logits ℓ t ∈ ℝ | 𝒱 | \ell_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|} , which are rescaled by the selected temperature τ t \tau_{t} to obtain the token distribution: π θ ​ ( y t ∣ h t , τ t ) = Softmax ⁡ ( ℓ t τ t ) \pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid h_{t},\tau_{t})=\mathrm{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{\ell_{t}}{\tau_{t}}\right) . This temperature-conditioned formulation enables adaptive entropy modulation, directly addressing the token-level heterogeneity identified in § 3 . During training, { τ t } \{\tau_{t}\} is treated as fixed environmental parameters, allowing the token policy to focus on content generation while the temperature policy controls exploration intensity. The joint optimization procedure is detailed in § 4.3 .
B58: 4.2 Temperature Policy Design
B59: The temperature policy π ϕ ​ ( τ t ∣ h t ) \pi_{\phi}(\tau_{t}\mid h_{t}) controls exploration by mapping the model’s internal hidden states to dynamic sampling temperatures. This introspective mechanism enables the model to increase stochasticity at exploration steps while maintaining stability during exploitation. To balance expressive control with optimization stability, we formulate π ϕ \pi_{\phi} as a mixed discrete–continuous policy. This design allows the model to learn both when to adjust temperature and how much to adjust it, avoiding unnecessary variance from frequent changes while maintaining adaptation flexibility.
B60: Architectural Parameterization. We implement the temperature policy as a lightweight MLP head f ϕ f_{\phi} branching from the final decoder layer. Given hidden representation h t ∈ ℝ d h_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} at step t t , the policy head projects it through a low-dimensional bottleneck to produce a 3-dimensional control vector 𝐮 t = [ u c , u α , u β ] ⊤ \mathbf{u}_{t}=[u_{c},u_{\alpha},u_{\beta}]^{\top} :
B61: 𝐮 t = 𝐖 2 ⋅ ReLU ​ ( 𝐖 1 ​ h t + 𝐛 1 ) + 𝐛 2 , \mathbf{u}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{2}\cdot\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{1}h_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{1})+\mathbf{b}_{2}, (8)
B62: where 𝐖 1 ∈ ℝ d 2 × d \mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{2}\times d} and 𝐖 2 ∈ ℝ 3 × d 2 \mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times\frac{d}{2}} are linear projections. This architecture ensures temperature decisions are conditioned on the full semantic and uncertainty context encoded in h t h_{t} , enabling the policy to dynamically balance exploration and exploitation.
B63: Mixed Discrete–Continuous Action Space. Temperature selection proceeds through a two-stage stochastic process:
B64: Stage 1: Update Decision. The policy first decides whether to adjust temperature via a Bernoulli random variable: c t ∼ Bernoulli ​ ( σ ⁡ ( u c ) ) , c_{t}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\sigma(u_{c})), where σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) is the sigmoid function. When c t = 0 c_{t}=0 , temperature remains constant ( τ t = τ t − 1 \tau_{t}=\tau_{t-1} ), reducing optimization variance by avoiding redundant adjustments when the current temperature is appropriate.
B65: Stage 2: Value Sampling. Conditioned on c t = 1 c_{t}=1 , the policy samples a new temperature intensity z t z_{t} from a Beta distribution ℬ ⁡ ( α , β ) \mathcal{B}(\alpha,\beta) , which is naturally bounded in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] and provides flexible bimodal and unimodal behaviors:
B66: z t ∼ Beta ​ ( softplus ​ ( u α ) + ϵ , softplus ​ ( u β ) + ϵ ) , z_{t}\sim\text{Beta}(\text{softplus}(u_{\alpha})+\epsilon,\text{softplus}(u_{\beta})+\epsilon), (9)
B67: where ϵ = 10 − 6 \epsilon=10^{-6} ensures numerical stability. The final sampling temperature is obtained via affine transformation to [ τ min , τ max ] [\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}] : τ t = τ min + z t ⋅ ( τ max − τ min ) . \tau_{t}=\tau_{\min}+z_{t}\cdot(\tau_{\max}-\tau_{\min}).
B68: Joint Log-Likelihood. For policy gradient updates (§ 4.3 ), we compute the joint log-probability of a realized sample ( c t , z t ) (c_{t},z_{t}) as:
B69: log ⁡ π ϕ ​ ( τ t ∣ h t ) = log ⁡ P ⁡ ( c t ) + c t ​ log ⁡ p ⁡ ( z t ∣ α , β ) , \log\pi_{\phi}(\tau_{t}\mid h_{t})=\log P(c_{t})+c_{t}\log p(z_{t}\mid\alpha,\beta), (10)
B70: where c t ∈ { 0 , 1 } c_{t}\in\{0,1\} gates the continuous component. This factorization enables gradient computation while maintaining the coupling between discrete and continuous decisions.

B90: 4.3 Joint Policy Optimization
B91: Jointly optimizing both policies presents a challenge: simultaneous updates can lead to non-stationary learning dynamics where each policy’s gradient depends on the other’s changing behavior. We address this through coordinate ascent, alternating between token policy updates (with fixed temperatures τ \tau ) and temperature policy updates (with fixed tokens y y ). This decoupling stabilizes training while maintaining policy interaction through the shared reward R ⁡ ( x , y ) R(x,y) . Algorithm 1 details the complete procedure.
B92: Hierarchical Rollout. During rollout, generation proceeds via a nested stochastic process. At each step t t , the temperature policy samples ( c t , z t ) (c_{t},z_{t}) to determine τ t \tau_{t} , and the token policy samples y t y_{t} conditioned on τ t \tau_{t} . We persist the complete trajectory 𝒯 = { ( c t , z t , τ t , y t ) } t = 1 T \mathcal{T}=\{(c_{t},z_{t},\tau_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T} in the experience buffer, where { y t } \{y_{t}\} enables reward evaluation and { ( c t , z t , τ t ) } \{(c_{t},z_{t},\tau_{t})\} enables on-policy likelihood recomputation.
B93: On-Policy Likelihood Recomputation. To compute policy gradients, we must evaluate the log-probabilities of the actions taken during rollout under the current policy parameters. During each training iteration, we perform a single forward pass to recompute likelihoods for both policies:
B94: 1. Token Policy: We compute log ⁡ π θ ​ ( y t ∣ h t , τ t ) \log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid h_{t},\tau_{t}) by scaling token logits with the cached temperatures { τ t } \{\tau_{t}\} from the stored trajectory. This ensures gradients reflect the exact exploration context of the original rollout.
B95: 2. Temperature Policy: We compute log ⁡ π ϕ ​ ( c t , z t ∣ h t ) \log\pi_{\phi}(c_{t},z_{t}\mid h_{t}) for the recorded temperature actions. This allows the exploration strategy to be reinforced based on the sequence-level rewards it produced.
B96: Both likelihoods are then used in the GRPO objective to update the respective policies via coordinate ascent.
B97: Coordinate Ascent Optimization. We optimize θ \theta and ϕ \phi via coordinate ascent, alternating updates within each GRPO iteration. Given a group of trajectories 𝒯 = { ( c ( i ) , z ( i ) , τ ( i ) , y ( i ) ) } i = 1 G \mathcal{T}=\{(c^{(i)},z^{(i)},\tau^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{G} :
B98: 1. Token Policy Update. Fixing τ \tau as constants, we update θ \theta to maximize reward conditioned on the realized exploration:
B99: θ ← arg ⁡ max θ ​ 𝔼 𝒯 ​ [ 1 G ​ ∑ i = 1 G ∑ t = 1 T ℒ i , t token ​ ( θ ∣ τ t ( i ) ) ] , \textstyle\theta\leftarrow\arg\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{i,t}^{\text{token}}(\theta\mid\tau_{t}^{(i)})\right],
B100: where ℒ token \mathcal{L}^{\text{token}} is the GRPO clipped objective from Equation ( 4 ) applied to token actions y t y_{t} , using the importance ratio r t θ = π θ ​ ( y t | h t , τ t ) / π θ old ​ ( y t | h t , τ t ) r_{t}^{\theta}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|h_{t},\tau_{t})/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}|h_{t},\tau_{t}) .
B101: 2. Temperature Policy Update. Fixing tokens y y as constants, we update ϕ \phi to optimize the exploration strategy that produced those rewards:
B102: ϕ ← arg ⁡ max ϕ ​ 𝔼 𝒯 ​ [ 1 G ​ ∑ i = 1 G ∑ t = 1 T ℒ i , t temp ​ ( ϕ ∣ y t ( i ) ) ] , \textstyle\phi\leftarrow\arg\max_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{i,t}^{\text{temp}}(\phi\mid y_{t}^{(i)})\right],
B103: where ℒ temp \mathcal{L}^{\text{temp}} applies the same GRPO objective (Equation ( 4 )) to temperature actions ( c t , z t ) (c_{t},z_{t}) with importance ratio r t ϕ = π ϕ ​ ( c t , z t | h t ) / π ϕ old ​ ( c t , z t | h t ) r_{t}^{\phi}=\pi_{\phi}(c_{t},z_{t}|h_{t})/\pi_{\phi_{\text{old}}}(c_{t},z_{t}|h_{t}) .
B104: Both updates share the same trajectory-level advantage A i A_{i} (Equation ( 3 )), computed from the final reward R ⁡ ( x , y ( i ) ) R(x,y^{(i)}) and normalized across the group. This shared advantage ensures both policies receive consistent credit assignment: if a trajectory achieves high reward, both the temperature decisions and token choices that produced it are reinforced proportionally. This alternating scheme enables the token policy to learn optimal content generation under diverse exploration regimes, while the temperature policy learns to identify high-reward exploration strategies. By isolating gradient flows, we mitigate the non-stationarity of simultaneous updates, achieving stable convergence.

B105: 5 Experiments
B106: Datasets and Benchmarks. We train all models on the MATH ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) training set and evaluate on a comprehensive suite of reasoning tasks: AIME 2024, AMC 2023, MATH-500 ( Lightman et al., 2023 ) , Minerva Math ( Lewkowycz et al., 2022 ) , OlympiadBench ( He et al., 2024 ) , and Omni-Math ( Gao et al., 2024 ) .
B107: Baselines. We compare IntroLLM against three categories of baselines: (i) Standard GRPO: GRPO training with fixed temperatures τ ∈ { 0.6 , 1.0 , 1.2 } \tau\in\{0.6,1.0,1.2\} to establish performance bounds for non-adaptive sampling; (ii) Heuristic Adaptive (EAD) ( Yang et al., 2025 ) : A pre-defined annealing schedule that monotonically decays temperature to balance early-stage exploration and late-stage convergence; (iii) Sequence-level Adaptive (TAMPO) ( Dang et al., 2026 ) : A learnable meta-policy that optimizes a static temperature for the entire sequence generalization.
B108: Implementation Details. All models are trained for 4 epochs with batch size 128, learning rate 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} , group size G = 8 G=8 , and maximum response length 3072 tokens. For IntroLLM, we initialize the temperature policy head to a maximum-entropy state ( P ⁡ ( c t = 1 ) = 0.5 P(c_{t}=1)=0.5 , z t ∼ Uniform ​ ( 0 , 1 ) z_{t}\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1) ) with temperature bounds restricted to [ τ min , τ max ] = [ 0.6 , 1.5 ] [\tau_{\text{min}},\tau_{\text{max}}]=[0.6,1.5] .
B109: Evaluation Metrics. We report Avg@8 (expected Pass@1 performance across multiple samples) and Pass@8 to assess both reasoning accuracy and generation diversity. All methods use their respective temperature strategies with maximum response length 3072. Further details are provided in Appendix B .

B154: 5.3 Ablation Study
B155: We ablate two key design choices of IntroLLM’s temperature policy: (i) control granularity (prompt-level vs. token-level), and (ii) update frequency (selective vs. every-token). Table 4 compares three variants:
B156: Prompt-level predicts a single temperature for the entire response based on the prompt, achieving 35.91%/49.42% average Avg@8 (1.7B/4B). This coarse-grained control cannot adapt to reasoning dynamics within a sequence.
B157: Always-update samples a new temperature at every token without the Bernoulli trigger, achieving 37.84%/47.87%. While offering maximum flexibility, this introduces excessive variance that destabilizes learning—on 4B, it underperforms even prompt-level control by 1.55%, despite being more fine-grained.
B158: IntroLLM uses selective token-level updates via the mixed discrete-continuous action space, achieving 38.72%/50.80%. By learning when to update temperature (Bernoulli) and how much (Beta), IntroLLM outperforms prompt-level by 2.81%/1.38% and always-update by 0.88%/2.93%.

B231: Appendix B Implementation Details
B232: B.1 Model Architecture
B233: The model extends the base architecture with a lightweight MLP head branching from the final hidden state to predict temperature parameters. This lightweight design allows the temperature head to serve as a plug-and-play module, making it easily adaptable to models of the same family that have undergone fine-tuning ( Qing et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025c ; Yin et al., 2025 ; Feng et al., 2024 ; Han et al., 2025b ) or compression ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Lu et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025b ) . Additionally, this design facilitates straightforward migration to other architectures, such as diffusion language models ( Han et al., 2025a ) .
