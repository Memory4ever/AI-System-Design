B28: 3 Theoretical Analysis: The Dynamics of Reasoning Extinction
B29: In this section, we analyze the optimization dynamics of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). While Policy Gradient Theorem Sutton et al. (1999) guarantees convergence to optimal policies under infinite sampling, we demonstrate that the practical constraints of low-temperature sampling and finite rollouts introduce a systemic selection bias. Our analysis reveals a critical failure mode: valid reasoning patterns that fall below a Visibility Barrier are subject to exponential extinction, regardless of their correctness.

B33: Let 𝒯 \mathcal{T} denote the discrete space of reasoning traces. Standard RLVR algorithms (e.g., GRPO Shao et al. (2024) , PPO Schulman et al. (2017) ) aim to maximize a reward objective subject to a KL constraint against a reference policy π ref \pi_{\text{ref}} . The theoretical update at iteration k k is often formulated as:
B34: θ k + 1 = arg ⁡ max θ 𝔼 τ ∼ π θ k [ R ( τ ) ] − β KL ( π θ ∥ π ref ) . \theta_{k+1}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta_{k}}}\left[R(\tau)\right]-\beta\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}). (1)
B35: However, practical implementations approximate the expectation using a finite set of samples 𝒮 k = { τ 1 , … , τ N } \mathcal{S}_{k}=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{N}\} drawn from a proposal distribution Q k Q_{k} . This induces a Sampling-Bias Gap : the gradient update is computed exclusively on the sparse empirical support 𝒮 k \mathcal{S}_{k} , creating a disconnect between the theoretical objective and the realized optimization trajectory.
B36: Assumption (Function Space Dynamics).
B37: To isolate the effects of selection bias from generalization noise, we analyze the dynamics in the function space of reasoning traces. We assume that distinct reasoning paths are approximately orthogonal in the representation space, such that updating the logits of sampled traces does not arbitrarily boost unsampled traces via parameter sharing.
B38: Practical Constraint: Low-Temperature Filtering.
B39: While unbiased exploration ( T = 1 T=1 ) is theoretically ideal, state-of-the-art reasoning methods typically operate in a low-temperature regime to ensure logical coherence and reduce hallucinations Zelikman et al. (2022) ; Cobbe et al. (2021) . Leading reasoning frameworks, including DeepSeek-R1 DeepSeek-AI and others (2025) and ReST Gulcehre et al. (2023) , explicitly utilize sharpened sampling or rejection mechanisms to filter out low-quality traces:
B40: Q k ​ ( τ ) ∝ π k ​ ( τ ) 1 / T , with ​ T ∈ [ 0.6 , 0.8 ] . Q_{k}(\tau)\propto\pi_{k}(\tau)^{1/T},\quad\text{with }T\in[0.6,0.8]. (2)
B41: Even when T = 1 T=1 , the constraint of finite rollouts ( N ≪ | 𝒯 | N\ll|\mathcal{T}| ) imposes an implicit High-Pass Likelihood Filter . A reasoning trace with π ⁡ ( τ ) ≪ 1 / N \pi(\tau)\ll 1/N is statistically invisible to the optimization process, effectively creating a truncation equivalent to T eff < 1 T_{\text{eff}}<1 .

B81: 4 Methodology
B82: To counteract the boundary contraction issue identified in Section 3 , we propose Amortized Reasoning Tree Search (ARTS) . Unlike coupled RLVR methods that collapse the search space to the base model’s priors, ARTS structurally decouples the Proposer (System 1) from the Verifier (System 2) Kahneman (2011) ; Yao et al. (2024) . This allows the Verifier to learn a global value function that transcends the myopic biases of the generative policy. This section details the ARTS framework, the Sparse Deep Fork data construction, and formally derives our training objective as a Flow Matching Lower Bound grounded in Generative Flow Network (GFlowNet) theory.
B83: 4.1 Problem Setup
B84: We formalize multi-step reasoning as a sequential decision process on a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) 𝒢 \mathcal{G} . A state s t = ( x , a 1 , … , a t ) s_{t}=(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{t}) represents a partial reasoning chain. The environment provides feedback only at terminal states s T s_{T} . To mitigate the vanishing gradient problem inherent in sparse reward environments and ensure exploration continuity , we adopt a Log-Flow Smoothing reward scheme:
B85: R ⁡ ( s T ) = { 10 , if ​ s T ​ is correct , 0.1 , otherwise . R(s_{T})=\begin{cases}10,&\text{if }s_{T}\text{ is correct},\\ 0.1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} (6)
B86: This contrastive design assigns high flow magnitude to correct reasoning ( 100 × 100\times ) while maintaining a non-zero background flow for incorrect paths, preventing the flow manifold from collapsing to singularity ( log ⁡ 0 → − ∞ \log 0\to-\infty ).
B87: 4.2 Amortized Reasoning Tree Search (ARTS)
B88: The goal of ARTS is to train a Verifier V θ ​ ( s ) V_{\theta}(s) that guides search at inference time to recover correct paths invisible to standard sampling. We utilize the frozen base model π 0 \pi_{0} as a Proposer . While π 0 \pi_{0} may assign low probability to a correct trajectory τ ∗ \tau^{*} , the probability of discovering τ ∗ \tau^{*} via a stochastic tree search with width K K expands geometrically as K H ⋅ π 0 ​ ( τ ∗ ) K^{H}\cdot\pi_{0}(\tau^{*}) . We term this phenomenon Search-Based Probability Amplification : theoretically, the probability of discovering a rare trajectory τ ∗ \tau^{*} expands geometrically as 1 − ( 1 − π 0 ​ ( τ ∗ ) ) K ≈ K ⋅ π 0 ​ ( τ ∗ ) 1-(1-\pi_{0}(\tau^{*}))^{K}\approx K\cdot\pi_{0}(\tau^{*}) in the small probability limit.
B89: Figure 2: Visualizing Sparse Deep Fork (SDF). Instead of exhaustive tree search, SDF constructs a cost-effective DAG by stochastically branching (Orange) from the base model’s main trajectory (Blue). This structure efficiently exposes the verifier to local decision boundaries and alternative outcomes—such as discovering a correct path ( R = 10 R=10 ) diverging from an incorrect one—without the exponential cost of full expansion.
B90: 4.3 Data Construction: Sparse Deep Fork
B91: Training a flow function requires observing the branching topology of the reasoning space. However, constructing a full tree is computationally prohibitive. We propose Sparse Deep Fork (SDF) , a topological approximation strategy illustrated in Figure 2 .
B92: The graph construction proceeds as follows:
B93: 1.
B94: Main Trajectory (Blue): For a given problem x x , we sample N N independent reasoning trajectories from the base policy π 0 \pi_{0} . These serve as the ”backbone” of our topological map.
B95: 2.
B96: Stochastic Branching (Orange): We iterate through intermediate nodes s t s_{t} in the main trajectory. With probability p = 0.5 p=0.5 , we select s t s_{t} as a pivot point and perform one additional rollout (fork) using π 0 \pi_{0} .
B97: Efficiency Analysis. This generates a sparse DAG 𝒢 sdf \mathcal{G}_{\text{sdf}} where specific nodes have two observed children (the original path and the augmented branch). As shown in Figure 2 , this structure allows the verifier to observe counterfactual outcomes —e.g., a correct branch ( R = 10 R=10 ) diverging from an incorrect main path ( R = 0.1 R=0.1 )—using only 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1.5 ​ N ) \mathcal{O}(1.5N) compute. This efficiently exposes the local decision boundaries necessary for training a discriminative flow function.
B98: 4.4 Reasoning Flow Matching (RFM)
B99: Our training objective is grounded in the theory of Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) Bengio et al. (2021) , which learn a flow function F ⁡ ( s ) F(s) such that the flow into any state equals the flow out of it. However, standard GFlowNet training assumes access to either the full graph or an on-policy exploration policy. In our sparse, offline setting, we adapt the objective to learn from the observed support .
B100: The Partial Consistency Objective.
B101: We parameterize the verifier F θ ​ ( s ) : 𝒮 → ℝ + F_{\theta}(s):\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{+} in log-space. For any non-terminal state s t ∈ 𝒢 sdf s_{t}\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{sdf}} with the set of observed children 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s ​ ( s t ) \mathcal{C}_{obs}(s_{t}) , we minimize the local flow mismatch:
B102: ℒ RFM ( θ ) = 𝔼 s t ∼ 𝒢 sdf [ ( log F θ ( s t ) − log ∑ s ′ ∈ 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s ​ ( s t ) F θ ( s ′ ) ) 2 ] + λ ℒ Leaf , \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s_{t}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\text{sdf}}}\left[\left(\log F_{\theta}(s_{t})-\log\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{obs}(s_{t})}F_{\theta}(s^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right]+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{Leaf}}, (7)
B103: where ℒ Leaf \mathcal{L}_{\text{Leaf}} regresses the terminal flow to the reward R ⁡ ( s T ) R(s_{T}) .
B104: Analysis: Learning a Ranking-Preserving Lower Bound.
B105: A critical theoretical question arises: Does minimizing Eq. 7 on a sparse graph recover the true flow values? Formally, the true flow consistency equation requires summing over all possible children 𝒞 a ​ l ​ l ​ ( s t ) \mathcal{C}_{all}(s_{t}) :
B106: F ∗ ​ ( s t ) = ∑ s ′ ∈ 𝒞 a ​ l ​ l ​ ( s t ) F ∗ ​ ( s ′ ) . F^{*}(s_{t})=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{all}(s_{t})}F^{*}(s^{\prime}). (8)
B107: Since our observed set is a subset 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s ⊂ 𝒞 a ​ l ​ l \mathcal{C}_{obs}\subset\mathcal{C}_{all} , the training target is inherently an underestimation:
B108: Target ​ ( s t ) = ∑ s ′ ∈ 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s F ⁡ ( s ′ ) ≤ ∑ s ′ ∈ 𝒞 a ​ l ​ l F ⁡ ( s ′ ) = F ∗ ​ ( s t ) . \text{Target}(s_{t})=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{obs}}F(s^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{all}}F(s^{\prime})=F^{*}(s_{t}). (9)
B109: Consequently, minimizing the local mismatch on 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s \mathcal{C}_{obs} enforces F θ F_{\theta} to converge to a Conservative Flow Estimate :
B110: F θ ​ ( s t ) → ∑ s ′ ∈ 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s F ⁡ ( s ′ ) ≤ ∑ s ′ ∈ 𝒞 a ​ l ​ l F ∗ ​ ( s ′ ) . F_{\theta}(s_{t})\to\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{obs}}F(s^{\prime})\leq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{all}}F^{*}(s^{\prime}). (10)
B111: This effectively creates a Ranking-Preserving Lower Bound where the flow value represents the confirmed probability mass of success discovered so far.
B112: Sufficiency for ARTS.
B113: While F θ F_{\theta} underestimates the exact probability, we argue that this lower bound is sufficient and effective for the specific task of ranking reasoning paths in ARTS for three reasons:
B114: 1.
B115: Dominance of Correct Flows: Due to our reward design ( 10 10 vs 0.1 0.1 ), the magnitude of flow is dominated by the presence of correct paths. Even if we miss 90 % 90\% of the children, observing a single correct child contributes + 10 +10 to the sum, whereas observing ten incorrect children contributes only + 1 +1 . Thus, the lower bound F θ ​ ( s ) F_{\theta}(s) remains high if and only if at least one successful path is discovered.
B116: 2.
B117: Monotonicity Preservation: The flow matching objective enforces F ⁡ ( s t ) ≈ ∑ F ⁡ ( s t + 1 ) F(s_{t})\approx\sum F(s_{t+1}) . Since flows are non-negative, this implies F ⁡ ( s t ) ≥ F ⁡ ( s t + 1 ) F(s_{t})\geq F(s_{t+1}) (for single-child chains). The learned function preserves the monotonicity of the value landscape, ensuring that the gradient points towards the sources of reward.
B118: 3.
B119: Ranking Consistency: For inference, we only require the correct ranking F ⁡ ( s good ) > F ⁡ ( s bad ) F(s_{\text{good}})>F(s_{\text{bad}}) . Consider a state s good s_{\text{good}} that leads to a correct answer. Even with sparse sampling, there is a probability that the correct path is included in 𝒞 o ​ b ​ s \mathcal{C}_{obs} , pushing F ⁡ ( s good ) → 10 F(s_{\text{good}})\to 10 . Conversely, for a dead-end state s bad s_{\text{bad}} , all possible children lead to 0.1 0.1 . No matter how many children we miss, the sum cannot exceed the background noise level. Therefore, the contrastive margin between valid and invalid states is topologically robust: sparse sampling may underestimate the magnitude of a correct node, but it strictly bounds the invalid nodes to the noise floor, preserving the necessary gradient for search guidance.

B200: 6 Discussion and Conclusion
B201: In this work, we investigated the optimization dynamics of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Our theoretical analysis identifies a structural vulnerability in standard coupled optimization: the extinction of valid but rare reasoning traces caused by the mechanistic interplay between low-temperature sampling and softmax normalization. To mitigate this ”High-Pass Filter” effect, we proposed Amortized Reasoning Tree Search (ARTS) , a framework that fundamentally decouples the search space generator (Proposer) from the value estimator (Verifier) using a flow-matching objective.
B202: 6.1 The Trade-off: Internalization vs. Deliberation
B203: Our findings illuminate a fundamental trade-off in the design of reasoning systems, balancing parameter updates against inference compute :
B204: •
B205: Internalization (System 1): Coupled methods like GRPO aim to internalize successful reasoning patterns directly into the policy’s weights. This ”compiles” search into intuition, yielding high zero-shot efficiency. However, as shown in our extinction analysis, this process can aggressively prune the long-tail diversity required for hard problems.
B206: •
B207: Deliberation (System 2): In contrast, ARTS prioritizes deliberation . By maintaining a high-entropy generator and relying on a flow-calibrated verifier to guide inference-time search, it preserves the model’s latent boundaries. While this shifts the computational burden from training to inference, recent scaling laws for inference compute Brown et al. (2024) suggest that this may be a more scalable path for solving complex reasoning tasks where a single ”best” path is insufficient.
B208: We view these approaches not as mutually exclusive, but as complementary strategies on the Pareto frontier of intelligence: RLVR for efficiency in common scenarios, and Flow-Guided Search for robustness in the long tail.
B209: 6.2 Limitations
B210: We acknowledge several limitations in our current study that merit further investigation:
B211: •
B212: Bounded by the Proposer: ARTS operates as a selector, not a creator. It cannot generate solutions that are structurally absent from the base model’s support. If the frozen proposer fails to cover the correct reasoning path within the sampling budget ( N N ), the verifier is powerless to recover it.
B213: •
B214: Inference Latency: Unlike end-to-end RL policies which can be deployed greedily, ARTS relies on Best-of- N N sampling or tree search. This incurs higher latency and cost, making it less suitable for latency-sensitive applications.
B215: •
B216: Heuristic Reward Shaping: Our Flow Matching objective currently relies on a manually designed soft reward scheme ( 10 10 vs 0.1 0.1 ) to ensure numerical stability. While empirically effective, deriving these values from a principled probabilistic framework remains an open question.

B260: A.1 Setup and Definitions
B261: Let the policy at iteration k k be parameterized by a scoring function (logits) f k : 𝒯 → ℝ f_{k}:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R} , such that the probability of a trace τ \tau is given by the Softmax distribution:
B262: π k ​ ( τ ) = exp ⁡ ( f k ​ ( τ ) ) Z k , where Z k = ∑ τ ′ ∈ 𝒯 exp ⁡ ( f k ​ ( τ ′ ) ) . \pi_{k}(\tau)=\frac{\exp(f_{k}(\tau))}{Z_{k}},\quad\text{where}\quad Z_{k}=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\exp(f_{k}(\tau^{\prime})). (11)
B263: In standard RLVR (e.g., GRPO/PPO), the optimization objective is approximated using a finite batch of samples 𝒮 k \mathcal{S}_{k} . The gradient update is sparse: it only affects the logits of traces present in 𝒮 k \mathcal{S}_{k} . The update rule for the logits can be written as:
B264: f k + 1 ​ ( τ ) = { f k ​ ( τ ) + R ⁡ ( τ ) β if ​ τ ∈ 𝒮 k ( Reinforced ) f k ​ ( τ ) if ​ τ ∉ 𝒮 k ( Frozen ) f_{k+1}(\tau)=\begin{cases}f_{k}(\tau)+\frac{R(\tau)}{\beta}&\text{if }\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\quad(\text{Reinforced})\\ f_{k}(\tau)&\text{if }\tau\notin\mathcal{S}_{k}\quad(\text{Frozen})\end{cases} (12)
B265: where R ⁡ ( τ ) R(\tau) is the reward and β \beta is the KL coefficient (temperature of the update).
B266: A.2 Derivation of Partition Function Shift
B267: We first analyze how the normalization constant (partition function) Z k + 1 Z_{k+1} changes relative to Z k Z_{k} . By definition:
B268: Z k + 1 = ∑ τ ∈ 𝒯 exp ⁡ ( f k + 1 ​ ( τ ) ) . Z_{k+1}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\exp(f_{k+1}(\tau)). (13)
