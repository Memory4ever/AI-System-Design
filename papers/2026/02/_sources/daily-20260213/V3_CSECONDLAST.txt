L384: #### Sampled Lyapunov Initialization
L385: Sampled Lyapunov Initialization is a refined strategy to counter depth-dependent stochastic fluctuations. Indeed, the Central Limit Theorem predicts that the logarithm of the activation norm, $\log|X_{\ell}|$, behaves as a Gaussian variable with a standard deviation of $O(\mkern-1.0mu\sqrt{\ell}\mkern-1.0mu)$. This causes the magnitude $|X_{\ell}|$ to fluctuate between $e^{-O(\mkern-1.0mu\sqrt{\ell}\mkern-1.0mu)}$ and $e^{O(\mkern-1.0mu\sqrt{\ell}\mkern-1.0mu)}$ as depth $\ell$ increases.
L386: To counter this, we generate $O(\mkern-1.0mu\sqrt{l}\mkern-1.0mu)$ candidate initializations. Denote by $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{input}}$ the input distribution on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, for example the uniform distribution on the input values. We then select the initialization where the expected output norm $\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{input}}}\!\left[|X_{\ell}|\right]$ is closest to $1$.
L387: We stress that the calculation of $\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{input}}}\!\left[|X_{\ell}|\right]$ should be performed before training and hyperparameter tuning, and is therefore a computationally efficient way to improve Lyapunov initialization.

L402:

L405: We test each of the $7$ initialization methods with $100$ seed values and $12$ hyperparameter-combinations, leading to a total of $8,400$ optimization runs.
L406: We present the median training loss in cite111†Figure 3 , where we observe that all 4 Lyapunov initializations outperform the previous initialization methods. L409: #### Score
L410: 
L411: Learning the score of a probability distribution is the learning task in diffusion models (cite112†Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015 ; cite113†Song et al., 2021 ; cite114†Ho et al., 2020 ).
L412: The quantity of interest is the score $\nabla\ln p$ where $p$ is typically a mixture of Gaussians. In our experiment we learn the score of a mixture of three Gaussians on the 2d interval $[-8,+8]^{2}$ (we depict the resulting vector field in cite115†Figure 6 in the Appendix). We learn the score with a network of width $d=2$, and depth $30$, which is a challenging task and therefore more learning steps are necessary.
L413: We compare He and conventional orthogonal initialization to our sampled Lyapunov methods, testing each method on $8$ hyperparameter configurations and on $15$ initialization samples per hyperparameter and method. We depicted the short time scale ($1,000$ learning steps) in cite116†Figure 4 , and observe that the loss of both sampled Lyapunov methods decrease faster than with existing initialization methods.
L414: Consistently with the polynomial experiment, Sampled Lyapunov Orthogonal is the fastest learning initialization. For more epochs as shown in Appendix cite52†I we observe the following: He performs worst on all timescales and Sampled Lyapunov Orthogonal performs best on all timescales. Moreover, conventional Orthogonal Initialization improves over Sampled Lyapunov Gaussian initialization in the medium timescale, but gets eventually outperformed by Sampled Lyapunov Gaussian in the longer run.
L415: These results are somewhat consistent with the proven benefits of orthogonal over Gaussian initialization as established in (cite70†Saxe et al., 2014 ; cite117†Hu et al., 2020 ).
L416: #### Discussion
L417: Our experiments show that (Sampled) Lyapunov initialization yields quicker, more accurate training for narrow and deep networks than conventional methods. While our theoretical results ensure that by setting the Lyapunov exponent to zero, the network behaves well before training, empirical evidence confirms this initial stability translates into long-term learning advantages. We hypothesize that this long-term efficacy is a consequence of the Law of Large Numbers established in this work.
L418: When the Lyapunov exponent is zero, small weight updates during optimization result in stable changes in the network’s output. This consistency likely creates a smoother loss landscape, allowing optimizers like Adam to navigate the parameter space more effectively. In contrast, traditional approaches such as He initialization yield a negative Lyapunov exponent in narrow, deep architectures, causing activations to vanish rapidly.
L419: In such regimes, the optimizer needs to overcome exponential decay and any small weight fluctuation keep the output near zero. This pressure may lead the algorithm into sharp local optima as it seeks to escape the vanishing activation and gradient zone, ultimately making further optimization significantly more difficult.
L420: ## 6 Conclusion
L421: In this work, we established a rigorous probabilistic analysis of deep unbiased Leaky ReLU networks by proving a Law of Large Numbers and a Central Limit Theorem for the logarithm of network activations. Our results demonstrate that activation growth is governed by the Lyapunov exponent, a parameter that characterizes the sharp phase transition between vanishing and exploding activations.
L422: We revealed that standard initialization methods, such as He or orthogonal initialization, fail to guarantee stability in deep networks of low width because they result in negative Lyapunov exponents. To address this, we proposed Lyapunov initialization, which sets the Lyapunov exponent to zero to ensure maximum stability, alongside a sampled refinement to counter depth-dependent stochastic fluctuations resulting from the central limit theorem.
L423: Empirical experiments confirm that these novel methods outperform previous initialization strategies in low-dimensional regimes, resulting in more efficient learning in deep architectures.
L424: The limit theorems established in this paper only hold for Leaky ReLU activations and fail for other activations such as ReLU or $\tanh$. These remarkable mathematical results could be a reason to prefer Leaky ReLU activation functions when working with deep networks.

L1442: Note that if $Z$ is a symmetric one dimensional random variable (that is $Z$ and $-Z$ have the same distribution), then we have for $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$ that
L1443: 
L1444:  | $$\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z)^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}|Z\geq 0]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\alpha^{2}Z^{2}|Z\leq 0]=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}].$$  |
L1445: Denote as in the rest of the paper by $X_{\ell}$ a neural network as in (cite88†6 ). Assume further that the matrix coefficients $W_{\ell}^{ij}$ are all independent and identically distributed like a fixed symmetric distribution of mean zero and variance $\sigma^{2}$ for some $\sigma>0$. We fix for simplicity $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}$ with $X_{0}=x_{0}$ and write
L1446: 
L1447:  | $$s_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell}^{j}|^{2}].$$  |
L1448: Then it follows that $\sum_{j}W_{\ell}^{ij}X^{j}_{\ell-1}$ is symmetric because the $W_{\ell}^{ij}$ are symmetric and therefore we conclude by using lineary of expecations and independence of the $W_{ij}$ that
L1449: 
L1450:  | $$s_{\ell}=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}(W_{\ell}^{ij}X^{j}_{\ell-1})^{2}\right]=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2}d\sigma^{2}s_{\ell-1}.$$  |
