L258: ### 4.2 From-scratch RL: GSM8K
L259: Next, we consider the GSM8K dataset. We use the Llama-3.1-8B-base model (as opposed to Qwen base models as common in the literature), since Qwen models have been trained on data to improve their math performance, and we wish to understand what is learned through policy optimization algorithms with different weightings/advantages. We performed experiments with $M=16$ completions, learning rate of 1e-6, and batch sizes of 128 and 256.
L260: The base model performs, as expected, relatively poorly initially, only achieving a Pass@1 rate of 1.3%. In this regime, most prompts have no correct completions ($\rho=0$), and dominate the gradient for Plateau-R and Linear-R. We therefore set the advantages of Plateau-R and Linear-R for prompts with success rate $\rho=0$ to $0$ for a warmup period of 50/100 steps for batchsizes 256/128.
L261: Figure cite70†4 shows that Plateau-R slightly outperforms GRPO for both batch-sizes, demonstrating the benefit of asymmetric prompt weighting.

L264: ### 4.3 Post-SFT RL: MATH
L265: Next, we consider the MATH dataset [cite48†9 ] consisting of 12k training examples and 500 test examples. Each task is a math problem of varying difficulty levels (1-5) with an answer that is easy to verify, for example a number or fraction. The binary verifier simply compares the solution suggested by the LLM with the provided solution, being lenient with regards to formatting (e.g., `\frac{13}{21}` is the same as $13/21$).
L266: MATH is frequently used for evaluating GRPO-style algorithms since it provides a challenging set of problems with an automatic and final-answer checker as reward.
L267: We use the Llama-3.2-3b-instruct model rather than models from the Qwen-series (as common in the literature) because, on MATH, Qwen trained with GRPO-style RLVR improves even with random, incorrect, or format-only rewards [cite72†22 ], suggesting the benchmark signal may be confounded by model-specific priors and/or contamination. Thus, using Qwen would make it difficult to attribute performance differences to differences in advantage design.
L268: We choose $M=16$ and our batch-size is 256 (256 distinct math problems and for each $M=16$ completions).
L269: From the test-loss (Pass@1) during training for four epochs in Figure cite73†5 (left) it can be seen that the performance of all advantage estimators is very similar. Starting from a score of $0.38$, all variants achieve scores in the range $0.53-56$. See Figure cite74†7 for the distributions of $\hat{\rho}$ during training with Linear-R. For TinyZero, we saw that Linear-R improves over the other advantage estimators in the second half of training by improving on examples with low values of $\rho$.
L270: For MATH, we don’t see such an improvement which is somewhat expected, considering that at step 90 there are almost no examples with close-to-zero (but not equal to zero) values of $\hat{\rho}$.
L271: Figure 5: Post-SFT RL: Test error (i.e., fraction of correctly solved problems) during reinforcement learning on the MATH dataset (left) and on DAPO math (right). All methods performs similarly well.
L272: ### 4.4 Post-SFT RL: DAPO math
L273: 
L274: Finally, we consider a post-SFT RL setup where we take a model that is already very good at reasoning through supervised finetuning (SFT) on reasoning traces, and improve it further through RL. We choose this setup because models are often SFT’d first and then further improved through RL.
L275: We follow closely the JustRL setup [cite75†8 ] for its simple and clean setup, and so that we can start from well-chosen hyperparameters. We train the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model, and use the DAPO-math dataset (the english subset) [cite45†30 ] for training. The batch size is 256 prompts, and we take $M=8$ samples per prompt. The learning rate is 1e-6 and we perform on-policy training without KL penalty nor clipping.
L276: Figure cite73†5 (right) shows the results: Similar as in Section cite16†4.3 for MATH; all four considered methods perform similarly well, and there is no benefit of up-weighting the difficult prompts (Plateau-R, Linear-R, Sqrt-R), but it also does not hurt performance.
L277: ## 5 A policy-dynamics perspective to weighting
L278: Here, we ask: How do different prompt weights $\omega_{x}$ shape the dynamics of RLVR. To answer this, we study how the success probability evolves over training and how different choices of weight functions $\omega_{x}$ in Equation (cite63†5 ) induce qualitatively different convergence behaviors. We build on the policy-dynamics analysis of [cite76†15 ], extending it using the general reweighting framework of Section cite12†3 to cover a broad family of policy optimization algorithms.
L279: We find that in a low-success regime an asymmetric prompt weighting (Sqrt-R) is best, and otherwise GRPO is better; which supports our empirical finding that asymmetric prompt weightings are best in from-scratch RL setups that start from a low-success regime.
L280: ### 5.1 Theoretical model
L281: 
L282: To isolate the effect of the weighting $\omega_{x}$ on the learning dynamics, we consider an idealized, population-level version of policy optimization objectives for RLVR that captures the core ingredients relevant for dynamics: binary rewards, response-level advantages, and a proximal regularization that prevents overly large policy updates. We consider the following policy optimization per prompt $x$ at each iteration $t$ of the algorithm:
L283:  | $\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{t}(\cdot|x)}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot|x)}A_{y}(\rho_{t-1})-\beta D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi_{t}(\cdot|x)||{\pi_{t-1}(\cdot|x)}\big),$  |  | (7)
L284: where $A_{y}(\rho)=\omega_{x}(\rho)\cdot(r(y)-\rho)$ is the advantage and $\omega_{x}$ the weight function, as throughout (cf. Equation (cite63†5 )). Here, $\pi_{t}$ denotes the policy to be optimized at time $t$ and $\pi_{t-1}$ is the policy of the previous iteration.
L285: Unlike the rest of the paper, here we consider the population limit over generated responses $M\rightarrow\infty$; thus, the advantage $A_{y}(\rho_{t-1})$ of a response $y$ depends on the (population) success rate $\rho_{t-1}$ of the sampling policy $\pi_{t-1}$ at the previous iteration. $D_{\text{KL}}$ is the KL divergence and $\beta>0$ a regularization parameter.
L286: Optimizing directly over the policy distribution, rather than over a parametric representation is standard abstraction in the theoretical RL literature, e.g., [cite77†18 , cite76†15 , cite78†27 , cite79†19 ]; it allows us to focus on how probability mass shifts between correct and incorrect responses over time.
L287: ### 5.2 Success-rate dynamics
L288: 
L289: As shown in Appendix cite33†D.1 by taking the continuous time limit to the solution of (cite80†7 ), the success rate evolves according to the ordinary differential equation (ODE):
L290: 
L291:  | $\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{\beta}\rho_{t}(1-\rho_{t})\cdot\omega(\rho_{t})\,.$  |  | (8)
L292: Thus, the rate of improvement is governed by the uncertainty term $\rho_{t}(1-\rho_{t})$ and the weight $\omega(\rho_{t})$. Different weights emphasize different regions of the success probabilities.
L293: In practical RLVR, each unit of optimization time corresponds to drawing a finite number of rollouts per prompt. When the success rate $\rho$ is small, the dominant cost is not captured by the optimization clock $t$, but rather by the number of rollouts required before observing a successful response that yields a positive reward.
L294: Under a Bernoulli outcome model, the probability of observing at least one success in $M$ rollouts is $1-(1-\rho)^{M}\approx M\rho$ in the low-success regime $\rho\ll 1/M$. Thus, the expected number of rollouts per informative signal scales as $1/(M\rho)$. In this rare-success regime, one unit of population time costs on the order of $g(\rho)=1/\rho$ rollout units. This motivates measuring progress in an effective time variable $\tau$ that upweights time spent at small $\rho$:
L295:  | $\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tau=g(\rho_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad g(\rho)=1/\rho.$  |  | (9)
L296: 
L297: Applying this change of variables to Equation (cite81†8 ), the success rate $\rho_{\tau}$ evolves according to the effective-time ODE:
L298: 
L299:  | $\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{\tau}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{1}{\beta}\,\rho_{\tau}^{2}(1-\rho_{\tau})\cdot\omega(\rho_{\tau}).$  |  | (10)
L300: Figure 6: Comparison of success-probability dynamics $\rho$ governed by the ODE in Eq. (cite81†8 ) (with $\beta=1$) for RLOO, GRPO, Linear-R and Sqrt-R, with their weights normalized to satisfy the same total budget constraint Eq. (cite82†11 ) (with $B=1$). Left: regular time $t$ with initialization $\rho_{0}=0.1$. Right: effective time $\tau$ with initialization $\rho_{0}=0.03$, where $\tau$ accounts for rollout cost in the low-success regime.
L301: As predicted by Propostions cite83†1 and cite84†2 , GRPO is optimal in regular time, whereas Sqrt-R is optimal in effective time in terms of time required to reach target success rate $\rho^{*}=1$. The right and left plot are obtained by plotting the dynamics stated explicitly in Propositions cite85†3 and cite86†4 , using the budget normalization from Lemma cite87†1 in the appendix.
L302: ### 5.3 Optimal weighting
L303: 
L304: Given the dynamics for regular and effective time, we now ask which choice of the weight function $\omega$ leads to the fastest improvement in success probability. We find that for regular time GRPO is optimal, while for effective time Sqrt-R is optimal.
L305: ##### Regular-time optimality of GRPO.
L306: 
L307: Fix prompt $x$ and let its average success probability $\rho_{t}$ at iteration $t$ evolve according to the ODE in Equation (cite81†8 ). Our goal is to minimize the time required for the success rate to reach a target value $\rho_{*}$ from initial value $\rho_{0}$. To exclude trivial solutions, and to ensure a fair normalization across all weight functions, we impose a budget $B$ on the total allowed change of the weights in the interval $[\rho_{0},\rho_{*}]$:
L308:  | $\displaystyle\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\rho_{*}}\omega(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\leq B\,,$  |  | (11)
L309: where we also consider $\omega(\rho)\geq 0$. Without loss of generality, fix $B=1$ onwards. Such a budget constraint allows us to fairly compare among algorithms with different weights by normalizing the total “update budget”. Specifically, Eq. (cite82†11 ) bounds the cumulative magnitude of the per-prompt weighting coefficient $\omega_{x}(\rho)$ used in Equation (cite88†3 ).
L310: While one could alternatively normalize the optimization using the effective weight $\omega(\rho)\cdot\rho(1-\rho)$ that scales the gradient difference $\widehat{\nabla}_{1}-\widehat{\nabla}_{0}$ (see Equation (cite89†4 )), this specific alternative normalization does not qualitatively alter the main conclusion of our analysis: regular-time dynamics favor symmetric weightings, whereas effective-time dynamics in the rare-success regime favor asymmetric weightings.
L311: ###### Proposition 1.
L312: Suppose the success rate $\rho_{t}$ of a fixed prompt evolves as in Equation (cite81†8 ) with initialization $\rho_{0}$. Let $T(\rho_{0},\rho_{*};\omega)$ be the time required so that $\rho_{t}=\rho_{*}$ for target success rate $\rho_{*}>\rho_{0}$. The non-negative weight $\omega:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ that minimizes $T$ subject to the budget constraint (cite82†11 ) with $B=1$ is: $\omega_{\text{opt}}(\rho)\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}},\quad\text{for }\rho\in[\rho_{0},\rho_{*}].$
L313: Here, time $T$ is the continuous analogue to the number of non-zero gradient updates applied to the model. The proposition gives an optimality interpretation to GRPO’s weighting as the weighting minimizing the optimization clock, i.e., the number of updates to reach a target success rate, when all weight functions are normalized to satisfy the same budget constraint.
L314: Figure cite90†6 (left) visualizes this optimality result, showing the evolution of the success rate for Linear-R, Sqrt-R, RLOO, and GRPO with weights normalized to satisfy the budget constraint for $\rho_{*}=1$ (see Proposition cite85†3 for analytical expressions). As predicted, GRPO reaches the target $\rho_{*}=1$ first. However, the gain over other algorithms is modest, and asymmetric weights, while not optimal, perform comparably.
L315: This is consistent with our Post-SFT experimental findings where all methods achieved similar performance.
L316: ##### Effective time-optimality of Sqrt-R in low-success regimes.
L317: 
L318: We now characterize the weight function that minimizes the effective time to reach a target success rate in effective time. As before, for fair comparison across different weights, we normalize by imposing the same budget constraint (cite82†11 ) on the total accumulated weight.
L319: ###### Proposition 2.
L320: 
L321: Suppose the success rate $\rho_{t}$ of a fixed prompt evolves as in Equation (cite81†8 ) with initialization $\rho_{0}\in(0,1)$ and target $\rho_{*}>\rho_{0}$. For $g(\rho)$ given in Equation (cite91†9 ), define effective time-to-target as
L322: 
L323:  | $$T_{g}(\rho_{0},\rho_{*};\omega):=\int_{\rho_{0}}^{T}g(\rho_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\text{where }\rho_{T}=\rho_{*}.$$  |
L324: Under the budget constraint (cite82†11 ) with $B=1$, the non-negative weight function minimizing $T_{g}$ over $[\rho_{0},\rho_{*}]$ is
L325: 
L326:  | $\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)\propto\frac{1}{\rho\sqrt{1-\rho}}.$  |  | (12)
L327: Propositions cite83†1 and cite84†2 optimize the same population dynamics (cite81†8 ) but under different notions of time. The optimization time $t$ yields the GRPO weighting whereas the rollout-cost-adjusted effective time (cite91†9 ), which more accurately reflects computational costs when $\rho$ is small, yields the weight (cite92†12 ). This is exactly the weight of our Sqrt-R. Also, it behaves as Linear-R’s $1/\rho$ weight in the low-success regime.
L328: Figure cite90†6 (right) visualizes this optimality under budget normalization, showing that Sqrt-R reaches $\rho_{*}=1$ significantly faster than all other algorithms in effective time, with Linear-R also substantially outperforming GRPO and RLOO (see Proposition cite86†4 in the appendix for analytical expressions).
L329: This distinction is practically relevant: from-scratch RL often operates for a substantial period in a low-success regime where successful rollouts are scarce, making effective time the more appropriate metric. In these settings, both our theory and experiments yield that Sqrt-R and Linear-R outperform the strong GRPO baseline.
L330: ## 6 Discussion and limitation
L331: 
L332: In this paper, we have demonstrated that asymmetric prompt weightings can be beneficial, in particular for from-scratch RLVR.
L333: While our analysis focuses on binary rewards, the proposed weightings and advantage estimators extend naturally to rewards in $[0,1]$ by taking $\hat{\rho}$ to be the empirical mean reward. Several directions merit further study: asymmetric weighting beyond binary rewards; applications to training agentic systems; and scaling to larger models and datasets. Our theory also suggests allocating more rollouts when $\rho$ is close to $0$.
L334: While interesting, we were not able to pursue these directions due to compute limitations (each reported run in this paper already costs several hundreds of H100 hours).
