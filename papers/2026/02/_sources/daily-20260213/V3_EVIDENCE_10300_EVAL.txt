L181: After excluding runs that are unstable, non-converged, or accidentally terminated (details in cite34†Section A.1 ), we obtain the Marin dataset and StepLaw dataset of 2,549 and 2,581 training logs respectively. We designate all runs with model sizes larger than 430M parameters as an out-of-distribution (OOD) validation set. The remaining runs are split into training and in-distribution (ID) validation sets with an 8:2 ratio.
L182: To make the splits between training set and ID validation set more meaningful and challenging, we randomly split on the level of (optimizer, model size, data size) tuples. Concretely, we first group runs that share the same $(\text{optimizer},N,D)$ tuple, and then randomly assign each group to either the training set or the ID validation set.
L183: This strategy makes sure that every run in the ID validation set (or the OOD validation set) does not have another run with the same optimizer, model size, data size in the training dataset. In total, the dataset comprises 3,225 training runs, 796 ID validation runs, and 1,109 OOD validation runs. ^{5}^{5} 5 The dataset is available at cite85†https://huggingface.co/datasets/zhqwqwq/NCPL-Pretraining-Logs†huggingface.co .

L193: ### 4.2 Main results
L194: #### Configuration-dependent final loss prediction
L195: We first examine NCPL’s ability to predict the final pretraining loss based on the full training configuration. In cite68†Figure 1 Left and cite87†Figure 3 , each point shows the predicted versus ground-truth final pretraining loss for a single training run of a particular training configuration. NCPL takes the full training configuration of each run as the input, whereas the Chinchilla Law baseline yields a single configuration-agnostic prediction for each model-data size pair.
L196: Consequently, NCPL aligns much more closely with the ground truth, achieving lower prediction errors and higher Spearman correlations than the Chinchilla Law baseline on both ID and OOD validation sets, as summarized in cite78†Table 1 .
L197: Fig cite88†4 further visualizes fine-grained predictions over learning rate and batch size sweeps for held-out in-distribution model-data size pairs from the StepLaw dataset. NCPL closely tracks the ground-truth U-shaped loss profiles across hyperparameters, illustrating its ability to learn how training hyperparameters affect the final loss.
L198: #### Hyperparameter selection.
L199: 
L200: Hyperparameter selection plays a crucial role in large-scale language model pretraining and is a main application of modeling Configuration-to-Performance scaling laws. With the learned NCPL, one can sweep over candidate training configurations and use NCPL’s estimated loss as a proxy for the true pretraining loss, thereby identifying optimal hyperparameters without performing expensive training sweeps (see cite17†Section 3.3 ).
L201: In this section, we evaluate this capability on held-out ID and OOD model-data size pairs from the StepLaw dataset. We sweep over NCPL’s predictions to select the optimal learning rate and batch size, and compare its predictions with the power-law scaling rule proposed in cite58†Li et al. [49] (cite86†Equation 2 ). As shown in Figure cite89†5 , the hyperparameters predicted by NCPL closely match the true optima obtained from exhaustive sweeps, in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings.
L202: The relative losses are comparable to that of the fitted power-law baseline. Additional results are provided in cite44†Section B.1 .
L203: We observe that, in the out-of-distribution setting, NCPLtends to suggest larger learning rates, especially for model-data pairs that are farther from the training distribution (e.g., Figure cite90†5(b) , $N{=}1073$M, $D{=}57$B). We hypothesize that this behavior is because NCPL biases towards its training set of small models which use larger learning rates, and discuss it further in Section cite31†6 .
L204: Figure 6: NCPL learns interactions between weight decay and optimizer choice. It’s known that Lion requires substantially larger weight decay than AdamW [cite91†12 , cite83†56 , cite81†76 ]. NCPL predicts this phenomenon on the OOD validation sets (Marin Dataset, $N$=520M, $D$=10B).

L207: #### Characterizing interactions between training configurations
L208: Different hyperparameters in the training configuration do not affect final pretraining loss independently; instead, their effects can interact in complicated ways. For example, different optimizers often prefer different hyperparameter choices (e.g., cite93†Liu et al. [55] , cite81†Wen et al. [76] , cite94†Marek et al. [61] ). By learning a mapping from full training configurations to pretraining performance, we expect NCPL to learn such interactions from large-scale pretraining logs.
L209: Here we illustrate this potential with a concrete example. The Lion optimizer [cite91†12 ] is known to require substantially larger weight decay than AdamW [cite83†56 , cite81†76 ]. As shown in cite95†Figure 7 , when using larger weight decay (e.g., $\geq 0.4$), AdamW’s performance deteriorates markedly, whereas Lion improves. NCPL learns this interaction from the training set and generalizes it to the OOD setting.
L210: Table 1: Comparison of various versions of NCPL, XGBoost, and Chinchilla-law baselines for final-loss prediction and loss-curve prediction on both ID and OOD splits. For NCPL, we ablate fine-tuning (ft) versus training from scratch (scratch) with two backbone sizes (1.7B and 135M). We report mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and Spearman correlation ($\rho$).
L211: Overall, NCPL achieves substantially lower error and higher rank correlation than the Chinchilla-law by leveraging full training configurations. On StepLaw dataset, where only a small set of hyperparameters vary (learning rate and batch size), NCPL (training from scratch) are competitive with NCPL with fine-tuning; in contrast, on Marin dataset, which has more diverse configurations, NCPL with fine-tuning provides a clear advantage over other variants and baselines.

L226: #### NCPL for loss curve prediction
L227: Beyond accepting the full training configuration as inputs, the generality of NCPL also allows us to target other metrics beyond final pretraining loss. We train a variant of NCPL to perform loss curve prediction. Specifically, we train NCPL to predict the pretraining loss at a specified intermediate training step, and reconstruct the loss curve by querying losses at multiple intermediate steps. cite95†Figure 7 demonstrates the true loss curves and NCPL’s predictions from the Marin Dataset.
L228: NCPL accurately predicts the loss curve under different hyperparameter choices on both ID and OOD generalization settings. This task previously required hand-designing complex multi-component power laws [cite53†59 , cite54†72 ], and we show that the shapes of loss curves can be learned directly from data. This also resonates with the recent finding on scaling collapse [cite96†66 ], which suggests that the shapes of loss curves are the same across scales up to an affine transformation.
L229: Moreover, NCPL can also predict loss curves for different optimizers; see cite68†Figure 1 and cite92†Figure 15 .
L230: ### 4.3 Ablation on the Backbone Architecture of NCPL
L231: 
L232: In this section, we ablate alternative instantiations of CPL, including both NCPL and non-neural predictors. Results are reported in Table cite78†1 . We compare NCPL with XGBoost [cite97†11 ] and Chinchilla Law [cite52†28 ] baselines on final-loss prediction and loss curve prediction tasks. For NCPL, we further ablate fine-tuning versus training from scratch with different model sizes, using the Qwen3-1.7B [cite67†79 ] and SmolLM2-135M [cite98†3 ] architectures.
L233: NCPL achieves substantially lower prediction error and higher Spearman correlation than the configuration-agnostic Chinchilla-law baseline by leveraging the full training configuration, rather than only $(N,D)$. On the StepLaw dataset, where the configuration variation is restricted to a small set of continuous factors ($N$, $D$, learning rate, and batch size), training from scratch can outperform fine-tuning, and XGBoost achieves comparable performance.
L234: In contrast, on the Marin dataset, where many heterogeneous fields vary jointly (e.g., optimizer choices and a wide range of hyperparameters), fine-tuning yields a clear advantage over both training from scratch and XGBoost. Therefore, we adopt the fine-tuned NCPL as our main method.
L235: ## 5 Related work
L236: #### Scaling law.
L237: cite51†Kaplan et al. [39] , cite52†Hoffmann et al. [28] showed the pretraining loss of the Transformer-based LLMs follows a power-law relation with the model size and data size. Subsequent work explores refined fitting protocols and functional forms [cite57†65 , cite99†48 , cite100†10 ], different model architectures [cite101†58 , cite72†74 ], incorporating other training hyperparameters such as learning rate [cite54†72 , cite55†78 , cite53†59 , cite69†46 ] and loss curve prediction [cite53†59 , cite54†72 , cite96†66 ].
L238: Scaling law is also applied to broader domains and settings, including multimodal models [cite102†26 ] , hyperparameter optimization [cite103†38 , cite104†25 ], data mixture [cite105†34 ], adversarial attacks [cite106†53 ], transfer learning[cite107†27 ], etc. Recent works extends scaling laws to model downstream tasks performance rather than the pretraining loss while some other works point out practical challenges [cite108†19 , cite109†69 , cite110†6 , cite111†40 , cite112†57 ].
L239: Scaling laws are also extended to predict the optimal hyperparameters with the scale of training resources [cite56†15 , cite58†49 , cite113†5 , cite70†7 , cite94†61 , cite114†47 , cite73†83 ]. However, these approaches fit only a small subset of hyperparameters, making it difficult to ensure that the remaining hyperparameters stay optimal during fitting or extrapolation; moreover, they do not model non-parametric configuration choices such as the optimizers.
L240: Recently, cite115†Lin et al. [51] proposes to use advanced LLMs in a scaffold to propose the functional form of scaling laws under different setups. However, identifying the correct functional form mapping from the entire configuration $\mathrm{C}$ to performance $\mathrm{P}$ can be extremely difficult due to the complicated interactions of different factors. We adopt a different methodology to directly use LLM as the regressor to predict the performance from training configurations.
L241: #### Theory-motivated studies of hyperparameter effects and transfer.
L242: As complement to data-driven scaling-law fitting, an orthogonal approach studies the effect of hyperparameters and its transfer across training scales through theoretical lens. Using SDEs as a proxy for stochastic gradient optimization, prior work has proposed scaling rules for how the learning rate should grow with batch size [cite116†35 , cite117†60 ].
L243: The critical batch size, beyond which large-batch training stops yielding proportional efficiency gains, can be characterized via the gradient-noise-scale proxy [cite64†63 ], and its scaling behavior has been further analyzed under simplified assumptions [cite63†82 ]. cite118†Wang and Aitchison [75] interpret model weights as an exponential moving average of recent updates, and use this view to derive practical scaling rules for weight decay as model and data size grow.
L244: $\mu$P tackles how optimal learning rate can be transferred from smaller to larger models [cite60†80 ]. Later works extended from the original width scaling setup to depth scaling and other architectural variants [cite119†9 , cite120†16 , cite61†8 , cite59†17 ]. However, $\mu P$ primarily focuses on model scaling and does not directly address learning-rate transfer under data scaling, and offers limited guidance for hyperparameters beyond the learning rate.
L245: Recent work argues weight decay plays an important role in stabilizing the update dynamics and thus facilitating learning rate transfer [cite121†44 ], and propose joint transfer principle of learning rate and weight decay [cite122†18 ]. While these works offer valuable insights into how hyperparameters influence pretraining performance and provide practical tuning and transfer guidelines, they typically address only a limited subset of hyperparameters.
L246: #### Foundation models as regressors.
L247: Transformer-based foundation models have demonstrated strong capability in modeling complex input-output relationships beyond natural language processing. Recent work has shown that pretrained language models can be used as general-purpose regressors by direct prompting pretrained models [cite123†73 ] or through supervised learning [cite124†20 , cite125†29 , cite126†33 ].
L248: Compared to classical regression approaches, foundation models offer several key advantages, including leveraging the semantic meaning of features to perform feature selection [cite127†36 ], enabling large-scale pretraining on diverse data sources [cite128†30 ], and supporting online adaptation fine-tuning [cite129†71 ]. At the same time, recent studies have highlighted potential limitations, including sensitivity to data representation that are irrelevant to the underlying learning task [cite130†54 ].
L249: Foundation models as regressors have been explored in a wide range of domains, for instance, time-series forecasting [cite131†23 , cite132†37 , cite133†14 , cite134†22 ] and performance prediction of complex engineered systems [cite79†2 ]. Our work focuses on predicting the pretraining outcomes of LLMs from full training configurations. To the best of our knowledge, this setting has not been systematically studied in prior work.
L250: #### Learning-curve extrapolation and hyperparameter optimization.
L251: Beyond fitting parametric scaling laws, another line of work aims to predict the training trajectories (learning curves) from partially-observed training trajectories, enabling early stopping and grey-box resource allocation in hyperparameter optimization.
L252: Early neural and probabilistic approaches include Bayesian neural networks with specialized learning-curve parameterizations [cite135†43 ] and probabilistic rollouts for learning-curve extrapolation across hyperparameter settings using models such as Bayesian RNNs [cite136†21 ]. Ranking-based formulations further learn to rank partially observed curves to guide early termination [cite137†77 ].
L253: More recently, transformer-based Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) enable fast approximate Bayesian learning-curve extrapolation in a single forward pass [cite138†1 ], and extend naturally to freeze-thaw Bayesian optimization via in-context surrogates [cite139†68 ]. These PFN surrogates can also be specialized to optimizer hyperparameters, e.g., Adam tuning [cite140†4 ]. In parallel, Deep Power Laws exploit power-law structure for grey-box HPO and budget allocation [cite103†38 ]. A very recent work cite141†Hu et al.
L254: [31] tries to train a language model to predict downstream performance with the entire accuracy trajectories from a partial run, using token-level validation loss as input.
L255: ## 6 Conclusion and Discussion
L256: In this work, we propose to learn the mapping from full training configurations to training outcomes using generic neural networks, by training on large-scale and diverse open-source pretraining logs. We instantiate this idea by fine-tuning a pretrained language model (Qwen3-1.7B [cite67†79 ]), resulting in the Neural Configuration–Performance Scaling Law (NCPL).
L257: Empirically, NCPL accurately predicts how configuration choices affect final pretraining performance, supports joint hyperparameter tuning and loss-curve prediction, and qualitatively reveals interaction effects among configurations.
L258: Despite these promising results, our current study should be viewed as a proof of concept due to the limited accessibility of open-source pretraining logs. In particular, our training dataset only includes models with up to 430M parameters and OOD validation set only includes models with at most 1.2B parameters. Moreover, the diversity of the configurations in the pretraining logs are limited.
L259: For example, in our collected open-source pretraining logs, for AdamW, hyperparameter $\beta_{1}$, $\beta_{2}$, and $\epsilon$ are rarely tuned, making it difficult to reliably learn their effects and interactions from the logs; also, hyperparameters are swept over only a few discrete values, making predictions on the unseen values less reliable.
L260: Another example is that the pretraining logs do not have any MoE models or models with linear attention [cite142†13 , cite143†42 , cite144†81 ], and only contain two choices of pre-training datasets [cite145†50 ]
L261: Looking ahead, we expect NCPL-style predictors to improve with the community’s collective efforts to open-source pretraining experiments spanning diverse setups. Continuously incorporating new data should ultimately enable more reliable prediction for large-scale pretraining.
