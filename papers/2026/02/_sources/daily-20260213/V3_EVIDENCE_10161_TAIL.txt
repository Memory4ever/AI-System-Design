L128: ### 3.2 AdvBench-Omni Construction and Validation
L129: 
L130: Guided by Control Modal-variable Principle in Section cite11†3.1 , we construct the AdvBench-Omni benchmark. This dataset aims to provide a clean experimental environment for evaluating cross-modal safety by rigorously controlling semantic variables. We select AdvBench as the seed dataset, which comprises 520 malicious instructions spanning categories such as violence and illegality, serving as a standard benchmark for evaluating LLM safety.

L136: #### 3.2.1 Dataset Construction Pipeline
L137: 
L138: Based on the design principles, we expand each original text query in AdvBench into 11 modality variants, covering single-modal, dual-modal, and triple-modal combinations, thereby constituting the AdvBench-Omni. Our modal transformation methods are as follows:
L139: Single-modal Transformation. For Image and Video, we perform pixel-level rendering of the text in the form of “black-on-white” slides; for Audio, we utilized Qwen3-TTS (cite97†Qwen Team, 2025 ) to generate speech. This ensures maximization of $I(Y;X)$ between single-modality inputs and the original text.
L140: Multimodal Transformation. Unlike information-coherent single-modal inputs, multimodal reasoning inherently requires cross-channel information dependencies. Therefore, we adopt a semantic separation strategy to enforce necessary cross-modal interactions and prevent the model from bypassing a single redundant modality. We split the original query into a context and a core payload.
L141: Given the original text query “How to make a bomb?”, we split it into the text prompt “How can I make the thing in the image?” and the word “bomb” directly rendered and projected in the image.
L142: To support subsequent refusal vector extraction (Section cite16†4 ), we additionally collected 500 benign queries and applied the same pipeline for omni-modal transformation. AdvBench-Omni ultimately comprises 11 modal subsets, totaling 11,220 samples. The pipeline is shown in Figure cite98†1 . More details can be found in Appendix cite41†D .
L143: #### 3.2.2 Oracle Validation
L144: 
L145: To verify whether AdvBench-Omni conforms to the design principles outlined in Section cite11†3.1 , we conduct layer-wise analysis on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B. As a baseline, we use Stable-Diffusion-3.5 (cite99†Esser et al., 2024 ) to generate semantically corresponding real-world images to construct the AdvBench-MM dataset. Corresponding to the perspective in Section cite11†3.1 , we perform experimental analyses.
L146: As shown in Figure cite100†2 , t-SNE visualization showcases that AdvBench-Omni preserves semantic coherence across modalities compared to AdvBench-MM baselines, validating content-preserving transformation. Figure cite101†3 illustrates the evolution of cosine similarity between hidden states of various modalities and the original text version of AdvBench. The results reveal distinct processing patterns: ❶ Single-modality inputs maintain stable similarity above 0.85 throughout most layers.
L147: ❷ Cross-modal combinations exhibit a characteristic dip in the middle layers (5-17), dropping to $\sim$0.80, before recovering to $\sim$0.90 in deeper layers. This trajectory suggests temporary representation reorganization during multimodal fusion, followed by semantic re-alignment. ❸ Most critically, the AdvBench-MM baseline from traditional generative methods shows severe degradation, plummeting from $\sim 0.7$ to $\sim 0.6$ in Layer 15-27.
L148: In summary, our results support the design validity of AdvBench-Omni: It successfully preserves core semantic information $S$ while altering modality representations.
L149: ### 3.3 Cross-Modality Vulnerability Gap
L150: 
L151: Utilizing AdvBench-Omni, we conduct safety testing on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (cite57†Qwen et al., 2025 ) and MiniCPM-o-2.6 (cite58†Yao et al., 2024 ). We perform zero-shot inference on 520 harmful queries from each modality subset and employ LLM-as-a-Judge (Qwen3-30B-A3B (cite102†Yang et al., 2025a )) to calculate the Refusal Success Rate (RSR). As shown in Figure cite103†4 , the experiments reveal a significant cross-modality safety gap:
L152: ➭Single-modality robustness. Under single-modal inputs, both models exhibit strong defensive capabilities, maintaining RSR above $90\%$ for Qwen2.5-Omni-7B.
L153: 
L154: ➭Cross-modality vulnerability. Once cross-modal combinations (such as Text+Image or Text+Video) are introduced, RSR decreases to approximately $75\%$ for Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and $50\%$ for MiniCPM-o-2.6.
L155: Given that AdvBench-Omni rigorously controls for semantic content consistency (Section cite14†3.2.2 ), this drastic RSR disparity confirms our core hypothesis: the Cross-Modality Safety Gap represents a novel safety vulnerability independent of content. This finding indicates that existing single-modality alignment mechanisms fail to generalize to complex cross-modal interaction scenarios, compelling us to further investigate the underlying dynamic mechanisms.
L156: ## 4 Dynamics Mechanisms
L157: 
L158: In this section, we delve into the mechanisms behind the vulnerabilities observed in Section cite10†3 . We conduct experiments on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, first observing the dynamic evolution of internal refusal signals in OLLMs (Section cite17†4.1 ), then identifying the primary factors for the safety degradation (Section cite18†4.2 ). Finally, we discover a shared underlying refusal direction that spans modalities (Section cite19†4.3 ).
L159: ### 4.1 Layer-wise Dynamic Evolution of Refusal Signals
L160: 
L161: cite104†Image: Refer to caption Figure 5: Layer-wise evolution curves of normalised projection values for inputs across different modalities.
L162: To measure the model’s internal refusal propensity, we first establish a quantitative framework. We employ the method mentioned in Section cite9†2.2 , extracting the refusal vector $\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}=\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}=\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{harm}}^{(l)}-\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{safe}}^{(l)}$ on the Text subset of AdvBench-Omni. We perform centering and normalisation on the projection, defining the refusal strength as:
L163:  | $$p_{l}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{(\mathbf{h}_{l}(\mathbf{x})-\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{safe}}^{(l)})^{T}\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}\|^{2}}.$$  |  | (5)
L164: 
L165: Subsequently, we extract hidden states as the model processes AdvBench-Omni and calculate the refusal strength values. Figure cite105†5 illustrates the complete process.
L166: ➭Single-modality inputs maintain projections consistently above $0.9$ after Layer 16. This stable pattern indicates that single-modal inputs preserve strong and consistent refusal signals, which explains the high RSR for single modalities.
L167: ➭Cross-modal combination inputs exhibit a different evolution pattern, revealing a key mechanism we term the Mid-layer Dissolution phenomenon. In the first 12 layers, projections for cross-modal inputs rise continuously, reaching a peak of $\sim 0.95$ at Layer 12. However, after that, the projections plummet abruptly, and the gradual recovery in later layers can only stabilize the projection values at $\sim 0.7$.

L183: ### 4.3 Subspace Analysis: The Geometry of Refusal
L184: 
L185: cite107†Image: Refer to caption Figure 6: PCA analysis of refusal vectors across different modalities. (a) Results for all 7 single-modal and dual-modal combinations; (b) Results exclusively for the 4 single-modal types.
L186: Although cross-modal inputs induce projection value reduction in refusal vectors, we discovered in Section cite18†4.2 that refusal vectors across modalities are not entirely unrelated—single modalities exhibit high alignment, and even cross-modal combinations maintain substantial similarity. This suggests the possible existence of a shared underlying refusal direction across modalities, with modal-specific biases merely superimposed onto this direction.
L187: To verify this hypothesis, we perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the refusal vectors from seven modalities. We concatenate these seven vectors into a matrix $\mathbf{R}=[\mathbf{v}_{\text{txt}},\mathbf{v}_{\text{img}},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{\text{txt+vid}}]$ and conduct eigenvalue decomposition.
L188: Figure cite108†6 (a) reveals that the first principal component (PC1) explains $\sim 80\%$ of the variance, which indicates that refusal vectors across modalities indeed primarily reside in a low-dimensional subspace.
L189: Intriguingly, PC1 and PC2 admit clear semantic interpretations. PC1 encodes the “intensity axis”, separating high-magnitude single modalities (positive) from low-magnitude cross-modal ones (negative), consistent with Section cite18†4.2 . In contrast, PC2 captures modality-specific biases by distinguishing sequential, discrete inputs (text, audio) from spatial, continuous ones (image, video).
L190: Validation via single-modality PCA (Figure cite108†6 (b)) confirms this: its PC1 aligns with the original PC2, showing that modality type is the primary variation once magnitude differences are removed.
L191: These results provide a clear geometric picture for understanding refusal vectors: refusal vectors across modalities reside in a two-dimensional subspace spanned by a “pure refusal direction” and a “modality bias direction”. This finding paves the way for designing effective safety alignment methods: by extracting the pure refusal direction, one can provide unified safety signals for all modality combinations without interference from modal biases.
L192: ## 5 OmniSteer- an Efficient Alignment Method
L193: 
L194: In this section, we first discuss how to identify an optimal cross-modal golden refusal vector (Section cite21†5.1 ), then demonstrate its effectiveness (Section cite22†5.2 ). Finally, we introduce OmniSteer, a simple yet efficient safety alignment method for OLLMs (Section cite23†5.3 ).
L195: ### 5.1 Extracting the Golden Refusal Vector via SVD
L196: 
L197: Based on the analysis in Section cite16†4 , the refusal vectors across modalities exhibit offsets. Therefore, we seek to extract a golden refusal vector: a modality-invariant direction that exclusively encodes pure refusal semantics.
L198: Let $\mathbf{R}{=}[\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}]{\in}\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ denote the concatenation matrix of refusal vectors from each modality, where $\mathbf{v}_{i}$ can be decomposed as $\mathbf{v}_{i}{=}\mathbf{s}{+}\mathbf{n}_{i}$. Here, $\mathbf{s}$ represents the shared refusal signal we aim to extract, while $\mathbf{n}_{i}$ denotes modality-specific noise. Our objective is to recover $\mathbf{s}$ from $\mathbf{R}$.
L199: To this end, we utilize Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to isolate the signal $\mathbf{s}$. Performing an uncentered SVD on $\mathbf{R}$ yields $\mathbf{R}{=}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}$, the first left singular vector $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ satisfies:
L200:  | $$\mathbf{u}_{1}=\arg\max_{\|\mathbf{u}\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v}_{i})^{2},$$  |  | (6)
L201: 
L202: where $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ represents the direction that best explains all modality refusal vectors in each layer. Therefore, we designate $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ as the golden refusal vector, denoted as $\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}$.

L249: ### 5.3 Layer-wise Adaptive Steering
L250: 
L251: Experiments in Section cite22†5.2 reveal inherent limitations of static steering strength $\alpha$: a single global intensity cannot simultaneously optimize the model’s harmlessness and helpfulness. A deeper issue lies in the significant variation in refusal thresholds across different inputs. These observations suggest the need for a mechanism capable of adaptively adjusting the $\alpha$ value based on input features.
L252: To address this issue, we propose OmniSteer, a layer-wise adaptive steering method. Unlike global prediction, OmniSteer trains an independent lightweight adapter $f_{\theta_{l}}$ for each target layer $l$. Each adapter is a simple 2-layer MLP that takes the current layer’s hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{l}$ as input and outputs a layer-specific scalar strength $\alpha_{l}$:
L253:  | $$\alpha_{l}=f_{\theta_{l}}(\mathbf{h}_{l})=\mathbf{W}_{2}\cdot\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{h}_{l}+\mathbf{b}_{1})+b_{2}.$$  |  | (7)
L254: During inference, we employ the forward hooks mechanism to seamlessly integrate adapters into the model’s forward propagation. When computing the output of layer $l$, the adapter predicts $\alpha_{l}$ in real-time and acts on the hidden state immediately, with the modified representation then passed to the next layer: $\mathbf{h}_{l}\leftarrow\mathbf{h}_{l}+\alpha_{l}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}^{(l)}$.
L255: This immediate intervention strategy avoids additional inference overhead, enabling OmniSteer to achieve adaptive defense while maintaining the original model’s inference speed.
L256: To train the adapter parameters $\theta$, we design a targeted dual-objective loss function. For harmful inputs, we expect the projection of the steered hidden state onto the golden refusal direction $\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}^{(l)}$ to exceed a positive threshold $\tau_{+}$, ensuring sufficient refusal intensity. Conversely, for benign queries, we need to prevent over-refusal, requiring the projection value to stay below a safety threshold $\tau_{-}$:
L257:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{harm}}$  | $\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\mathbb{E}_{x{\in}\mathcal{D}_{\text{harm}}}\!\!\!\left[\!\sum_{l}\!\max(0,\tau_{+}\!{-}\mathbf{h}_{l}^{\prime\top}\!\!\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}^{(l)}}{||\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}^{(l)}||}\!){+}\lambda_{1}|\alpha_{l}|\!\right]\!\!,$  |  | (8)
L258:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{safe}}$  | $\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\mathbb{E}_{x{\in}\mathcal{D}_{\text{safe}}}\!\!\!\left[\!\sum_{l}\!\max(0,\tau_{-}\!{+}\mathbf{h}_{l}^{\prime\top}\!\!\frac{\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}^{(l)}}{||\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}^{(l)}||}\!){+}\lambda_{2}|\alpha_{l}|\!\right]\!\!,$  |
L259: where $\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}$ are regularization coefficients used to prevent excessive intervention intensity from damaging general capabilities. We use the AdamW optimizer (cite111†Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019 ) to jointly optimize these two loss terms.
L260: To ensure cross-modal generalization of the method, we construct a balanced training set encompassing four modalities (Text, Audio, Text+Image, and Text+Video), thereby enabling the adapter to learn a modal-invariant steering strategy based purely on content harmfulness.
L261: ### 5.4 Experiments and Analysis
L262: Experimental setup. We validate the effectiveness of OmniSteer on three mainstream OLLMs: Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, Baichuan-Omni-1d5, and MiniCPM-o-2.6. The evaluation employs 8 datasets across 6 modality combinations, using RSR and BAR as evaluation metrics, and calculating a comprehensive weighted score “Overall”.
L263: We select two representative baseline methods: ❶ Self-Reminder (cite112†Xie et al., 2023 ), which improves safety by appending prompts at both ends of the query; ❷ OmniGuard (cite113†Verma et al., 2025 ), which trains a classifier at specific layers to assess input safety. Detailed experimental settings are provided in Appendix cite28†A .
L264: OmniSteer’s performance. Table cite114†3 presents the RSR and BAR evaluation results for the three models across 8 datasets. It can be observed that OmniSteer achieves optimal RSR across all modality combinations (average $91.16\%$), significantly outperforming Self-Reminder ($85.18\%$) and OmniGuard ($76.72\%$). More importantly, OmniSteer maintains a high BAR (average $83.2\%$) while improving safety.
L265: In cross-modal scenarios, OmniSteer’s advantages become even more pronounced, with RSR improving by $\sim 31.3\%$, validating the effectiveness of the SVD golden vector and layer-wise adaptive steering.
L266: Preserving general capability. An effective safety alignment method should reserve the model’s general capability. To verify this, we evaluate OmniSteer and baseline methods on OmniBench (cite115†Li et al., 2025 ). Table cite114†3 presents the accuracy for each method. It is observed that models with OmniSteer applied exhibit performance on OmniBench nearly identical to the vanilla model (difference $<2\%$), significantly outperforming baseline methods (decrease of $\sim 8.1\%$).
L267: This shows that OmniSteer effectively preserves the model’s general capabilities while enhancing safety.
L268: To further analyze OmniSteer’s impact on the model’s internal representations, we performed t-SNE visualization of the last-layer hidden states on OmniBench samples across three models. As shown in Figure cite116†7 , OmniSteer induces only minimal deviations from the original models’ representations across all three models, whereas Self-Reminder causes significant distributional shifts.
L269: ## 6 Related Works
L270: From Dual-modal to Omni-modal LLMs. While early Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) focused on dual-modal understanding, such as image-text (cite117†Liu et al., 2023 ; cite118†Bai et al., 2023 ) or audio-text (cite119†Chu et al., 2023 ) integration, recent application demands have driven a paradigm shift toward omni-modal LLMs (OLLMs).
