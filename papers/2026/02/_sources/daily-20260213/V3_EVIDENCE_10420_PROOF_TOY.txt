L340: ## Appendix A Proof of Theorem cite70†IV.4 L341: 
L342: In this section, we provide the formal derivation of the mismatch singularity across different signal manifolds.
L343: 
L344: See cite70†IV.4 L345: ###### Proof.
L346: 
L347: The second moment of the stochastic gradient for the mismatched objective is:
L348: 
L349:  | $$\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\theta)\|^{2}]=\frac{4}{(1-t)^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}}\left[\|(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}-\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{\theta}\|^{2}\right].$$  |  | (12)
L350: 
L351: Applying the non-degeneracy condition (Assum. cite104†IV.2 ), we have:
L352: 
L353:  | $$\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}(\theta)\|^{2}]\geq\frac{4c}{(1-t)^{4}}R(t).$$  |  | (13)
L354: The optimization stability is determined by the integral $\mathcal{I}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|^{2}]dt$.
L355: #### Continuous Correlated Case.
L356: 
L357: Let $\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma})$. The Bayes-optimal residual $R^{*}(t)$ is:
L358: 
L359:  | $$R^{*}(t)=\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}-t^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}(t^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}+(1-t)^{2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\right).$$  |  | (14)
L360: 
L361: As $t\to 1$, using the matrix expansion $(I+\epsilon\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})^{-1}\approx I-\epsilon\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}$ with $\epsilon=\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{t^{2}}$:
L362:  | $$\begin{split}R^{*}(t)\approx\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{I}-\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{t^{2}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})\right)\\
L363: =\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{t^{2}}\text{Tr}(\mathbf{I})=\frac{D(1-t)^{2}}{t^{2}}.\end{split}$$  |  | (15)
L364: 
L365: Substituting $R(t)=\mathcal{O}((1-t)^{2})$ into the integral:
L366:  | $$\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{cont}&\geq\int_{t^{*}}^{1}\frac{4c\cdot D(1-t)^{2}}{(1-t)^{4}}dt\\
L367: &=4cD\int_{t^{*}}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}dt\\
L368: &=\left[\frac{4cD}{1-t}\right]_{t^{*}}^{1}=\infty.\end{split}$$  |  | (16)
L369: 
L370: The integral exhibits a first-order divergence. While divergent, this instability is often numerically circumvented in practice by specialized time-sampling heuristics that avoid the $t\approx 1$ boundary.
L371: #### Binary Discrete Case.
L372: 
L373: For $\mathbf{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{D}$, the network maintains a residual $R(t)\geq\epsilon^{2}$ as $t\to 1$ (Assum. cite69†IV.3 ). Substituting this into the integral:
L374: 
L375:  | $$\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{bin}&\geq\int_{t^{*}}^{1}\frac{4c\epsilon^{2}}{(1-t)^{4}}dt\\
L376: &=\left[\frac{4c\epsilon^{2}}{3(1-t)^{3}}\right]_{t^{*}}^{1}=\infty.\end{split}$$  |  | (17)
L377: The integral exhibits a third-order divergence. Unlike the continuous case, this explosive variance leads to immediate numerical overflow in the absence of specialized sampling or structural alignment. ∎
L378: ## Appendix B Asymptotic Stability Analysis of Sampling Schedules
L379: 
L380: We rigorously analyze the interaction between the Logit-Normal schedule and the mismatch singularity. Let $u=\text{logit}(t)$, then $1-t=\frac{e^{-u}}{1+e^{-u}}\sim e^{-u}$ for large $u$. The gradient variance under $\pi_{LN}(t)$ transforms as follows:
L381:  | $$\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{\pi}&\propto\int_{u^{*}}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}/2s^{2}}\cdot\frac{R(u)}{(e^{-u})^{4}}\cdot(\text{Jacobian factor})du\\
L382: &\sim\int_{u^{*}}^{\infty}R(u)\exp\left(4u-\frac{u^{2}}{2s^{2}}\right)du.\end{split}$$  |  | (18)
L383: #### Case 1: Continuous Manifolds.
L384: 
L385: For Gaussianized continuous data, the residual $R(u)\sim(1-t)^{2}\sim e^{-2u}$. The integrand becomes $\exp(-u^{2}/2s^{2}+2u)$. The peak of this effective density is shifted to $u_{peak}=2s^{2}$. For standard settings ($s=1$), the density remains substantial near the boundary, allowing JiT [cite60†9 ] to achieve high quality.
L386: #### Case 2: Binary Manifolds.
L387: 
L388: For binary data, $R(u)\sim\Omega(1)$. The integrand becomes $\exp(-u^{2}/2s^{2}+4u)$. The peak is shifted further to $u_{peak}=4s^{2}$. To prevent numerical overflow from the $e^{4u}$ term, the sampler must ensure that the Gaussian tail $\exp(-u^{2})$ decays significantly before $e^{4u}$ hits the floating-point limit. This creates an Effective Sampling Gap: the model spends its ”sampling budget” in regions where $t$ is far from 1.
L389: #### Conclusion.
L390: 
L391: For binary flow matching, Logit-Normal sampling creates a fundamental conflict: one must choose between numerical survival (by suppressing the boundary) and binary fidelity (by sampling the boundary). Our proposed Prediction-Loss Alignment resolves this conflict by ensuring $\mathcal{O}(1)$ variance regardless of the sampling measure, enabling the model to learn the critical $t\to 1$ transition without instability.

L392: ## Appendix C Proof of Proposition cite105†IV.6 L393: 
L394: We rigorously demonstrate that alignment between the model’s prediction target and the loss space ensures numerical stability by analytically canceling the singular terms.
L395: 
L396: See cite105†IV.6 L397: 
L398: ###### Proof.
L399: 
L400: We analyze the second moment of the stochastic gradient $\mathbf{g}_{t}(\theta)$ by considering the target manifold’s geometry. Stability is achieved if $\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|^{2}]<\infty$ for all $t\in[0,1]$.
L401: ### C-A Case 1: Continuous Manifolds (Standardized Signals)
L402: 
L403: For standardized continuous signals $\mathbf{x}\sim P_{x}$, the prediction and loss are aligned in either velocity or signal space.
L404: #### Velocity Alignment ($v$-pred + $v$-loss).
L405: 
L406: The gradient is $\mathbf{g}_{t}^{v}=2(\mathbf{v}_{\theta}-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{e}))^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{\theta}^{v}$. In the standardized paradigm, the target velocity $\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{e}$ has finite moments. Since the Jacobian $\|\mathbf{J}_{\theta}^{v}\|\leq K$ (Assum. cite68†IV.1 ) and no singular factors of $(1-t)$ are introduced by the objective, we have:
L407:  | $$\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{v}\|^{2}]\leq 4K^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{v}_{\theta}-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{e})\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1).$$  |  | (19)
L408: #### Signal Alignment ($x$-pred + MSE-loss).
L409: 
L410: The gradient is $\mathbf{g}_{t}^{mse}=2(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}-\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{\theta}^{x}$. For standardized signals in the bipolar range $[-1,1]^{D}$, the residual is bounded by $\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq 2\sqrt{D}$. Thus:
L411: 
L412:  | $$\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{mse}\|\leq 4K\sqrt{D}\implies\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{mse}\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1).$$  |  | (20)
L413: This confirms that signal-space alignment restores stability to $x$-prediction in continuous domains, rendering boundary-avoiding samplers optional.
L414: ### C-B Case 2: Binary Manifolds (Discrete Priors)
L415: 
L416: For binary signals $\mathbf{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{D}$, the model is aligned in the signal space using either geometric (MSE) or categorical (BCE) objectives.
L417: #### Geometric Alignment (MSE).
L418: 
L419: As in the continuous case, the residual $\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}-\mathbf{x}\|$ is bounded by the hypercube diameter $2\sqrt{D}$. Despite the non-vanishing residual $R(t)\sim\Omega(1)$ at the Dirac peaks, the absence of the $(1-t)^{-2}$ weighting in the objective ensures:
L420: 
L421:  | $$\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{mse}\|^{2}]\leq 16K^{2}D=\mathcal{O}(1).$$  |  | (21)
L422: #### Categorical Alignment (BCE).
L423: 
L424: With logits $\mathbf{a}_{\theta}$ and sigmoid predictions $\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{\theta})\in[0,1]^{D}$, the BCE gradient is $\mathbf{g}_{t}^{bce}=(\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{\theta})-\mathbf{x}_{01})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{a}_{\theta}$. The residual $(\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{\theta})-\mathbf{x}_{01})$ is strictly bounded within $[-1,1]^{D}$. Thus:
L425: 
L426:  | $$\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{bce}\|\leq\sqrt{D}\cdot K\implies\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{bce}\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1).$$  |  | (22)
L427: #### Conclusion.
L428: 
L429: In both continuous and binary cases, alignment ensures that the gradient norm is independent of the singularity at $t\to 1$. The cumulative variance $\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{O}(1)dt$ is uniformly bounded, enabling robust, sampler-agnostic convergence. ∎

L239: ### IV-E Loss-Induced Signal Topologies for Binary Data
L240: 
L241: Under prediction–loss space alignment, the optimization instability caused by parameterization mismatch is eliminated. As a result, the choice of loss function no longer serves a numerical stabilizing role, but instead encodes an explicit structural assumption on the organization of binary signals.
L242: Consider binary data $\mathbf{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{D}$ and model outputs $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$, which are interpreted according to the chosen loss function.
L243: #### Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE).
L244: 
L245: The binary cross-entropy loss is defined as
L246: 
L247:  | $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}})=-\sum_{i=1}^{D}\Big[\frac{1+x_{i}}{2}\log p_{i}+\frac{1-x_{i}}{2}\log(1-p_{i})\Big],$$  |  | (7)
L248: 
L249: where $p_{i}=\sigma(\hat{x}_{i})$. This objective corresponds to the negative log-likelihood of a factorized Bernoulli model,
L250: 
L251:  | $$p(\mathbf{x}\mid\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{i=1}^{D}p(x_{i}\mid\hat{x}_{i}),$$  |  | (8)
L252: and therefore assumes conditional independence between bits given the model output. From a structural perspective, BCE treats the binary signal as an independent symbolic stream, applying supervision locally at the level of individual bits.
L253: #### Mean Squared Error (MSE).
L254: 
L255: In contrast, the mean squared error loss is given by
L256: 
L257:  | $$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}.$$  |  | (9)
L258: 
L259: This loss corresponds (up to a constant) to the negative log-likelihood of an isotropic Gaussian model over the full vector,
L260: 
L261:  | $$p(\mathbf{x}\mid\hat{\mathbf{x}})\propto\exp\left(-\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\right),$$  |  | (10)
L262: and therefore treats the binary signal as a single point embedded in a continuous Euclidean space. Consequently, MSE enforces global geometric consistency across all dimensions and implicitly preserves spatial or structural correlations among bits.
L263: #### Discussion.
L264: 
L265: Once prediction–loss alignment guarantees stable optimization, the loss function selection is dictated by the intrinsic topology of the binary signal. Probabilistic objectives such as BCE are naturally suited for independent symbolic recovery, whereas geometric regression losses such as MSE are more appropriate when binary data exhibit structured or correlated organization.
