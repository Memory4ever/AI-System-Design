L215: ## 4 Experiments
L216: We evaluate our method in the practical LLM setting using the Task of Fictitious Unlearning (TOFU) benchmark [cite72†MFSLK24 ]. TOFU consists of a fine-tuned LLM and a dataset of question-answer pairs regarding 200 fictitious authors generated by GPT-4 [cite98†Ope23 ].
L217: The unlearning task requires forgetting all question-answer pairs from a subset of the authors (the forget set) while preserving knowledge of the remaining authors (retain set) as well as heldout real-world authors (RA) and general world facts (WF) datasets.
L218: ### 4.1 Unlearning Metrics
L219: 
L220: TOFU evaluates unlearning using several metrics built around the Truth Ratio ($R_{\text{truth}}$), an intermediate statistic comparing the model’s relative probability of incorrect “perturbed” answers $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}$ to that of the paraphrased true answer $\bm{a}^{\dagger}$ for a given question $\bm{q}$:
L221:  | $$R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})=\frac{\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}\right|}\sum_{\bm{\tilde{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pert}}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\tilde{a}}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{\tilde{a}}\right|}}{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\mid\bm{q})^{1/\left|\bm{a}^{\dagger}\right|}}.$$  |
L222: 
L223: From this statistic, TOFU defines two summary metrics.
L224: Forget Quality (FQ) quantifies statistical indistinguishability from a retrained reference model. It is computed as the p-value of a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test comparing the Truth Ratio distributions generated by the unlearned model and the reference model over the forget set.
L225: Model Utility (MU) measures retained performance on tasks unrelated to the forget set. It is defined as the harmonic mean, over the retain, RA, and WF datasets, of three components: (i) Probability, the length-normalized probability of the true answer; (ii) ROUGE, the ROUGE-L recall [cite99†Lin04 ]; and (iii) TR+, an inverted confidence score given by $\text{TR{+}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})=\max\{0,1-R_{\text{truth}}(p_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{q})\}$.
L226: While prior work often conflates $R_{\text{truth}}$ and TR+ under the term “Truth Ratio,” we distinguish them explicitly for clarity. See Appendix cite35†F.2 for detailed definitions for all metrics.
L227: MU-ROUGE (Generative Utility). While Model Utility provides a broad summary of retained performance, it aggregates heterogeneous quantities with distinct semantic meanings (Probability, ROUGE, and TR+) into a single harmonic mean. In particular, increases in token-level probability or confidence can raise the overall score even when the quality of generated text does not improve.
L228: To isolate generative performance, we propose MU-ROUGE, defined as the harmonic mean of ROUGE-L scores across the retain, RA, and WF datasets. By focusing on generation quality, MU-ROUGE provides a more direct and interpretable measure of retained utility. We report both Model Utility and MU-ROUGE but emphasize MU-ROUGE as the primary utility metric.

L261: In contrast, MU-ROUGE provides a more faithful measure of utility, as it does not meaningfully exceed the original model’s performance after unlearning.

L342: ### 7.2 Larger Margin Leads to Higher Accuracy
L343: 
L344: Our theory suggests that larger score margins lead to higher accuracy, since moderate estimation errors are less likely to flip the ordering of well-separated pairs. To isolate the effect of margin from other factors, we compare learning from the original score $r^{*}$ against a rank-normalized version $r^{*}_{\text{rank}}$ that preserves the ordering but maximizes the minimum margin between any two responses.
L345: #### Rank normalization.
L346: 
L347: For each context $x$, we sort responses by $r^{*}(x,y)$ and assign:
L348: 
L349:  | $$r^{*}_{\text{rank}}(x,y)=-1+\frac{2\cdot\text{rank}(y)}{m},$$  |  | (27)
L350: 
L351: where $\text{rank}(y)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$ is the rank of $y$ among all responses for context $x$ (ascending). This transformation preserves the ordering while maximizing the minimum pairwise margin to $2/m$. We construct BT-consistent distributions for both $r^{*}$ and $r^{*}_{\text{rank}}$ using the same uniform $p^{-}$.
L352: #### Results.
L353: As shown in Figure cite115†1 , learning from $r^{*}_{\text{rank}}$ achieves substantially higher accuracy than learning from $r^{*}$, particularly in the small-sample regime. As $n$ increases, the gap narrows, consistent with our theory: with sufficient data, estimation error becomes small enough that even small margins suffice for correct ordering. To further validate our theory, we analyze accuracy on pairs with the smallest margins (bottom 10$\%$ and 30$\%$).
L354: The accuracy gap between $r^{*}_{\text{rank}}$ and $r^{*}$ is most pronounced on these pairs, confirming that margin amplification primarily benefits pairs that are otherwise difficult to distinguish. See Appendix cite33†D for more details.
L355: Figure 1: Effect of margin on learning efficiency. Learning from the rank-normalized score $r^{*}_{\text{rank}}$ (which has larger minimum margin) achieves higher accuracy than learning from $r^{*}$, especially when the number of triplets is small. The gap diminishes as sample size increases.
L356: ### 7.3 Low Connectivity Predicts Poor Accuracy
L357: 
L358: Our theory suggests that higher connectivity leads to lower estimation error. In this experiment, we fix the score function $r^{*}$ and vary the $p^{-}$ to change the connectivity of the triplet distribution.
L359: #### Varying the negative distribution.
L360: We fix $r^{*}$ and parameterize the negative distribution as $p^{-}(y\mid x)\propto\exp(\alpha\cdot r^{*}(x,y))$ for $\alpha\in[-16,16]$. Different values of $\alpha$ induce different sampling strategies: i) $\alpha<0$: Easy negatives, prefer sample with low scores ii) $\alpha=0$: Uniform sampling, iii) $\alpha>0$: Hard negatives, prefer sample with high scores.
L361: For each $\alpha$, we compute the corresponding BT-consistent $p^{+}$ and measure both accuracy and the connectivity degree $\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P^{\alpha})$.
L362: #### Results.
L363: 
L364: As shown in Figure cite116†2 , extreme values of $\alpha$ correspond to overly easy or hard negatives which lead to low connectivity. Moreover, low connectivity reliably predicts poor accuracy, though the converse does not hold: high connectivity does not guarantee high accuracy. This asymmetry reflects the worst-case nature of our bound: low connectivity means some functions in the hypothesis class are hard to learn, but the actual target may not be among them.
L365: Figure 2: Effect of comparison graph connectivity on learning. High connectivity degress values are associated with high accuracy, though the relationship is not strictly monotonic.
L366: ### 7.4 Optimizing Connectivity Helps When Connectivity is the Bottleneck
L367: 
L368: We investigate whether directly optimizing $p^{-}$ to increase the connectivity degree improves learning.
L369: #### Method.
L370: 
L371: We scale the target reward by a factor of $\beta$, $r^{*}_{\beta}=\beta\cdot r^{*}(x,y)$ for different values of $\beta$. This provides a way to control the difficulty of the task where smaller $\beta$ corresponds to a task with small margin and large connectivity while a larger $\beta$ corresponds to a task with large margin and small connectivity. We compare two negative distributions

L374: 
L375: Baseline: uniform negative distribution $p^{-}(\cdot\mid x)=\frac{1}{m}$
L376: 
L377:   * •
L378: Optimized: we optimize $p^{-}$ to maximize the connectivity degree $\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P^{\beta})$.
L379: 
L380: Figure 3: Effect of connectivity optimization on learning. Optimizing $p^{-}$ to increase the connectivity can help with the accuracy when the task is hard where $\beta$ is large.
L381: #### Results.
L382: First, optimizing $p^{-}$ leads to higher connectivity than uniform sampling across all values of $\beta$. However, higher connectivity only improves accuracy in the connectivity-dominated regime (large $\beta$), where the margin is sufficient but connectivity is the bottleneck. In the margin-dominated regime (small $\beta$), the margin is too small for learning to succeed—no amount of connectivity optimization can compensate.
L383: This validates that connectivity can be a meaningful quantity to optimize in practice when designing a data distribution.
L384: ## 8 Discussion
L385: 
L386: We provide a framework for understanding preference learning from pairwise comparisons through a data-centric lens. The key object is CPRD, which captures the preference information encoded in the triplet data distribution. We establish precise conditions under which CPRD is representable by a BT model and analyze the learning efficiency of the BT objective. Our theoretical and empirical results identify margin and connectivity as the governing factors of learning efficiency.
L387: Our framework provides a foundation for understanding how data distribution impacts preference learning, but several practical challenges remain. First, since the target score function is implicit, it is unclear how to evaluate or increase the margin in practice. Second, although connectivity is computable from data, actionable strategies for improving it remain an open question.
L388: Third, our analysis assumes the target score function is known when designing the distribution, but in practice it is exactly what we want to learn. One possible relaxation is to allow distribution changes that also shift the target, provided the new target remains desirable. Developing practical data curation strategies that yield distributions which are both easy to learn from and induce good target score functions is a promising direction for future work.
