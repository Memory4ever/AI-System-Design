L145: ###### Abstract
L146: We develop a theoretical analysis of LLM-guided formal theorem proving in interactive proof assistants (e.g., Lean) by modeling tactic proposal as a stochastic policy in a finite-horizon deterministic MDP. To capture modern representation learning, we treat the state and action spaces as general compact metric spaces and assume Lipschitz policies.
L147: To explain the gap between worst-case hardness and empirical success, we introduce problem distributions generated by a reference policy $q$, including a latent-variable model in which proofs exhibit reusable cut/lemma/sketch structure represented by a proof DAG. Under a top-$k$ search protocol and Tsybakov-type margin conditions, we derive lower bounds on finite-horizon success probability that decompose into search and learning terms, with learning controlled by sequential Rademacher/covering complexity.
L148: Our main separation result shows that when cut elimination expands a DAG of depth $D$ into a cut-free tree of size $\Omega(\Lambda^{D})$ while the cut-aware hierarchical process has size $O(\lambda^{D})$ with $\lambda\ll\Lambda$, a flat (cut-free) learner provably requires exponentially more data than a cut-aware hierarchical learner. This provides a principled justification for subgoal decomposition in recent agentic theorem provers.
L149: ## 1 Introduction
L150: Recent agentic theorem provers couple a verifier with an LLM and often include explicit subgoal decomposition steps such as lemma discovery, proof sketches, or draft-refine loops (e.g., Draft-Sketch-Prove (cite128†Jiang et al., 2023 ), DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (cite129†Xin et al., 2024 ; cite130†Ren et al., 2025 ), Hilbert (cite131†Varambally et al., 2025 ), Prover Agent (cite132†Baba et al., 2025 ), SeedProver (cite133†Chen et al., 2025 ), and Aristotle (cite134†Achim et al., 2025 )).
L151: These components can appear as ad-hoc heuristics, since generating a sketch or intermediate lemma is itself a hard task and may seem a detour toward the low-level search required to close goals. This study provides theoretical evidence that such hierarchical procedures can be rational and even exponentially more sample-efficient when the underlying proof distribution exhibits reusable high-level structure.
L152: We model interactive theorem proving as follows. A prover state is a finite set of goals; applying a tactic rewrites the goals into a new set of goals. The kernel transition is deterministic, whereas an LLM proposes tactics stochastically, yielding a finite-horizon deterministic MDP. We evaluate a prover by its finite-horizon success probability, i.e., the probability of reaching the terminal “no goals” state within time $T$.
L153: Since tactics typically carry parameters (e.g., lemma arguments) and goal sets range over an infinite space of propositions, we formalize states and actions as any compact metric spaces, and restrict to Lipschitz policy classes; this abstracts the data embeddings and representations acquired in the real-world LLMs.
L154: Because theorem proving is computationally hard and often worst-case intractable (e.g., 3-SAT problems are NP-hard, and first-order logic is undecidable in general), we explicitly incorporate data distributions. We introduce a reference policy $q$ that generates successful proofs and induces a distribution over theorem instances.
L155: We study two regimes: a cut-free regime where proofs are essentially trees with an average length parameter, and a cut-structured regime where proofs admit reusable subproofs (cuts/lemmas/sketches) represented as a latent proof DAG $Z$, together with an observation model that generates cut-free traces from $Z$.
L156: The latent-variable formulation allows a broad family of structural biases to be described with a small number of interpretable parameters, including a depth $D$, an effective unique branching factor, and a contraction parameter governing how decomposition reduces local proof complexity.
L157: We analyze two learning-and-search pipelines. The flat (cut-free) model learns a single policy by ERM on cut-free traces and performs top-$k$ search. The hierarchical (cut-aware) model learns a high-level decomposition policy and a low-level solver policy, and alternates policy learning with latent-structure inference via an EM/VAE-style procedure.
L158: For both pipelines, we derive success probability lower bounds under top-$k$ search and Tsybakov-type margin assumptions (cite135†Audibert and Tsybakov, 2007 ; cite136†Arlot and Bartlett, 2011 ); the bounds separate intrinsic search difficulty from one-step imitation errors.
L159: We further control the latter via sequential Rademacher/covering complexity (cite137†Rakhlin et al., 2015a ; cite138†Rakhlin et al., 2015b ; cite139†Block et al., 2021 ), yielding explicit generalization terms for posterior-weighted ERM under latent DAGs.
L160: Our main contributions are: (i) a compact-metric MDP formalization of LLM theorem proving with a finite-horizon success criterion; (ii) distributional models induced by a generator $q$, including a latent proof-DAG model for cut structure; (iii) success probability guarantees for flat and hierarchical top-$k$ search under margin conditions; (iv) sequential generalization bounds (sequential Rademacher/covering) for posterior-weighted ERM with latent DAGs, with an explicit inference error term controlled by the ELBO gap; (v) an exponential separation showing that, under cut-elimination blow-up, flat cut-free learning requires exponentially more data than cut-aware hierarchical learning.
L161: Together, these results support the view that subgoal decomposition leverages an inductive bias present in realistic mathematical proof distributions, without contradicting classical worst-case hardness results.
L162: ### 1.1 Related work (cut/lemma sharing and modern agentic provers)
L163: 
L164: The study of decomposition and sharing is motivated not only by heuristics but by proof complexity. While cut elimination is a fundamental meta-theorem, eliminating cut may cause an enormous blow-up in proof size. cite140†Boolos (1984) ’ “Don’t Eliminate Cut” exhibits an inference with a short proof using cut but with astronomically large cut-free proofs, emphasizing that cut/lemma mechanisms are essential for tractability in practice.
L165: This classical lesson reappears concretely in modern LLM-guided formal theorem proving, where agentic pipelines explicitly produce intermediate claims and reuse them:

L168: 
L169: Draft, Sketch, and Prove (cite128†Jiang et al., 2023 ) uses informal proofs to produce formal proof sketches that guide search by explicitly decomposing into easier subproblems.
L170: 
L171:   * •
L172: DeepSeek-Prover-V2 (cite130†Ren et al., 2025 ) describes a Lean 4 prover trained with reinforcement learning in a pipeline that explicitly leverages recursive subgoal decomposition.
L173: 
L174:   * •
L175: 
L176: Seed-Prover (cite133†Chen et al., 2025 ) proposes lemma-style whole-proof reasoning with iterative refinement using Lean feedback and proved lemmas, explicitly emphasizing lemma reuse and broad/deep search.
L177: 
L178:   * •
L179: Aristotle (cite134†Achim et al., 2025 ) integrates Lean proof search with an informal lemma-generation system and dedicated geometry solver, emphasizing lemma-based decomposition as a scaling mechanism.
L180: 
L181: These systems suggest that lemma/cut-style subgoal decomposition is not an optional refinement but a structural driver of provability at scale. The hierarchical generator below is designed to isolate and quantify this phenomenon.
L182: ### 1.2 Related Work (hierarchical policies and exponential separations)
L183: ##### Hierarchical policies via options and goal-conditioned HRL.
L184: A large body of work studies hierarchical decision making through options (macro-actions) and related HRL formalisms. In lifelong RL, cite141†Brunskill and Li (2014) analyze PAC-style option discovery under the assumption that there exists a small option set that is $\varepsilon$-optimal across tasks drawn i.i.d. from an unknown task distribution, making explicit how well-chosen temporal abstractions can improve sample efficiency. In online RL, cite142†Fruit et al.
L185: (2017) study regret minimization in MDPs with options without prior knowledge of option duration/reward parameters, combining the semi-MDP view with the internal Markov structure of option execution. More recently, cite143†Robert et al. (2023) derive sample-complexity lower bounds for a class of goal-conditioned hierarchical RL algorithms, clarifying when a hierarchical decomposition can help and motivating a corresponding Q-learning-type method.
L186: These works provide strong motivation and formal guarantees for hierarchical policies, but they primarily target unknown stochastic environments and regret/value criteria. In contrast, our setting is a deterministic, verifier-defined MDP arising from interactive theorem proving, and our performance metric is finite-horizon goal-reaching probability under top-$k$ search.
L187: Conceptually, our decomposition/lemma policies play the role of high-level options, but our main separation mechanism is specific to proof systems: reusable cut/lemma/sketch structure yields a compact proof DAG that can be exponentially more succinct than its fully unfolded cut-free proof tree.
L188: ##### Exponential gains from exploiting structure: factored MDPs.
L189: The clearest precedents for exponential separations in RL come from exploiting graphical structure in factored MDPs.
L190: Starting with cite144†Kearns and Koller (1999) , and continuing through structure-learning and near-optimal regret analyses (cite145†Strehl et al., 2007 ; cite146†Osband and Roy, ; cite147†Xu and Tewari, 2020 ; cite148†Chen et al., 2021 ), this line of work shows that when transitions and rewards factor according to a sparse dependency graph, one can obtain learning guarantees whose dependence is polynomial in the number of factors and parent-set sizes, rather than exponential in the raw state-space cardinality.
L191: Our exponential separation is analogous in spirit but arises from a different structural primitive: sharing in proof DAGs (cuts/lemmas) versus duplication in cut-free proof trees.
L192: Under a cut-elimination blow-up assumption, the effective number of decision points for a flat (cut-free) prover scales as $\Omega(\Lambda^{D})$ while a cut-aware hierarchical prover scales as $O(\lambda^{D})$ with $\lambda\ll\Lambda$, yielding an exponential gap in the one-step accuracy (and thus sample size) required to achieve a target success probability under margin-based top-$k$ analysis.
L193: Unlike factored-MDP results, we do not learn the transition model (it is given by the proof assistant kernel) and instead quantify the joint effect of representation class (Lipschitz policies on compact metric embeddings), imitation learning (ERM), and search.
L194: ## 2 Interactive theorem proving in Lean and an MDP formulation
L195: ### 2.1 Lean-style interaction: goals, tactics, and “no goals”
L196: 
L197: Interactive theorem proving (ITP) systems such as Lean (cite149†de Moura et al., 2015 ; cite150†de Moura and Ullrich, 2021 ), Rocq (cite151†The Coq Development Team, 2024 ), and Isabelle/HOL (cite152†Nipkow et al., 2002 ), expose proof construction as an interactive process: at any moment the prover maintains a finite collection of goals (proof obligations). Informally, each goal is shown as a sequent/entailment/judgment form:
L198: 
L199:  | $$\Gamma\vdash G,$$  |
L200: (read as either “$\Gamma$ entails $G$”, “Assume $\Gamma$, therefore $G$”, or “From context $\Gamma$, derive $G$”), where $\Gamma$ is a context (a set of assumptions, definitions, and declarations), and $G$ is a goal formula (a conclusion, or a target proposition). In Lean, applying a tactic (an inference rule / proof procedure) rewrites the current goals $\{\Gamma_{i}\vdash G_{i}\}$ into new goals $\{\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}\vdash G_{i}^{\prime}\}$.
L201: Crucially, a single tactic application may generate multiple subgoals. When all goals are discharged, Lean reports no goals, meaning the proof has been completed.

L233: ##### Proof trees and AND–OR structure.
L234: 
L235: From a search viewpoint, interactive theorem proving induces an AND–OR structure:
L236: 
L237:   * •
L238: 
L239: OR: at a goal state, one chooses a tactic (and its parameters).
L240: 
L241:   * •
L242: 
L243: AND: a chosen tactic may produce multiple subgoals, all of which must be solved.
L244: A complete proof corresponds to a finite AND–OR proof tree. In practice, intermediate results (lemmas, proof sketches) can be reused across branches, turning the tree into a proof DAG. This is the operational face of cut introduction: one proves a lemma once and reuses it. Conversely, cut elimination (normalization to a cut-free proof) expands shared DAG structure into a tree, potentially causing a large blow-up in size (cite140†Boolos, 1984 , we refer to the seminal paper “Don’t Eliminate Cut” by).
L245: More detailed concrete examples are provided in cite48†Appendix A .
