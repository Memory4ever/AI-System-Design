L307: ## Appendix A Prompting and Generation Details
L308: 
L309: We describe the prompting design and generation parameters used to construct cue dialogues and trigger queries in our dataset. These prompts are used exclusively for data generation and do not involve any evaluation or judgment procedures.
L310: We adopt a structured prompting strategy in which each prompt is decomposed into semantically annotated components, including role setting, task logic, constraints, and output format. This decomposition clarifies the intended function of each prompt segment and helps ensure consistency and reproducibility across generations. Cue dialogues are generated as short two-turn conversations that introduce a salient and recallable memory anchor while remaining naturalistic and self-contained.
L311: Trigger queries are then generated conditioned on the cue dialogue and relation type, with explicit constraints encouraging diversity in perspective, temporal gap, and cognitive angle. Complete prompt specifications with semantic annotations are provided in Tables cite134†5 and cite135†6 .
L312: #### Generation Parameters.
L313: 
L314: Cue dialogues and trigger queries are generated using a set of commercially available large language models, including gpt-5-nano, gpt-4o, gpt-4.1 cite92†Achiam et al. (2023) , gemini-2.5-flash, and gemini-2.5-pro cite93†Team et al. (2023) . All models are used exclusively for data generation in this stage.
L315: To ensure reproducibility across different model APIs, we restrict generation control to a shared set of supported parameters. Specifically, we enable stochastic decoding with a non-zero temperature ($T=0.7$) to encourage diversity in linguistic realization and topic coverage. We additionally control the maximum generation length (256 tokens) and the number of generated instances per relation type (num_samples=50).
L316: Aside from enforcing the specified structural and output-format constraints, no additional filtering or post-processing is applied. All prompting and generation procedures are implemented in our open-source codebase, which is released alongside this paper to support full reproducibility.
L317: ## Appendix B Judge Design and Evaluation Protocol
L318: For factual and commonsense questions, model responses are often open-ended and may partially satisfy the information need. For example, when a question asks what a user ate for dinner and the reference answer specifies a concrete item (e.g., “grilled salmon”), a response such as “seafood” captures the correct coarse concept but misses the required level of specificity. Treating such responses as fully incorrect would conflate coarse-grained recall with complete failure.
L319: We therefore retain a three-level label scheme (correct, partial, wrong) to distinguish partial factual recall from entirely incorrect or hallucinated answers.
L320: In contrast, temporal and adversarial questions admit precise decision boundaries. Temporal reasoning requires exact calculation or ordering, where near misses or approximate answers are not semantically acceptable. Similarly, adversarial questions evaluate whether the model correctly refuses to answer or identifies a conflict; any attempt to hallucinate an answer constitutes a failure. For these categories, we adopt binary labels to avoid ambiguity and ensure consistent evaluation.
L321: Finally, cognitive awareness questions assess whether a response explicitly acknowledges or adapts to a previously stated memory cue. Because the core criterion is the presence or absence of memory-aware behavior rather than surface correctness, we likewise use binary labels to reflect recall success versus omission. The full task-specific evaluation prompts are summarized in Table cite136†7 .

L346: 
L347: To concretely illustrate how our dataset differs from fact-centric benchmarks such as LoCoMo, we present several representative cognitive memory cases sampled from the full dataset. All examples below are shown verbatim (lightly anonymized) and are drawn from different cognitive relation types. Rather than querying explicitly stated facts, these cases require models to retain and apply implicit personal constraints expressed earlier in the dialogue.
L348: #### Example 1: Causal Memory (Habit Change Triggered by an Event).
L349: 
L350: Cue Dialogue (Causal):
L351: 
L352: > A: Since my cousin got diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes, I cut sugary drinks completely out of my diet.
L353: > B: That family scare clearly changed your habits.
L354: 
L355: Trigger Query (Months Later):
L356: 
L357: > A: It’s strange, I barely recognize the person who used to grab whatever sounded good without thinking about long-term consequences.
L358: Analysis. The trigger does not explicitly mention diabetes or dietary choices. Correct interpretation requires recalling the earlier causal event and connecting it to a later shift in self-perception. This goes beyond factual recall (e.g., “Why did you stop drinking sugary drinks?”) and instead evaluates whether the model can implicitly link a past cause to a present reflection.
L359: #### Example 2: State-Based Memory (Emotional State Driving Later Behavior).
L360: 
L361: Cue Dialogue (State):
L362: 
L363: > A: I got yelled at by my boss for a mistake I can’t stop thinking about, and I feel hollow.
L364: > B: That’ll pass; give yourself credit for fixing it and move on.
L365: 
L366: Trigger Query (Weeks Later):
L367: 
L368: > A: I redesigned the workflow to make sure the same mistake can’t happen again and asked two coworkers to double-check it.
L369: Analysis. The later utterance does not restate the emotional state. Correct handling requires remembering the earlier distress and inferring that lingering anxiety motivated cautious, preventive behavior. This type of memory cannot be evaluated by checking for factual overlap, as the relevant signal lies in the persistence of an internal state.
L370: #### Example 3: Goal-Oriented Memory (Long-Term Intention and Re-evaluation).
L371: 
L372: Cue Dialogue (Goal):
L373:

L381: #### Example 4: Value-Based Memory (Consistency Across Contexts).
L382:

L391: Analysis. No explicit fact is queried in the trigger. Correct interpretation requires recalling the earlier value statement and recognizing a value-consistency tension across different social roles. Such cases emphasize reasoning over personal principles rather than retrieval of explicit information.
L392: #### Summary.
L393: These examples demonstrate that cognitive memory cases in our dataset differ fundamentally from fact-based benchmarks. VESPO: Variational Sequence-Level Soft Policy Optimization for Stable Off-Policy LLM Training (https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10693v1)
citeturn28933view1 [wordlim: 200] Crawled: today; Content type: text/html; Source: open({"ref_id":"https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10693v1","lineno":413}); Total lines: 578
L368: The token-level formulation cannot be expressed as importance sampling toward any single proposal distribution $Q$, because such a representation requires a uniform weight across all token gradients within each trajectory. This token-wise weighting breaks the coherence of sequence-level credit assignment: tokens in the same trajectory that share a common outcome receive inconsistent gradient signals.
L369: #### Token-level IS as a First-Order Approximation.
L370: 
L371: We now show that token-level IS is a first-order approximation to sequence-level IS, following cite60†Zheng et al. (2025a) . Consider the unclipped case where $\phi_{t}(\rho_{t})=\rho_{t}$ and $\phi(W)=W$. The sequence-level gradient (without advantage for clarity) is
L372:  | $$\nabla J_{\text{seq}}=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\Bigl[W\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\Bigl[\prod_{s=1}^{T}\rho_{s}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\Bigr].$$  |  | (25)
L373: 
L374: When $\rho_{t}\approx 1$ for all $t$ (near on-policy), we can expand $W=\prod_{s}\rho_{s}$ via Taylor series:
L375: 
L376:  | $$W=\prod_{s=1}^{T}\rho_{s}\approx 1+\sum_{s=1}^{T}(\rho_{s}-1)+\sum_{s<s^{\prime}}(\rho_{s}-1)(\rho_{s^{\prime}}-1)+\cdots$$  |  | (26)
L377: Substituting into the gradient and keeping only first-order terms in $(\rho_{s}-1)$:
L378:  | $\displaystyle\nabla J_{\text{seq}}$  | $\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\Bigl[\Bigl(1+\sum_{s=1}^{T}(\rho_{s}{-}1)\Bigr)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\Bigr]$  |  | (27)
L379:  |  | $\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\Bigr]}_{\text{REINFORCE (no IS)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s,t}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[(\rho_{s}{-}1)\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\bigr]}_{\text{first-order IS correction}}.$  |  | (28)
L380: 
L381: The token-level gradient is
L382:  | $$\nabla J_{\text{tok}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[\rho_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\Bigr]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[(\rho_{t}{-}1)\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\bigr].$$  |  | (29)
L383: 
L384: Comparing, we see that token-level IS only retains the diagonal terms $(s=t)$ of the first-order correction, discarding cross-token interactions where $s\neq t$. The approximation error is
L385:  | $$\nabla J_{\text{seq}}-\nabla J_{\text{tok}}\approx\sum_{s\neq t}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[(\rho_{s}-1)\nabla\log\pi(y_{t}|\cdot)\bigr]+O\bigl((\rho-1)^{2}\bigr).$$  |  | (30)
L386: 
L387: This error captures the fact that changing the policy at position $s$ affects the importance of the gradient at position $t$—a cross-token dependency that token-level methods ignore.
L388: ###### Remark A.1 (When Token-Level Approximation is Reasonable).
L389: 
L390: The approximation $\nabla J_{\text{tok}}\approx\nabla J_{\text{seq}}$ is reasonable when:
L391: 
L392:   1. 1.
L393: 
L394: Near on-policy: $\rho_{t}\approx 1$ for all $t$, so higher-order terms and cross-token terms are small.
L395: 
L396:   2. 2.
L397: 
L398: Short sequences: Fewer cross-token pairs $(s,t)$ with $s\neq t$ means smaller accumulated error.
L399: In practice, RL for LLMs often violates both conditions: off-policy updates are common, and reasoning tasks require long sequences. This motivates sequence-level methods that preserve the full product structure of importance weights.
L400: #### Length normalization (GSPO).
L401: 
L402: GSPO uses the geometric mean $\phi(W)=W^{1/T}$, which induces a proposal distribution that explicitly depends on sequence length:
L403: 
L404:  | $$Q_{\text{GSPO}}(\tau)\propto\mu(\tau)^{1-1/T}\cdot\pi(\tau)^{1/T}.$$  |  | (31)
L405: 
L406: As $T\to\infty$, $Q_{\text{GSPO}}\to\mu$, meaning longer sequences receive vanishingly small corrections toward the target policy. See cite30†Appendix C for a detailed analysis of the length-dependent bias this introduces.
L407: #### Sequence-level hard clipping.
L408: 
L409: If one were to apply hard clipping at the sequence level (truncating $W$ at threshold $c$), the reshaping function would be $\phi(W)=\min(W,c)$, inducing
L410: 
L411:  | $$Q_{\text{clip}}(\tau)\propto\min\bigl(\pi(\tau),c\cdot\mu(\tau)\bigr).$$  |  | (32)
L412: This is a truncated distribution: trajectories with $W>c$ are capped rather than weighted proportionally. The discontinuity at $W=c$ can cause optimization difficulties when trajectories cross the boundary during training. Note that standard PPO applies clipping at the token level rather than the sequence level, combining the issues of both token-level weighting and hard truncation.
L413: ## Appendix B Derivation of the Proposal Distribution
L414: 
L415: We derive the closed-form solution to the constrained optimization problem in cite134†14 .
L416: ###### Proposition B.1 (Solution to the Constrained Problem).
L417: 
L418: The solution to
L419: 
L420:  | $\displaystyle\min_{Q}\;$  | $\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\mu)+\alpha\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\pi)$  |
L421:  | s.t.   | $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[W]\leq C,\quad\textstyle\int Q=1$  |  | (33)
L422: 
L423: is given by
L424: 
L425:  | $$Q^{*}(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}\mu(\tau)^{1-\alpha}\,\pi(\tau)^{\alpha}\,\exp(-\lambda W(\tau)),$$  |  | (34)
L426: where $\lambda\geq 0$ is the Lagrange multiplier for the moment constraint and $Z$ is the normalization constant.
L427: ###### Proof.
L428: 
L429: The Lagrangian for this problem is:
L430: 
L431:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Q,\lambda,\gamma)=\;$  | $\displaystyle(1{-}\alpha)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\mu)+\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\pi)$  |
L432:  |  | $\displaystyle+\lambda\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{Q}[W]-C\bigr)+\gamma\bigl(\textstyle\int Q-1\bigr).$  |  | (35)
L433: 
L434: Expanding the KL divergences:
L435:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\int Q(\tau)\bigl[$  | $\displaystyle\log Q(\tau)-(1{-}\alpha)\log\mu(\tau)$  |
L436:  |  | $\displaystyle-\alpha\log\pi(\tau)+\lambda W(\tau)\bigr]d\tau+\text{const}.$  |  | (36)
L437: 
L438: Taking the functional derivative with respect to $Q$ and setting it to zero:
L439: 
L440:  | $\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta Q}=\;$  | $\displaystyle\log Q(\tau)+1-(1{-}\alpha)\log\mu(\tau)$  |
L441:  |  | $\displaystyle-\alpha\log\pi(\tau)+\lambda W(\tau)+\gamma=0.$  |  | (37)
L442: 
L443: Solving for $Q$:
L444:  | $\displaystyle\log Q^{*}(\tau)$  | $\displaystyle=(1{-}\alpha)\log\mu(\tau)+\alpha\log\pi(\tau)-\lambda W(\tau)+\text{const}$  |  | (38)
L445:  | $\displaystyle Q^{*}(\tau)$  | $\displaystyle\propto\mu(\tau)^{1-\alpha}\,\pi(\tau)^{\alpha}\,\exp(-\lambda W(\tau)).$  |  | (39)
L446: 
L447: Using $W=\pi/\mu$:
L448:  | $\displaystyle Q^{*}(\tau)$  | $\displaystyle\propto\mu(\tau)^{1-\alpha}\cdot\mu(\tau)^{\alpha}\cdot W(\tau)^{\alpha}\cdot\exp(-\lambda W(\tau))$  |  | (40)
L449:  |  | $\displaystyle=\mu(\tau)\cdot W(\tau)^{\alpha}\cdot\exp(-\lambda W(\tau)).$  |  | (41)
L450: 
L451: Comparing with $Q(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}\mu(\tau)\phi(W(\tau))$ from cite62†Equation 10 , we identify
L452: 
L453:  | $$\phi(W)=W^{\alpha}\cdot\exp(-\lambda W).$$  |  | (42)
L454: 
L455: ∎
L456: #### Shifted Form.
L457: 
L458: In practice, we use the shifted form $\phi(W)=W^{c_{1}}\exp(c_{2}(1-W))$, which satisfies $\phi(1)=1$. This ensures that on-policy samples receive unit weight. We treat $(c_{1},c_{2})$ as tunable hyperparameters, allowing flexibility beyond the specific values implied by the variational derivation.
L459: ## Appendix C Length Normalization Introduces Bias
L460: 
L461: We analyze how length normalization in sequence-level importance sampling introduces length-dependent bias that conflates distinct trajectories.
L462: #### Length-Dependent Bias in GSPO.
L463: 
L464: GSPO uses $\phi(W)=W^{1/T}$, which induces a proposal distribution that explicitly depends on the sequence length $T$:
L465: 
L466:  | $$Q_{\text{GSPO}}(\tau)\propto\mu(\tau)^{1-1/T}\cdot\pi(\tau)^{1/T}.$$  |  | (43)
L467: 
L468: As $T\to\infty$, $Q_{\text{GSPO}}\to\mu$: the normalized weight converges to $\exp(\mathbb{E}[\log\rho_{t}])$, a constant independent of the specific trajectory. This causes two fundamental problems:
L469: 
L470:   1. 1.
L471: Signal dissipation: All weights collapse toward a constant, losing discriminative power.
L472: 
L473:   2. 2.
L474: 
L475: Conflation of distinct sequences: Sequences with identical per-token statistics but different lengths receive identical weights, despite having different true importance weights.
L476: ###### Proposition C.1 (Conflation under Length Normalization).
L477: 
L478: For any two sequences $\tau_{1}$, $\tau_{2}$ with lengths $T_{1}\neq T_{2}$, if $\frac{\log W_{1}}{T_{1}}=\frac{\log W_{2}}{T_{2}}$, then GSPO assigns identical weights: $\phi_{\text{GSPO}}(W_{1})=\phi_{\text{GSPO}}(W_{2})$. However, their true importance weights differ: $W_{1}=e^{T_{1}\bar{r}}$ vs $W_{2}=e^{T_{2}\bar{r}}$ for $\bar{r}=\frac{\log W_{1}}{T_{1}}=\frac{\log W_{2}}{T_{2}}$.
L479: This conflation is problematic: a short sequence that is moderately off-policy and a long sequence that is severely off-policy may receive identical gradient weights, even though their contributions to the policy gradient should differ substantially.
L480: #### VESPO Avoids Length-Dependent Bias.
L481: VESPO uses $\phi(W)=W^{c_{1}}\exp(c_{2}(1{-}W))$ without any length normalization. The reshaping function depends only on the sequence-level importance weight $W$, not on the sequence length $T$. Two sequences with the same average per-token log-ratio $\bar{r}$ but different lengths receive different weights: the longer sequence has $W=e^{T\bar{r}}$, which is appropriately transformed by $\phi$.
L482: This preserves the discriminative power of the importance weight while the soft-shaping kernel controls variance through exponential suppression of extreme weights.
