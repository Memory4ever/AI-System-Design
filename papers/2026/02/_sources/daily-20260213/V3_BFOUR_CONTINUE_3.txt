L52: ###### Abstract
L53: Reward models learned from human preferences are central to aligning large language models (LLMs) via reinforcement learning from human feedback, yet they are often vulnerable to reward hacking due to noisy annotations and systematic biases such as response length or style. We propose Bayesian Non-Negative Reward Model (BNRM), a principled reward modeling framework that integrates non-negative factor analysis into Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model.
L204: Base Models and Training Details. In reward modeling experiments, we firstly used gemma-2b-it (cite96†Gemma, 2024b ) and gemma-2-2b-it (cite97†Gemma, 2024a ) as base models, accelerated by Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (cite98†Hu et al., 2021 ) for 2 epochs. In addition, we fully fine-tune the larger Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B (cite40†Liu et al., 2024a ) reward model on the Skywork-Preference-v0.2 (SP) dataset within 1 epoch.
L205: Best-of-N (BoN) sampling test was performed exclusively with the two Gemma models, and our BNRM obtained via LoRA as proxy reward models. In the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (cite99†Schulman et al., 2017 ) of real LLM fine-tuning, we applied LoRA to fine-tune Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (cite100†Meta, 2024 ) and OpenRLHF-Llama3-8B-SFT (cite101†Dong et al., 2024 ) for 1 epoch. More detailed training configurations are provided in Appendix cite30†C .

L250: ### 5.4 Reward Debiasing and Interpretability
L251: Despite being proposed without explicit debiasing supervision, BNRM possesses an inherent theoretical capacity to mitigate common reward biases. This section empirically validates debiasing capability by focusing on length and formatting biases.
L252: Beyond robust performance, we find that the global factors $\Phi$ can effectively rectify specific preference errors made by the local factors $\theta$, which brings a mechanistic interpretability that is typically unattainable in conventional scalar-based reward models. Unless otherwise stated, all reward models are LoRA fine-tuned on Gemma-2B-it using a 40K subset of the training data.
L253: Figure 5: Pearson correlation and mean reward score between response length and reward score on the RM-Bench Hard subset. The top plot shows the correlation between response length and reward score. The x-axis is log-scaled for better visual clarity. The bottom plot reports the average reward score within each length bucket, which visually highlights the non-negative property of our BT-BNRM.
L254: Length Debiasing and Format Debiasing. We evaluate the debiasing capabilities of reward models using the RM-Bench. Specifically, the RM-Bench Hard subset contains samples where rejected responses are deliberately crafted to be longer and better-formatted than the preferred ones. We leverage this subset to quantify the sensitivity of reward scores to response length using the Pearson correlation coefficient (cite110†Benesty et al., 2009 ).

L544: Figure cite182†10 presents the BoN results on the Gemma-2B and Gemma-2-2B Instruct models, where we adopted reward-model-Mistral-7B-instruct-Unified-Feedback (cite90†Yang et al., 2024b ) as our gold reward model to approximate true human preference scores. L545: Next, the top responses selected by the proxy scores were subsequently evaluated using the gold reward model. Furthermore, to balance quality and computational cost, we varied the KL-divergence budget from 0 to 5, corresponding to $N$ ranging from 1 to 405, according to $\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{BoN}}=\log N-\dfrac{N-1}{N}$. Notably, both proxy and gold scores were averaged and normalized to enable clear comparison.
L546: While Figure cite183†10(c) shows that the scores of all reward models increase as the KL divergence grows, with BT assigning the highest scores among the three.
