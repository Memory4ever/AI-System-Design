L42: # Unlocked Backpropagation using Wave Scattering
L43: 
L44: Christian Pehle Affiliation: Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, Cold Spring Harbor, NY 11724 Correspondence to: pehle@cshl.edu    Jean-Jacques Slotine Affiliation: Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA 02139 Correspondence to: jjs@mit.edu
L45: ###### Abstract
L46: Both the backpropagation algorithm in machine learning and the maximum principle in optimal control theory are posed as a two-point boundary problem, resulting in a “forward-backward” lock. We derive a reformulation of the maximum principle in optimal control theory as a hyperbolic initial value problem by introducing an additional “optimization time” dimension.
L47: We introduce counter-propagating wave variables with finite propagation speed and recast the optimization problem in terms of scattering relationships between them. This relaxation of the original problem can be interpreted as a physical system that equilibrates and changes its physical properties in order to minimize reflections. We discretize this continuum theory to derive a family of fully unlocked algorithms suitable for training neural networks.
L48: Different parameter dynamics, including gradient descent, can be derived by demanding dissipation and minimization of reflections at parameter ports. These results also imply that any physical substrate that supports the scattering and dissipation of waves can be interpreted as solving an optimization problem.

L50: 
L51: Machine Learning, ICML
L52: ## 1 Introduction
L53: The training of deep neural networks is traditionally dominated by the backpropagation algorithm. While highly successful, backpropagation imposes a strict sequential dependency known as the ”forward-backward lock”: the weights in earlier layers cannot be updated until the forward pass has reached the output and the error signal has propagated all the way back.
L54: This requirement for global locking prevents fully parallel updates and creates memory pressure, as the intermediate activations of every layer must be buffered to compute gradients during the backward pass. Furthermore, it lacks biological plausibility, as real synapses do not buffer past states while waiting for distant, global error signals to undergo plasticity.
L55: Indeed, enforcing this rigid orchestration and steep memory footprint is notoriously difficult to realize outside of a narrow set of conventional physical substrates (e.g. (cite30†Pai et al., 2023 )).
L56: Mathematically, backpropagation is the discrete realization of the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) (cite31†Bryson and Ho, 1969 )^{1}^{1} 1 Sections 2.1 and 2.2. It treats the training process as a Two-Point Boundary Value Problem (TPBVP): the network state is fixed at the input (time $t=0$), while the costate (adjoint) is fixed at the output (time $t=T$). Standard numerical methods often solve this iteratively, strictly enforcing the lock.
L57: Physical systems do not solve boundary value problems instantaneously. When a mechanical force is applied to one end of a steel rod, the other end does not move immediately. Instead, a wave of stress propagates through the material at the speed of sound. The system reaches equilibrium not through a global solver, but through local interactions and the eventual dissipation of transient energy.
L58: In fact this is the principle by which any physical system supporting the propagation and dissipation of wave-like excitations adapts. Taking the finite speed of propagation into account is also critical for control (cite32†Niemeyer and Slotine, 1991 ) and our approach is inspired by the introduction of wave-variables in this context.
L59: In this work, we propose a relaxation of the optimal control problem underpinning deep learning. By introducing an auxiliary “optimization time” dimension $\tau$, we lift the training dynamics from a trajectory on a line to a field theory on a $(1+1)$-dimensional worldsheet. We reinterpret the neural network not as a function composition, but as a physical transmission line.
L60: Signals propagate as counter-propagating waves with finite speed, and the enforcement of optimality conditions occurs via local wave scattering.
L61: This reformulation yields the following contributions:
L62: 
L63:   1. 1.
L64: 
L65: Unlocked Parallelism: We derive a hyperbolic relaxation of the Maximum Principle where layers are decoupled. Information propagates at a finite speed, allowing all layers to update their states and parameters simultaneously based solely on local variables.
L66: 
L67:   2. 2.
L68: Wave-PMP Algorithm: We discretize this continuum theory into a concrete training algorithm (Alg. cite33†1 ) that handles variable layer widths via impedance-matching junctions, enabling fully asynchronous distributed training.
L69: 
L70:   3. 3.
L71: 
L72: The Principle of Minimal Reflection: We provide a physical derivation for parameter update laws. We show that Gradient Descent, Momentum, and Newton’s method arise naturally from the boundary condition of minimizing wave reflections at the parameter ports.
L73: This perspective suggests that the “forward-backward lock” is an artifact of taking the speed of information propagation to infinity. By restoring a finite speed of propagation, we recover a physical system that supports scattering, dissipation, and local relaxation — concepts that may bridge the gap between backpropagation in artificial systems and physical learning systems.
L74: ## 2 Maximum Principle in Wave-Variables
L75: ###### Theorem 2.1 (Pontryagin Maximum Principle).
L76: 
L77: Consider the control problem of minimizing a cost functional
L78: 
L79:  | $$J=\Phi(x(T))+\int_{0}^{T}L(x(t),u(t))\mathrm{dt}$$  |  | (1)
L80: 
L81: where $x$ is the state and $u$ the control input, subject to the dynamics
L82: 
L83:  | $$\dot{x}=f(x,u).$$  |
L84: 
L85: Let $H(x,\lambda,u)=L(x,u)+\lambda^{\top}f(x,u)$ be the Hamiltonian.
L86: 
L87: The necessary conditions for optimality are
L88:  | $\displaystyle\dot{x}$  | $\displaystyle=\nabla_{\lambda}H=f(x,u),\,x(0)=x_{0}$  |  | (2)
L89:  | $\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}$  | $\displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}H,\quad\lambda(T)=\nabla_{x}\Phi(x(T))$  |  | (3)
L90:  | $\displaystyle 0$  | $\displaystyle=\nabla_{u}H$  |  | (4)
L91: 
L92: These equations constitute a Two-Point Boundary Value Problem (TPBVP) due to the split boundary conditions at $t=0$ and $t=T$.
L93: We introduce an auxiliary ”optimization time” dimension $\tau$. The problem is lifted from a the trajectory line $[0,T]$ to a $1+1$ dimensional Worldsheet $\Sigma$.
L94: ###### Definition 2.2 (Worldsheet $\Sigma$).
L95: 
L96: The domain of our fields is the strip $\Sigma=[0,T]_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{\tau}$. We equip $\Sigma$ with a Lorentz metric,
L97: 
L98:  | $$ds_{\Sigma}^{2}=-c^{2}\,\mathrm{d\tau}^{2}+\mathrm{dt}^{2}$$  |
L99: 
L100: where $t$ is the physical time of the control system, $\tau$ is the algorithmic time of the solver and $c$ is the speed of information propagation.
L101: The main idea is to use the additional optimization dimension $\tau$, to formulate relaxation dynamics. We introduce continuous time residuals for the maximum principle
L102: 
L103:  | $\displaystyle r_{x}(t,\tau)$  | $\displaystyle=\partial_{t}x(t,\tau)-\nabla_{\lambda}H$  |  | (5)
L104:  | $\displaystyle r_{\lambda}(t,\tau)$  | $\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\lambda(t,\tau)+\nabla_{x}H$  |  | (6)
L105:  | $\displaystyle r_{u}(t,\tau)$  | $\displaystyle=\nabla_{u}H$  |  | (7)
L106: where we lifted the state and co-state to fields on the world sheet.
L107: 
L108: Let $M$ be a positive definite metric (symmetric, $M\succ 0$). Factor it as
L109: 
L110:  | $$M=\Theta^{\top}\Theta$$  |  | (8)
L111: 
L112: with $\Theta$ invertible (often chosen diagonal or triangular in computation).
L113: ###### Definition 2.3 (Wave variables and wave residuals).
L114: 
L115: Let $\Theta\in GL(n)$ and define
L116: 
L117:  | $$p=\Theta\,\partial_{t}x,\,q=\Theta^{-\top}\lambda,\,w_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(p\pm q).$$  |  | (9)
L118: 
L119: Define the wave residuals
L120: 
L121:  | $$E_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\Theta r_{x}\pm\Theta^{-\top}r_{\lambda}\bigr).$$  |  | (10)
L122: 
L123: This change of variables is invertible
L124: 
L125:  | $$\dot{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Theta^{-1}(w_{+}+w_{-}),\,\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Theta^{\top}(w_{+}-w_{-})$$  |  | (11)
L126: ###### Proposition 2.4 (Cross-coupled $\tau$-dynamics yields forward/backward waves).
L127: 
L128: Assume $\Theta$ is independent of $t$. If $(p,q)$ evolve by
L129: 
L130:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}p+c\,\partial_{t}q$  | $\displaystyle=-c\,\Theta r_{x}$  |
L131:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}q+c\,\partial_{t}p$  | $\displaystyle=-c\,\Theta^{-\top}r_{\lambda},$  |
L132: 
L133: then the wave variables satisfy
L134:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}w_{+}+c\,\partial_{t}w_{+}$  | $\displaystyle=-c\,E_{+}$  |  | (12)
L135:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}w_{-}-c\,\partial_{t}w_{-}$  | $\displaystyle=-c\,E_{-}.$  |  | (13)
L136: 
L137: We choose the $\tau$-dynamics such that (i) information propagates at finite speed $c$, and (ii) the wave energy is non-increasing up to boundary power injections, yielding a stable relaxation (see cite34†2.6 ).
L138: The motivation for introducing wave variables $w_{\pm}$ is in part because they diagonalize the pairing
L139: 
L140:  | $$\lambda^{\top}\dot{x}=\frac{1}{2}(w^{\top}_{+}w_{+}-w^{\top}_{-}w_{-}).$$  |  | (14)
L141: 
L142: In the literature $\dot{x}$ is sometimes called flow $f$ and $\lambda$ effort $e$ (cite35†Van Der Schaft et al., 2014 ). They are conjugate variables whose product is power.
L143: ###### Lemma 2.5 (Boundary conditions in scattering form).
L144: 
L145: Fix a boundary time $t_{b}\in\{0,T\}$ and suppose that a boundary condition prescribes either flow $f(t_{b},\tau)=f_{b}(\tau)$ or effort $e(t_{b},\tau)=e_{b}(\tau).$
L146: 
L147: Then it can be written as an affine relation between the outgoing and incoming waves:
L148:  |  | $\displaystyle w_{+}(t_{b},\tau)=-\,w_{-}(t_{b},\tau)+\sqrt{2}\,\Theta(t_{b},\tau)\,f_{b}(\tau),$  |
L149:  |  | $\displaystyle w_{-}(t_{b},\tau)=\;\,w_{+}(t_{b},\tau)-\sqrt{2}\,\Theta(t_{b},\tau)^{-\top}\,e_{b}(\tau).$  |
L150: 
L151: The boundary condition (cite36†3 ) prescribes an effort. By lemma  cite37†2.5 :
L152: 
L153:  | $$w_{-}(T,\cdot)=w_{+}(T,\cdot)-\sqrt{2}\,\Theta(T,\cdot)^{-\top}\,\nabla\Phi\bigl(x(T,\cdot)\bigr).$$  |  | (15)
L154: ###### Theorem 2.6 (Wave energy balance and dissipation).
L155: 
L156: Let $w_{\pm}(t,\tau)$ satisfy the wave equations
L157: 
L158:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}w_{+}+c\,\partial_{t}w_{+}$  | $\displaystyle=-c\,E_{+},$  |  | (16)
L159:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}w_{-}-c\,\partial_{t}w_{-}$  | $\displaystyle=-c\,E_{-},$  |  | (17)
L160: 
L161: on $t\in[0,T]$, $\tau\geq 0$. Define the wave energy
L162: 
L163:  | $$V(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\|w_{+}(t,\tau)\|^{2}+\|w_{-}(t,\tau)\|^{2}\Big)\,dt.$$  |  | (18)
L164: Then $V$ satisfies the exact balance identity
L165: 
L166:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}V(\tau)$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2}\Big(\|w_{+}(0,\tau)\|^{2}-\|w_{-}(0,\tau)\|^{2}\Big)$  |  | (19)
L167:  |  | $\displaystyle+\frac{c}{2}\Big(\|w_{-}(T,\tau)\|^{2}-\|w_{+}(T,\tau)\|^{2}\Big)$  |
L168:  |  | $\displaystyle-c\int_{0}^{T}\big(w_{+}^{\top}E_{+}+w_{-}^{\top}E_{-}\big)\,dt.$  |  | (20)
L169: 
L170: In particular, if the boundary conditions are passive in scattering form, i.e.
L171:  | $\displaystyle\|w_{+}(0,\tau)\|$  | $\displaystyle\leq\|w_{-}(0,\tau)\|,$  |  | (21)
L172:  | $\displaystyle\|w_{-}(T,\tau)\|$  | $\displaystyle\leq\|w_{+}(T,\tau)\|$  |  | (22)
L173: 
L174: (for all $\tau$), and if the distributed forcing is dissipative in the sense that
L175: 
L176:  | $$\int_{0}^{T}\big(w_{+}^{\top}E_{+}+w_{-}^{\top}E_{-}\big)\,dt\;\geq\;0,$$  |  | (23)
L177: 
L178: then $\partial_{\tau}V(\tau)\leq 0$.
L179: 
L180: We can rewrite the residual for the control $r_{u}$ in terms of wave variables
L181:  | $$r_{u}=\nabla_{u}L+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(w_{+}-w_{-})^{\top}\Theta\nabla_{u}f.$$  |  | (24)
L182: 
L183: At each $t$, view the control update direction $v(t,\tau)=\partial_{\tau}u(t,\tau)$ as a flow and the stationarity residual $r_{u}(t,\tau)=\nabla_{u}H$ as an effort. Their instantaneous port power is
L184: 
L185:  | $$P_{u}(t,\tau)=r_{u}(t,\tau)^{\top}v(t,\tau).$$  |  | (25)
L186: If we define a control metric $M_{u}$ (symmetric, $M_{u}\succ 0$) and write $M_{u}=\Theta^{\top}_{u}\Theta_{u}$, then we can define wave variables for the control (sign convention based on descent)
L187: 
L188:  | $\displaystyle z_{+}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\Theta_{u}\partial_{\tau}u-\Theta_{u}^{-\top}\nabla_{u}H)$  |  | (26)
L189:  | $\displaystyle z_{-}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\Theta_{u}\partial_{\tau}u+\Theta_{u}^{-\top}\nabla_{u}H)$  |  | (27)
L190: 
L191: Then the power factorizes as
L192:  | $$P_{u}(t,\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|z_{+}(t,\tau)\|^{2}-\|z_{-}(t,\tau)\|^{2}\big).$$  |  | (28)
L193: 
L194: Imposing a passive termination means $\|z_{+}\|\leq\|z_{-}\|$ and hence $P_{u}\leq 0$.
L195: 
L196: We observe that demanding that no reflection occurs $z_{-}=0$ corresponds to
L197: 
L198:  | $$\partial_{\tau}u=-\Theta^{-1}\Theta_{u}^{-\top}\nabla_{u}H=-M^{-1}_{u}\nabla_{u}H.$$  |  | (29)
L199: Choosing $M=\frac{1}{\eta}I$, with $\eta>0$ the learning rate, recovers gradient descent flow in optimization time $\tau$. Other laws for the control update can be considered.
L200: ## 3 Physical Interpretation
L201: The lifted dynamics on the worldsheet $\Sigma=[0,T]_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{\tau}$ can be interpreted as a transmission line in the physical time direction $t$, evolving in the solver time $\tau$. The characteristic variables $w_{+}$ and $w_{-}$ represent right- and left-traveling waves (forward and backward in $t$) with finite propagation speed $c$. Local PMP residuals act as distributed sources that inject corrections into the waves, while any explicit damping in the $\tau$-dynamics acts as dissipation.
L202: At fixed $\tau$, the waves encode a candidate primal/adjoint pair $(\partial_{\tau}x(\cdot,\tau),\lambda(\cdot,\tau))$ via the inverse wave transform. A solution of the maximum principle corresponds to vanishing residuals $r_{x}=r_{\lambda}=r_{u}=0$, equivalently vanishing wave residuals $E_{\pm}=0$.
L203: Boundary conditions appear as terminations of the line. At $t=0$ the input boundary enforces the prescribed initial state, and at $t=T$ the terminal boundary enforces the transversality condition $\lambda(T)=\nabla\phi(x(T))$ (or, in the learning case, $\lambda_{N}=\nabla\ell(x_{N},y)$). These terminations induce reflections: incoming waves are computed from outgoing waves by satisfying the boundary constraints. When the termination is passive, the boundary does not inject net energy into the system.
L204: ## 4 Training Algorithm for Neural Networks
L205: 
L206: Based on the continuous time formulation we can derive a family of optimization algorithms for artificial neural networks.
L207: We will begin by describing the algorithm in the simplest formulation. To simplify notation and fix ideas we will focus on a simple feedforward network, modifications to more general cases should be self-evident. The basic idea is to discretize the world-sheet $\Sigma_{t,\tau}=[0,T]\times[0,\infty)$, with the time variable $t_{k}=k\Delta t$ now related to layer depth $k$. We then want to again introduce a finite propagation speed and define an unlocked parallel update scheme.
L208: To do so we will also go from a continuous time dynamics to a discretized layer map and interpret changes in dimension as internal junctions, where the transmission line changes dimension.
L209: 
L210: The resulting algorithm cite33†1 allows parallel unlocked updates in all phases and requires only local communication. Other variants could be derived by starting from continuous time formulation and making other choices of worldsheet and PDE discretization.
L211: Algorithm 1 Unlocked training by worldsheet upwinding + PMP sources
L212: 
L213: 0:  mini-batch $(x_{\mathrm{in}},y)$, maps $\{f_{k}(\cdot;\theta_{k})\}$, penalties $\{R_{k}\}$, impedances $\{\Theta_{k}\}$, stepsizes $\alpha,\eta$, Courant $\nu\in(0,1]$.
L214: 
L215: 1:  Initialize waves $\{w_{+,k}^{0},w_{-,k}^{0}\}_{k=0}^{N}$ (e.g. zeros) and parameters $\{\theta_{k}^{0}\}$.
L216: 
L217: 2:  for $n=0,1,2,\dots$ do
L218:

L221:

L223: 
L224: 6:   (B) Reconstruct states (parallel over $k$)
L225: 
L226: 7:   for $k=0$ to $N$ in parallel do
L227:

L229:

L231: 
L232: 10:   end for
L233: 11:   (C) Local PMP residuals + source injection (parallel)
L234:

L236:

L238: 
L239: 14:    $E_{+,k}^{n}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\Theta_{k}r_{x,k}^{n}+\Theta_{k}^{-\top}r_{\lambda,k}^{n}\bigr)$
L240: 
L241: 15:    $E_{-,k}^{n}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\Theta_{k}r_{x,k}^{n}-\Theta_{k}^{-\top}r_{\lambda,k}^{n}\bigr)$
L242:

L245: 
L246: 18:   end for
L247:

L249:

L253: 
L254: 22:    $\theta_{k}^{n+1}\leftarrow\theta_{k}^{n}-\eta\,r_{\theta,k}^{n}$
L255: 
L256: 23:   end for
L257: 
L258: 24:   (E) Boundary scattering (ports)
L259: 25:   Enforce $x_{0}=x_{\mathrm{in}}$:  $w_{+,0}^{n+1}\leftarrow\sqrt{2}\,\Theta_{0}x_{\mathrm{in}}-w_{-,0}^{n+1}$.
L260: 
L261: 26:   Enforce $\lambda_{N}=\nabla\ell$: $w_{-,N}^{n+1}\leftarrow{w_{+,N}^{n+1}-\sqrt{2}\,\Theta_{N}^{-\top}\nabla_{x_{N}}\ell(x_{N}^{n},y)}$.
L262: 
L263: 27:  end for
L264: ### 4.1 Wave variable discretization
L265: ###### Definition 4.1 (Naive Discretized Worldsheet).
L266: 
L267: We introduce a $(t,\tau)$ grid. Let
L268: 
L269:  | $\displaystyle t_{k}$  | $\displaystyle=k\Delta t,\quad k=0,\ldots,N,\quad N\Delta t=T$  |
L270:  | $\displaystyle\tau^{n}$  | $\displaystyle=n\Delta\tau,\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots$  |
L271: 
L272: and define the courant number
L273: 
L274:  | $$\nu=\frac{c\Delta\tau}{\Delta t}.$$  |  | (30)
L275: We can discretize the equations in section cite7†2 in several ways. Perhaps the simplest are to discretize the partial derivatives $\partial_{t},\partial_{\tau}$ in (cite41†12 ) and (cite42†13 ). For $w_{+}$ and $w_{-}$ we can use forward and backward (upwind) differences
L276: 
L277:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{+}(t_{k},\tau^{n})\approx\frac{w^{n}_{+,k}-w^{n}_{+,k-1}}{\Delta t}$  |  | (31)
L278:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{-}(t_{k},\tau^{n})\approx\frac{w^{n}_{-,k+1}-w^{n}_{-,k}}{\Delta t}$  |  | (32)
L279: and we can use forward Euler in $\tau$
L280: 
L281:  | $\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}w_{\pm}(t_{k},\tau^{n})\approx\frac{w^{n+1}_{\pm,k}-w^{n}_{\pm,k}}{\Delta\tau}$  |  | (33)
L282: 
L283: Inserting into (cite41†12 )–(cite42†13 ) we obtain
L284: 
L285:  | $\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{+,k}$  | $\displaystyle=(1-\nu)\,w^{n}_{+,k}+\nu\,w^{n}_{+,k-1}-c\,\Delta\tau\,E^{n}_{+,k},$  |  | (34)
L286:  | $\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{-,k}$  | $\displaystyle=(1-\nu)\,w^{n}_{-,k}+\nu\,w^{n}_{-,k+1}-c\,\Delta\tau\,E^{n}_{-,k}.$  |  | (35)
L287: Here $\nu=c\Delta\tau/\Delta t$ is the Courant number and $E^{n}_{\pm,k}=E_{\pm}(t_{k},\tau^{n})$. In particular, when $\nu=1$ the transport becomes a pure shift:
L288: 
L289:  | $\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{+,k}$  | $\displaystyle=w^{n}_{+,k-1}-\Delta t\,E^{n}_{+,k},$  |  | (36)
L290:  | $\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{-,k}$  | $\displaystyle=w^{n}_{-,k+1}-\Delta t\,E^{n}_{-,k}.$  |  | (37)
L291: Instead of using finite differences for the advection, we could also use the exact characteristic solution of the homogeneous part or use other discretization approaches. More sophisticated world-sheet discretizations and solvers are also possible.
L292: 
L293: At each depth node $k$ we choose a metric factor $\Theta_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}(n_{k})$ (where $n_{k}$ is the layer width at node $k$). We use the same scattering coordinates as in Definition cite43†9 , but now indexed by depth:
L294:  | $$w_{\pm,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\Theta_{k}\,x_{k}\pm\Theta_{k}^{-\top}\lambda_{k}\Big),$$  |  | (38)
L295: 
L296: with inverse
L297: 
L298:  | $\displaystyle x_{k}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Theta_{k}^{-1}(w_{+,k}+w_{-,k})$  |  | (39)
L299:  | $\displaystyle\lambda_{k}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Theta_{k}^{\top}(w_{+,k}-w_{-,k}).$  |  | (40)
L300: 
L301: This choice keeps the boundary conditions in the same scattering form as Lemma cite37†2.5 .
L302: ### 4.2 Upwind transport for constant width
L303: 
L304: Discretizing the advection part of (cite41†12 )–(cite42†13 ) by forward Euler in $\tau$ and upwind differences in $t$ yields the usual transport step
L305: 
L306:  | $\displaystyle\bar{w}_{+,k}^{n}$  | $\displaystyle=(1-\nu)\,w_{+,k}^{n}+\nu\,w_{+,k-1}^{n},\,k=1,\dots,N,$  |  | (41)
L307:  | $\displaystyle\bar{w}_{-,k}^{n}$  | $\displaystyle=(1-\nu)\,w_{-,k}^{n}+\nu\,w_{-,k+1}^{n},\,k=0,\dots,N-1,$  |  | (42)
L308: with $\bar{w}_{+,0}^{n}:=w_{+,0}^{n}$ and $\bar{w}_{-,N}^{n}:=w_{-,N}^{n}$. When all widths are constant ($n_{k}\equiv n$), these are well-typed shifts in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.
