L151: #### Lyapunov exponents and random matrix products
L152: From a theoretical perspective, our work is inspired by classical results on random matrix products (cite68†Furstenberg and Kesten, 1960 ; cite74†Le Page, 1982 ). Lyapunov exponents arising from such products play a central role in probability theory and dynamical systems. Our work differs from these classical results by analyzing compositions of random matrices and nonlinear activation functions. This difference fundamentally changes the dynamics, meaning that existing linear results do not directly apply.
L153: To our knowledge, our work provides the first concrete non-linear Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem.

L166: #### Edge of Chaos
L167: In the literature, the edge of chaos refers to a critical phase transition of neural network, where the parameters are calibrated to a regime that maximizes stability and learning performance. Early papers studying this phenomenon were (cite78†Bertschinger et al., 2004 ) and (cite79†Boedecker et al., 2011 ). More recently, edge of chaos has been analyzed in the context of infinite-width networks using mean field theory (cite80†Poole et al., 2016 ; cite81†Schoenholz et al., 2017 ).
L168: In contrast, the Lyapunov exponent studied in this paper quantifies the edge of chaos for the regime of infinite-depth neural networks.

L170: #### Probabilistic Setup and Notation
L171: 
L172: For our results to be valid, we require that the weight matrices $W_{\ell}$ are independent and identically distributed. With this assumption, the stochastic process $(X_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}$ is a Markov chain in the sense that the transition probabilities do not depend on the layer.
L173: 
L174: Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$, the space of real $d\times d$ matrices. We work with the probability space
L175:  | $$(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})=(M_{d}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{N}},\mathscr{B}(M_{d}(\mathbb{R}))^{\mathbb{N}},\mu^{\mathbb{N}}),$$  |  | (5)
L176: that is the space of $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$-sequences endowed with the the natural product $\sigma$-algebra and the product measure coming from $\mu$. So every $\omega\in\Omega$ is a sequence of matrices $\omega=(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\ldots)$ and for a given $\omega\in\Omega$ we denote by $W_{n}(\omega)$ the projection of $\omega$ to the n-th coordinate. So (cite82†1 ) formally means that for $\omega\in\Omega$ we have for a fixed $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,
L177:  | $$X_{\ell}(\omega)=\phi(W_{\ell}(\omega)X_{\ell-1}(\omega))\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{0}=x_{0}.$$  |  | (6)

L187: 
L188:   2. (2)
L189: 
L190: Finite Second Moment: Each distribution has a finite second moment: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u^{2}p_{ij}(u)\,du<\infty.$
L191: 
L192:   3. (3)
L193: Local Positivity: The density is strictly positive in a neighborhood of the identity matrix. That is, there exists $\epsilon>0$ such that for each $i,j$ it holds that $\inf_{|u|\leq\epsilon}p_{ij}(\delta_{ij}+u)>0,$ where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta defined as $\delta_{ij}=1$ if $i=j$ and otherwise $\delta_{ij}=0$.
L194: For example, when $p_{ij}$ is $\mathcal{N}(m_{ij},\sigma_{ij})$ with $m_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\sigma_{ij}>0$ or $\mathrm{Unif}([-a_{ij},b_{ij}])$ for $a_{ij},b_{ij}>0$, then Assumption cite83†3.1 is satisfied. However, when $p_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,b]$ for some $b>0$, then Assumption cite83†3.1 (3) does not hold and we will actually show that our main theorems fail in this case.

L209: #### Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem
L210: 
L211: We are now ready to state our version of the law of large numbers.
L212: ###### Theorem 3.3.
L213: 
L214: (Law of large numbers for $|X_{\ell}|$) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$ satisfying either Assumption cite83†3.1 or Assumption cite86†3.2 . Let $(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})$ be as in (cite87†5 ), $(X_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}$ be as in (cite88†6 ) and let $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ be a Leaky ReLU activation function with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$.
L215: Then there exists a real number $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}$ depending on $\mu$ and $\phi$ such that for all $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}$ and for $\mathbb{P}_{\mu}$-almost all $\omega\in\Omega$ it holds that
L216: 
L217:  | $$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}(\omega)|=\lambda_{\mu,\phi}.$$  |  | (9)
L218: 
L219: The convergence is moreover in $L^{1}$, uniformly for $x_{0}$ of constant modulus, i.e.,
L220:  | $$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}}\left[\bigg|\frac{1}{\ell}\log\frac{|X_{\ell}(\omega)|}{x_{0}}-\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\bigg|\right]=0.$$  |
L221: 
L222: We now formally define the Lyapunov exponent of the neural network (cite82†1 ) as the number $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}$ from (cite89†9 ). Next, we state a version of the central limit theorem in this setting.
L223: ###### Theorem 3.4.
L224: 
L225: (Central Limit Theorem for $|X_{\ell}|$) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$ satisfying either Assumption cite83†3.1 or Assumption cite86†3.2 . Let $(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})$ be as in (cite87†5 ), $(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}$ be as in (cite88†6 ) and let $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ be a Leaky ReLU activation function with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$.
L226: Then there exists $\gamma_{\mu,\phi}>0$ such that for any $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}$ it holds that as $\ell\to\infty$,
L227: 
L228:  | $$\frac{\log|X_{\ell}|-\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{\mu,\phi}),$$  |
L229: 
L230: where the convergence holds in distribution and $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}$ is from Theorem cite66†3.3 .
L231: 
L232: Theorem cite66†3.3 is visualized in Figure cite90†1 and Theorem cite77†3.4 with Figure cite91†2 .
L233: We remark that with the employed methods, as discussed in Appendix cite33†C , we can establish a Law of Large Numbers and a Central Limit Theorem for more general measures $\mu$ and for any activation function of the form $\phi(x)=\max(\alpha_{1}x,\alpha_{2}x)$ with $\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$. However, the results do not carry over to other standard activation functions.
L234: The proof idea is explained in Appendix cite34†C.1 , also exposing what part of the argument fails for other activation functions. In particular, we show in Lemma cite92†3.5 that the Law of Large numbers (Theorem cite66†3.3 ) fails for ReLU activations and the Central Limit Theorem (Theorem cite77†3.4 ) is false for $\tanh$, as we demonstrate in Figure cite93†5 in the appendix.
L235: #### Counterexamples to Law of Large Numbers
L236: 
L237: The Law of Large Numbers and especially the Central Limit Theorem as stated above are subtle results. Indeed, we observe that the conclusion of our Law of Large Numbers (Theorem cite66†3.3 ) is not valid in the stated form for ReLU activation functions or for the Leaky ReLU case when all the matrices in the support of $\mu$ have positive entries. We state the following precise lemma.
L238: ###### Lemma 3.5.
L239: 
L240: (Counterexample to Law of Large Numbers for $|X_{\ell}|$) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$. Let $(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})$ be as in (cite87†5 ), $(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}$ be as in (cite88†6 ). Then the conclusion of Theorem cite66†3.3 is false under either of the following assumptions:
L241: 
L242:   1. (1)
L243: 
L244: (ReLU activation function) When $\phi(x)=\max(x,0)$ and the entries of $\mu$ are independent and all distributed as $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})$ for $\sigma>0$.
L245:   2. (2)
L246: 
L247: (Measure supported on matrices with positive entries) When $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ for $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$ and the entries of $\mu$ are independent and all distributed as $\mathrm{Unif}[0,a]$ for some $a>0$.

It is remarkable that for the most standard initializations and for Leaky ReLU activation functions, we can actually give effective formulas for the Lyapunov exponents. L254:

L256:

L260:  | $$I(d,\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-\frac{1}{2^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2t}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha^{2}t}}\right)^{d}}{2t}dt.$$  |  | (11)

L208: ### 4.2 Main Results on Accuracy and Speed
L209: Table cite54†1 summarizes the performance of different decoding methods across both models and three benchmarks. In addition, we plot the variation of average accuracy and SpeedUp among these decoding methods in Figure cite59†3 for better illustration. In most settings, PBS consistently improves accuracy over greedy decoding, with Dream-7B achieving gains of up to $+7.3\%$ on HumanEval.
L210: However, this quality improvement comes at the cost of nearly doubled inference latency (average SpeedUp $\times 0.54$), as exploring multiple decoding paths increases computation.
L211: PBS with parallel token decoding ($n=2$) restores decoding speed to the same level as greedy decoding, yet the average accuracy also decreases significantly compared to PBS with single token. We attribute this to the fact that forcing a fixed number of tokens to be decoded in parallel can undermine the confidence-based selection that underlies PBS’s effectiveness.
L212: In contrast, SOAR switches between search and parallel decoding based on confidence, avoiding excessive search that affects decoding speed while also preventing mandatory parallel decoding from harming global confidence, thereby achieving a balance between speed and quality improvement.
L213: ### 4.3 Ablation Study
L214: Confidence Threshold. We examine how the confidence threshold $\tau$ affects decoding quality and speed. Figure. cite60†4 (a) plots the cumulative distribution of token confidence scores on GSM8K under standard decoding, showing a long tail—about 50% of tokens have probability $>0.95$. We vary $\tau\in[0.8,0.98]$ and evaluate accuracy and SpeedUp with coding (MBPP) and math (GSM8K) tasks on Dream-7B.
L215: As shown in Figure. cite60†4 (b)-(c), raising $\tau$ from $0.8$ to $0.98$ steadily improves accuracy but reduces decoding speed. Notably, for $\tau>0.8$, SOAR consistently beats standard decoding in accuracy while matching or exceeding its speed, demonstrating robustness. We conducted the same analysis on LLaDA-8B, and ultimately set $\tau=0.95$ for LLaDA-8B and $\tau=0.9$ for Dream-7B.

L217: Beam Size. We further investigate whether expanding the beam size can yield additional performance gains. Figure cite62†5 illustrates the trade-off between generation quality and inference speed under different beam sizes (ranging from 1 to 4) on the HumanEval benchmark using the Dream-7B model. Setting beam size to 1 corresponds to using only confidence-based parallel decoding.
L218: While increasing the beam size may offer modest improvements in accuracy, the computational overhead grows linearly with beam width, resulting in significant slowdowns in inference speed. Therefore, we select a beam size of 2 as the default setting to balance quality and efficiency in our experiments.
L219: ### 4.4 Analysis of Confidence and AR-ness
L220: 
L221: cite63†Image: Refer to caption Figure 6: Left: Accuracy with average confidence. Right: Accuracy with negative Global AR-ness
L222: 
L223: Confidence of Decoding Sequence. To test the hypothesis proposed in Section cite6†1 —Can we improve decoding quality by exploring alternative unmasking sequences with higher confidence beyond greedy selection?—we analyze the average confidence of decoded sequences.
L224: ###### Definition 4.1.
L225: 
L226: Average Confidence: For each sample, we extract the reasoning tokens that precede the answer keyword (“answer” token in GSM8K). Each token’s confidence score is defined as the model’s maximum softmax probability. The sample-level average confidence is computed as the mean of these token-wise scores. We then average across all samples for each decoding method to obtain the method-level average confidence.
L227: Figure cite64†6 (a) illustrates the relationship between average confidence and accuracy for various decoding methods on the GSM8K benchmark using Dream-7B. Here, “Parallel” refers to decoding exactly two tokens per step, “Adaptive Parallel” decides whether to perform parallel decoding based on confidence thresholds, and the maximum sequence length is 512.
L228: Our results show a positive correlation between accuracy and average confidence. PBS achieves an average accuracy 1.9% higher than Greedy Search, confirming our hypothesis. In contrast, Parallel decoding forces the generation of two tokens per step regardless of confidence, which significantly lowers average confidence and leads to a notable accuracy drop.
L229: When Parallel decoding is combined with PBS, the confidence gains from PBS are negated by the confidence loss from Parallel decoding, resulting in a 1.3% accuracy reduction compared to PBS alone. SOAR, however, dynamically switches between Parallel decoding and PBS based on confidence, preserving higher average confidence and maintaining competitive accuracy.
L230: AR-ness of Decoding Sequence. We further investigate why SOAR yields better decoding sequences using another metric: the Global AR-ness proposed by (cite65†Gong et al., 2025b ), which quantifies how much the unmasking schedule of a diffusion model resembles an autoregressive pattern, specifically, whether it follows a “left-first” pattern.
L231: ###### Definition 4.2.
L232: Global AR-ness: At each decoding step $t$, we examine whether the predicted token lies within the first $k$ masked positions. The Global AR-ness@$k$ is defined as the average ratio of such steps across the entire decoding process, measuring the tendency to always unmask the earliest remaining token and thus capturing a left-to-right filling strategy. This ratio increases with $k$, as the criterion becomes easier to satisfy when more early positions are allowed.
L233: A higher value indicates that the generation is more autoregressive.
L234: We set $k=5$ and compute the mean Global AR-ness across all samples. Figure cite64†6 (b) illustrates the relationship between the negative Global AR-ness and accuracy, revealing a negative correlation: methods with lower Global AR-ness (i.e., less left-to-right bias) tend to achieve higher accuracy. We hypothesize that this may be because SOAR can mitigate the “entropy sink” issue in DLMs.
L235: Specifically, the model is inherently biased toward tokens immediately to the right of the given prefix, as these positions receive stronger positional signals and closer context, leading to disproportionately high confidence. This bias may limit the DLM’s ability to explore potentially better decoding paths. Although SOAR is not explicitly designed to address this problem, by enabling position beam search, it can alleviate the issue.

L268: ## 5 Conclusion
L269: We introduce S earch O r A ccele R ate (SOAR), an adaptive inference framework that dynamically balances generation quality and speed for Diffusion Language Models. By allowing the model to widen its search when uncertain and accelerate when confident, SOAR consistently improves generation quality across coding and mathematical reasoning tasks while maintaining competitive inference speed. Extensive experiments confirm its robustness to different unmask metrics, sequence lengths, and base models.
L270: As a training-free method, SOAR offers a practical and effective solution to the quality-speed trade-off in DLM inference.
