L97: # Learning Self-Interpretation from Interpretability Artifacts:
L98: Training Lightweight Adapters on Vector-Label Pairs
L99: Keenan Pepper Affiliation: AE Studio Correspondence to: keenan@ae.studio    Alex McKenzie Affiliation: AE Studio    Florin Pop Affiliation: AE Studio    Stijn Servaes Affiliation: AE Studio    Martin Leitgab Affiliation: AE Studio    Mike Vaiana Affiliation: AE Studio    Judd Rosenblatt Affiliation: AE Studio    Michael S. L100: ###### Abstract
L101: Self-interpretation methods prompt language models to describe their own internal states, but remain unreliable due to hyperparameter sensitivity. We show that training lightweight adapters on interpretability artifacts, while keeping the LM entirely frozen, yields reliable self-interpretation across tasks and model families.
L102: A scalar affine adapter with just $d_{\text{model}}+1$ parameters suffices: trained adapters generate sparse autoencoder feature labels that outperform the training labels themselves (71% vs 63% generation scoring at 70B scale), identify topics with 94% recall@1 versus 1% for untrained baselines, and decode bridge entities in multi-hop reasoning that appear in neither prompt nor response, surfacing implicit reasoning without chain-of-thought.
L103: The learned bias vector alone accounts for 85% of improvement, and simpler adapters generalize better than more expressive alternatives. Controlling for model knowledge via prompted descriptions, we find self-interpretation gains outpace capability gains from 7B to 72B parameters. Our results demonstrate that self-interpretation improves with scale, without modifying the model being interpreted.
L104: ###### Keywords:
L105: 
L106: Machine Learning, Interpretability, Activation Steering, Large Language Models
L107: Figure 1: Training self-interpretation from interpretability artifacts. In this case, the “interpretability artifact” consists of $(h,y)$ pairs where the vector $h$ is a contrastive activation vector from the source prompt about a specific topic, and the label $y$ is one of several synthetic descriptions of that topic. The activation $h$ is extracted from layer $\ell$ at the final token position of the source prompt (the \n\n following the chat template’s assistant header).
L108: A lightweight adapter transforms $h$ and injects it at layer 0 at the placeholder position of an explanation-seeking target prompt. Cross-entropy loss on the label tokens trains only the $d_{\text{model}}{+}1$ adapter parameters; the language model remains frozen. The case of training on an SAE dataset is similar except that $h$ is an SAE decoder vector and $y$ is a natural language feature label, e.g. from automated interpretability.
L109: ## 1 Introduction
L110: Mechanistic interpretability research has produced extensive structured knowledge about neural network internals: labeled sparse autoencoder features, annotated circuits, contrastive activation vectors. Yet this knowledge remains external to the models it describes.
L111: Recent work on self-interpretation (cite85†Chen et al., 2024 ; cite86†Ghandeharioun et al., 2024 ) prompts language models to describe their own activations in natural language, but these methods are inconveniently sensitive to the scale parameter (cite87†Kharlapenko et al., 2024 ), limiting their reliability. A central failure mode of untrained self-interpretation is not merely instability but confident, fluent descriptions ungrounded in activation semantics, making errors difficult to detect.
L112: One response is to fine-tune the language model itself (cite88†Li et al., 2025 ), but this modifies the very model being interpreted, raising questions about whether the resulting explanations reflect the original model’s representations or artifacts of the fine-tuning process. We propose instead to freeze the model entirely and train only a lightweight adapter mapping activations into token embedding space.
L113: We train on existing interpretability artifacts: vector-label pairs from sparse autoencoder features (cite89†Cunningham et al., 2023 ) and contrastive activation vectors (cite90†Zou et al., 2023 ). This reframes interpretability artifacts not as endpoints of analysis, but as supervision: structured training data that can be reused to teach models how to interpret their own internal representations.
L114: This single technique improves self-interpretation quality across diverse tasks: labeling SAE features (outperforming the training labels themselves, 71% vs 63% at 70B scale), identifying topics encoded in activation vectors (94% vs 1% recall@1), and decoding implicit reasoning without chain-of-thought. On multi-hop reasoning tasks, trained adapters extract bridge entities that appear in neither prompt nor response, revealing “what the model is thinking” even when no verbalized reasoning is available.
L115: The approach also benefits from scale: since the interpreter is the model itself, a larger subject model automatically implies a more capable interpreter.
L116: Surprisingly, a scalar affine adapter with just $d_{\text{model}}+1$ parameters suffices. The learned bias vector alone accounts for $\sim$85% of improvement over untrained baselines, and simpler adapters generalize better across datasets than more expressive alternatives. Full-rank transformations overfit catastrophically on SAE data but succeed on contrastive vectors, likely due to their lower intrinsic dimensionality.
L117: We release code and trained adapters at cite91†https://github.com/agencyenterprise/selfie-adapters†github.com .
L118: ## 2 Methods
L119: ### 2.1 Background: Self-Interpretation via Patching
L120: 
L121: We adopt the Patchscopes framework (cite86†Ghandeharioun et al., 2024 ): an activation $h_{i}^{\ell}$ extracted from layer $\ell$ at position $i$ is transformed via mapping function $f$ and injected into a target prompt $T$ at layer $\ell^{*}$ and position $i^{*}$. Following cite87†Kharlapenko et al. (2024) , we use an explanation-seeking template for $T$:
L122: 
L123: > User:
L124: > What is the meaning of "TOKEN"?
L125: >
L126: > Assistant:
L127: > The meaning of "TOKEN" is "
L128: injecting the transformed activation at the placeholder position at the token embedding layer $\ell^{*}=0$.
L129: ### 2.2 Trained Adapters
L130: 
L131: Untrained SelfIE (Self-Interpretation of Embeddings) uses $f(h)=\alpha\cdot h$, but the optimal $\alpha$ varies across vectors and most have narrow valid ranges (cite87†Kharlapenko et al., 2024 ). We propose learning $f$ from data, considering adapters with increasing expressivity:
L132: 
L133:   * •
L134: 
L135: Identity: $f(h)=h$ (0 parameters)
L136: 
L137:   * •
L138: 
L139: Scale-only: $f(h)=\alpha\cdot h$ (1 parameter)
L140: 
L141:   * •
L142: 
L143: Scalar affine: $f(h)=\alpha\cdot h+b$ ($d+1$ parameters)
L144: 
L145:   * •
L146: Scalar affine + low-rank: $f(h)=\alpha\cdot h+UV^{\top}h+b$, where $U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ ($d+1+2dr$ parameters)
L147: 
L148:   * •
L149: 
L150: Low-rank only: $f(h)=UV^{\top}h+b$
L151: ($d+2dr$ parameters)
L152: 
L153:   * •
L154: 
L155: Full-rank affine: $f(h)=Wh+b$
L156: ($d^{2}+d$ parameters)
L157: Scalar affine adapters treat all directions identically, uniformly scaling and recentering. Every parameter affects all inputs the same way, leaving no direction-specific fitting capacity and thus little room for memorization. Scalar affine + low-rank (SA+LR) adds limited direction-specific capacity via the $UV^{\top}h$ term while preserving most directions through $\alpha h$. Without this identity-preserving term, low-rank only collapses most directions while full-rank can transform them all arbitrarily.
L158: ### 2.3 Training Data: Interpretability Artifacts
L159: We train on vector-label pairs $(h,y)$ from two sources: SAE decoder vectors paired with auto-interpretability labels, and contrastive activation vectors paired with synthetic topic descriptions. We use decoder rather than encoder vectors (Appendix cite46†A.1 ). All input vectors are normalized to unit L2 norm; the adapter learns to map directions to descriptions, with a separate scale factor controlling injection magnitude at inference. Dataset details appear in Section cite13†3.1 .
L160: ### 2.4 Training Objective
L161: 
L162: We minimize cross-entropy loss averaged over label tokens (standard supervised learning with no autoregressive generation during training). Given $(h,y)$, we inject $f(h)$ at the placeholder position and compute loss against $y$ (see Appendix cite46†A.1 for label formatting details). The language model remains frozen; only adapter parameters are trained.

L164: ## 3 Experiments
L165: 
L166: ### 3.1 Setup
L167: #### Models.
L168: 
L169: We experiment primarily on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, with additional validation on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-IT. For scaling analysis, we use the Qwen-2.5-Instruct family (7B, 14B, 32B, 72B). Additional cross-family results appear in Appendix cite69†G .
L170: #### Datasets.
L171: 
L172: We train and evaluate on three sources of vector-label pairs:
L173: 
L174: (1) Goodfire SAE features: 45,418 decoder vectors from a layer 19 residual stream SAE on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (cite92†Balsam et al., 2025 ), paired with auto-interpretability labels. For Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, we use 61,521 features from the layer 50 SAE.
L175: (2) Llama Scope SAE features: decoder vectors from 32k and 131k-width SAEs trained on layers 0–31 of Llama-3.1-8B (base) (cite93†He et al., 2024 ), with auto-interpretability labels available on Neuronpedia. Despite being trained on the base model, these SAEs transfer well to the instruction-tuned variant (cite94†Kissane et al., 2024 ).
L176: (3) Wikipedia contrastive vectors: Following cite95†Lindsey (2025) , we compute activations for “Tell me about [topic]” prompts at layer 19 (arbitrarily chosen to be the same layer as the Goodfire SAE) and subtract the mean to obtain contrast vectors for 49,637 Wikipedia “Vital Level 5” article titles. Each vector is paired with $\sim$15–20 synthetic labels describing the topic at varying levels of detail and including synonyms and paraphrases.
L177: #### Evaluation.
L178: We report cross-entropy loss on held-out vector-label pairs. For SAE latents, we additionally report generation scoring (cite96†Juang et al., 2024 ): given a generated label, we prompt the model to generate text containing the label feature and measure how often the latent actually activates on these generations.
L179: Generation scoring is particularly natural for self-interpretation: traditional auto-interpretability requires activating examples as input, making detection scoring (which reuses those examples) a natural evaluation; self-interpretation requires only the feature vector itself, so generation scoring (which creates test cases from scratch) is the more natural counterpart. For Llama Scope we also report detection scoring (cite97†Paulo et al., 2024 ) for comparison with prior work.
L180: For contrastive vectors, we evaluate via embedding-based retrieval: we embed each of the $\sim$50k topics as a single document (title plus all descriptions; see Appendix cite47†A.2 ) using an off-the-shelf text embedding model (GTE-large, cite98†Li et al. (2023) ) and embed each generated label, then measure recall@$k$: the fraction of labels for which the correct topic appears in the top $k$ nearest neighbors.
L181: ### 3.2 Contrastive Activation Vector Results
L182: 
L183: We train a full-rank affine adapter $f(h)=Wh+b$ for one epoch on the Wikipedia contrastive vectors dataset.
L184: At scale 1.0, the full-rank adapter achieves 82.9% recall@1 on held-out topics compared to 0.04% for untrained SelfIE, and 98.4% versus 0.9% at recall@100. Trained adapters require no additional scale tuning for this task. With the best-of-6 protocol used throughout Table cite99†2 , results improve further to 93.7% recall@1. The full-rank adapter outperformed constrained architectures (Appendix cite60†D ).
L185: As later analysis shows, this success depends critically on the low intrinsic dimensionality of the contrastive topic vectors, which implicitly regularizes the full-rank transformation.

L210: ### 3.3 Self-Interpretation Scales with Model Size
L211: Figure 2: Scaling comparison on Qwen-2.5 models (7B to 72B). Trained SelfIE (below): recall@100 on held-out topics for full-rank adapters trained on contrastive topic vectors from the middle half of each model’s layers. Taboo baseline (above): the model describes each topic without naming it, scored with the same embedding retrieval. While SelfIE consistently performs below the Taboo ceiling, the gap decreases with model scale as SelfIE’s performance increases more rapidly.
L212: Error bars show 95% confidence intervals. See Appendix cite66†F for additional metrics.
L213: Larger models generally perform better at nearly every task, so showing that self-interpretation improves with scale would prove little on its own. To isolate self-interpretation gains from general capability improvements, we compare against a “Taboo” baseline: the model describes each topic without mentioning its name, scored with the same embedding-based retrieval.
L214: This measures how much topic knowledge the model can express directly, providing a ceiling for what self-interpretation could hope to extract.
L215: We train full-rank adapters on contrastive activation vectors from Qwen-2.5 models at four scales (7B, 14B, 32B, 72B). For each model, we pool vectors from the middle half of all layers (e.g., layers 7–20 for a 28-layer model), training a single adapter that receives no layer index yet interprets activations from any training layer. This cross-layer generalization replicates findings from Llama (Appendix cite65†E ).
L216: Figure cite100†2 shows the results. The model’s underlying knowledge of the topics saturates at an intermediate scale, but the performance of trained SelfIE grows faster, revealing more of the semantic information that’s present. Untrained SelfIE achieves $<$2% recall@100 at all sizes. Note that unlike Table cite99†2 , these results use only the trained adapter with no additional input scaling and no best-of-N protocol.

L237: #### The bias vector is critical.
L238: 
L239: Scale-only improves just 0.29 over identity, but adding the bias vector (scalar affine) yields an additional 2.75 improvement. This single $d$-dimensional vector accounts for approximately 85% of the total gain from our best adapter.
L240: 
L241: #### Scalar affine is a strong minimal baseline.
L242: 
L243: With only $d+1$ parameters, scalar affine achieves validation loss of 1.787, already a large improvement over untrained approaches with essentially zero train-val gap.
L244: #### Low-rank additions provide meaningful gains.
L245: 
L246: Adding a low-rank component on top of scalar affine yields consistent improvements, with rank 64 achieving the best validation loss of 1.62. Returns diminish beyond rank 64.
L247: #### The identity structure matters.
L248: 
L249: Comparing low-rank only versus scalar affine + low-rank at matched ranks reveals a consistent gap favoring the identity-preserving variant. This gap is most dramatic at low rank: at rank 4, adding the scalar affine base improves validation loss from 2.00 to 1.69, a difference of 0.31, larger than the entire gain from increasing rank. The scaled identity term preserves directional information that pure low-rank transformations discard.
L250: #### Full-rank transformations overfit catastrophically on SAE data.
L251: Full-rank adapters have 4096$\times$ more parameters than scalar affine, yet achieve similar or worse validation loss while reaching train loss of 0.64. Early stopping cannot rescue full-rank: SA+LR already outperforms full-rank’s best point (1.66 vs 1.70) after just one epoch, and continues improving to 1.62 by epoch 3. Full-rank transformations overfit on SAE data by effectively learning a high-dimensional lookup table, destroying identity-preserving structure in the activation space.
L252: See Appendix cite57†B for training curves.
L253: #### Multi-scale generation protocol.
L254: While trained adapters reduce the scale sensitivity problem identified by (cite87†Kharlapenko et al., 2024 ), SAE features still benefit from trying multiple scales. We generate at $N=6$ scales from a calibrated grid and report the best score. To ensure fair comparison, baselines get equal attempts (6 copies of each label, or the original plus 5 paraphrases).
L255: Trained adapters increase the number of scales producing accurate outputs (Appendix cite50†A.5 ), making scale selection a simple grid search rather than per-latent optimization.

L279: The first thing to note is that scalar affine adapters generalize better across datasets than higher-capacity adapters: the Wikipedia SA adapter achieves 41.1% and 49.7% generation scoring hit rates on SAE latents despite never seeing SAE features during training, while the Wikipedia full-rank adapter (optimal in-distribution) achieves only 23.4% and 34.7%. This pattern replicates on Gemma-2-9B-IT (Appendix cite69†G ).
L280: Second, Goodfire-trained adapters achieve the highest generation scores even on Llama Scope evaluation, suggesting the Goodfire labels may be higher quality or better suited to instruction-tuned models.
