At the first stage $P_{0}=P^{*}$, i.e.
L86: the data corpus $D_{0}$ consists of samples only from the true data distribution. Subsequently, at $t>0$, the training data $D_{t}$ consists of samples from mainly two different source types (i) fresh samples from true data distribution $P^{*}$ (which we refer to as real data), and (ii) synthetic data generated from previously fitted models $\{\hat{P}_{s}:s<t\}$.
L87: Another potential source is reusing data from prior times (accumulation), see Section cite15†3.1.1 , however our focus is primarily on the two main sources. For simplicity, assuming that only the latest version of the model is used for the synthetic generation, then the data generative mechanism at the $t+1$ time point can be modeled as a mixture
L88:  | $$P_{t+1}=\alpha_{t+1}P^{*}+(1-\alpha_{t+1})\hat{P}_{t}$$  |  | (1)
L89: where $\alpha_{t}$ captures the proportion of fresh real data in the $t$-th stage data $D_{t}$. The complete training process is illustrated in Figure cite57†1 . Note that even through the data arises from different heterogeneous sources, the model is still fitted using the same optimization method with the fixed model class $\mathcal{P}$.
L90: It is also important to recognize that the mixture assumption is at the population level – this is different from an empirical mixture involving adding some of the real samples from the first time point to potentially all synthetic samples generated at time $t$.
L91: The goal of this study is to understand under what conditions the iterative training with mixture of real and synthetic data can actually improve the models over time. Precisely, our interest is to examine how the expected distance of the fitted model from the true distribution, $\mathbb{E}d(P^{*},\hat{P}_{t})$ evolves with training iterations $t\geq 0$, where $d(.,.)$ is some appropriate distance measure.
L92: We specifically explore the conditions on the mixture weights $(\alpha_{t})_{t\geq 1}$ and sample sizes $(n_{t})_{t\geq 0}$ such that not only collapse is avoided, but also models eventually improve with training iterations, i.e. $\mathbb{E}d(P^{*},\hat{P}_{t})\lesssim\mathbb{E}d(P^{*},\hat{P}_{0})$ and ideally achieves limiting consistency, i.e. $\mathbb{E}d(P^{*},\hat{P}_{t})\to 0$ with growing sample sizes $n_{t}\uparrow\infty$ as $t\to\infty$.
L93: ##### Related Work:
L94: The problem of model collapse was first identified and studied in (cite52†Shumailov et al., 2024 ) and was empirically validated by (cite53†Alemohammad et al., 2023 ; cite58†Bohacek and Farid, 2023 ; cite59†Martínez et al., 2023a ; cite60†Martínez et al., 2023b ; cite61†Guo et al., 2024 ) for a wide variety of models and web-scale datasets. However, there are relatively fewer works that theoretically study the effect of model collapse.
L95: Theoretical analysis of model collapse was done in (cite62†Shumailov et al., 2023 ) for Gaussian data, (cite63†Dohmatob et al., 2024a ) in regression settings and (cite64†Dohmatob et al., 2024b ) study the change in scaling laws when iterative training is done with synthetic data.
L96: (cite53†Alemohammad et al., 2023 ) conducts experiments across different mixed training setups, indicating that incorporating real data can help alleviate model collapse. For simplified language models, (cite56†Seddik et al., 2024 ) examine training with discrete mixtures of real and synthetic data, deriving upper bounds on synthetic data proportions to prevent collapse.
L97: However, note our population level mixture modeling differs from data mixtures considered in (cite56†Seddik et al., 2024 ), where real data is injected at each training step from a fixed (finite) original sample. (cite54†Bertrand et al., 2023 ) considers a general maximum likelihood estimation framework in the mixture setting involving a proportion of data from the true data distribution and analyze the stability of training process at the distribution level under a locality assumption in parameter space.
L98: For diffusion models, (cite55†Fu et al., 2024 ) provide a control of the ${\sf TV}$ distance between $\hat{P}_{t}$ and $P^{*}$, and also shows importance of balancing portions of real and synthetic data. Our work is in similar spirit with the above settings, but is directed towards studying statistical improvement or consistency instead of just focusing on preventing collapse.
L99: Furthermore, in contrast with most existing works, we consider asymptotic regimes with sample sizes growing at each iteration and seek to characterize the limiting behavior of models risk.
L100: Finally, we also note some recent studies have also emerged as potential solutions to model collapse. A line of work (cite65†Gerstgrasser et al., 2024 ; cite66†Kazdan et al., 2024 ) explores data accumulation to prevent model collapse. However, we show accumulation cannot improve performance over the iterations (see Section cite15†3.1.1 ).
L101: While few recent works investigate strategies for curating or filtering synthetic data to prevent model collapse (cite67†Feng et al., 2024 ; cite68†Ferbach et al., 2024 ), a thorough evaluation of their effectiveness remains absent.
L102: ## 3 Iteratively Trained Language Models
L103: To theoretically study the evolution of the iterative training process for Large Language models, we focus on a simplified statistical language model with context-based next token prediction.
L104: This modeling approach is similar to (cite56†Seddik et al., 2024 ) and theoretically boils down to multinomial parameter estimation with $K$ target tokens with model class $P_{\theta}=\{\text{Cat}(\theta):\theta\in\Delta^{K-1}\}$, , where Cat denotes a categorical distribution and $\Delta^{k-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:x_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i}x_{i}=1\}$ is the $(k-1)$-dimensional probability simplex
L105: ##### Background on Language Models:
L106: Say we have a vocabulary of size $K$ and $C\leq K^{l}$ many possible contexts of maximum length $l$. Assuming that the language data is generated from some unknown conditional probabilities given the contexts, the probability of the next token being $k\in[K]:=\{1,\ldots,K\}$ given some context $j=\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\right)\in[C]$ is denoted by $\mathbb{P}\left[Y=k\mid X=j\right]$ where $X$ and $Y$ are discrete random variables specifying a context and the next token respectively.
L107: In practice, conditional probabilities are unknown but we have access to a (large) corpus of training dataset $\left\{\left(x_{l},y_{l}\right)\right\}_{l\in[N]}$ of $N$ samples of contexts and next-token pairs represented by $x_{l}\in\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{C}\right\}$ and $y_{l}\in\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{K}\right\}$ where $e_{i}$’s denote the canonical vectors. Then we consider estimating the conditional probabilities via the Softmax classifier, which minimizes the categorical cross-entropy loss function:
L108:  | $$\underset{\mathbf{W}=\left[w_{1},\ldots,w_{K}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times K}}{\arg\min}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}y_{l}^{\top}\log\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{\top}x_{l}\right)$$  |
L109: where $\sigma(v)=\frac{\exp(v)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(v_{k}\right)}$ is the Softmax function and the functions exp and log are applied entry-wise. Note that we choose to work with one-hot embeddings as representations for tractable theoretical analysis even though current state-of-the-art language models, the $x_{l}$’s are context representations computed using attention or transformer based networks. The estimates of conditional probability solving the Softmax problem are the empirical means:
L110:  | $\displaystyle\mathbb{\widehat{P}}\left[Y=k\mid X=j\right]$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\hat{w}_{k}^{\top}e_{j}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\exp\left(\hat{w}_{i}^{\top}e_{j}\right)}=\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{C}_{j}\right|}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}y_{lk}$  |
L111:  | $\displaystyle\text{ with }\quad\mathcal{C}_{j}$  | $\displaystyle=\left\{l\in[N]\mid x_{l}=e_{j}\right\}.$  |
L112: 
L113: Fixing a context say $j\in[C]$ (equivalently setting $x=e_{j}$), denote true conditional probabilities as
L114:  | $\displaystyle\theta^{*}(k)$  | $\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[Y=k\mid X=j\right],\ \forall k\in[K],$  |
L115:  | $\displaystyle\theta^{*}$  | $\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{*}(1)\cdots\theta^{*}(K)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\Delta^{K-1}.$  |
L116: Then the above classification problem boils down to a statistical problem of categorical proportion estimation with data $\left\{\left(e_{j},y_{l}\right)\right\}_{l\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}$. Here, the true data distribution distribution $P_{\theta^{*}}:=\text{Cat}(\theta^{*})$ which lies in the model class $\{\text{Cat}(\theta):\theta\in\Delta^{K-1}\}$.
L117: ##### Iterative Training Process:
L118: 
L119: Now, let’s frame the problem for iterative training setting where the next stage new data is generated from a Categorical with class probabilities as mixture of the true class probabilities and the current fitted probabilities. The first stage data $Y^{0}=\left(Y^{0}_{1},\ldots,Y^{0}_{n_{0}}\right)$ are $n_{0}$ i.i.d samples from the true data distribution $P_{\theta^{*}}=\text{Cat}(\theta^{*})$, using them we get the initial estimates as
L120:  | $$\widehat{\theta}_{0}(k)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\mathds{1}[Y^{0}_{j}=k]}{n_{0}}.$$  |
L121: 
L122: For any training time $t\geq 0$, given the current estimate $\widehat{\theta}_{t}$, the next stage data $Y^{t+1}=\left(Y^{t+1}_{1},\ldots,Y^{t+1}_{n_{t+1}}\right)$ is generated ${\sf iid}$ from
L123:  | $\displaystyle P_{t+1}:=$  | $\displaystyle\text{Cat}(\theta_{t+1})=\alpha_{t+1}P_{\theta^{*}}+(1-\alpha_{t+1})\widehat{P}_{t}$  |
L124:  | $\displaystyle=$  | $\displaystyle\text{Cat}(\alpha_{t+1}\theta^{*}+(1-\alpha_{t+1})\widehat{\theta}_{t}),$  |
L125: which recursively yields the next stage estimate $\widehat{\theta}_{t+1}$ using the corresponding sample proportions from $Y^{t+1}$ (agnostic of data source). Our aim here is to understand the entire trajectory of $\hat{\theta}_{t}$ (equivalently $\widehat{P}_{t}=P_{\widehat{\theta}_{t}}$) in terms of
L126: 
L127:  | $$R_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2},$$  |
L128: 
L129: the risk of $t$-th estimate, measuring the quality of model fit.
L130: ###### Theorem 3.1.
L131: 
L132: In the above setting, the sequence $\left(R_{t}\right)_{t\geq 1}$(as defined above) satisfies the following recurrence
L133: 
L134:  | $$R_{t}=\frac{n_{0}}{n_{t}}R_{0}+\frac{n_{t}-1}{n_{t}}(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}R_{t-1}$$  |
L135: 
L136: where $R_{0}=\frac{\sum_{k}\theta^{*}(k)(1-\theta^{*}(k))}{n_{0}}=\frac{1-\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{n_{0}}.$
L137: From now on, let $R_{\infty}$ denote the limiting model risk $\lim_{t\to\infty}R_{t}$, when the limit exists (otherwise $\liminf_{t}R_{t}$). Observe that the trajectory of $\left(R_{t}\right)_{t\geq 1}$ is governed by the mixture weights $\left(\alpha_{t}\right)_{t\geq 1}$ and sample sizes $\left(n_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}$.
L138: Under different regimes of $\left(\alpha_{t}\right)_{t\geq 1}$ and $\left(n_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}$, we analyze the long-term behavior of the model risk, focusing on the following scenarios based on the degree of collapse quantified by the excess limiting risk $R_{\infty}-R_{0}$:
L139:   1. 1.
L140: 
L141: Consistency: models consistently improves over time, achieving vanishing risk in the limit, $R_{\infty}=0$.
L142: 
L143:   2. 2.
L144: 
L145: Iterative Improvement: models avoid collapse and continue to improve or stabilize at a risk level better than or close to the initial risk: $R_{\infty}\leq R_{0}$.
L146: 
L147:   3. 3.
L148: 
L149: Collapse: models fail to improve, with limiting risk exceeding the standard risk, e.g. $R_{\infty}>R_{0}$.
L150: 
L151: First we analyze the case when the mixture weight of real data is bounded away from 0.
L152: ###### Corollary 3.2.
L153: 
L154: Assume the mixture weight of real data maintains $\inf_{t}\alpha_{t}=\alpha>0$. Then based on the evolution of the training size $n_{t}$, the following holds:
L155: 
L156:   1. 1.
L157: 
L158: If $n_{t}=n$, then $R_{t}\in[R_{0},nR_{0})$. More precisely,:
L159: 
L160:  | $$R_{0}\leq R_{\infty}\leq R_{0}\left(1-\frac{n-1}{n}(1-\alpha)^{2}\right)^{-1}.$$  |
L161: 
L162:   2. 2.
L163: 
L164: If $n_{t}\uparrow\infty$, then $R_{\infty}=0$ (consistency for any increasing sample size sequence $n_{t}$).
L165: Note with fixed sample size, future models can never be better than the initial model, but the gap $R_{\infty}-R_{0}$ depends on the mixture weights and is smaller for bigger $\alpha$.
L166: ###### Remark 3.3.
L167: 
L168: The proof of the above corollary demonstrates that if $\alpha_{t}=\alpha$ is fixed and $n_{t}=n$ for all $t$, then the limiting risk is exactly the upper bound, i.e., $R_{\infty}=R_{0}\left(1-\frac{n-1}{n}(1-\alpha)^{2}\right)^{-1}.$ The bound appears due to the condition that $\inf_{t}\alpha_{t}=\alpha$. When $\alpha=0$ (purely synthetic data), the limit becomes $nR_{0}$, while when $\alpha=1$ (fresh real data), the limit is $R_{0}$, as expected.
L169: Now, let’s consider the situation when truth mixture weights are $\alpha_{t}>0$ but decays to 0, that is $\alpha_{t}\downarrow 0$.
L170: ###### Proposition 3.4.
L171: 
L172: (Decaying Truth Mixture Weights)
L173: 
L174:   1. 1.
L175: 
L176: For any sequence $\alpha_{t}\downarrow 0$, a ‘large enough’ sample size:
L177: 
L178:  | $$n_{t}>\left\lceil\frac{n_{t-1}-1}{\alpha_{t}(2-\alpha_{t})}\right\rceil+1$$  |
L179: 
L180: ensures $t$-th stage improvement i.e. $R_{t}<R_{t-1}$. Consequently, if the above recursive inequality is satisfied for all $\left(n_{t}:t\geq 1\right)$ , then $\left(R_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}$ is a strictly decreasing sequence and $R_{\infty}<R_{0}$.
L181: 
L182:   2. 2.
L183: If $\alpha_{t}$ satisfies $\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty$, then any sequence of sample sizes with $\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}1/n_{t}<\infty$ ensures consistency, that is $R_{\infty}=0$.
L184: ###### Remark 3.5.
L185: 
L186: The condition in Part (2) interprets as sufficiently slowly decaying $\alpha_{t}$ and examples include $\alpha_{t}\asymp 1/\log(t)$ or $\alpha_{t}\asymp 1/\sqrt{t}$ or $\alpha_{t}\asymp 1/t$ or $\alpha_{t}\asymp 1/t(\log t)^{\gamma}$ for some $\gamma\in(0,1)$ – in all of such cases, the choice of $n_{t}\asymp t^{2}$ ensures consistency.
L187: The above result provides sufficient (but not necessary) conditions under which improvement or consistency is achieved at the limit $t\to\infty$. Unlike the case 2 in Corollary cite69†3.2 , where any sequence $n_{t}\uparrow\infty$ ensures consistency, these conditions on the evolution of sample sizes depend on the sequence of decaying mixture weights.
L188: Observe (from part 1) that to ensure improvement $n_{t}$ needs to grow faster for smaller truth mixture weight $\alpha_{t}$, essentially suggesting adjusted scaling laws while training with synthetic data, as shown in (cite64†Dohmatob et al., 2024b ). One potential limitation of Proposition cite70†3.4 is that the sample size $n_{t}$ depends on the potentially unknown $\alpha_{t}$, which is problematic in real-life scenarios – although this is expected.
L189: However, the purpose of the result is to demonstrate that there exists sequences $n_{t}$ such that estimation can indeed be improved even by agnostic model fitting with contaminated data.
L190: #### Using a classifier before training
L191: Recent work (cite71†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite67†Feng et al., 2024 ) has proposed using human or AI detector to filter out synthetic data to avoid model collapse. We provide a brief discussion of this idea under our framework. Assume that at stage $t$, there is a classifier $\mathcal{C}_{t}$ that is trained to differentiate between $P^{*}$ (real) and $\hat{P}_{t}$. The data $Y^{t+1}$ is then passed through this classifier and only the ones that are classified as ‘real’ are retained for training $\hat{P}_{t+1}$.
L192: Assume a simplified setting where label-agnostic $\mathcal{C}_{t}$ has type 1 error rate $e_{1t}$ and type 2 error rate $e_{2t}$ (where the null corresponds to $P^{*}$). Recall that under our assumptions, $Y^{t+1}$ is drawn from a mixture distribution with $\alpha_{t+1}$ proportion coming from $P^{*}$ and $(1-\alpha_{t+1})$ from $\hat{P}_{t}$.
L193: If $\tilde{Y}^{t+1}$ is the filtered data obtained from $Y^{t+1}$ by taking only those classified as coming from $P^{*}$, then it is easy to see that $\tilde{Y}^{t+1}$ also has a mixture distribution, with new mixture weight corresponding to $P^{*}$ as
L194:  | $$\tilde{\alpha}_{t+1}=\frac{\alpha_{t+1}(1-e_{1t})}{\alpha_{t+1}(1-e_{1t})+(1-\alpha_{t+1})e_{2t}}$$  |
L195: 
L196: and the remaining $1-\tilde{\alpha}_{t+1}$ from $\hat{P}_{t}$. Also, note that if the size of $Y^{t+1}$ is $n_{t}$, then the expected size of $\tilde{Y}^{t+1}$ is
L197: 
L198:  | $$\tilde{n}_{t+1}=[\alpha_{t+1}(1-e_{1t})+(1-\alpha_{t+1})e_{2t}]n_{t+1}.$$  |
L199: 
L200: For example, for a random classifier with $e_{1t}=e_{2t}=1/2$, we observe that $\tilde{\alpha}_{t+1}=\alpha_{t+1}$, while $\tilde{n}_{t+1}=n_{t+1}/2$.
L201: ##### Oracle classifier:
L202: 
L203: In practice, training of $\mathcal{C}_{t}$ has to be done through samples from both $P^{*}$ and $\hat{P}_{t}$. Since $\hat{P}_{t}$ is the model trained at the last step, one can generate infinitely many samples from this distribution. Assume that there is an oracle who also has access to infinite samples from $P^{*}$. In this case, the information theoretic lower bound of the test is
L204: 
L205:  | $$e_{1t}+e_{2t}\geq 1-{\sf TV}(P^{*},\hat{P}_{t}).$$  |
L206: If the oracle classifier attains this (e.g., the Neyman-Pearson LRT) – for a fixed type 1 error rate $e_{1t}=e_{1}$, it has a type 2 error rate $e_{2t}=1-e_{1}-{\sf TV}(P^{*},\hat{P}_{t})$. In this case, the training of the model in the next stage is based on $\tilde{Y}_{t+1}$ whose mixture weight for $P^{*}$ is given by
L207:  | $\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{t+1}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t+1}(1-e_{1})}{\alpha_{t+1}(1-e_{1})+(1-\alpha_{t+1})(1-e_{1}-{\sf TV}(P^{*},\hat{P}_{t}))}$  |
L208:  |  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t+1}(1-e_{1})}{1-e_{1}-0.5(1-\alpha_{t+1})\|\hat{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{*}\|_{1}}\geq\alpha_{t+1}.$  |
L209: 
L210: This gives us a way to analyze $R_{\infty}$ in such cases, using same technique from Theorem cite72†3.1 . See Appendix cite31†D , cite32†E for more details.
L211: ### 3.1 Iterative Retraining On Purely Synthetic Data
L212: Now, we turn to the case when there is no fresh real data except $D_{0}$, i.e. for all $t\geq 1$, $\alpha_{t}=0$ and the $t$-th model $\widehat{P}_{t}$ is trained on purely synthetic data $Y^{t}$, generated from previous model fit $\widehat{P}_{t-1}$. For fixed sample sizes $n_{t}=n$, collapse cannot be avoided and the limiting risk is $nR_{0}$, as discussed in Remark cite73†3.3 . The following corollary shows that increasing $n_{t}$ might be beneficial in this case.
L213: ###### Corollary 3.6.
L214: 
L215: For iterative retraining with purely synthetic data with sample sizes $\left(n_{t}:t\geq 0\right)$, the following holds: $n_{0}R_{0}\sum_{s\leq t}(1/2^{s}n_{s})<R_{t}<n_{0}R_{0}\sum_{s\leq t}(1/n_{s})$.
L216: Unlike previous situations, there is no hope of improvement in the sense $R_{t}<R_{0}$ by growing sample size $n_{t}$. However, $R_{t}$ can be made arbitrarily close to $R_{0}$ (this is intuitively obvious since if we have access to unlimited samples from $P_{\theta_{t}}$, then $\theta_{t}$ can be estimated without any statistical error, hence we only incur error at the first round with finite $n_{0}$ samples from $P^{*}$).
L217: #### 3.1.1 Data Accumulation
L218: To mitigate model collapse, a line of work (cite65†Gerstgrasser et al., 2024 ; cite66†Kazdan et al., 2024 ) suggests training with accumulated data from past stages. Here we study the training evolution with accumulated data in our setting. Using the same notations as before, $Y^{0}$ be the initial data and $\widehat{\theta}^{a}_{0}:=\widehat{\theta}_{0}$ be the initial estimate. For training time $t\geq 1$, the new stage synthetic data $Y^{t}$ of size $n_{t}$, generated from the previous model fit.
L219: Then the next stage model is fitted by estimating $\widehat{\theta}^{a}_{t}$ from data accumulated through all previous stages $Y^{0},\cdots,Y^{t}$. We derive the entire trajectory of risks $R^{a}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\theta}^{a}_{t}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}$ under accumulated training (Theorem cite74†A.1 , Appendix cite22†A ) and state some key findings below.
L220: ###### Proposition 3.7.
L221: 
L222: For fixed number of samples per iteration $n_{t}=n$, the sequence of $\left(R^{a}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}$ is eventually strictly increasing and it’s limit, say $R_{\infty}^{a}$ can be bounded both sides as
L223: 
L224:  | $$R_{0}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-1\right)\right)\leq R_{\infty}^{a}\leq R_{0}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right).$$  |
L225: ###### Proposition 3.8.
L226: 
L227: For sample sizes growing at each iteration with $n_{t}\uparrow\infty$, the limit $\lim_{t\to\infty}R^{a}_{t}=R_{\infty}^{a}$ exists and $R_{\infty}^{a}>R_{0}$. Moreover, if $R_{\infty}^{a}<n_{0}R_{0}$, then $R^{a}_{t}$ is eventually monotonically increasing.
L228: Our findings aligns with the results in the previous works on accumulation (cite65†Gerstgrasser et al., 2024 ; cite66†Kazdan et al., 2024 ). Proposition cite75†3.7 for fixed sample sizes $n_{t}=n$, gives a stronger upper-bound on the limiting risk than what we would obtain by standard training. This demonstrates the benefit of accumulation. However, also note (from Proposition cite76†3.8 ) for sample sizes growing at each iteration, there is no extra benefit from accumulation and $R_{t}$ is eventually increasing.
L229: Added with the increasing memory requirement for the accumulation process, it seems that standard retraining should be preferred.
L230: ## 4 Importance of fresh real data to improve iterative training
L231: Results from previous sections show (i) with fresh real data at each stage, improvement is always possible and consistency also holds in many cases, (ii) with no fresh real data after $D_{0}$, it is not possible (even with accumulation) to do better than the initial fit. In this section, we argue that this is a more general phenomenon. Consider a single step of the general iterative training process described in Section cite7†2 , with model class $\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}$.
L232: Let $\hat{\theta}_{0}$ be an estimator trained using data $D_{0}$ and $\hat{\theta}_{1}$ be the estimator based on $D_{1}$ in the following iteration. Our aim is to understand, under what conditions can $\hat{\theta}_{1}$ be statistically better than $\hat{\theta}_{0}$. The following two results respectively deal with the necessity and sufficiency of fresh samples from the true data distribution to improve estimation.
L233: Denote $\mathcal{R}(\theta,P):=\mathbb{E}[\ell(\theta;Z)]$ where $\ell$ is some loss function, and the expectation is taken with respect to $Z\sim P$ and any randomness in $\theta$ (if estimator).
L234: Typical examples with $Z=(X,Y)$ include the regression setting with $\ell(\theta;Z)=(Y-X^{\top}\theta)^{2}$ (linear regression), $\ell(\theta,Z)=(Y-X^{\top}\theta)^{2}+l\left\|\theta\right\|_{1}$ (LASSO regression), classification setting with $\ell(\theta,Z)=-Y\log(\sigma(X^{\top}\theta))-(1-Y)\log(1-\sigma(X^{\top}\theta))$ (for logistic regression), $\ell(\theta,Z)=\max\{0,1-YX^{\top}\theta\}$ (for linear SVM) and with $Z=X$, the density estimation setting with $\ell(\theta,X)=-\log p_{\theta}(X)$.
L235: ###### Theorem 4.1.
L236: 
L237: If $D_{1}|D_{0}$ is independent of $P^{*}$ and known, then for any convex loss $\ell(\theta;X)$ (convex in $\theta$), for any estimator $\hat{\theta}_{1}$ based on $D_{1}$, there exists $\hat{\theta}_{0}$ based on $D_{0}$, such that $\mathcal{R}(\hat{\theta}_{0},P^{*})\leq\mathcal{R}(\hat{\theta}_{1},P^{*})$.
L238: The result shows that if $D_{1}$ has no additional information about $P^{*}$ other than that already in $D_{0}$, then for any estimator based on $D_{1}$, one can construct an estimator based only on $D_{0}$ which outperforms the former. It is important that the distribution of $D_{1}|D_{0}$ is known, otherwise although $\hat{\theta}_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{1}|D_{0}]$ has better risk, it is not a computable statistic from the data.
L239: As an example, suppose given $D_{0}$, an adversary chooses a distribution for $D_{1}$ without any knowledge of $P^{*}$ – in this case, the learner without access to this extra information cannot compute $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{1}|D_{0}]$. Secondly, the result does not say that given an estimation method (e.g. empirical risk minimization), using it on $D_{0}$ is as good as using it on $D_{1}$.
L240: In the mixture setting $P_{1}=\alpha P^{*}+(1-\alpha)\hat{P}_{0}$, where $\hat{P}_{0}$ is a function of $D_{0}$; hence for $\alpha=0$, $D_{1}|D_{0}$ is independent of $P^{*}$. It is interesting to note that the result applies even to the case of accumulation, since $D_{1}|D_{0}$ is still independent of $P^{*}$ (reusing ‘real’ data from $D_{0}$ is very different from having ‘fresh real’ data).
