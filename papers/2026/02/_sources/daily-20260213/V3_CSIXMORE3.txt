L120: ### 3.2 SafeThink Algorithm
L121: 
L122: To address the constrained generation problem at inference time (Eq. cite62†6 ), we propose a two-step, lightweight algorithm that enforces safety constraints while minimizing perturbation to the original reasoning policy.

L131: Step 1: Monitoring. The goal is to detect unsafe reasoning steps based on a threshold and trigger intervention only when the safety threshold is violated. Let the adversarial input be $x_{\text{adv}}$, and let the reasoning trace be $z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{T},\texttt{[EoT]}),$ where $z_{t}$ denotes the $t$-th reasoning step and [EoT] marks the end-of-thinking token. At step $t$, the model proposes a candidate step $z_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{\text{adv}},z_{<t})$.
L132: We evaluate the safety of the partial reasoning trace using $r_{t}=R_{\text{safe}}(x_{\text{adv}},z_{\leq t})$ where $R_{\text{safe}}$ returns a normalized safety score. In practice, we instantiate $R_{\text{safe}}$ using publicly available harmlessness reward models (e.g., RewardBench (cite70†Lambert et al., 2024 )), and normalize the score to $[-1,1]$. If $r_{t}\geq\tau$, we accept $z_{t}$. If $r_{t}<\tau$, we reject the unsafe step and trigger steering.
L133: Step 2: Lightweight Steering via Safety Tokens. The goal of steering is to identify a safety token $s$ such that conditioning on $s$ increases the probability of generating a safe continuation, while minimally deviating from the base reasoning policy. For an intermediate state $x^{\prime}=(x_{\text{adv}},z_{<t}),$ we consider a finite set of candidate steering tokens $\mathcal{S}$ and define the safety success probability as
L134:  | $\displaystyle P_{\text{safe}}(s\mid x^{\prime})\;=\;\Pr_{z_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime},s)}\Big[R_{\text{safe}}(x^{\prime},z_{t})\geq\tau\Big].$  |  | (7)
L135: 
L136: Specifically, for each $s\in\mathcal{S}$: (i) sample $k$ candidate next steps $z_{t}^{(i)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime},s),\quad i=1,\dots,k.$ (ii) Evaluate the safety score $R_{\mathrm{safe}}(x^{\prime},z_{t}^{(i)})$ for each sample. (iii) Estimate $P_{\mathrm{safe}}(s\mid x^{\prime})$ via Monte Carlo:
L137:  | $\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}(s\mid x^{\prime})\;=\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}\!\left[R_{\mathrm{safe}}(x^{\prime},z_{t}^{(i)})\geq\tau\right].$  |  | (8)
L138: 
L139: Among all steering tokens that satisfy the safety constraint $\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}(s\mid x^{\prime})\geq\tau$, we select the one that minimizes deviation from the base policy:
L140: 
L141:  | $$s^{*}\;=\;\arg\min_{s}\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime},s)\;\middle\|\;\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime})\right).$$  |
L142: We propose an augmented flow where an auxiliary steering variable $s_{t}$ is injected at each step:
L143: 
L144:  | $$x\to(z_{1},s_{1})\to(z_{2},s_{2})\to\dots\to(z_{T},s_{T})\to y.$$  |  | (9)
L145: 
L146: This two-step procedure implements a lightweight, inference-only safety recovery mechanism that intervenes only when necessary, preserves reasoning utility, and enforces a satisficing safety constraint rather than maximizing safety.
L147: Practical instantiation of the steering token $s$. A natural question is how to construct the candidate set $\mathcal{S}$ of steering tokens. Rather than relying on manual design, we construct $\mathcal{S}$ offline using a held-out validation set of $500$ samples randomly drawn from four benchmarks: JailbreakV-28K (cite67†Luo et al., 2024 ), HADES (cite43†Li et al., 2024 ), FigStep (cite69†Gong et al., 2023 ), and MM-SafetyBench (cite71†Liu et al., 2024 ).
L148: Specifically, we prompt GPT-4 with a set of unsafe reasoning traces sampled from the validation set and instruct it to generate short corrective phrases (1–5 tokens) that could redirect the reasoning toward safer continuations. We collect all unique generated phrases across multiple samples to form the candidate set $\mathcal{S}$.
L149: We then evaluate each candidate $s\in\mathcal{S}$ along two criteria: (1) safety success probability $\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}(s\mid x^{\prime})$, measuring the likelihood that conditioning on $s$ yields a safe continuation, and (2) KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime},s)\|\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime}))$, quantifying deviation from the base reasoning policy.
L150: An ideal steering token $s$ should maximize safety success probability while minimizing distributional shift to preserve reasoning coherence.
L151: Figure cite72†5 presents the offline evaluation on JailbreakV-28K (cite67†Luo et al., 2024 ). We observe a clear notion: tokens lacking explicit safety language (e.g., “Wait, think again”, “Let’s rethink step by step again”) yield low safety success probability and fail to redirect harmful trajectories. In contrast, tokens containing explicit safety cues (e.g., “think safely”, “rethink safely”) achieve substantially higher $\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}(s\mid x^{\prime})$.
L152: Among these, “Wait, think safely” emerges as the optimal choice, as it attains the highest safety success probability while inducing a low KL divergence. At inference time, for experiments, we use this pre-selected token $s^{*}:=$ “Wait, think safely” as the fixed steering signal, incurring no additional search overhead.

We evaluate each steering token $s\in\mathcal{S}$ on two criteria: (a) safety reward $R_{\text{safe}}$, measuring effectiveness at redirecting reasoning toward safe continuations, and (b) KL divergence from the base policy, measuring distributional shift.
L154: Tokens lacking explicit safety language (“Wait, think again”, “Lets rethink step by step again”) remain below the safety threshold ($\tau=0$) across reasoning steps. Tokens with safety cues (“Lets rethink step by step safely”, “Wait, think safely”) consistently exceed the threshold. Among these, “Wait, think safely” achieves the highest safety reward while maintaining a low KL divergence, making it the optimal choice for inference-time steering.
L155: ## 4 Safety Recovery with Few Step Steering
L156: Safety recovery is often only a few early steering steps away. In Section 3, we introduced SafeThink, which triggers steering only when a proposed reasoning step violates the safety threshold and then selects a steering token to recondition the next-step distribution.
L157: Our central empirical finding is that safety recovery typically requires steering only a small number of early reasoning steps: once the trajectory is redirected into a safe region, subsequent steps tend to remain safe without further intervention. Figure cite50†4 shows a sharp phase transition: Attack Success Rate (ASR) drops steeply within the first few steering steps and then saturates, indicating diminishing returns from steering deeper into the trace.
L158: Experiment underlying Figure cite50†4 . We operationalize steering depth $m$ as follows: steering (i.e., the monitor-triggered token-selection procedure from Section 3) is allowed to activate only within the first $m$ reasoning steps, and is disabled thereafter (with $m=0$ corresponding to no steering). For each model–benchmark pair, we vary $m$ and evaluate ASR under jailbreak prompts.
L159: Across three representative MLRMs and three jailbreak benchmarks (details in the caption), ASR reduces from the jailbreak region (red) to the safe region (green) within $m\leq 3$ steps, and often within $m\leq 2$.
L160: Interpretation: early steps set the trajectory. Early reasoning steps establish the latent intent and high-level plan of the chain of thought. Under jailbreak prompts, unsafe intent is often formed early and then elaborated. Few-step steering, therefore, acts as a trajectory-level correction: by reconditioning the model in the initial steps, subsequent generations proceed under a context where safe continuations have higher conditional probability, so safety is maintained without continuous intervention.
L161: Why few-step steering preserves utility. Because steering is applied only for the first $m$ steps, the deviation from the base reasoning policy is localized. Let $\pi$ denote the unsteered policy and $\pi^{(m)}$ the policy induced by steering the first $m$ steps. Then the cumulative deviation along the trajectory is bounded by the sum of the per-step deviations at the steered steps:
L162:  | $\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}$  | $\displaystyle\!\Big(\pi^{(m)}(\cdot\mid x_{\mathrm{adv}})\,\Big\|\,\pi(\cdot\mid x_{\mathrm{adv}})\Big)$  |  | (10)
L163:  |  | $\displaystyle\leq\;\sum_{t=1}^{m}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\Big(\pi(\cdot\mid x_{\text{adv}},z_{<t},s)\,\Big\|\,\pi(\cdot\mid x_{\text{adv}},z_{<t})\Big),$  |
L164: 
L165: which remains small when $m$ is small. This helps explain why few-step steering can recover safety while largely preserving reasoning utility on benign inputs.

L168: ## 5 Experiments
L169: ### 5.1 Experimental Setup
L170: Benchmarks and Models. We evaluate on both text-based and image-based jailbreak attacks. For text-based attacks, we use JailbreakV-28K (cite67†Luo et al., 2024 ), which pairs adversarial prompts with diverse visual inputs. For image-based attacks, we use HADES (cite43†Li et al., 2024 ), FigStep (cite69†Gong et al., 2023 ), and MM-SafetyBench (cite76†Liu et al., 2023a ).
L171: We evaluate SafeThink on six state-of-the-art open-source MLRMs: R1-Onevision-7B (cite44†Yang et al., 2025 ), OpenVLThinker-7B (cite68†Deng et al., 2025 ), VLAA-Thinker-7B (cite77†Chen et al., 2025 ), Vision-R1-7B (cite78†Huang et al., 2025b ), LlamaV-o1 (cite66†Thawakar et al., 2025 ), and LLaVA-CoT (cite79†Xu et al., 2024 ). Further details are in Appendix cite27†E .
L172: Baselines and Metrics. We compare against inference-time defenses: ZeroThink, LessThink (cite41†Jiang et al., 2025 ), ZS-SafePath (cite46†Jeung et al., 2025 ), and AdaShield (cite80†Wang et al., 2024b ). Baseline descriptions are in Appendix cite31†E.2 . Following prior work (cite39†Fang et al., 2025 ; cite80†Wang et al., 2024b ), we report Attack Success Rate (ASR), measuring the fraction of jailbreak attempts that elicit harmful content in either the thinking trace or final answer:
L173:  | $$\text{ASR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{adv}}|}\sum_{x_{\text{adv}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{adv}}}\mathbb{I}[\mathcal{C}^{*}(x_{\text{adv}},[z,y])=\text{True}],$$  |  | (11)
L174: where $z$ denotes the thinking trace, $y$ the final answer, and $\mathcal{C}^{*}$ is an oracle classifier (GPT-4). We use Llama-Guard-3 (cite81†Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) as the safety evaluator ($R_{\text{safe}}$ for all main experiments, with additional results using Qwen-Guard-3 (cite82†Zhao et al., 2025 ) in Appendix cite24†D . We select $\tau=0$ and $k=3$ based on ablations in Figure cite83†14 (Appendix cite24†D ) and Figure cite61†2 , respectively.
L175: To account for randomness, we sample three independent responses for each adversarial query and consider the model successfully jailbroken if any one of the three responses is flagged as jailbroken by the oracle classifier.

L177: ### 5.2 Main Results
L178: 
L179: SafeThink outperforms baselines on JailbreakV. Figure cite86†6 presents results on the JailbreakV benchmark (cite67†Luo et al., 2024 ). The vulnerability of MLRMs is striking: even state-of-art models exhibit ASR as high as $64.3\%$ (LlamaV-o1), corroborating recent findings that stronger reasoning capabilities amplify susceptibility to adversarial prompts (cite39†Fang et al., 2025 ; cite40†Huang et al., 2025a ; cite41†Jiang et al., 2025 ).
L180: A natural hypothesis is that curtailing the reasoning process might mitigate this risk. However, truncation-based defenses (ZeroThink, LessThink (cite41†Jiang et al., 2025 )) prove largely ineffective; unsafe outputs persist even when reasoning is suppressed. Input-level safety steering defenses (ZS-SafePath (cite46†Jeung et al., 2025 ), AdaShield (cite80†Wang et al., 2024b )) offer marginal improvements but fail to address the root cause.
L181: In contrast, SafeThink applies targeted safety steering during the early stages of reasoning rather than suppressing the reasoning process entirely. This approach yields substantial improvements: ASR reductions of $57.6\%$ on LlamaV-o1 and $44.6\%$ on OpenVLThinker relative to undefended models. Compared to the strongest baseline, SafeThink achieves further reductions of 39.2% and 18.3%, respectively.
L182: These results are consistent with our analysis in Figure cite50†4 , indicating that steering interventions at early reasoning steps suffice to redirect unsafe trajectories toward safe completions.
L183: SafeThink achieves strong ASR reduction on Image-Based attacks. We next consider a more challenging threat model: jailbreak attacks embedded directly within visual inputs. On the HADES benchmark (cite43†Li et al., 2024 ) (Figure cite87†7 ), ASR reaches $69.1\%$ for R1-Onevision and $66.8\%$ for LlamaV-o1.
L184: Existing defenses provide limited mitigation: ZS-SafePath (cite46†Jeung et al., 2025 ) and AdaShield (cite80†Wang et al., 2024b ) reduce ASR by at most $25.3\%$ on R1-Onevision, leaving the majority of attacks successful. In contrast, SafeThink reduces ASR from $69.1\%$ to $5.7\%$, a $63.4\%$ absolute reduction, indicating that early-step steering remains effective even when harmful intent originates in the visual modality.
L185: We further examine whether these gains generalize across attack strategies. On FigStep (cite69†Gong et al., 2023 ) (Figure cite88†8 ), which embeds typographic attacks within images, a consistent pattern holds: LlamaV-o1 exhibits the highest baseline vulnerability, while SafeThink achieves substantial ASR reductions of $31.6\%$ for R1-Onevision and $30.8\%$ for Vision-R1.
L186: Results on MM-SafetyBench (cite71†Liu et al., 2024 ) (Figure cite89†11 ; Appendix cite24†D ) also corroborate these findings, with SafeThink yielding reductions of $39.8\%$ on VLAA-Thinker and $50.2\%$ on LLaVA-CoT.
L187: Takeaway. Across models, the strongest gains come from intervening early in reasoning rather than suppressing it or only steering the input. This supports our mechanism: early-step reconditioning restores conditional coverage of safe continuations, after which the trajectory remains safe with little additional intervention.

L190: ### 5.3 Additional Insights
L191: SafeThink preserves reasoning capabilities. A critical question for any safety intervention is whether it compromises the model’s core capabilities. We evaluate this trade-off on MathVista (cite93†Lu et al., 2024 ), a benchmark requiring fine-grained visual perception and multi-step mathematical reasoning. Figure cite94†9 presents results across multiple MLRMs and defense strategies.
L192: We observe a clear contrast: truncation-based methods incur substantial performance degradation, LessThink (cite41†Jiang et al., 2025 ) reduces accuracy by $15\%$ on VLAA-Thinker and $17.6\%$ on Vision-R1, consistent with prior observations that suppressing the reasoning process impairs downstream task performance (cite40†Huang et al., 2025a ). SafeThink, by contrast, maintains accuracy on par with the undefended model across all evaluated MLRMs.
L193: This result demonstrates that safety and capability need not be at odds: targeted early-step steering can effectively mitigate jailbreak vulnerabilities without degrading the reasoning abilities that make MLRMs valuable.

L207: ## 6 Related Works
L208: Multi-modal Large Reasoning Models. Building on Chain-of-Thought reasoning in LLMs (cite97†Wei et al., 2022 ), recent work has developed Multi-modal Large Reasoning Models (MLRMs) that employ reinforcement learning to achieve deeper, and more structured reasoning before generating a final answer (cite54†Guo et al., 2025 ; cite44†Yang et al., 2025 ; cite98†Peng et al., 2025 ; cite66†Thawakar et al., 2025 ; cite68†Deng et al., 2025 ).
L209: However, these enhanced reasoning capabilities introduce new safety vulnerabilities to adversarial attacks.
L210: Safety in MLRMs. Recent studies demonstrate that stronger reasoning capabilities can amplify jailbreak susceptibility rather than improve robustness (cite39†Fang et al., 2025 ; cite41†Jiang et al., 2025 ; cite40†Huang et al., 2025a ). Existing defenses either suppress reasoning entirely (cite41†Jiang et al., 2025 ) or prepend fixed safety prefixes (cite46†Jeung et al., 2025 ), facing an inherent trade-off between safety and reasoning quality.
L211: In contrast, we propose targeted safety steering during early reasoning steps, preserving reasoning capabilities while effectively mitigating attacks.
L212: Jailbreak Attacks. Jailbreak methods for LLMs optimize adversarial suffixes or prompts to bypass safety mechanisms (cite99†Zou et al., 2023 ; cite100†Zhu et al., 2024 ). Multi-modal extensions target visual inputs through adversarial perturbations (cite101†Qi et al., 2024a ; cite102†Niu et al., 2024 ), embed malicious instructions in images (cite69†Gong et al., 2023 ), or adapt text-based attacks (cite67†Luo et al., 2024 ). We evaluate SafeThink against both text-based and image-based attacks.
L213: Extended related work is provided in Appendix cite32†F .
L214: ## 7 Conclusion
L215: As multi-modal large language models are increasingly fine-tuned with reinforcement learning to enhance reasoning capabilities, recent studies reveal that such fine-tuning often weakens safety alignment. We investigate this safety–reasoning trade-off and find that vulnerability arises from RL objectives that prioritize task accuracy over safety constraints.
L216: To address this, we propose SafeThink, an inference-time defense that applies targeted safety steering during early reasoning steps rather than suppressing reasoning entirely. Through comprehensive evaluations on diverse jailbreak benchmarks, we show that SafeThink substantially improves safety robustness across various multi-modal reasoning models while preserving their reasoning capabilities.
L217: ## 8 Impact Statement
L218: This work addresses the challenge of maintaining safety alignment in multimodal large reasoning models (MLRMs), which are increasingly deployed in real-world applications. By demonstrating that safety recovery can be achieved through lightweight inference-time interventions rather than costly retraining, SafeThink offers a practical pathway for deploying reasoning-capable AI systems more responsibly.
L219: The societal benefits include reduced risk of AI systems generating harmful content in response to adversarial attacks, which is particularly important as these models are integrated into user-facing products, educational tools, and decision-support systems.
