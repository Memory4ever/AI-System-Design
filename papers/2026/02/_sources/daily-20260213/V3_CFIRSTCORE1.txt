L116: ## 2 Related Work
L117: ### 2.1 Corpus Search for NLP and Language Models
L118: In corpus linguistics and quantitative linguistics, contextually grounded examples of words or phrases, known as KWIC (cite84†Luhn, 1960 , Key Word In Context;), are used with concordancers to analyze patterns of actual language usage (cite85†Lexical Computing, 2015 ; cite86†NINJAL, 2011 ; cite87†Berlin-Brandenburgische Akademie der Wissenschaften, 2026 ).
L119: In traditional natural language processing, corpus searching serves as a general subroutine for extracting world knowledge from text corpora (information extraction) or as a preprocessing step for analyzing product reviews (sentiment analysis). In addition, language models are fundamentally built from large-scale corpora, making the study of language models inseparable from corpus research (cite88†Dodge et al., 2021 ).
L120: For instance, crucial studies that identify harmful biases and inappropriate expressions in training corpora, or detect benchmark contamination, are grounded in corpus search cite89†Guo et al. (2022) ; cite90†Ippolito et al. (2023) ; cite72†Deng et al. (2023) ; cite73†Xu et al. (2024) .
L121: Moreover, with the discovery of scaling laws (cite91†Kaplan et al., 2020 ) and the exponential growth of training corpora (cite92†Gokaslan et al., 2019 ; cite93†Raffel et al., 2019 ; cite94†Penedo et al., 2024b ), corpora have reached sizes that are no longer manually verifiable, thereby increasing the necessity for efficient corpus searching methods in recent years (cite47†Liu et al., 2024 ; cite48†Xu et al., 2025 ; cite49†Deguchi et al., 2025 ).
L122: ### 2.2 String Matching Algorithms
L123: 
L124: String matching, or matching a pattern string against a target string, has been a fundamental research topic in computer science with a long history; see, e.g., (cite95†Adjeroh et al., 2008 ) for the details. Traditionally, string matching has been designed to match strings at the letter level; however, most of the string-matching algorithms can be naturally applied to token-level search rather than letter-level search.
L125: A popular approach for scalable string matching is to build an index of the target text in advance and use it at runtime. Suffix arrays (cite96†Manber and Myers, 1990 ) and inverted indexing (cite97†Zobel and Moffat, 2006 ) are representative indexing schemes. Using a suffix array, one can locate the occurrences of the query text in $O(\log\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert)$ time, where $\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert$ is the corpus size. In contrast, a search with an inverted index takes linear time w.r.t.
L126: the frequency of the rarest letter in the target text—prohibitively costly for a massive corpus.
L127: String matching is generalized for approximate matching (i.e., matching a pattern to a target text modulo substitution, insertions, and deletions of letters), where the distance from the query text is measured by an edit distance (cite98†Wagner and Fischer, 1974 ). In addition to relaxation based on the semantic distance, our method supports matching with word insertions and deletions, inspired by this line of work.
L128: ### 2.3 Text Search for Massive Corpora
L129: This section discusses directly related prior work to this paper. For the search of trillion-scale corpora, infini-gram (cite47†Liu et al., 2024 ) and infini-gram mini (cite48†Xu et al., 2025 ) apply suffix arrays (cite96†Manber and Myers, 1990 ) and a variant of suffix array (i.e., FM-indexes (cite99†Ferragina and Manzini, 2005 )), which are powerful data structures for exact string matching, respectively.
L130: Our work follows the research line, significantly improving exact-match search speed (cite13†§§​ 3.2 and cite18†5.2 ), while also incorporating semantic substitutions, insertions, and deletions. SoftMatcha (cite49†Deguchi et al., 2025 ) is, to our knowledge, the only prior work that successfully incorporates semantic elements into text search for large-scale corpora.
L131: Note that, due to the characteristics of the inverted index (cite97†Zobel and Moffat, 2006 ) they adopted, it does not scale well to trillion-scale corpora. Our research uses suffix arrays, which are robust to increases in corpus size, and employs powerful pruning algorithms (cite14†§§​ 3.3 and cite19†5.3 ) to mitigate the exponential explosion of search space that arises when relaxing queries semantically. We examine the differences between our work and these three prior studies in experimental section.
L132: It is also worth mentioning methods that share the objective of “retrieving documents from queries” but differ somewhat in their goals and primary purposes. In corpus linguistics and classical natural language processing, regular expressions have been used as versatile and powerful pattern matchers cite100†Knight (2013) ; cite101†Manning and Schuetze (1999) .
L133: However, natural language frequently involves synonymous variants, phrase-level paraphrases, and various insertions and deletions of pronouns and adverbs, making it challenging to use regular expressions for large-scale corpus searches, such as contamination detection (cite72†Deng et al., 2023 ; cite73†Xu et al., 2024 ) that we discuss in cite22†§​ 6 .
L134: In the field of information retrieval, lexical search methods such as TF-IDF (cite102†Salton and Buckley, 1988 ) and BM25 (cite103†Robertson et al., 1994 ) have been widely used cite104†Ceri et al. (2013) . These methods use a form of set similarity between a query and a document to retrieve “related” documents, rather than performing strict text searches. Dense vector search (cite105†Karpukhin et al., 2020 ) is a more modern approach for finding “related” documents to a query.
L135: Technically, it uses text vectors (cite106†Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 ; cite107†Gao et al., 2021 ) and approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search cite108†Indyk and Motwani (1998) ; cite109†Andoni and Indyk (2008) ; cite110†Malkov and Yashunin (2020) , and has recently been particularly popular in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (cite111†Lewis et al., 2020 , RAG;).
L136: ## 3 SoftMatcha 2: Our Soft and Fast Corpus Search Algorithm
L137: Our direct predecessor SoftMatcha cite49†Deguchi et al. (2025) , which realizes soft search on billion-scale corpora, uses a “soft” inverted index and enumerated positions in the corpus directly. However, as the inverted index runs in linear time in the corpus size, it is unsuitable for trillion-scale corpora.
L138: Therefore, we take a new approach, inverting the perspective: our tool enumerates patterns (token sequences) similar to the query and checks whether each occurs in the corpus via exact lookup in a suffix array.
L139: There are two challenges to this approach: (i) lookup in a naive suffix array is slow due to many random disk accesses; and (ii) with naive search, the number of candidates increases exponentially with the query length. We solve (i) by speeding up exact lookups using a new disk-aware suffix array (cite13†§​ 3.2 ). Moreover, we address (ii) pruning the search space by dynamically exploiting the power-law distribution of $n$-grams in the corpus (cite14†§​ 3.3 ).
L140: The combination of the two gives a blazingly fast soft search for large corpora (see cite16†§​ 5 for the experimental results).
L141: ### 3.1 Problem Setting
L142: Our basic problem setting is as follows. The input is a query $\bar{q}=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m}$, a sequence of tokens, and a similarity threshold $\alpha$. The output is a set of patterns $\bar{p}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{n}$ that are semantically similar to the query $\bar{q}$ with similarity $\alpha$ or more. The similarity between patterns is calculated based on word (or token) embeddings. At a high level, this problem setting inherits that of SoftMatcha cite49†Deguchi et al.
L143: (2025) , but we introduce the following new twists.
L144: Adaptive Similarity Thresholding. Practically, it is often hard to find a similarity threshold $\alpha$ that is not too tight or loose. To mitigate that, our tool sets the similarity threshold adaptively as follows. First, we set the number $K$ of the matching patterns we want. Our tool first enumerates the soft matches (i.e., solves the above problem) with some pre-determined initial $\alpha$.
L145: If fewer than $K$ soft matches are found, our tool relaxes the threshold $\alpha$ (i.e., decreases its value) by some amount and enumerates the soft matches again. The tool repeats this until more than $K$ soft matches are obtained or the threshold $\alpha$ becomes too low.
L146: Definition of Similarity. We propose a new definition of pattern similarity $\mathop{\mathsf{sim}}(\bar{q},\bar{p})$ to improve search quality. First, the basic similarity (without deletion or insertion) between a query $\bar{q}=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m}$ and a pattern $p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}$ is defined as follows, letting $c_{i}$ ($\leq 1$) be the cosine similarity between the word embeddings of $q_{i}$ and $p_{i}$:
L147: 
L148:  | $$1-\log_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i}(\beta^{1-c_{i}}-1)+1\right).$$  |
L149: This is a smooth minimum of $c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m}$, with a parameter $\beta$, which is set to $\beta=10^{4}$ in our experiments.
L150: This improves over the plain minimum $\min(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})$ (the limit $\beta\to\infty$) used by SoftMatcha, which problematically ignores the similarities among all words except the one with the lowest score.^{3}^{3} 3 For example, the similarity between “gold medal” and “bronze medals” is approximately 0.62, if the cosine similarity of “gold” and “bronze” is 0.63 and that of “medal” and “medals” is 0.85.
L151: We extend the similarity above to allow insertions and deletions by scaling the similarity by some factor less than $1$ for each insertion or deletion. To keep the penalty for low-information words like “the” and “of” small, we use the word vector norm after Zipfian whitening cite112†Yokoi et al. (2024) , which correlates strongly with information entropy cite113†Oyama et al. (2023) . Letting $v$ be the squared norm of the inserted or deleted word, the scaling factor is $\exp\,(-v/\gamma)$.
L152: Here, $m$ is the length of the query. In our experiments, we set $\gamma=m\gamma^{\prime}$ for $\gamma^{\prime}$ such that the scaling factor equals $1/e$ when $m=5$ and the word with the 50th-lowest norm is inserted (e.g., $\gamma^{\prime}\approx 21.70$ for FineWeb-Edu).^{4}^{4} 4 Precisely speaking, we take the maximum similarity over the multiple ways to perform insertions and deletions.

L154: ### 3.2 Fast Disk-Aware Suffix Array
L155: Our soft search method is built on top of exact corpus lookup, and its efficiency is essential for scaling our method to a trillion-scale corpus. A naive corpus lookup implementation based on a suffix array faces a critical bottleneck in index access: the index for a trillion-scale corpus is enormous ($>$5\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}$$), which prevents storing it entirely in RAM and requires storing it on disk (e.g., SSD).
L156: Since disk access latency is significantly slower ($\approx\times 10^{3}$) than RAM, reducing the number of disk accesses is vital.
L157: To achieve this key goal, we newly design a disk-aware, staged suffix array. See cite38†Appendix H for the details. Our approach uses a two-staged indexing scheme, similar to Google’s BigTable cite114†Chang et al. (2008) : (1) identifying the range of potential matches in the first stage and (2) determining the precise match in the second stage, focusing on the identified area.
L158: This scheme requires only one disk access per exact match query, and the search time is about 33 times faster than infini-gram cite47†Liu et al. (2024) , a well-known corpus search engine based on suffix arrays. Moreover, we reduce the index size via run-length compression; in our experience, this compression reduced the overall index size from $56.0\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}$ to $21.6\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}$ for the FineWeb-Edu corpus.

L179: ### 3.3 Dynamic Corpus-Aware Pruning of Search Space
L180: 
L181: To reduce the exponential growth of the search space in soft pattern matching, we employ iterative pruning, which drastically reduces the number of candidate patterns by exploiting the power-law distribution of $n$-grams in the corpus. Below, we describe our algorithm (shown in cite115†Algorithm 1 ), through an example of searching for “olympics gold medal” with a given initial value of $\alpha$ (see cite116†Figure 2 for illustration).
L182: Step 1. Enumerate all the patterns $L^{\prime}_{1}$ whose similarity to the first query token, “olympics”, is at least $\alpha$. This step works only using the dictionary, without referencing the corpus. Then, filter $L^{\prime}_{1}$ into the patterns $L_{1}$ that occur in the corpus, using exact lookup by the fast suffix array (cite13†§​ 3.2 ).
L183: Step 2. For each $w\in L_{1}$, enumerate all the patterns $w\cdot w^{\prime}$ that have $w$ as a prefix and whose similarity to “olympics gold” is at least $\alpha$. We denote this set by $L^{\prime}_{2}$. As before, filter $L^{\prime}_{2}$ into those that appear in the corpus $L_{2}$.
L184: Step 3. Compute $L^{\prime}_{3}$ and $L_{3}$ analogously using the similarity to “olympics gold medal”. The final result is $L_{3}$.
L185: Other Techniques. To further improve the performance, we also introduce the following two techniques. Details are shown in cite39†Appendix I . (i) $k$-gram pruning: For the 2-grams and 3-grams consisting only of high-frequency words, we check if they appear in the corpus beforehand and keep this information in RAM; at runtime, we determine if they appear without any extra disk access.
L186: (ii) Last-bits pruning: When the word $w\in L_{i-1}$ has only a few occurrences in the corpus, we can directly enumerate all its occurrences and check if they match the query, instead of appending some suffixes to $w$ and looking for them in the corpus.
L187: ## 4 Theoretical Analysis
L188: 
L189: Why can our algorithm (cite11†§​ 3 ) work so effectively, as observed in cite16†§​ 5 ? How robustly is it efficient, across a number of parameters (language, corpus $\mathcal{C}$, threshold $\alpha$, query $\bar{q}$, constant $\gamma$, etc.)? To answer this, we here analyze our algorithm theoretically. See cite43†Appendix J for details.
L190: To begin with, let $S_{i}$ be $\{w\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\mid\mathop{\mathsf{sim}}(q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{i},w)\geq\alpha\}$, the set of patterns that are similar to the $i$-token prefix of the query $\bar{q}$. Note $L_{i}=\{w\in S_{i}\mid w\ \text{occurs in}\ \mathcal{C}\}$. Also, let $\mathcal{C}^{*}_{i}$ be the set of $i$-grams in the corpus. For analysis, we assume the following hypothesis:
L191: ###### Hypothesis 1 (Corpus occurrence ratio of similar words).
L192: 
L193: The corpus occurrence ratio $\lvert L_{i}\rvert/\lvert S_{i}\rvert$ over query-similar patterns on average is approximately order of $\lvert\mathcal{C}^{*}_{i}\rvert/\lvert\mathcal{V}^{i}\rvert$.
L194: Note that the ratio is very small for large $i$, since $\lvert\mathcal{C}^{*}_{i}\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert$. We believe that this hypothesis is valid from a linguistic perspective. The occurrence probability of $n$-grams depends on grammatical correctness and collocations. The set $S_{i}$ is roughly the direct product of the sets of words similar enough to each word in the query, which should show an occurrence tendency similar to the direct product $\mathcal{V}^{i}$.
L195: Assuming the hypothesis, we enjoy the following.
L196: ###### Theorem (Informal) 1 (No exponential explosion over $m$).
L197: The total number of exact lookups in cite115†Algorithm 1 on average is approximately bounded by a constant over the query length $m$, for fixed $\alpha$ and moderate $\gamma$.^{5}^{5} 5 Beware that this analysis is for fixed $\alpha$ and $\gamma$. Practically, $\alpha$ is adaptively relaxed so that $\lvert L_{m}\rvert\approx K$ holds. Also, $\gamma$ is made proportional to $K$ in our experiments. This explains the mild exponentiality we experimentally observed (cite117†Figure 5 , cite19†§​ 5.3 ).
L198: Assuming Zipf’s law of the $n$-gram distribution, we can further analyze the effect of the corpus size $\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert$.
L199: ###### Theorem (Informal) 2 (Sub-linearity over the corpus size $\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert$).
L200: Assume that, for each $n$, the occurrence probability of the $k$-th most frequent $n$-gram is $O\left\lparen k^{-\delta}\right\rparen$ for $\delta>1$. Then the total number of exact lookups in cite115†Algorithm 1 on average is approximately $O\left\lparen\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert^{1/\delta}\right\rparen$ with respect to $\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert$.^{6}^{6} 6 Again, beware that this analysis is for fixed $\alpha$.
L201: For larger $\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert$, $\alpha$ tends to be adaptively made smaller, as $\lvert L_{m}\rvert$ tends to be larger. This explains the scalability we observed (cite118†Figure 4 , cite19†§​ 5.3 ).
L202: Empirically, $\delta$ is typically $1.5$ or so for many languages cite119†Bellina and Servedio (2025) ; cite120†Silva and Cunha (2020) .

L241: ### 5.4 Disk Usage and Index Construction
L242: 
L243: For the full corpus of FineWeb-Edu (1.4T tokens, 6.7TB), the index of our method is 21.6TB, including raw text. This is only 2.2 times greater than infini-gram’s index size (9.9TB, excluding raw text), even though infini-gram supports only exact search. For index construction, our method took 53.8 hours, which was faster than infini-gram (61.2 h), infini-gram mini (timeout), and SoftMatcha (timeout).
