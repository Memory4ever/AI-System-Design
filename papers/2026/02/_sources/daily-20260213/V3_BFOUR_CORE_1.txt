L108: ### 3.2 Non-negative Factor Analysis (NFA)
L109: Non-negative factor analysis methods, such as Poisson factor analysis (PFA) (cite61†Zhou et al., 2012 ), are widely used as topic models (cite79†Blei and Lafferty, 2009 ). They impose non-negative stochastic latent variables on the model parameters to learn interpretable, parts-based representations of data. Specifically, representing document $\bm{x}$ as a BOW vector $\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{V}$, where $\mathbb{Z}=\{0,1,\cdots\}$ and $V$ is the vocabulary size, PFA models $\bm{b}$ under the Poisson likelihood as
L110:  | $$\bm{b}\sim\mbox{Poisson}\left(\bm{\Phi}\bm{\theta}\right),\bm{\theta}\sim\mbox{Gamma}\left(\alpha,1\right).$$  |  | (1)
L111: Here, the matrix $\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{V\times K}$ is the dictionary, where each column represents a topic as a distribution over words. The vector $\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}$ contains the document-specific topic proportions (i.e., document features) that represent the strength of each topic in the document.
L112: Benefiting from the sparsi ty of latent variables, which is often encouraged by placing Gamma priors with hyperparameter $\alpha$ on $\bm{\theta}$, NFA effectively handles overdispersed data and exhibits strong generalization ability.

L118: ## 4 The Bayesian Non-negative Reward Model
L119: 
L120: Our methodology is rooted in a Bayesian perspective, comprising a generative process that describes our idealized assumptions about how preferences are formed, and an inference process that details how we approximate this model in practice using deep neural networks.
L121: ### 4.1 BT from a Bayesian Viewpoint
L122: 
L123: The BNRM framework, introduced intuitively in the previous section, can be formally derived as a hierarchical Bayesian extension of the standard Bradley-Terry (BT) model (cite78†Bradley and Terry, 1952 ). This viewpoint clarifies how BNRM systematically addresses the limitations of the deterministic approach. From a Bayesian perspective, as shown in Figure cite82†2(a) , the standard BT model is a special case of the following integral formulation:
L124:  | $\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{1}\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}$  | $\displaystyle|\,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2})=\int_{{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}}p({\bm{y}}_{1}\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}\,|\,{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2})\,$  |  | (2)
L125:  |  | $\displaystyle p({\bm{z}}_{1}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{x}})\,p({\bm{z}}_{2}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{2},{\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{z}}_{1}\,d{\bm{z}}_{2}.$  |
L126: Here, the deterministic nature of the underlying neural network $f$ means that the conditional probability $P({\bm{z}}\,|\,{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})$ is a Dirac delta distribution, $P(\bm{z}\,|\,{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})=\delta({\bm{z}}-f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))$, which offers no mechanism to capture uncertainty. Our BNRM generalizes this model in two steps:
L127: #### Modeling Aleatoric Uncertainty.
L128: 
L129: We first replace the deterministic latent representation $z$ with a stochastic latent variable $\theta$. This allows the model to capture the inherent randomness and ambiguity in human preference data. The preference probability is now marginalized over the distribution of $\theta$:
L130:  | $\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{1}$  | $\displaystyle\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}\,|\,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2})=\int_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}P({\bm{y}}_{1}\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}\,|\,\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,$  |  | (3)
L131:  |  | $\displaystyle p(\theta_{1}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{x}})\,\cdot P(\theta_{2}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{2},{\bm{x}})\,d\theta_{1}\,d\theta_{2}.$  |
L132: #### Modeling Epistemic Uncertainty.
L133: 
L134: To further account for the model’s own uncertainty about the global reward factors, we treat the final layer weights (denoted as $\Phi$) as a global stochastic variable. This leads to the full, formal generative process for BNRM, as shown in Figure cite83†2(b) :
L135:  | $\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{1}$  | $\displaystyle\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}\,|\,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2})=\int_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\Phi}p({\bm{y}}_{1}\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}\,|\,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\Phi)\,$  |  | (4)
L136:  |  | $\displaystyle p(\theta_{1}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{x}})\,p(\theta_{2}\,|\,{\bm{y}}_{2},{\bm{x}})\,p(\Phi)\,d\theta_{1}\,d\theta_{2}\,d\Phi,$  |
L137: 
L138: which final integral represents the complete generative process of our proposed model.
L139: ### 4.2 The BNRM Generative Process
L140: Recall that Eq. cite84†4 is designed to systematically capture both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in reward modeling. Building on this formulation, we propose a fully probabilistic generative model in which human preferences arise from sparse, non-negative latent factors. This framework replaces the standard deterministic reward formulation, $r=f({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})^{\top}W_{\text{bt}}$, with a structured Bayesian alternative that explicitly models uncertainty, disentanglement, and bias.
L141: The generative process introduces two complementary sets of latent variables:
L142: 1. Local Sparse Representation (Disentanglement). For each prompt–response pair $(\bm{x},\bm{y})$, we introduce a non-negative latent vector $\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}$ that captures the sparse activations of global reward factors specific to $\bm{y}$. Sparsity in $\bm{\theta}$ encourages instance-level disentanglement by activating only a small subset of latent factors, thereby improving identifiability and reducing reliance on entangled or spurious features (cite85†Zheng et al., 2022 ).
L143: 2. lobal Reward Dictionary (Debiasing). We further introduce a global dictionary of reward factors $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}$, shared across all data points, which defines a universal non-negative basis for evaluating response quality. Sparsity imposed on $\Phi$ acts as a population-level regularizer, selectively retaining invariant and semantically meaningful factors while suppressing spurious correlations in reward estimation (cite86†Zhang et al., 2021 ; cite62†Zhou et al., 2022 ).
L144: To jointly enforce non-negativity and sparsity, we place Gamma priors, a standard choice in non-negative factor analysis (cite61†Zhou et al., 2012 ), on both sets of latent variables:
L145: 
L146:  | $$\Phi\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\gamma_{0},\delta_{0}),\qquad\bm{\theta}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}).$$  |  | (5)
L147: 
L148: Given these latent factors, the scalar reward associated with a response $({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})$ is generated as
L149: 
L150:  | $$r({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Phi,$$  |  | (6)
L151: which yields a non-negative, interpretable reward constructed from sparse factor activations. Finally, for a pair of candidate responses $({\bm{y}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{2})$ with corresponding rewards $(r_{1},r_{2})$, the observed human preference is generated via a Bradley–Terry likelihood:
L152: 
L153:  | $$p({\bm{y}}_{1}\succ{\bm{y}}_{2}\mid r_{1},r_{2})=\sigma(r_{1}-r_{2}),$$  |  | (7)
L154: where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. This completes a coherent probabilistic account of how human preferences emerge from sparse, non-negative latent reward factors, naturally supporting uncertainty quantification, disentanglement, and debiasing within a unified Bayesian framework.
L155: ### 4.3 Variational Inference and Training Objective
L156: Given the generative formulation of BNRM defined in Eqs. cite84†4 and cite87†5 , the exact posterior distributions over the latent variables, $p(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{y})$ and $p(\Phi\,|\,\mathcal{D})$, are analytically intractable. We therefore resort to variational inference (VI) to obtain a scalable approximation. Specifically, we introduce tractable variational distributions $q(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{y})$ and $q(\Phi)$ to approximate the true posteriors.
L157: Importantly, we re-purpose the deep LLM backbone $f$ not as part of the generative model, but as a powerful inference network (encoder) for amortized inference of the local latent variables. As illustrated in Figure cite88†3 , for each prompt–response pair $(\bm{x},\bm{y})$, the inference proceeds as follows:
L158: We first extract a deterministic, high-dimensional and dense feature representation using the LLM backbone with parameter $W_{\text{llm}}$: $\bm{z}=f(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$. The feature vector ${\bm{z}}$ is then used to parameterize the variational distribution of the corresponding local latent variable $\bm{\theta}_{i}$.
L159: Following prior work on scalable inference for non-negative latent variable models (cite66†Zhang et al., 2020 ), we adopt a Weibull distribution due to its convenient reparameterization and its ability to model sparse, positive random variables:
L160:  | $\displaystyle q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x},\bm{y})$  | $\displaystyle=\mathrm{Weibull}(\bm{k},\bm{\lambda}),$  |  | (8)
L161:  | $\displaystyle(\bm{k},\bm{\lambda})$  | $\displaystyle=\mathrm{Activation}(\bm{z}W_{\text{vi}}),$  |
L162: where $W_{\text{vi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times 2K}$, $\bm{k},\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}$ denote the shape and scale parameters, respectively. Within the inference network, we parameterize the shape parameter $\bm{k}_{i}$ using a Softplus activation to ensure differentiability and numerical stability, while the scale parameter $\bm{\lambda}_{i}$ is parameterized using a ReLU activation, which empirically encourages sparsity in the sampled latent variables.
L163: cite89†Image: Refer to caption Figure 3: Variational Inferencer for BNRM.
L164: 
L165: The variational distribution for the global latent variable $q(\Phi)$ is parameterized analogously as a Weibull distribution with its own set of learnable parameters $W_{\Phi}$. We train the entire model, including the LLM backbone $f$ and the parameters of all variational distributions, by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) on the log-likelihood of the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$:
L166:  | $\displaystyle\max_{W_{\text{llm}},W_{\text{vi}},{\Phi}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})=$  | $\displaystyle\max_{W_{\text{llm}},W_{\text{vi}},{\Phi}}[\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{y})q(\Phi)}\big[\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\bm{\theta},\Phi)\big]$  |  | (9)
L167:  |  | $\displaystyle-\eta\mbox{KL}\!\left(q(\bm{\theta}\,|\,\bm{x},\bm{y})\,\|\,p(\bm{\theta})\right)$  |
L168:  |  | $\displaystyle-\eta\mbox{KL}\!\left(q(\Phi)\,\|\,p(\Phi)\right)].$  |
L169: The first term corresponds to the reconstruction likelihood, ensuring that the inferred latent variables explain the observed human preferences. The KL divergence regularizes the variational posteriors toward the respective priors, thereby enforcing sparsity, controlling model complexity, and improving robustness against reward over-optimization. $\eta$ is a trade-off balancing likelihood and KL divergence. We summarize the algorithm in Appendix cite26†A.1 .
L170: ### 4.4 Intuition on Disentanglement and then Debias for Reward Modeling
L171: We provide an intuitive analysis of why BNRA effectively mitigates spurious correlations in reward modeling. As illustrated in Figure cite65†1 (bottom), sparsity constraints imposed on the local and global latent variables play complementary and synergistic roles. Specifically, sparsity in the local latent variables $\bm{\theta}$ encourages instance-level disentanglement by activating only a small subset of semantic factors that are sufficient to explain a given prompt–response pair.
L172: This selective activation suppresses incidental or idiosyncratic features, thereby improving interpretability and reducing sensitivity to spurious patterns at the individual sample level. In contrast, sparsity in the global latent variables $\bm{\Phi}$ enforces population-level invariance, effectively identifying and down-weighting systematic but non-causal biases that persist across the dataset.
L173: Crucially, the interaction between local and global sparsity yields a structured reward function that is both interpretable and robust.
