L834: ### C.4 Experimental verification of equivalence
L835: To verify Theorem cite109†2.5 , we perform experiments on MLPs, ResNets, and Transformers with variants of SGD, Adam, and AdamW. For each model, we first train a base model from scratch for several epochs under $\mu$P, then construct a widened model whose weights are obtained using the rule in Table cite105†1 . We next create an optimizer for the widened model and transfer the internal state from the base model to the widened model (explained in Appendix cite41†D ).
L836: Finally, we train both the base and widened models under $\mu$P using the same base hyperparameters and compare their equivalence after each training step.
L837: All models pass this verification. We directly use the $\mu$P package introduced in cite91†Yang et al. [2022] , with slight modifications to recover our specific scaling in Table cite110†2 that is essential for exact equivalence. We explain these modifications below.

L890: ##### Transfer weights: implementation of Step 1.
L891: 
L892: With the modified $\mu$P package in place, we implement weight transfer from the base to the widened model via a generic routine that works for standard architectures that we consider in our experiments. Concretely, the routine operates on both named_parameters (model weights) and, when present, named_buffers (e.g., BatchNorm running statistics).
L893: For each tensor in named_parameters saved in the checkpoint, we query its $\mu$P infshape to classify it as scalar-like, vector-like, or matrix-like, and then duplicate/rescale according to the rules in the “Widening operation” column of Table cite105†1 .
L894: For named_buffers, usually simple duplication and copy-over is sufficient. In particular, BatchNorm running_mean and running_var are vector-like and are duplicated without rescaling, while scalar buffers such as num_batches_tracked are copied as-is. Typically no matrix-like buffers occur, so no rescaling is needed.
L895: ##### Transfer optimizer’s internal state: implementation of Step 3.
L896: One detail not elaborated in Meta-Algorithm cite111†1 is how to transfer the optimizer’s internal state, as in Step 3. In Theorem cite109†2.5 , for simplicity of analysis, we construct the widened model before any training and show that the widened and base models undergo equivalent updates when the hyperparameters are set appropriately. However, in practical upscaling, we perform widening mid-training.
L897: To ensure that the widened model (without adding any noise) is updated equivalently to the base model, as if no upscaling had occurred, it is necessary to transfer the optimizer’s internal state.
L898: We implemented a generic routine for transferring optimizer, which works for SGD, Adam, and AdamW (and their variants). Extension to other optimizers is possible but may require additional adjustments. The procedure mirrors the transfer of weights. For each tensor in named_parameters saved in the optimizer’s checkpoint, we query its $\mu$P infshape to classify it as scalar-like, vector-like, or matrix-like, and then duplicate and rescale accordingly. Scalar-like tensors are copied as is.
L899: For vector-like and matrix-like tensors, in SGD the relevant state is the saved momentum, and in Adam/AdamW this is the saved exp_avg or exp_avg_sq, for the corresponding vector-like or matrix-like weight. Recall that the gradients of the equivalent widened model satisfy
L900:  | $$dW^{\uparrow}=k_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}dW\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\mathrm{out}}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\mathrm{in}}}^{\top}\quad\text{ if $W$ is matrix-like};$$  |
L901:  | $$dW^{\uparrow}=k^{-1}dW\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k}\quad\text{ if $W$ is vector-like}.$$  |
L902: Saved momentum and exp_avg should be transferred in the same way as the gradient $dW$. We treat the exp_avg_sq case separately, as it requires a distinct rescaling: the scaling factors $k_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}$ or $k^{-1}$ should be squared, consistent with second-moment accumulation.
L903: ##### Practical considerations for weight, buffer, and optimizer-state transfer.
L904: As detailed previously, our algorithm requires transferring not only model weights, but also the model’s named_buffers and the optimizer’s internal state. Transferring model weights is the most essential component. Transferring named_buffers and optimizer state is needed to guarantee that, with zero added noise, the upscaled model trains exactly as if no upscaling had occurred.
L905: In practice, however, especially when upscaling a published model for which optimizer checkpoints may be unavailable, it is acceptable to omit the transfer of named_buffers and optimizer state, and performance may remain strong.
L906: ## Appendix E Infinite-width training dynamics for upscaling
L907: We present in Appendix cite47†E.1 a modified Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program tailored to analyzing infinite-width limits under mid-training upscaling. In Appendix cite49†E.2 , we prove its correctness. The proof strategy is to show that the modified program can be expressed as compositions of operations in the original Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program, thereby reducing the analysis to the standard framework and enabling the application of Tensor Program techniques.
L908: Finally, to illustrate the usefulness of our modified Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program for analyzing infinite-width limits with upscaling, we work through two simple examples in Appendix cite50†E.3 and Appendix cite55†E.4 , from which we derive several insights.
L909: ### E.1 Main result: modified Tensor Program
L910: #### A short recap of the original Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program
L911: The original Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program, introduced in cite89†Yang and Littwin [2023] , captures infinite-width training dynamics for standard neural network architectures. First, one writes all neural computations during training in the Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ language, including both forward and backward passes at each training step.
L912: Given this Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program, one then defines the “ket” construction recursively: each vector appearing in the program (for example, layer preactivations) is represented as a random variable (called a “ket”) that encodes its distribution as the width $n\to\infty$, and the limiting model outputs or losses at each step are expressed as expectations of functions of these random variables.
L913: This reduction yields a deterministic system of “limit equations” describing the evolution of activation and weight distributions over time. Finally, the Master Theorem provides the theoretical foundation, rigorously characterizing the sense in which finite-width dynamics converge to the limiting object defined by the ket construction. We now present our modified Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program, which retains this structure.
L914: #### Modified Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program language
L915: We characterize the infinite-width limit $n\to\infty$ for the following training-and-upscaling procedure: Consider a network architecture specified in the Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ language with hidden width $n$. After training for $T$ epochs at width $n$, we upscale to a wider model with hidden width $N=kn$, where $k$ is a fixed constant, and then continue training for an additional $T^{\prime}$ epochs.
L916: (Because we focus on asymptotic training dynamics, it suffices to take all hidden layers to have the same width, as in the original Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ construction; this contrasts with Appendix cite31†C.2 , where we allow heterogeneous hidden widths and expansion factors to study exact finite-width equivalence.)
L917: Rather than extending the language to directly implement the upscaling operation, we seek the minimal modification of Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ that preserves its structure as a composition of matrix multiplications and elementwise nonlinearities while enabling the expression of upscaling. By the analysis in Section cite8†2 , the training dynamics at width $n$ over the first $T$ epochs can be represented equivalently at width $kn$.
L918: Consequently, instead of changing dimension mid-training, we model the entire procedure at the higher dimension $kn$ by lifting the pre-upscaling stage to that dimension. We therefore define a modified Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program whose only deviation from Definition 2.6.1 of cite89†Yang and Littwin [2023] is that all hidden-layer dimensions are $nk$, where $n$ is the pre-upscaled hidden width and $k$ is the multiplier.

L966: #### The “ket” construction
L967: In the infinite-width limit ($n\to\infty$), the training dynamics of a neural network can be analyzed using a calculus of random variables. Without upscaling, in the original Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program, the preactivation vector in each layer is distributed like $n$ i.i.d. random variables. Given a Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program, cite89†Yang and Littwin [2023] constructs the associated limit objects using the ket notation, which are random variables that track these preactivations at every step of training.
L968: We proceed analogously with upscaling; the difference is that, in this case, each layer’s preactivations behave like $n$ blocks consisting of random vectors of dimension $k$, where these small vectors may have depending entries, but different vectors are i.i.d. Hence, we need to keep track of random $k$-vectors rather than just scalar random variables.
L969: We first clarify the notation, which is based on but slightly deviates from that in cite89†Yang and Littwin [2023] .
