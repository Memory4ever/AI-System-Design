L461: ## Appendix C Extensions of Subspace Camouflage
L462: 
L463: In this section, we extend our defense strategy subspace camouflage to ROME and AlphaEdit by formalizing the constraint problems and providing their solutions. Here we still require the same construction of $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}$ (for ROME, $N=1$, $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}$ reduces to the vector $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{*}$), while the mathematical forms of defense weight updates $\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense}$ are slightly different.
L464: We recall the constraint problem and its solution for MEMIT in §cite17†5.1 . The constraint problem is formulated by
L465: 
L466:  | $$\begin{cases}\row(\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,MEMIT}\mathbf{C})&\subseteq\col(\tilde{\mathbf{K}}),\\
L467: \Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,MEMIT}\mathbf{K}&=\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm MEMIT}\mathbf{K}.\end{cases}$$  |  | (15)
L468: 
L469: Its solution is (proven in Theorem cite110†E.22 , Appendix cite44†E.5 )
L470:  | $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,MEMIT}$  | $\displaystyle=\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm MEMIT}\mathbf{K}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{K}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}.$  |  | (16)
L471: ### C.1 Extension to ROME
L472: 
L473: ROME is designed for a single-edit setting ($N=1$). Following similar arguments in §cite17†5.1 , now the constraints become:
L474: 
L475:  | $$\begin{cases}\row(\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,ROME}\mathbf{C})&\subseteq\spn(\{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{*}\}),\\
L476: \Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,ROME}\mathbf{k}_{*}&=\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm ROME}\mathbf{k}_{*}.\end{cases}$$  |  | (17)
L477: 
L478: Here $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{*}$ is the exactly the $N=1$ case of $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}$.
L479: By solving the constraints above (detailed in Theorem cite110†E.22 , Appendix cite44†E.5 ) we obtain the new weight update as:
L480: 
L481:  | $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,ROME}$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm ROME}\mathbf{k}_{*}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}}{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{*}}.$  |  | (18)
L482: ### C.2 Extension to AlphaEdit
L483: 
L484: For AlphaEdit, since Algorithm cite29†B.2 only reconstructs $\col(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{K})$, we slightly modify the constraint on the linear subspaces (change the right side from $\col(\tilde{\mathbf{K}})$ to $\col(\mathbf{P}\tilde{\mathbf{K}})$), while retaining the consistency of $\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,AlphaEdit}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm AlphaEdit}$ on edited subjects $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}$:
L485:  | $$\begin{cases}\row(\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,AlphaEdit})&\subseteq\col(\mathbf{P}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}),\\
L486: \Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,AlphaEdit}\mathbf{K}&=\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm AlphaEdit}\mathbf{K}.\end{cases}$$  |  | (19)
L487: 
L488: By solving the constraints (detailed in Theorem cite110†E.22 , Appendix cite44†E.5 ) we obtain
L489:  | $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm defense,AlphaEdit}$  | $\displaystyle=\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\rm AlphaEdit}\mathbf{K}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{K}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}.$  |  | (20)

L492: ## Appendix D Implementation Details & Additional Experiments
L493: ### D.1 Detailed Experimental Setup for Attack
L494: 
L495: This section provides additional details regarding the experimental configurations, library construction, and metric definitions for our attack framework.
L496: Implementation Details. For each dataset, we randomly sample $N$ knowledge tuples from the first 2,000 entries. We set $N=1$ for the single-edit algorithm (ROME) and vary $N\in\{10,50,100\}$ for batched editors (MEMIT and AlphaEdit). To ensure statistical significance, all reported results are averaged over five independent runs. Furthermore, we follow the configurations of AlphaEdit (cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ) for the calculation of the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}$ and the projection matrix $\mathbf{P}$.
L497: Candidate Library Construction. To simulate a realistic attack scenario where the attacker does not have exact knowledge of the edited subjects and prompts, we construct the following candidate libraries:
L498: 
L499:   * •
L500: 
L501: Subject Library: We aggregate the subjects from the first 2,000 examples of the target dataset (CounterFact or zsRE) to form the candidate pool.
L502: 
L503:   * •
L504: Prompt Library: We build a unified candidate pool of 1,000 prompts. This is achieved by aggregating and deduplicating prompts from the first 2,000 examples of both CounterFact and zsRE. Additional diverse prompts are added to reach the fixed size of 1,000.
L505: Gray-box Baseline Details. The gray-box baseline serves to quantify the advantage of accessing internal weights ($\Delta\mathbf{W}$) over observing external behavior. For each candidate subject $s_{i}^{c}$, we query both the pre-edit model $\theta$ and the post-edit model $\theta^{\prime}$ with a generic template. We compute the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence between their next-token logit distributions: $D_{JS}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}})$.
L506: The $N$ subjects with the highest divergence are identified as the predicted targets. Regarding the recovery of prompt templates, the procedure is identical to stage II of our white-box attack, as it relies solely on output behavior rather than internal parameter computations.
L507: Detailed Metric Definitions.
L508: 
L509:   * •
L510: 
L511: Subject Inference: Subject recall@N measures the percentage of cases where the ground-truth subject is included in the top-$N$ predicted candidates. The average projection coefficient quantifies the spectral alignment between the subject’s key vector and the principal linear subspace derived by parameter update $\Delta\mathbf{W}$.
L512: 
L513:   * •
L514: Prompt Recovery: Top-$N_{r}$ recall ($N_{r}\in\{1,5,20\}$) evaluates the exact-match success rate. Semantic similarity (Sim.) utilizes Sentence-BERT (SBERT) (cite96†Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 ) to compute the average cosine similarity between the ground-truth prompt and the top-5 recovered candidates, reflecting the recovery of semantic essence.
L515: 
L516: For all of these metrics, higher values indicate better performance.
L517: Once the subject and the prompt are successfully recovered, the sensitive information can be easily extracted by querying the pre-edit model $\theta$.
L518: ### D.2 Detailed Experimental Setup for Defense
L519: 
L520: This section provides the implementation details and the set of metrics used to evaluate our defense mechanism.
L521: Implementation Details. We evaluate the defense on Llama3-8B-Instruct. The defense is integrated into three representative editors: ROME (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ), MEMIT (cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ), and AlphaEdit (cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ). For MEMIT and AlphaEdit, we adopt a batch editing setting with a batch size of $N=100$, while for ROME, we use the default single-edit setting ($N=1$).
L522: The strength of the defense is controlled by the camouflage coefficient $\alpha$, where $\alpha=0$ represents the vanilla, unprotected model. To ensure a rigorous evaluation, we employ our white-box subject inference attack (described in  §cite11†4 ) as the attacker. We construct the decoy key matrix by sampling $N$ decoy subjects from the first 2,000 examples for batch editing, whereas for single editing, the decoy key vector is obtained by averaging the key vectors of 10 sampled subjects.
L523: All results are the average of 5 independent runs.
L524: Editing Metrics. Distinct from previous works (cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ) that often rely on relative probability (e.g., $P(y_{target})>P(y_{original})$), we implement a stricter exact match standard based on greedy decoding to better reflect real-world editing quality:
L525: 
L526:   * •
L527: 
L528: Efficacy (Eff): The rate at which the model’s greedy generation exactly matches the target output $y_{\text{target}}$.
L529: 
L530:   * •
L531: Generalization (Gen): The exact match rate when the model is queried with semantically equivalent but lexically different rephrased prompts.
L532: 
L533:   * •
L534: 
L535: Specificity (Spe): The exact match rate with the original ground-truth answer for neighborhood (unrelated) prompts, ensuring no over-generalization.
L536: 
L537:   * •
L538: 
L539: Fluency (Flu): Measured by the $n$-gram entropy of generated sequences to monitor for repetitive patterns or linguistic collapse.
L540: 
L541:   * •
L542: Consistency (Consis): The TF-IDF cosine similarity between generated text and a set of reference texts to measure semantic stability.
L543: Editing protection. To quantify the effectiveness of concealing edited subjects, we utilize the average rank of true edited subjects within the constructed candidate library. During an attack, the attacker ranks all candidates based on their inference scores; a higher average rank for the true subject indicates that it is effectively obscured among decoys, reflecting stronger editing protection.
L544: Model Utility. To evaluate whether the defense mechanism compromises the model’s general capabilities, we test the edited models on six standard benchmarks: SST, MMLU, MRPC, CoLA, RTE, and NLI (cite165†Hendrycks et al., 2020 ; cite166†Wang et al., 2018 ). We report the F1 scores across these tasks. A minimal performance drop compared to the pre-edit model indicates that the defense successfully preserves the model’s general utility.
L545: 
L546: For all of these metrics, higher values indicate better performance.

L553: 
L554: Subject Inference Performance on ROME. We evaluate the performance of our subject inference attack on ROME in Table cite98†4 . Specifically, for ROME (Table cite105†5 ), the Top-20 recall for all three models consistently reaches $1.00$, with semantic similarity remaining above $0.84$.
L556: For the more complex AlphaEdit (Table cite106†6 ), the recovery quality remains stable as the number of edits $N$ scales from 10 to 100, where both GPT-J and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct achieve Top-20 recall rates exceeding $0.95$ on CounterFact. Although performance on zsRE shows a slight decline compared to CounterFact due to the QA format, the semantic similarity scores ($0.77\text{--}0.89$) confirm that the recovered prompts maintain a high degree of semantic correlation with the original prompts.
L557: Semantic Ambiguity in case of “Anaal Nathrakh.” Figure cite108†6 illustrates the score ranking of candidate prompts for the subject “Anaal Nathrakh,” revealing the phenomenon of semantic ambiguity” during the editing process. In this case, although the original prompt ({}, that was created in {}) is successfully recovered, it ranks only 17th and is masked by several unrelated candidate prompts.
L558: The top-five prompts primarily consist of location-related templates, such as The headquarters of {} is in {} and {} was developed in {}. This indicates that existing editing methods fail to precisely identify the “musical group” entity category, instead treating it as an entity with location attributes (such as a company or software). This exposes the deficiencies of existing editing algorithms, consequently limiting the performance of our prompt recovery attack.

L446: ### A.3 Metrics
L447: 
L448: We employ Refusal Success Rate (RSR), Benign Acceptance Rate (BAR), and Accuracy (Acc.) to evaluate the multifaceted performance of alignment methods.
L449: 
L450:   * •
L451: Refusal Success Rate. This metric measures the model’s capability to refuse answering when receiving harmful queries, essentially assessing whether the model can align with human values. We utilize Qwen3-30B-A3B as an LLM-as-a-Judge, employing the prompt shown in Appendix cite244†9 for evaluation, which returns binary classification results of safe or unsafe. We compute the average of classification results across all harmful queries.
L452: 
L453:   * •
L454: Benign Acceptance Rate. This metric measures the model’s capability to respond normally when receiving benign queries, evaluating the model’s understanding and application of the “safety” concept. We utilize Qwen3-30B-A3B as an LLM-as-a-Judge, employing the prompt shown in Appendix cite245†10 for evaluation, which returns binary classification results of acceptance or over-refusal. We compute the average of classification results across all benign queries.
