L48: # Stop Training for the Worst:
L49: Progressive Unmasking Accelerates Masked Diffusion Training
L50: 
L51: Jaeyeon Kim Affiliation: Harvard University Correspondence to: jaeyeon_kim@g.harvard.edu    Jonathan Geuter Affiliation: Harvard University Affiliation: Kempner Institute    David Alvarez-Melis Affiliation: Harvard University Affiliation: Kempner Institute    Sham Kakade Affiliation: Harvard University Affiliation: Kempner Institute    Sitan Chen Affiliation: Harvard University
L52: ###### Abstract
L53: Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) have emerged as a promising approach for generative modeling in discrete spaces. By generating sequences in any order and allowing for parallel decoding, they enable fast inference and strong performance on non-causal tasks.
L54: However, this flexibility comes with a training complexity trade-off: MDMs train on an exponentially large set of masking patterns, which is not only computationally expensive, but also creates a train–test mismatch between the random masks used in training and the highly structured masks induced by inference-time unmasking.
L55: In this work, we propose Progressive UnMAsking (PUMA), a simple modification of the forward masking process that aligns training-time and inference-time masking patterns, thereby focusing optimization on inference-aligned masks and speeding up training. Empirically, PUMA speeds up pretraining at the 125M scale by $\approx 2.5\times$ and offers complementary advantages on top of common recipes like autoregressive initialization. We open-source our codebase at cite36†(Codebase link)†github.com .

L59: ## 1 Introduction
L60: 
L61: Figure 1: PUMA (blue) accelerates Masked Diffusion Model training (red) by changing the forward process. Experiment on 125M-scale, from scratch trained on TinyGSM (cite37†Liu et al., 2023 ). Moreover, PUMA is compatible with autoregressive initialization. (purple curve over green curve)
L62: In recent years, Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) (cite38†Lou et al., 2023 ; cite39†Shi et al., 2024 ; cite40†Sahoo et al., 2024 ) have emerged as a compelling alternative to autoregressive models for discrete generative modeling, driven by rapid scaling efforts from 7B (cite41†Ye et al., 2025 ; cite42†Nie et al., 2025 ; cite43†Gong et al., 2025 ; cite44†Song et al., 2025 ) to 100B parameters (cite45†Bie et al., 2025 ), as well as industry-scale deployments (cite46†Labs et al., 2025 ; cite47†DeepMind, 2025 ).
L63: Much of their appeal comes from inference-time advantages: parallel decoding speeds up generation, while any-order inference better accommodates tasks that require non-autoregressive generation.
L64: At the same time, MDMs come with a well-known trade-off (cite48†Kim et al., 2025b ), Train for the Worst: to have any-order generation ability, they must train on unmasking tasks with an exponential number of masking patterns, substantially increasing the complexity of training. Under this paradigm, compute is spread across all masking patterns, while at inference, the masking patterns occupy a negligible fraction of the ones seen during training.
L65: This is because adaptive inference prioritizes positions for which the model’s predictions are most certain, causing unmasking decisions to concentrate on a small subset of masking patterns.
L66: Yet, this mismatch can be turned into an opportunity: training complexity can be reduced by focusing on inference-aligned masking patterns. However, despite training efficiency being essential to scaling MDMs, recent work has largely emphasized inference, leaving MDM training comparatively underexplored.
L67: In this work, we propose P rogressive U n MA sking (PUMA). With a modified forward process, PUMA generates training examples whose masking patterns provably match those observed at inference, thereby resolving the train-test mismatch.
L68: To our knowledge, PUMA is the first approach to accelerate discrete diffusion training by redesigning the forward masking process itself, rather than architectural (cite49†Arriola et al., 2025b ; cite50†Ni and team, 2025 ) or recipe-level remedies, such as autoregressive initialization (cite45†Bie et al., 2025 ; cite41†Ye et al., 2025 ; cite43†Gong et al., 2025 ; cite47†DeepMind, 2025 ) or block-size curriculum (cite45†Bie et al., 2025 ; cite51†Fan et al., 2026 ), or loss reweighting (cite52†Shi and Titsias, 2025 ).
L69: Empirically, PUMA accelerates MDM pretraining by up to $2.5\times$ in iteration–accuracy comparisons at the 125M scale, while introducing only one additional hyperparameter and incurring no forward-pass overhead. We further demonstrate that PUMA is fully compatible with widely adopted approaches that instead modify the architecture or training recipe, such as autoregressive initialization and block-size curricula, offering additional speedups on top of these.
L70: ## 2 Preliminaries
L71: 
L72: We begin by reviewing the training and inference of Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs).
L73: 
L74: Notation. Assume we aim to learn the distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ over sequences of length $L$ with finite vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. Denote by ${\mathbf{x}}^{i}$ the $i$-th element of a given sequence ${\mathbf{x}}=({\mathbf{x}}^{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}^{L})$, and let $\Delta(\mathcal{V})$ be the simplex of probability distributions over $\mathcal{V}$.
L75: MDM training. MDMs (cite38†Lou et al., 2023 ; cite39†Shi et al., 2024 ; cite40†Sahoo et al., 2024 ) introduce an auxiliary mask token $\mathbf{m}$. At a high level, they learn the posterior marginal distributions over $\mathcal{V}$ at each mask token, conditioned on a partially masked sequence.
L76: Concretely, an MDM defines a forward masking process: for a given ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$, draw a time step $t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, then independently replace each token ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}$ by $\mathbf{m}$ with probability $t$.^{1}^{1} 1 We present the masking process using a linear schedule. This results in a partially masked sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\mathbf{m}\})^{L}$.
L77: This resulting joint distribution over $({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})$ induces the coordinate-wise posterior $p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)$, which we refer to as unmasking posterior: the distribution over clean token at masked position $i$ given $({\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)$.
L78: The masking process above has an intriguing property–the unmasking posterior is time-agnostic (cite53†Ou et al., 2024 ; cite54†Zheng et al., 2024 ); it depends only on the observed (partially masked) sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{t}$, not the time step $t$; therefore, we drop the time step in the notation and write $p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})=p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})$, adopting the notation ${\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}$.
L79: To learn this unmasking posterior, MDMs employ a neural network $f_{\theta}$ that takes a sequence ${\mathbf{z}}$ as an input and outputs a tensor of shape $|\mathcal{V}|\times L$. Its $i$-th column $f_{\theta}^{i}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})$ learns the unmasking posterior $f_{\theta}^{i}(v\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})\approx p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=v\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})$. To train $f_{\theta}$, MDMs minimize the following cross-entropy loss summed over all masked indices:
L80:  | $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0.2539,0.4102,0.8828}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})}}\biggl[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\colon{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{m}}-\log f_{\theta}^{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\biggr]\,.$$  |
L81: where the expectation is taken over the joint distribution of $({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})$ defined above. As desired, the unique minimizer $f_{\theta^{\star}}$ of the loss $\mathcal{L}_{\theta}$ satisfies $f_{\theta^{\star}}^{i}(v\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})=p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=v\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})$. This viewpoint connects the MDM training to any-order masked language models such as BERT (cite55†Devlin et al., 2019 ), which also model the posterior marginals at masked positions.
L82: MDM inference. MDM inference starts from a fully masked length-$L$ sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{1}=(\mathbf{m},\dots,\mathbf{m})$ and proceeds over a monotonically decreasing grid of times $t_{0}=1>\dots>t_{K}=0$. At each step $t_{j}$, given a partially masked sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\mathbf{m}\})^{L}$, inference proceeds by two steps to obtain ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j+1}}$:
L83: 
L84:   * (a)
L85: 
L86: Choose a subset of masked positions $\mathcal{S}$ to unmask.
L87: 
L88:   * (b)
L89: $i\in\mathcal{S}$, unmask ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}}^{i}$ with a clean token from $f_{\theta}^{i}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}})\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})$.
L90: The flexibility of choosing $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{i\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}}^{i}=\mathbf{m}\}$ lies at the core of MDM inference; selecting which positions to unmask next can dramatically influence downstream performance both in terms of accuracy, as well as efficiency.
L91: A large body of prior work (cite56†Chang et al., 2022 ; cite57†Zheng et al., 2023 ; cite48†Kim et al., 2025b ; cite42†Nie et al., 2025 ; cite58†Peng et al., 2025 ; cite59†Jazbec et al., 2025 ; cite60†Hong et al., 2025 ; cite61†Ma et al., 2025 ; cite62†Lee et al., 2025 ; cite63†Hayakawa et al., 2025 ; cite64†Mo et al., 2025 ) investigates this question.
L92: Formally, an unmasking policy $g_{\phi}$ – either heuristic or learned – maps a partially masked sequence and time $({\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\mathbf{m}\})^{L},\,t)$ to a (possibly stochastic) subset $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{i:{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{m}\}$ of the masked positions, which is used at each inference step to select a subset to unmask.
L93: A widely used instantiation scores each masked position and then sets $\mathcal{S}$ as the top-K positions: $g_{\phi}({\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)=\mathrm{TopK}_{i:\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{m}}\big[\mathrm{score}(i)\big]$, where $\mathrm{score}(i)$ is a confidence score for position $i$.
L94: Common choices for computing the score include the maximum predicted probability $\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}f_{\theta}(v\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)$ (“Top-K”), the margin between the top two probabilities $f_{\theta}(v_{1}\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)-f_{\theta}(v_{2}\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)$ where $v_{1},v_{2}$ are the top-2 tokens with the highest assigned probability (“Top-K margin”), the negative entropy of the categorical distribution (“entropy-based”), or a position-dependent schedule (e.g., semi-autoregressive inference).
L95: ### 2.1 Prior work on accelerating MDM training
L96: Given the any-order nature of the prediction task, MDM training is inherently more challenging and less compute-efficient than autoregressive training (cite65†Nie et al., 2024 ; cite48†Kim et al., 2025b ), where the model only needs to learn causal next-token prediction. Consequently, prior work has relied on architectural or training-recipe-level strategies applied on top of the core MDM procedure.
L97: At scale, a common practice is to initialize MDMs from pretrained autoregressive models (cite41†Ye et al., 2025 ; cite44†Song et al., 2025 ; cite45†Bie et al., 2025 ; cite51†Fan et al., 2026 ), along with mixture-of-experts variants (cite50†Ni and team, 2025 ).
L98: Block diffusion (cite66†Arriola et al., 2025a ; cite49†Arriola et al., 2025b ) combines block-wise autoregressive generation with MDM-style inference within each block, yielding speedups but essentially trading off full any-order flexibility: at each step, the set of unmasked positions must lie within a block. Notably, these approaches typically leave the underlying masking process unchanged.
L99: In contrast, PUMA operates at the level of the forward masking process; it is thus largely orthogonal to these designs and can be incorporated alongside them, potentially compounding speedups.
L100: ## 3 PUMA: Progressive UnMAsking
L101: 
L102: In this section, we introduce P rogressive U n MA sking (PUMA). In Section cite10†3.1 , we establish the theoretical foundation of PUMA and present how it resolves the MDM’s train-test mismatch stated in the introduction. We then describe our empirical design choices for PUMA in Section cite13†3.2 . In Section cite15†3.3 , we instantiate examples of distributions for which aligning training-time masking with inference-time structure provably improves the statistical efficiency.
L103: ### 3.1 Theoretical foundation of PUMA
L104: 
L105: We begin by formalizing the train–test mismatch in MDMs.
L106: Train-test mismatch of MDM. Recall that MDM inference proceeds by following an unmasking policy $g_{\phi}$ that looks at the currently revealed tokens and decides which masked positions to unmask next. As a consequence, inference does not visit masked sequences uniformly (as done in the training); instead, it induces a policy-dependent distribution over intermediate masked sequences.
L107: For example, under a semi-autoregressive unmasking policy (cite42†Nie et al., 2025 ), inference proceeds block-wise in an autoregressive manner, hence the maskings observed at inference are restricted to block-wise causal patterns.
L108: Meanwhile, the training allocates the compute uniformly across all masking patterns, since each position is masked i.i.d. This suggests that training could be more compute-efficient if we concentrate the training distribution on masking patterns that arise at inference. This raises the question:
L109: 
L110: Is there a masking procedure that resolves this mismatch?
L111: Challenge. The above question is subtle for two reasons. (1) At training time, we start from a clean sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$ and construct a masked example ${\mathbf{x}}_{t}$; at inference, however, intermediate states ${\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ are generated by progressively applying the unmasking policy to partially masked sequences, without access to any clean sequence.
L112: (2) We cannot arbitrarily change the forward process: modifying the joint distribution over $({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})$ can inadvertently change what MDM training aims to learn. Concretely, unless done carefully, changing ${\color[rgb]{0.2539,0.4102,0.8828}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})}$ can shift the minimizer of the loss
L113:  | $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0.2539,0.4102,0.8828}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})}}\biggl[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\colon{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{m}}-\log f_{\theta}^{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\biggr]\,.$$  |  | (1)
L114: 
L115: PUMA. We now present PUMA and explain how it resolves these challenges. The crux of PUMA is the so-called teacher-forced chain, which we use to generate training-time samples.
L116: At a high level, the teacher-forced chain is a progressive unmasking process that follows the same unmasking policy $g_{\phi}$ used at inference time, but instead of sampling clean tokens from a posterior, it reveals the corresponding ground-truth tokens from the data sample ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$. Surprisingly, this simple chain lets us align the distribution over masking patterns at training-time with the one at inference time.
L117: Figure 2: Illustration of PUMA. Under PUMA, training examples are generated via the teacher-forced chain with a given ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$ (purple) with a current model’s policy $g_{\phi}$ and ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$’s clean tokens. In contrast, standard MDM training yields (independently drawn) training samples by the random masking.
L118: Teacher-forced chain. Fix a clean sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$, an unmasking policy $g_{\phi}$, and an integer $K\geq 1$. Define a time grid $t_{j}:=1-j/K$ for $j=0,1,\dots,K$, so that $t_{0}=1$ and $t_{K}=0$. We initialize the chain at the fully masked state ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{0}}={\mathbf{x}}_{1}=(\mathbf{m},\dots,\mathbf{m})$.
L119: To clarify, in contrast to the inference (Section cite7†2 ), we do not draw a clean token from $f_{\theta}$; whenever positions are selected, we reveal ground-truth tokens from ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$. Concretely, given a partially masked state ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}}$, obtain ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j+1}}$ by:
L120:   * (a)
L121: 
L122: Choose a subset of masked positions $\mathcal{S}=g_{\phi}({\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}},t_{j})$.
L123: 
L124:   * (b)
L125: 
L126: For each $i\in\mathcal{S}$, unmask ${\mathbf{x}}_{t_{j}}^{i}=\mathbf{m}$ into clean token ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}$.
L127: The chain above is defined conditional on a particular ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$, i.e., it specifies a conditional distribution over trajectories $({\mathbf{x}}_{t_{0}},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{K}})$ given ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$. At training time, we first sample ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$ and then run the chain (conditioned on ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$).
L128: Therefore, each chain run yields multiple intermediate masked sequences, each of which serves as a training example paired with the ground-truth sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$. In contrast, standard MDM training generates a single training example with random masking. We illustrate this in Figure cite67†2 . This teacher-forced chain can be efficiently implemented in practice without any computational overhead, and we will revisit it in Section cite13†3.2 .
L129: We now informally state the theoretical guarantee of PUMA: marginal agreement and minimizer preservation.
L130: 
L131: Together, these two properties resolve the challenges identified earlier: PUMA aligns training with the inference-time distribution induced by an unmasking policy, while ensuring that modifying the forward process does not change what the model is trained to learn. As a result, PUMA enables more compute-efficient MDM training without affecting the minimizer of the underlying objective.
L132: Current model as an unmasking policy. An important practical consideration in operating the teacher-forced chain during pretraining is that the final unmasking policy, namely the one used at inference, is not available a priori: it is induced by the final pretrained network (see Section cite7†2 for the policy instances). Accordingly, we construct the teacher-forced chain using the unmasking policy defined by the current model at that point in the training process.
L133: Intuitively, as training proceeds and the model’s prediction gets better, the induced policy $g_{\phi}$ rapidly approaches the inference-time policy of the final model. In particular, the ranking of which positions to unmask might tend to stabilize earlier than full posterior calibration, so the policy becomes approximately final relatively early in training. We indeed validate this empirically in Section cite17†4.1 , and return to implementation details in Section cite13†3.2 .
L134: #### 3.1.1 PUMA’s marginal agreement property
L135: 
L136: In this section, we formalize the marginal agreement property of PUMA. The proof is deferred to Appendix cite25†A .
L137: Intuition. The unmasking posterior $p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=\cdot\mid{\mathbf{z}})$ used in the MDM inference step (b) is the data conditional distribution given ${\mathbf{z}}$.
L138: Therefore, “revealing a token from the true posterior” (idealized MDM inference) is distributionally equivalent to “first draw ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$, then reveal its tokens, and later marginalize over ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$” (PUMA): both generate the same joint law over revealed tokens and remaining masks. Since $g_{\phi}$ only reacts to what is currently revealed (and time), both procedures induce the same distribution over intermediate masked contexts at each $t$.
L139: ###### Proposition 1 (Informal).
L140: 
L141: Fix a policy $g_{\phi}$ and consider an idealized MDM inference procedure that, at step (b), samples clean tokens from the ground-truth unmasking posterior $p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{i}=\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{z}})$. Let $q_{t}$ denote the distribution of ${\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ under this idealized inference. Then, for every $t$, the marginal distribution of ${\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ induced by the teacher-forced chain with ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$ coincides with $q_{t}$.
L142: Remark. The precise way to present Proposition cite68†1 is under the idealized policy, where we only choose one position to unmask at each time step. This is because the token sampling step at inference is always done with $f_{\theta}$’s per-position posterior; the distributional dependency across positions is ignored.
L143: A more nuanced way to understand the teacher-forced chain is through continuous-time Markov chains (cite69†Campbell et al., 2022 ; cite70†Gat et al., 2024 ; cite71†Kim et al., 2025a ), under which the marginal agreement holds for all $t\in[0,1]$. We formalize this argument in Appendix cite26†A.1 .

L164: #### 3.1.2 PUMA’s minimizer guarantee
L165: 
L166: In this section, we demonstrate that PUMA does not alter the minimizer of the training loss despite intervening in the distribution $({\mathbf{x}}_{0},t,{\mathbf{x}}_{t})$.
L167: 
L168: Intuition. We first investigate how the design of the forward process can influence the minimizer of the training loss. Consider the i.i.d. forward process, whose conditional distribution takes the following form:
L169:  |  | $\displaystyle p({\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{z}}\,|\,{\mathbf{x}}_{0},t)$  |

L171: where $\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}}):=\{i\,|\,{\mathbf{z}}^{i}\neq\mathbf{m}\}$ is the set of unmasked tokens ${\mathbf{z}}$ and $\mathbf{1}\{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})}={\mathbf{z}}^{\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})}\}$ is the indicator function that identifies whether ${\mathbf{z}}$ matches ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$ on $\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})$. Now we observe how the first term (green factor) cancels out in the Bayes-optimal predictor, which is also the cross-entropy training loss’s minimizer.
L172: By Bayes’ rule,
L173:  |  | $\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.2539,0.4102,0.8828}\underline{p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{z}},t)}}\propto p({\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{z}}\mid{\mathbf{x}}_{0},t)\,p_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})$  |
L174:  |  | $\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0.625,0.5273}\underline{(1-t)^{|\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})|}\,t^{L-|\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})|}}}\cdot\mathbf{1}\big\{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})}={\mathbf{z}}^{\mathbf{um}({\mathbf{z}})}\big\}\,p_{\mathrm{data}}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})\,,$  |
