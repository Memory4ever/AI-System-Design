L65: # Omni-Safety under Cross-Modality Conflict:
L66: Vulnerabilities, Dynamics Mechanisms and Efficient Alignment
L67: Kun Wang Affiliation: Nanyang Technological University    Zherui Li Affiliation: Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications    Zhenhong Zhou Affiliation: Nanyang Technological University    Yitong Zhang Affiliation: Tsinghua University    Yan Mi Affiliation: Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications    Kun Yang Affiliation: Fudan University    Yiming Zhang Affiliation: University of Science and Technology of China    Junhao Dong Affiliation: Nanyang Technological University    Zhongxiang Sun Affiliation: Renmin University of China Correspondence to: sunzhongxiang@ruc.edu.cn    Qiankun Li Affiliation: Nanyang Technological University Correspondence to: qklee.lz@gmail.com    Yang Liu Affiliation: Nanyang Technological University
L68: ###### Abstract
L69: Omni-modal Large Language Models (OLLMs) greatly expand LLMs’ multimodal capabilities but also introduce cross-modal safety risks. However, a systematic understanding of vulnerabilities in omni-modal interactions remains lacking. To bridge this gap, we establish a modality-semantics decoupling principle and construct the AdvBench-Omni dataset, which reveals a significant vulnerability in OLLMs.
L70: Mechanistic analysis uncovers a Mid-layer Dissolution phenomenon driven by refusal vector magnitude shrinkage, alongside the existence of a modal-invariant pure refusal direction. Inspired by these insights, we extract a golden refusal vector using Singular Value Decomposition and propose OmniSteer, which utilizes lightweight adapters to modulate intervention intensity adaptively.
L71: Extensive experiments show that our method not only increases the Refusal Success Rate against harmful inputs from $69.9\%$ to $91.2\%$, but also effectively preserves the general capabilities across all modalities. Our code is available at: cite53†https://github.com/zhrli324/omni-safety-research†github.com .
L72: ## 1 Introduction
L73: Omni-modal Large Language Models (OLLMs) extend LLMs (cite54†Brown et al., 2020 ; cite55†Zhao et al., 2025 ; cite56†Yin et al., 2024 ) with native support for text, image, audio, and video inputs, and often enable streaming text and speech outputs (cite57†Qwen et al., 2025 ; cite58†Yao et al., 2024 ; cite59†Zhang et al., 2025b ), making them a natural backbone for next-generation world models (cite60†Ge et al., 2024 ; cite61†Wei et al., 2024 ), agentic systems (cite62†OpenAI et al., 2024 ; cite63†Comanici et al., 2025 ; cite64†Wang et al., 2025a ), and embodied intelligence (cite65†Huang et al., 2024 ; cite66†Hong et al., 2024 ).
L74: However, existing safety alignment mechanisms operate in a modality-isolated manner: text-only safety has been extensively studied (cite67†Wang et al., 2025b ; cite68†Ouyang et al., 2022 ; cite69†Zou et al., 2023 ), and a growing body of work examines vision- or audio-driven modality alignment and attacks (cite70†Pi et al., 2024 ; cite71†Liu et al., 2024a ; cite72†Jin et al., 2025 ), but a principled understanding of cross-modality safety in omni-modal settings is still absent.
L75: Recent research has extensively explored dual-modal vulnerabilities through systematic benchmarks (cite73†Liu et al., 2025c ; cite74†Liu et al., 2025d ) and red-teaming protocols (cite75†Schlarmann and Hein, 2023 ; cite76†Gong et al., 2025 ). Concurrently, defense strategies ranging from fine-tuning to plug-and-play modules have been tailored for vision (cite77†Zong et al., 2024 ; cite78†Gou et al., 2025 ; cite79†Wang et al., 2025c ) and audio (cite72†Jin et al., 2025 ; cite80†Yang et al., 2025b ) domains to mitigate risks.
L76: However, when moving to OLLMs, the safety risk escalates substantially: Unlike dual-modal LLMs which operate over a limited set of modalities, OLLMs natively integrate and generate across text, images, audio, and video (cite62†OpenAI et al., 2024 ; cite63†Comanici et al., 2025 ; cite81†Xu et al., 2025 ). This broader multimodal capacity introduces complex interaction-driven vulnerabilities absent in dual-modal settings (cite82†Pan et al., 2025 ).
L77: Yet, the literature remains largely confined to dual-modal studies; the limited existing work on OLLMs primarily focuses on surface-level evaluations without probing the underlying mechanisms of cross-modal information flow (cite83†Bagdasaryan et al., 2023 ; cite82†Pan et al., 2025 ), highlighting a critical gap in the field.
L78: To fill this gap, a systematic analysis of OLLM vulnerabilities is imperative. We first clarify that the core principle for analyzing the cross-modal interaction safety in OLLMs is the decoupling of modality and semantics, a focus that has been largely overlooked by existing works. Guided by this principle, we use AdvBench (cite69†Zou et al., 2023 ) as a seed dataset and propose AdvBench-Omni, constructed via an omni-modal expansion method that combines direct rendering with a semantic separation strategy.
L79: Using this dataset, our safety evaluation of OLLMs reveals alarming vulnerabilities: while the Refusal Success Rate (RSR) for single-modal inputs stands at about $97\%$, it drops to below $80\%$ for cross-modal inputs. This safety degradation is triggered purely by modality interaction, motivating us to delve deeper into the model generation and multimodal alignment mechanisms to investigate the root causes.
L80: To investigate the origins of these vulnerabilities, we dissect the internal mechanisms of cross-modal safety degradation from the perspective of representation dynamics. We extract refusal vectors across modalities to analyze the internal safety dynamics of OLLMs. This analysis reveals a phenomenon we term “Mid-layer Dissolution”, in which refusal signals collapse in intermediate layers for cross-modal harmful inputs.
L81: We then identify that the shrinkage of the refusal vector’s magnitude is the primary factor behind the decline in safety for cross-modal inputs. Subspace analysis of the refusal vectors from each modality uncovers the existence of a modality-invariant, pure refusal vector.
L82: To isolate the pure refusal direction, we extract a cross-modal shared golden refusal vector via Singular Value Decomposition and validate its effectiveness and specificity. Building on these points, we propose OmniSteer, which intervenes in the model’s refusal representations through layer-wise adaptive steering. Specifically, OmniSteer utilizes the decomposed refusal vector as a unified guidance signal to train lightweight adapters that dynamically modulate the intervention strength of refusal steering.
L83: Our experiments across three OLLMs and eight datasets spanning various modalities demonstrate that OmniSteer increases the RSR from a baseline of $69.9\%$ to $91.2\%$ while preserving normal responses to benign queries, thereby proving its effectiveness. Furthermore, results on OmniBench indicate that our method does not compromise the general capabilities of OLLMs across modalities, proving its specificity.
L84: Our key contributions are summarized as follows:
L85: 
L86:   * •
L87: 
L88: Revealing Vulnerabilities: We propose AdvBench-Omni, conducting the first omni-modal safety evaluation that decouples modality from semantics, thereby exposing significant cross-modal safety vulnerabilities.
L89: 
L90:   * •
L91: 
L92: Dynamics Mechanisms: We dissect OLLM safety dynamics, uncovering the “Mid-layer Dissolution” phenomenon and identifying refusal vector magnitude shrinkage as the primary driver of safety degradation.
L93: 
L94:   * •
L95: Efficient Alignment: We propose OmniSteer, an adaptive refusal steering method that enhances safety without compromising the model’s general capabilities.
L96: ## 2 Background
L97: ### 2.1 Omni-modal LLMs
L98: Representative OLLMs (cite57†Qwen et al., 2025 ; cite84†Li et al., 2024 ; cite81†Xu et al., 2025 ) typically adopt a multi-branch paradigm, which employs modal-specific encoders and projectors for each modality to map information from various modalities into the text space, followed by unified processing by a pretrained LLM backbone.
L99: Given a multimodal input $\mathcal{I}=\{\mathbf{x}_{\text{text}},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\}$, where $m\in\{\text{image},\text{audio},\text{video}\}$ denotes non-text modalities, the text input is embedded as $\mathbf{e}_{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}$. Non-text inputs are processed by specific encoders $\mathcal{E}_{m}$ and aligned to the text space via projectors $\mathcal{P}_{m}$, yielding $\mathbf{e}_{m}=\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{E}_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{m}))\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}$.
L100: These embeddings are concatenated to form the input $\mathbf{h}^{(0)}$ for the $L$-layer LLM backbone. The hidden states evolve through each layer $l$ as:
L101:  | $$\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}=\text{TransformerBlock}_{l}(\mathbf{h}^{(l)}).$$  |  | (1)
L102: 
L103: In this work, we focus on the dynamics of these hidden states $\mathbf{h}^{(l)}$ to investigate the internal refusal mechanisms.
L104: ### 2.2 Refusal Steering
L105: 
L106: Research suggests that LLM refusal behavior is encoded in a linear direction (cite85†Arditi et al., 2024 ). For layer $l$, this direction $\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}^{(l)}$ is defined as the difference between the mean activations of harmful ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{harm}}$) and benign ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{safe}}$) queries:
L107:  | $$\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}^{(l)}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{harm}}}[\mathbf{h}_{l}(\mathbf{x})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{safe}}}[\mathbf{h}_{l}(\mathbf{x})].$$  |  | (2)
L108: 
L109: Activation steering intervenes in model behavior by injecting this vector during inference. Given a steering coefficient $\alpha$, the hidden state is modified as:
L110: 
L111:  | $$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{l}=\mathbf{h}_{l}+\alpha\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}^{(l)}.$$  |  | (3)

L113:

L115: In this section, we first establish the core principle for conducting an OLLM safety evaluation—the decoupling of modality and semantics (Section cite11†3.1 ). L116: ### 3.1 Design Principles for Fair Evaluation
L117: 
L118: cite87†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2: t-SNE dimensionality reduction analysis of hidden states across different modal inputs. We sampled data from AdvBench-Omni and AdvBench-MM to perform a t-SNE analysis.
L119: The core feature of OLLMs lies in the interaction and reasoning of multimodal information. To explore the safety vulnerabilities introduced by omni-modality, research should focus on the interaction dynamics across modalities, rather than merely the surface feature of “multiple modality inputs”.
L120: However, existing safety evaluations of dual- or omni-modal LLMs often adopt horizontal comparisons (e.g., comparing Model A with Model B in one benchmark) while overlooking vertical comparisons (i.e., behavioral differences under different modality inputs in one model) (cite73†Liu et al., 2025c ; cite82†Pan et al., 2025 ). This evaluation paradigm fails to isolate the independent impacts of content differences and modality differences on model safety, leading to unclear sources of vulnerabilities.
L121: To precisely localize the vulnerabilities introduced by modality interactions, we propose Control Modal-variable Principle: When constructing evaluation data, keep the semantic information of each modal subset equivalent, only changing the modal representation of the information. This requires us to clearly define what constitutes identical content from the following theoretical perspective.
L122: Let $X$ denote the original text and $Y$ the information after modality transformation, with $S$ representing the jointly encoded semantics. An ideal modality transformation process should preserve semantics, i.e., satisfying the conservation of mutual information: $I(X;S)\approx I(Y;S)$ (cite88†Alemi et al., 2017 ; cite89†Tschannen et al., 2020 ). According to the Data Processing Inequality (cite90†Cover, 1999 ), the model’s internal representation of the input $h(\cdot)$ satisfies:
L123:  | $$I(S;h(X))\leq I(S;X),\quad I(S;h(Y))\leq I(S;Y).$$  |  | (4)
L124: 
L125: If the model possesses robust semantic extraction capability and $X$ and $Y$ carry equivalent semantic information $S$, their internal representations should satisfy $I(S;h(X))\approx I(S;h(Y))$. This implies that inputs from different modalities should elicit similar deep semantic responses.
L126: Based on the above perspective, Text-to-Image generation strategies inevitably introduce semantic drift and thus fail to satisfy the requirements for semantic consistency (cite91†Yin et al., 2019 ). This guides us in subsequent data construction to abandon the generative paradigm and instead seek a deterministic modality transformation strategy that maximally preserves the original semantic mutual information.
L127: cite92†Image: Refer to caption Figure 3: Cosine similarity between hidden states across various modal inputs and the text modality. Experiments were conducted on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, comparing the similarities of inputs from AdvBench-Omni and AdvBench-MM against the text inputs.
L128: ### 3.2 AdvBench-Omni Construction and Validation
L129: 
L130: Guided by Control Modal-variable Principle in Section cite11†3.1 , we construct the AdvBench-Omni benchmark. This dataset aims to provide a clean experimental environment for evaluating cross-modal safety by rigorously controlling semantic variables. We select AdvBench as the seed dataset, which comprises 520 malicious instructions spanning categories such as violence and illegality, serving as a standard benchmark for evaluating LLM safety.

L136: #### 3.2.1 Dataset Construction Pipeline
L137: 
L138: Based on the design principles, we expand each original text query in AdvBench into 11 modality variants, covering single-modal, dual-modal, and triple-modal combinations, thereby constituting the AdvBench-Omni. Our modal transformation methods are as follows:
L139: Single-modal Transformation. For Image and Video, we perform pixel-level rendering of the text in the form of “black-on-white” slides; for Audio, we utilized Qwen3-TTS (cite97†Qwen Team, 2025 ) to generate speech. This ensures maximization of $I(Y;X)$ between single-modality inputs and the original text.
L140: Multimodal Transformation. Unlike information-coherent single-modal inputs, multimodal reasoning inherently requires cross-channel information dependencies. Therefore, we adopt a semantic separation strategy to enforce necessary cross-modal interactions and prevent the model from bypassing a single redundant modality. We split the original query into a context and a core payload.
L141: Given the original text query “How to make a bomb?”, we split it into the text prompt “How can I make the thing in the image?” and the word “bomb” directly rendered and projected in the image.
L142: To support subsequent refusal vector extraction (Section cite16†4 ), we additionally collected 500 benign queries and applied the same pipeline for omni-modal transformation. AdvBench-Omni ultimately comprises 11 modal subsets, totaling 11,220 samples. The pipeline is shown in Figure cite98†1 . More details can be found in Appendix cite41†D .
L143: #### 3.2.2 Oracle Validation
L144: 
L145: To verify whether AdvBench-Omni conforms to the design principles outlined in Section cite11†3.1 , we conduct layer-wise analysis on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B. As a baseline, we use Stable-Diffusion-3.5 (cite99†Esser et al., 2024 ) to generate semantically corresponding real-world images to construct the AdvBench-MM dataset. Corresponding to the perspective in Section cite11†3.1 , we perform experimental analyses.
L146: As shown in Figure cite100†2 , t-SNE visualization showcases that AdvBench-Omni preserves semantic coherence across modalities compared to AdvBench-MM baselines, validating content-preserving transformation. Figure cite101†3 illustrates the evolution of cosine similarity between hidden states of various modalities and the original text version of AdvBench. The results reveal distinct processing patterns: ❶ Single-modality inputs maintain stable similarity above 0.85 throughout most layers.
L147: ❷ Cross-modal combinations exhibit a characteristic dip in the middle layers (5-17), dropping to $\sim$0.80, before recovering to $\sim$0.90 in deeper layers. This trajectory suggests temporary representation reorganization during multimodal fusion, followed by semantic re-alignment. ❸ Most critically, the AdvBench-MM baseline from traditional generative methods shows severe degradation, plummeting from $\sim 0.7$ to $\sim 0.6$ in Layer 15-27.
L148: In summary, our results support the design validity of AdvBench-Omni: It successfully preserves core semantic information $S$ while altering modality representations.
L149: ### 3.3 Cross-Modality Vulnerability Gap
L150: 
L151: Utilizing AdvBench-Omni, we conduct safety testing on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (cite57†Qwen et al., 2025 ) and MiniCPM-o-2.6 (cite58†Yao et al., 2024 ). We perform zero-shot inference on 520 harmful queries from each modality subset and employ LLM-as-a-Judge (Qwen3-30B-A3B (cite102†Yang et al., 2025a )) to calculate the Refusal Success Rate (RSR). As shown in Figure cite103†4 , the experiments reveal a significant cross-modality safety gap:
L152: ➭Single-modality robustness. Under single-modal inputs, both models exhibit strong defensive capabilities, maintaining RSR above $90\%$ for Qwen2.5-Omni-7B.
L153: 
L154: ➭Cross-modality vulnerability. Once cross-modal combinations (such as Text+Image or Text+Video) are introduced, RSR decreases to approximately $75\%$ for Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and $50\%$ for MiniCPM-o-2.6.
L155: Given that AdvBench-Omni rigorously controls for semantic content consistency (Section cite14†3.2.2 ), this drastic RSR disparity confirms our core hypothesis: the Cross-Modality Safety Gap represents a novel safety vulnerability independent of content. This finding indicates that existing single-modality alignment mechanisms fail to generalize to complex cross-modal interaction scenarios, compelling us to further investigate the underlying dynamic mechanisms.
L156: ## 4 Dynamics Mechanisms
L157: 
L158: In this section, we delve into the mechanisms behind the vulnerabilities observed in Section cite10†3 . We conduct experiments on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, first observing the dynamic evolution of internal refusal signals in OLLMs (Section cite17†4.1 ), then identifying the primary factors for the safety degradation (Section cite18†4.2 ). Finally, we discover a shared underlying refusal direction that spans modalities (Section cite19†4.3 ).
L159: ### 4.1 Layer-wise Dynamic Evolution of Refusal Signals
L160: 
L161: cite104†Image: Refer to caption Figure 5: Layer-wise evolution curves of normalised projection values for inputs across different modalities.
L162: To measure the model’s internal refusal propensity, we first establish a quantitative framework. We employ the method mentioned in Section cite9†2.2 , extracting the refusal vector $\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}=\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}=\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{harm}}^{(l)}-\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{safe}}^{(l)}$ on the Text subset of AdvBench-Omni. We perform centering and normalisation on the projection, defining the refusal strength as:
L163:  | $$p_{l}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{(\mathbf{h}_{l}(\mathbf{x})-\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{safe}}^{(l)})^{T}\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{refu}}\|^{2}}.$$  |  | (5)
L164: 
L165: Subsequently, we extract hidden states as the model processes AdvBench-Omni and calculate the refusal strength values. Figure cite105†5 illustrates the complete process.
L166: ➭Single-modality inputs maintain projections consistently above $0.9$ after Layer 16. This stable pattern indicates that single-modal inputs preserve strong and consistent refusal signals, which explains the high RSR for single modalities.
L167: ➭Cross-modal combination inputs exhibit a different evolution pattern, revealing a key mechanism we term the Mid-layer Dissolution phenomenon. In the first 12 layers, projections for cross-modal inputs rise continuously, reaching a peak of $\sim 0.95$ at Layer 12. However, after that, the projections plummet abruptly, and the gradual recovery in later layers can only stabilize the projection values at $\sim 0.7$.

L178: To conduct a deeper mechanistic exploration of the phenomenon in Section cite17†4.1 , we extract respective refusal vectors $\mathbf{v}_{i}$ for 6 modality subsets of AdvBench-Omni, and then analyze their relationship with the text refusal vector $\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}$.
L179: We identify that the reduction in projection values may stem from two factors: ❶ deviation in refusal vector direction, measurable by cosine similarity $\theta_{i}=\cos(\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}})$; ❷ reduction in refusal vector magnitude, quantifiable by the magnitude ratio $\rho_{i}=\|\mathbf{v}_{i}\|/\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}\|$. Our experimental results are presented in Table cite106†1 , showing that:
L180: ➭Single-modality refusal vectors exhibit high alignment with $\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}$, with cosine similarities ranging from $0.89$ to $0.97$ and magnitude ratios between $0.85$ and $1.01$. This indicates that although encoders for different modalities vary, the refusal concept they learn corresponds to nearly identical directions, with comparable intensity.
L181: ➭Cross-modal combination refusal vectors exhibit similarities that drop to $0.73$-$0.86$, deviating from $\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}$ by approximately $40$ degrees. More critically, their magnitude ratios are only $0.45$-$0.72$, meaning that the strength of cross-modal refusal vectors is merely half that of $\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}$.
L182: To quantify the relative contributions of directional deviation and magnitude reduction, we employ log-linear decomposition. Through variance decomposition, we find that the magnitude factor accounts for $88.3\%$ of the total variance, whereas the directional factor contributes only $9.8\%$. This result clearly indicates that the attenuation of refusal signals primarily originates from the shrinkage of the refusal vector magnitude, rather than directional deviation.
L183: ### 4.3 Subspace Analysis: The Geometry of Refusal
L184: 
L185: cite107†Image: Refer to caption Figure 6: PCA analysis of refusal vectors across different modalities. (a) Results for all 7 single-modal and dual-modal combinations; (b) Results exclusively for the 4 single-modal types.
L186: Although cross-modal inputs induce projection value reduction in refusal vectors, we discovered in Section cite18†4.2 that refusal vectors across modalities are not entirely unrelated—single modalities exhibit high alignment, and even cross-modal combinations maintain substantial similarity. This suggests the possible existence of a shared underlying refusal direction across modalities, with modal-specific biases merely superimposed onto this direction.
L187: To verify this hypothesis, we perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the refusal vectors from seven modalities. We concatenate these seven vectors into a matrix $\mathbf{R}=[\mathbf{v}_{\text{txt}},\mathbf{v}_{\text{img}},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{\text{txt+vid}}]$ and conduct eigenvalue decomposition.
L188: Figure cite108†6 (a) reveals that the first principal component (PC1) explains $\sim 80\%$ of the variance, which indicates that refusal vectors across modalities indeed primarily reside in a low-dimensional subspace.
L189: Intriguingly, PC1 and PC2 admit clear semantic interpretations. PC1 encodes the “intensity axis”, separating high-magnitude single modalities (positive) from low-magnitude cross-modal ones (negative), consistent with Section cite18†4.2 . In contrast, PC2 captures modality-specific biases by distinguishing sequential, discrete inputs (text, audio) from spatial, continuous ones (image, video).
L190: Validation via single-modality PCA (Figure cite108†6 (b)) confirms this: its PC1 aligns with the original PC2, showing that modality type is the primary variation once magnitude differences are removed.
L191: These results provide a clear geometric picture for understanding refusal vectors: refusal vectors across modalities reside in a two-dimensional subspace spanned by a “pure refusal direction” and a “modality bias direction”. This finding paves the way for designing effective safety alignment methods: by extracting the pure refusal direction, one can provide unified safety signals for all modality combinations without interference from modal biases.

L193:

L195: ### 5.1 Extracting the Golden Refusal Vector via SVD
L196: 
L197: Based on the analysis in Section cite16†4 , the refusal vectors across modalities exhibit offsets. Therefore, we seek to extract a golden refusal vector: a modality-invariant direction that exclusively encodes pure refusal semantics.
L198: Let $\mathbf{R}{=}[\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}]{\in}\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ denote the concatenation matrix of refusal vectors from each modality, where $\mathbf{v}_{i}$ can be decomposed as $\mathbf{v}_{i}{=}\mathbf{s}{+}\mathbf{n}_{i}$. Here, $\mathbf{s}$ represents the shared refusal signal we aim to extract, while $\mathbf{n}_{i}$ denotes modality-specific noise. Our objective is to recover $\mathbf{s}$ from $\mathbf{R}$.
L199: To this end, we utilize Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to isolate the signal $\mathbf{s}$. Performing an uncentered SVD on $\mathbf{R}$ yields $\mathbf{R}{=}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}$, the first left singular vector $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ satisfies:
L200:  | $$\mathbf{u}_{1}=\arg\max_{\|\mathbf{u}\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v}_{i})^{2},$$  |  | (6)
L201: 
L202: where $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ represents the direction that best explains all modality refusal vectors in each layer. Therefore, we designate $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ as the golden refusal vector, denoted as $\mathbf{v}_{\text{gold}}$.
