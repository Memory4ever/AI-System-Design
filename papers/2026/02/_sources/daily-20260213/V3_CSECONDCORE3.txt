L72:  | $$\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{T}\alpha_{im}\mathbf{v}_{m}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{T}\left[\underbrace{\alpha_{im}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{m}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}}_{\text{{Value Path}}}+\underbrace{\mathbf{v}_{m}\frac{\partial\alpha_{im}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}}_{\text{{Weight Path}}}\right]$$  |  | (1)
L73: 
L74: The Jacobian of the Value Path is:
L75:  | $$\sum_{m=1}^{T}\alpha_{im}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{m}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}=\alpha_{ij}W^{V}$$  |  | (2)
L76: 
L77: The Jacobian of the Weight Path consists of the softmax and the score gradient:
L78: 
L79:  | $$\frac{\partial\alpha_{im}}{\partial e_{ik}}=\alpha_{im}(\delta_{mk}-\alpha_{ik})\qquad\frac{\partial e_{ik}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\left(\delta_{ij}\mathbf{k}_{k}^{\!\top}W^{Q}+\delta_{kj}\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\!\top}W^{K}\right)$$  |  | (3)
L80: 
L81: The overall Jacobian without residual is thus:
L82:  | $$\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}=\underbrace{\alpha_{ij}W^{V}}_{\text{{Value Path}}}+\sum_{m=1}^{T}\left[\mathbf{v}_{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{T}\underbrace{\alpha_{im}(\delta_{mk}-\alpha_{ik})}_{\text{{Softmax Gradient}}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\left(\underbrace{\delta_{ij}\mathbf{k}_{k}^{T}W^{Q}}_{\text{{Query Path}}}+\underbrace{\delta_{kj}\mathbf{q}_{i}^{T}W^{K}}_{\text{{Key Path}}}\right)}_{\text{{Score Gradient}}}\right)\right]$$  |  | (4)
L83: We derive a uniform expectation of the Jacobian norm over the sequence length $j\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$ for the Value Path, Key Path, and Query Path separately:
L84:  |  | $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|J_{ij}^{(\mathrm{value})}\|\big]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\alpha_{ij}\,\|W^{V}\|=\frac{\|W^{V}\|}{T}$  |  | (5)
L85:  |  | $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|J_{ij}^{(\text{key})}\big\|\right]\;=\;\frac{1}{T\sqrt{d_{k}}}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\alpha_{ij}\left(\|\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{h}_{i}\|\;\cdot\;\|(W^{K})^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_{i}\|\right)\;\leq\;\frac{C_{K}}{T\sqrt{d_{k}}}$  |  | (6)
L86:  |  | $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big\|J_{ij}^{(\text{query})}\big\|\right]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{T\sqrt{d_{k}}}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\alpha_{ik}\left(\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{h}_{i}\|\cdot\|(W^{Q})^{\!\top}\mathbf{k}_{k}\|\right)\;\leq\;\frac{C_{Q}}{T\sqrt{d_{k}}}$  |  | (7)
L87: with $C_{K}:=\max_{j}\|\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{h}_{i}\|\;\|(W^{K})^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_{i}\|,\quad C_{Q}:=\max_{k}\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{h}_{i}\|\;\|(W^{Q})^{\!\top}\mathbf{k}_{k}\|$. We distinguish the diagonal case ($i=j$), corresponding to local self-updates, and the off-diagonal case ($i\neq j$), corresponding to non-local interactions. The query vector $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ is defined as $\mathbf{q}_{i}=W^{Q}\mathbf{x}_{i}$.
L88: Thus, the derivative of $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ with respect to $\mathbf{x}_{j}$ is zero if $i\neq j$, because $\mathbf{q}_{i}$ is independent of $\mathbf{x}_{j}$. We define bounds on the expected value of the Jacobian of the attention layer with a residual connection, using the expected norm for off-diagonal terms ($i\neq j$) and the norm for the diagonal term ($i=j$).^{1}^{1} 1 The corresponding code is published here: https://github.com/vicky-hnk/spatio-temp-parroting.
L89:  | $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\delta_{ij}I+J_{ij}^{(\mathrm{total})}\|\right]\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{T}\!\left(\|W^{V}\|+\dfrac{C_{K}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right),&i\neq j,\\[2.58334pt]
L90: \|I\|+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\alpha_{ii}\right]\!\left(\|W^{V}\|+\dfrac{C_{K}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}+\dfrac{C_{Q}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right),&i=j.\end{cases}$$  |  | (8)
L91: ### 3.2 Analysis and Regularization of Diagonal Attention Sinks
L92: The $1/T$-term in the upper part of Eq. cite35†8 shows that the non-local signal strength ($i\neq j$) decays as the sequences are longer ($O(1/T)$). The lower part of Eq. cite35†8 for the diagonal ($i=j$) has a larger bound since it additionally has the query term’s bound $C_{Q}/\sqrt{d_{k}}$. With a residual path, self-information remains stable regardless of sequence length ($O(1)$). $\alpha_{ii}$ often exceeds $\alpha_{ij}$ as positional encoding (PE) favors information near the diagonal.
L93: While emphasizing the diagonal makes sense given the importance of nearby time steps, the extreme case of self-copying does not. To reduce the diagonal attention sink, we evaluated three different regularization methods: a diagonal mask with diagonal entries $-\infty$ and others zero, similar to the approach in SparseBert [cite34†12 ], standard dropout on the diagonal of the attention matrix, and a negative scalar penalty added to the raw attention scores.
L94: ## 4 Empirical Evaluation
L95: We evaluate our model on the METR-LA traffic dataset, as described in [cite36†13 ]. The forecasting horizon is 12 steps, using the previous 12 steps as input. Optimization uses AdamW with cosine scheduling, 5 warm-up epochs, 150 epochs in total, and an initial learning rate $0.001$. We stack a temporal softmax attention block followed by a Graph Convolution Network with learned forward and backward adjacency matrix (T&S). The number of TA heads is $8$.
L96: We reduce the spatial influence by using a single-layer GCN with about four times fewer parameters than the TA module. The optimal diagonal penalty and dropout were $-0.1$ and $0.2$, respectively. Since relative PE introduces another bias towards the diagonal, we used absolute PE (tAPE from [cite37†14 ]).
L97: ### 4.1 Results
L98: Table cite38†1 shows the results over four runs with different seeds for a) without residual and without regularization, b) with residual and without regularization, c) with residual and with diagonal mask, d) with residual and with diagonal dropout, and e) with residual and diagonal penalty. The values are the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) for the forecasted steps $3$, $6$ and $12$.
L99: The baseline model without residuals shows substantially higher error than models with residual connections. Using a diagonal mask (c) gives similar results as the variant with residual but without regularization, while dropout and penalty show a significant improvement of around $2.5$ percent. Fig. cite39†1 shows the heat maps of final attention matrices after the last epoch. In (a) we see that the attention scores on the diagonal are very large and get smaller with distance if no residual term is used.
L100: Fig. cite39†1 (b) shows the attention matrix with residual but without regularization. It is diffuse without clear temporal patterns, as the residual already provides a stable identity mapping. By enforcing regularization on the diagonal elements of the weight matrix, we structurally incentivize the model to allocate learning capacity towards the off-diagonal entries. However, our experiments suggest that the partial control of the diagonal using dropout or penalty is superior to a full diagonal mask.
L101: With a full diagonal mask (c) we see similar results as without regularization (b). By masking the diagonal, we suppress the Query Path, making the attention less expressive (see Eq. cite35†8 ). In (d) and (e) we see clear temporal patterns with specific keys attending to specific queries.

L123: ## 5 Conclusion
L124: Recent work on over-squashing in spatio-temporal models has focused on Temporal Convolution [cite28†6 ]. We advance the understanding of information degeneration in spatio-temporal models by deriving sensitivity bounds on the Jacobian of temporal attention layers. We prove the existence of diagonal attention sinks, induced by the residual and the weight matrix of the query.
L125: While full diagonal masks suppress the query part of the softmax attention, resulting in attention emphasizing specific keys for the whole sequence, diagonal penalty and diagonal dropout significantly improve the performance of temporal attention layers.

L197: ## Appendix B Evaluation for Long Horizon Forecasting
L198: 
L199: As a robustness check, we extended the evaluation to long-horizon forecasting, predicting 36 future steps (i.e., 3 hours). We used the same experimental set-up and evaluation methods as in Section cite14†4 for the short-term forecasting of 12 time steps.
L200: Table cite45†2 reports the results obtained by training the model with a sequence length of 36 time steps and forecasting 36 future steps. The Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) are reported for the 24th and 36th forecasting steps. In Section cite14†4 Table cite38†1 we can see that the results with residual are better than without.
L201: For longer horizons the difference between the results without a residual (a) and with residual (b-e) becomes even more significant. This aligns well with the rank collapse in width shown in [cite32†10 ], as well as with our previous findings. The larger $T$ is, the smaller off-diagonal entries become.
L202: This can also be seen in Figure cite46†2a . Overall, the diagonal penalty gives best results. For longer horizons, we have more heterogeneous patterns, and the diagonal might provide an important anchor. Using a penalty enables the model to use the diagonal entries when beneficial, which is not possible with dropout or masking.
