L146: Several strategies have been proposed (e.g. cite84†Chen et al. (2015) ; cite85†Gong et al. (2019) ; cite86†Chen et al. (2022) ; cite87†Kim et al. (2024) ), typically employing function-preserving weight transformations that expand a pre-trained smaller model into a larger one so that the larger model produces identical outputs for any input, thereby allowing it to immediately inherit the smaller model’s performance before further optimization.
L147: However, the training dynamics of upscaled models could be quite different from those of large models trained from scratch. As a result, to use upscaling effectively requires finding good choices of several hyperparameters that are highly dependent on the model architecture and optimizer. In practice, hyperparameter tuning at the scale of modern large models is infeasible, so hyperparameters for training large models are typically chosen by extrapolating from smaller models using scaling laws.
L148: Unfortunately, upscaling appears a priori to be incompatible with this approach, since training a single model with upscaling involves training both at small and then at large scales. Previous work has relied only on informal heuristics for hyperparameter tuning in the presence of upscaling to address this issue.
L149: As our first contribution, presented in Section cite8†2 , we extend the theory of function-preserving model expansions (cite84†Chen et al., 2015 ). We introduce a procedure that combines weight transformations with a coordinated rescaling of the learning rate and other relevant optimizer hyperparameters, and we prove that the resulting widened model both is functionally identical at initialization and remains equivalent to the original model throughout the entire training trajectory.
L150: We further show that this procedure is closely connected to the Maximal Update Parametrization ($\mu$P) proposed in cite88†Yang and Hu (2020) ; cite89†Yang and Littwin (2023) ; see Section cite11†2.3 . Using this connection, we extend the $\mu$P software package to support the widening procedure, facilitating its implementation across standard architectures including multi-layer perceptron (MLP), ResNets, and transformers.
L151: For our second contribution, presented in Section cite13†3 , we propose an upscaling algorithm grounded in the above theory. Intuitively, this theory implies that the training dynamics of the narrower model evolve within a lower-dimensional subspace of the wider model’s ambient parameter space. Therefore, once we widen a model, we also inject a small symmetry-breaking perturbation into the parameters so that the upscaled dynamics can escape this lower-dimensional subspace; see Figure cite90†1(a) .
L152: We leverage the connection with $\mu$P to show that our upscaling algorithm—with the right amount of noise—induces training dynamics that are provably stable and exhibit “optimal” feature learning in the infinite-width limit. In the same vein as cite91†Yang et al.
L153: (2022) , our method aligns training dynamics across widths and enables zero-shot hyperparameter transfer: we can now easily tune the hyperparameters on narrow models using the same upscaling procedure, and the best hyperparameters found there can then directly be used for large model upscaling; see Figure cite92†1(b) . Our implementation based on the $\mu$P software package also enables straightfoward implementation of hyperparameter transfer.
L154: As our third contribution, presented at the end of Section cite15†3.2 with additional details in Appendix cite46†E , we use the Tensor Programs machinery to characterize the infinite-width limit of upscaled training for common network architectures, opening the door to further theoretical analysis of training dynamics involving upscaling.
L155: While here we focus on upscaling with respect to “width”, extensions to upscaling with respect to depth are a natural next step whose treatment we leave to future work (see for example cite93†Yang et al. 2023b ; cite94†Bordelon et al. 2023 for hyperparameter transfer across depths). In Section cite18†4 , we provide experiments showing the performance of the proposed upscaling algorithm across various architectures and datasets, and illustrating the hyperparameter transfer numerically.
L156: The related work is summarized in Appendix cite25†B , and the conclusions and limitations are given in Appendix cite82†G . Our implementation of model upscaling, and the accompanying experiments, is publicly available at cite95†https://github.com/yuxinma98/mupscaling†github.com .

L165: ## 2 Equivalent models of different widths
L166: Our goal is to use pre-trained small models as a warm start initialization for training larger models. To do that, we will widen the small model into a larger one that implements the same function so that whatever was learned in the small model is not lost when we train the larger one. In this section, we present a theoretical analysis of the equivalence between neural networks of different widths across a broad range of architectures and optimization methods. We distinguish two notions of equivalence.
L167: First, a static equivalence refers to two models of different widths parametrizing exactly the same function. Second, a dynamic equivalence concerns an architecture–optimizer pair and means that two models of different widths follow identical training trajectories in function space—that is, they parametrize the same function at every training step.
L168: These two perspectives are unified by the framework of tensor programs, which we leverage to obtain general results in Section cite11†2.3 . As a warm-up, in Section cite9†2.1 we first illustrate both notions separately using a bias-free MLP trained with vanilla stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The next two subsections generalize this analysis, first to other optimization methods and then to general architectures.
L169: ### 2.1 Warm-up: MLP trained with SGD
L170: 
L171: Consider an $L$-layer MLP parameterizing a function from $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ that maps $x^{(0)}\mapsto h^{(L)}$ by recursing
L172: 
L173:  | $\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{(\ell)}&=W^{(\ell)}x^{(\ell-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}},\qquad x^{(\ell)}=\phi(h^{(\ell)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}},\qquad\text{for }\ell=1,2,\dots,L,\end{split}$  |  | (1)
L174: where the input and output dimensions are $n_{0}=d_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $n_{L}=d_{\mathrm{out}}$, respectively, $(W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}})_{\ell=1}^{L}$ are the layer-wise weight matrices, and $\phi$ is the activation function applied elementwise at each layer. Fixing $n_{0}$ and $n_{L}$ and increasing the hidden widths $n_{1},\dots,n_{L-1}$ yields wider, more expressive MLP models.
L175: We first show that duplicating and appropriately rescaling the weight matrices of any base MLP produces a widened MLP that parameterizes the exact same function.
L176: ###### Proposition 2.1 (Static equivalence of MLPs).
L177: 
L178: Consider a base MLP with weight matrices $\big(W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}}\big)_{\ell=1}^{L}$. Construct a widened MLP that uses the same activation function and preserves the input and output dimensions, with weights obtained by duplicating and rescaling those of the base MLP as
L179:  | $$\big({W^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}k_{\ell-1}^{-1}\,W^{(\ell)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}\times N_{\ell-1}}\big)_{\ell=1}^{L},$$  |  | (2)
L180: where $N_{\ell}=k_{\ell}n_{\ell}$ for all $\ell$ and the width multipliers $k_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}$ satisfy $k_{0}=k_{L}=1$. Then, for any input $x^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, both networks produce identical outputs $h^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ and thus parameterize the same function.
L181: ###### Proof.
L182: 
L183: We check by induction over layers that the activations of the widened MLP, denoted by ${x^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}$, are duplicated versions of those of the base MLP, denoted by $x^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}$. Specifically, assume ${x^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell-1)}=x^{(\ell-1)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}$. Then
L184:  | $\displaystyle{h^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}$  | $\displaystyle={W^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}{x^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell-1)}$  |
L185:  |  | $\displaystyle={k^{-1}_{\ell-1}}\big(W^{(\ell)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}^{\top}\big)\big(x^{(\ell-1)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}\big)$  |
L186:  |  | $\displaystyle=h^{(\ell)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}},$  |
L187: 
L188: and hence ${x^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}=x^{(\ell)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}$ as well. ∎
L189: Following the forward pass, backpropagation for the MLP defined in (cite98†1 ) proceeds as follows:
L190: 
L191:  | $\displaystyle\begin{split}dh^{(L)}&=\nabla_{h^{(L)}}\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}},\\
L192: dx^{(\ell-1)}&=(W^{(\ell)})^{\top}dh^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell-1}},\\
L193: dh^{(\ell-1)}&=dx^{(\ell-1)}\odot\phi^{\prime}(h^{(\ell-1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell-1}},\\
L194: dW^{(\ell)}&=dh^{(\ell)}(x^{(\ell-1)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}},\quad\text{for }\ell=L,L-1,\dots,1,\end{split}$  |  | (3)
L195: where $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the loss, and each gradient $d\bullet$ equals $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bullet}$. Applying SGD with layer-wise learning rate $\gamma^{(\ell)}$ to $W^{(\ell)}$, the weights are updated at each training step $t$ by
L196: 
L197:  | $${W}^{(\ell)}_{t+1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{W}^{(\ell)}_{t}-\gamma^{(\ell)}{dW}_{t}^{(\ell)},$$  |
L198: where ${W}^{(\ell)}_{t},{dW}_{t}^{(\ell)}$ denote the weight and the gradient at $t$-th training step respectively. We further show that if the per-layer learning rates of the widened MLP are chosen as a specific rescaling of those in the base MLP, then the two equivalent models undergo equivalent SGD updates and hence follow identical training trajectories in function space.
L199: ###### Proposition 2.2 (Dynamic equivalence of MLPs trained with SGD).
L200: Suppose we have a base MLP with weights $\big(W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}}\big)_{\ell=1}^{L}$ trained by SGD with per-layer learning rates $\gamma^{(\ell)}$.
L201: Construct a widened MLP with the same activation function and with weights $\big({W^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{k^{-1}_{\ell-1}}\,W^{(\ell)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}\times N_{\ell-1}}\big)_{\ell=1}^{L}$, where $N_{\ell}=k_{\ell}n_{\ell}$ for all $\ell$ and $k_{0}=k_{L}=1$, and train it by SGD using per-layer learning rates ${\gamma^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{k_{\ell}}{k^{-1}_{\ell-1}}\gamma^{(\ell)}$.
L202: Then, for all training steps $t\geq 0$, the weights satisfy
L203:  | $${W^{\uparrow}_{t}}^{(\ell)}\;=\;{k^{-1}_{\ell-1}}\,{W}^{(\ell)}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}^{\top}$$  |  | (4)
L204: 
L205: for all $\ell=1,\dots,L$, and therefore both networks parametrize the same function at every step (assuming they access the same data and randomness).
L206: Note that if the width multipliers are equal across dimensions ($k_{1}=\dots=k_{L-1}$), then this procedure leaves the learning rates of all hidden weights unchanged (${\gamma^{\uparrow}}^{(\ell)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\gamma^{(\ell)}$ for all $\ell=2,\dots,L-1$), and only modify the learning rates for $W^{(1)}$ and $W^{(L)}$.

L315: To illustrate the idea, take the MLP example from Section cite9†2.1 . Specifically, the first forward propagation for the upscaled network is defined recursively as:
L316: 
L317:  | $\displaystyle h^{(\ell)}$  | $\displaystyle=(k_{\ell-1}^{-1}W^{(\ell)}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}^{\top}+\Delta^{(\ell)})x^{(\ell-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}},$  |
L318:  | $\displaystyle x^{(\ell)}$  | $\displaystyle=\phi(h^{(\ell)})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}},$  |
L319: where $\Delta^{(\ell)}$ encodes the Gaussian noise injected into the model after widening. Because of the $\otimes\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{\ell-1}}^{\top}$ operation, these equations do not give a Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program. However, they can be rewritten in terms of variables of the original, not widened, dimension:
L320:  | $\displaystyle h^{(\ell)}_{(i)}$  | $\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\ell-1}}\left(k_{\ell-1}^{-1}W^{(\ell)}+\Delta^{(\ell)}_{(i,j)}\right)x^{(\ell-1)}_{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}},$  |
L321:  | $\displaystyle x^{(\ell)}_{(i)}$  | $\displaystyle=\phi(h^{(\ell)}_{(i)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}},\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,k_{\ell},$  |
L322: where $h^{(\ell)}_{(i)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(h^{(\ell)}_{i},h^{(\ell)}_{i+k_{\ell}},h^{(\ell)}_{i+2k_{\ell}},\dots)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}},i\in[k_{\ell}]$ partition the entries of the widened vector $h^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}$, and similarly $(\Delta^{(\ell)}_{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n_{\ell-1}})_{i\in[k_{\ell}],j\in[k_{\ell-1}]}$ partition the entries of the widened matrix $\Delta^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}\times N_{\ell-1}}$.
L323: The two above sets of equations describe identical dynamics, but the latter is a combination only of matrix multiplications and elementwise nonlinearities, and therefore falls within the Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ framework.
L324: We generalize this observation in Appendix cite49†E.2 , showing that any network architecture expressible in the Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ program admits an upscaling procedure that can still be formulated within the Ne$\otimes$or$\top$ framework by introducing such partitioned variables. This result justifies the width-dependent parametrization of the injected noise in Step 2 of Meta-Algorithm cite111†1 , which is chosen to match that of a fresh initialization under $\mu$P.
L325: This choice, together with training both the base and upscaled models under $\mu$P, ensures that the entire training process (including mid-training upscaling) adheres consistently to $\mu$P. Consequently, hyperparameters can be transferred reliably for training procedures including upscaling.
L326: ##### Infinite-width training dynamics.
L327: Beyond hyperparameter transfer, employing $\mu$P throughout the entire upscaling process confers several practical advantages. Following the analysis in cite88†Yang and Hu (2020) ; cite113†Yang et al. (2023a) , this parametrization yields “optimal” training dynamics in the infinite-width limit (fix the width multiplier $k$ and let the base width $n\to\infty$). In particular, hidden activations stay at $\Theta(1)$ scale throughout training, reducing the incidence of vanishing or exploding gradients.
L328: At the same time, their updates also stay at $\Theta(1)$ scale updates, ensuring the model stays in the rich “feature learning” regime and allowing it to achieve lower loss. For upscaling specifically, this ensures that both the initialization and the injected noise make non-trivial contributions to the widened model’s training dynamics, a desirable regime that maintains some signal from the base model while exploiting the benefits of increased width.
L329: Finally, using the Tensor Program framework of cite89†Yang and Littwin (2023) , we can explicitly characterize the infinite-width limit of the entire training dynamics involving upscaling. To streamline future work, we introduce a modified Tensor Program tailored to upscaled training, which we present in Appendix cite47†E.1 . Without upscaling, the Tensor Program analysis shows that pre-activations converge to i.i.d. Gaussian random variables in the infinite-width limit.
L330: With upscaling by a factor $k$ (i.e., increasing the width from $n$ to $kn$), pre-activations instead converge to i.i.d. blocks, each a $k$-dimensional Gaussian random vector that may have non-trivial covariance structure. Our modified framework tracks these vectors explicitly—through their evolving covariance structure—across the entire training trajectory. We illustrate the resulting characterization on two simple MLPs trained with SGD in Appendices cite50†E.3 and cite55†E.4 .
