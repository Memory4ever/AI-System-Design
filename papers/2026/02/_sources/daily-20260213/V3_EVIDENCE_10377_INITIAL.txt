L205: # Hardware Co-Design Scaling Laws via Roofline Modelling for On-Device LLMs
L206: Luoyang Sun Affiliation: AI Lab, The Yangtze River Delta Affiliation: Institution of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences Affiliation: University of Chinese Academy of Sciences Affiliation: Li Auto    Jiwen Jiang Affiliation: AI Lab, The Yangtze River Delta Affiliation: Institution of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences Affiliation: Li Auto    Yifeng Ding Affiliation: Li Auto    Fengfa Li Affiliation: Li Auto    Yan Song Affiliation: University College London    Haifeng Zhang Affiliation: Institution of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences Affiliation: University of Chinese Academy of Sciences    Jian Ying Affiliation: AI Lab, The Yangtze River Delta    Lei Ren Affiliation: Li Auto    Kun Zhan Affiliation: Li Auto    Wei Chen Affiliation: Li Auto    Yan Xie Affiliation: Li Auto    Cheng Deng Affiliation: The University of Edinburgh
L207: ###### Abstract
L208: Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs) have emerged as a key paradigm of Physical AI and are increasingly deployed in autonomous vehicles, robots, and smart spaces. In these resource-constrained on-device settings, selecting an appropriate large language model (LLM) backbone is a critical challenge: models must balance accuracy with strict inference latency and hardware efficiency constraints.
L209: This makes hardware-software co-design a game-changing requirement for on-device LLM deployment, where each hardware platform demands a tailored architectural solution. We propose a hardware co-design law that jointly captures model accuracy and inference performance. Specifically, we model training loss as an explicit function of architectural hyperparameters and characterise inference latency via roofline modelling.
L210: We empirically evaluate 1,942 candidate architectures on NVIDIA Jetson Orin, training 170 selected models for 10B tokens each to fit a scaling law relating architecture to training loss. By coupling this scaling law with latency modelling, we establish a direct accuracy-latency correspondence and identify the Pareto frontier for hardware co-designed LLMs.
L211: We further formulate architecture search as a joint optimisation over precision and performance, deriving feasible design regions under industrial hardware and application budgets. Our approach reduces architecture selection from months to days. At the same latency as Qwen2.5-0.5B on the target hardware, our co-designed architecture achieves 19.42% lower perplexity on WikiText-2.
L212: To our knowledge, this is the first principled and operational framework for hardware co-design scaling laws in on-device LLM deployment. We will make the code and related checkpoints publicly available.

L304: Large language models are increasingly deployed in embodied AI systems such as autonomous vehicles and mobile robots, where they serve as high-level planners within Vision–Language-Action frameworks cite190†Kim et al. (2024) ; cite191†Zitkovich et al. (2023) ; cite192†Sapkota et al. (2025) . However, on-device platforms face strict constraints on memory, bandwidth, power, and latency that fundamentally reshape model design cite193†Zhou et al. (2025) ; cite194†Yang et al. (2023) .
L305: Architectures optimized for cloud GPUs often become infeasible on the edge: high-accuracy models may violate latency budgets, while latency-optimized pipelines can degrade accuracy. This tension motivates hardware–software co-design, where architectural choices are explicitly guided by hardware capabilities and deployment constraints cite195†Guo et al. (2025a) .
L306: The fundamental challenge stems from the irregular compute–memory profile of transformers cite196†Liu et al. (2024) ; cite197†Deng et al. (2025) : attention is bandwidth-bound, feedforward layers are compute-bound, and KV-cache stresses on-chip memory. Despite high theoretical throughput from AI-SoCs, LLM inference rarely reaches peak utilization. Instead, performance is dictated by arithmetic intensity, on-chip locality, and workload patterns like KV-cache footprint and MoE routing.
L307: Architectural modification can shift operations across regimes in the hardware Roofline model cite198†Williams et al. (2008) ; cite199†Hahnyuan (2024) , shown in cite200†Figure 2(a) . A representative example is scaling model depth and width in Transformer-based LLMs. Increasing depth results in linear growth in both computation and memory traffic, as each additional layer introduces a fixed amount of parameter reads and arithmetic operations.
L308: In contrast, increasing model width leads to quadratic growth in parameter size and memory I/O, since both attention and feed-forward layers scale with the square of the hidden dimension. Under batch-1 inference on edge devices, where weight reuse across tokens is limited and on-chip cache capacity is insufficient to hold model parameters, inference latency is dominated by repeated weight loads from off-chip memory.
L309: As a consequence, arithmetic intensity remains low and does not scale proportionally with model width, pushing execution into a memory bandwidth–limited regime in the roofline model cite201†Bian et al. (2025a) ; cite202†Bian et al. (2025b) . This mismatch between scaling behavior and hardware characteristics motivates hardware-aware architectural design beyond naive depth and width scaling.

L316: In order to find an optimal model, Neural architecture search (NAS) cite208†Zoph and Le (2016) usually is an appropriate way, shown in cite209†Figure 2(b) . NAS has traditionally focused on optimizing a single objective, such as validation loss or accuracy, often under loosely defined computational budgets cite206†Elsken et al. (2019) . While effective in unconstrained settings, these approaches are ill-suited to edge and on-device platforms, where latency–accuracy trade-offs are unavoidable.
L317: As shown in cite210†Table 1 , LLM inference system design involves several fundamental trade-offs cite211†Cheng et al. (2025) ; cite212†Yang et al. (2025b) ; cite213†Kurtic et al. (2025) ; cite214†Egiazarian et al. (2025) ; cite215†Yin et al. (2025) ; cite216†Xu et al. (2025) ; cite197†Deng et al. (2025) . This paper focuses on the interplay between model loss and inference latency in hardware co-designed LLMs (bold in cite210†Table 1 ), which is commonly observed in practice but has not been systematically characterized.
L318: This trade-off motivates a Pareto-based approach to architecture search cite207†Cheng et al. (2018) , visualized as cite217†Figure 2(c) . Instead of a single optimal model, Pareto optimization identifies a frontier of non-dominated architectures balancing accuracy and latency. This allows designers to directly select models fitting specific deployment constraints, avoiding exhaustive enumeration cite196†Liu et al. (2024) ; cite218†Tang et al. (2024) .

L326: We propose a hardware-aware modeling framework (shown in cite219†Figure 1 ) for on-device LLMs that jointly captures accuracy and inference performance. We model loss via architectural hyperparameters and predict latency using roofline analysis, providing a unified view of the accuracy–latency trade-off. We validate the framework on NVIDIA Jetson Orin by benchmarking thousands of candidate architectures and training a subset to fit a parameter–loss scaling law.
L327: Combining this law with hardware-level latency modeling yields the Pareto frontier for the target system. We extend this into a theoretical joint optimization formulation over precision and performance, deriving feasible design regions under practical constraints.

L345: ### 2.1 Efficient LLM Architectures
L346: Recent LLM designs prioritize inference efficiency beyond parameter scaling. Key directions include: (1) sparse activation via MoE routing cite220†Guo et al. (2025b) ; cite221†Agarwal et al. (2025) , (2) KV-cache reduction through multi-head latent attention (MLA) cite197†Deng et al. (2025) or sliding-window attention cite222†Team et al. (2025a) , (3) sub-quadratic attention using linear-complexity mechanisms cite223†Team et al.
L347: (2025b) ; cite224†MiniMax-AI (2025) , and (4) hybrid architectures combining SSM with attention cite225†Blakeman et al. (2025) . Gated attention variants cite226†Qiu et al. (2025) ; cite227†Sun et al. (2025) further enable token-dependent compute modulation. These innovations fundamentally shift inference bottlenecks from compute-bound to memory- or routing-sensitive regimes.
L348: ### 2.2 On-Device LLM Deployment
L349: Deploying LLMs on edge devices requires co-optimization across model, compression, and system layers. SLMs such as Qwen cite228†Yang et al. (2024) , MiniCPM cite229†Hu et al. (2024) , and SmolLM cite230†Bakouch et al. (2025) achieve strong performance through data-efficient training. Quantization methods (AWQ cite231†Lin et al. L350: (2024) ) enable efficient execution on heterogeneous hardware. Architecturally, deeper designs with shared embeddings cite196†Liu et al. (2024) and sparsity-aware mechanisms cite197†Deng et al. (2025) demonstrate favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-offs for resource-constrained deployment.
