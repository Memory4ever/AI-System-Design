L63: # Vulnerabilities in Partial TEE-Shielded LLM Inference with Precomputed Noise
L64: 
L65: CCS: Security and privacy Key management CCS: Security and privacy Cryptanalysis and other attacks CCS: Computing methodologies Natural language processing CCS: Security and privacy Domain-specific security and privacy architectures
L66: Abhishek Saini Affiliation: Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Piscataway, NJ, USA email: abhishek.saini@rutgers.edu , Haolin Jiang Affiliation: Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Piscataway, NJ, USA email: hj462@scarletmail.rutgers.edu and Hang Liu Affiliation: Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Piscataway, NJ, USA email: hang.liu@rutgers.edu
L67: ###### Abstract.
L68: The deployment of large language models (LLMs) on third-party devices requires new ways to protect model intellectual property. While Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) offer a promising solution, their performance limits can lead to a critical compromise: using a precomputed, static secret basis to accelerate cryptographic operations. We demonstrate that this mainstream design pattern introduces a classic cryptographic flaw, the reuse of secret keying material, into the system’s protocol.
L69: We prove its vulnerability with two distinct attacks: First, our attack on a model confidentiality system achieves a full confidentiality break by recovering its secret permutations and model weights. Second, our integrity attack completely bypasses the integrity checks of systems like Soter and TSQP.
L70: We demonstrate the practicality of our attacks against state-of-the-art LLMs, recovering a layer’s secrets from a LLaMA-3 8B model in about 6 minutes and showing the attack scales to compromise 405B-parameter LLMs across a variety of configurations.

L72: 
L73: Confidential Computing, Machine Learning Security, Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs), Large Language Models (LLMs), Cryptographic Attacks, Key Reuse
L74: ## 1. Introduction
L75: The initial wave of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been dominated by centralized, API-based services (cite51†OpenAI, 2025a ; cite52†Anthropic, 2025 ; cite53†Google, 2025 ). However, this paradigm is rapidly giving way to a new frontier: local deployment of models outside the provider’s direct control.
L76: This shift is not driven by a single factor, but by three distinct and essential requirements: First, enterprise users are increasingly running models on-premises to process sensitive, proprietary data, driven by concerns over exposing market secrets to competitors and the legal and compliance risks of sharing confidential information with third-party model providers (cite54†OpenAI, 2025b ; cite55†SentinelOne, 2025 ).
L77: Second, entire classes of mission-critical applications, such as autonomous driving and robotics, are non-negotiably edge-native; their need for real-time responsiveness and constant availability precludes any reliance on a remote server (cite56†Firoozi et al., 2023 ; cite57†Cui et al., 2024 ; cite58†Tesla, 2025 ; cite59†Karumbunathan, 2022 ).
L78: Finally, the proliferation of consumer devices like smartphones and smart assistants, now equipped with powerful AI accelerators, has created a demand for a new generation of responsive, always-on applications powered by local proprietary models (cite60†Apple, 2025 ; cite61†Gupta, 2023 ; cite62†Wilhelm, 2023 ).
L79: While each requirement is distinct, they converge on a single, critical security challenge: placing a multi-million dollar model, a core intellectual property asset, onto hardware not managed by the provider exposes it to unauthorized replication and reverse engineering (cite63†Sun et al., 2021 ).
L80: This tension has led to a clear call from the research community for a “middle path” that delivers the full benefits of local execution without sacrificing the model provider’s intellectual property (cite64†Huang et al., 2025 ; cite65†Cheng et al., 2025 ).
L81: One approach to securing inference relies on purely cryptographic methods like Homomorphic Encryption (HE), which allow computation on encrypted data. However, HE is often too slow for the demands of low-latency LLM inference (cite66†Gilad-Bachrach et al., 2016 ; cite67†Juvekar et al., 2018 ).
L82: Consequently, hardware-based solutions using Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) have emerged as a more practical alternative that offers a better balance of security and performance (cite68†Hanzlik et al., 2019 ; cite69†Ohrimenko et al., 2016 ). However, the memory and performance constraints of TEEs make running an entire state-of-the-art LLM within a secure enclave impractical (cite70†Lee et al., 2019 ; cite71†Kim et al., 2020 ).
L83: While recent confidential computing architectures like Intel TDX, AMD SEV, Arm CCA, and NVIDIA’s Confidential Computing are beginning to address these limitations, their practical adoption is hindered by limited availability or the persistent challenge of securing untrusted accelerators (cite72†Kaplan et al., 2021 ; cite73†Siby et al., 2024 ; cite74†Abdollahi et al., 2025 ; cite75†Dong and Wang, 2025 ; cite76†Zhu et al., 2024 ; cite77†Hunt et al., 2020 ; cite78†Vaswani et al., 2023 ; cite79†Volos et al., 2018 ; cite80†Chrapek et al., 2024 ).
L84: This has led to the mainstream paradigm of Partial TEE-Shielded Execution (PTSE), which offloads computationally intensive operations to powerful but untrusted hardware like Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), while the TEE retains a critical role in managing the overall secure execution of the model (cite81†Tramèr and Boneh, 2019 ; cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite85†Stoica et al., 2017 ).
L85: Early PTSE strategies focused on model partitioning, where the model is split, and only the most sensitive layers or components are executed inside the TEE (cite86†Mo et al., 2020 ; cite87†Liu et al., 2023 ; cite88†Schlögl and Böhme, 2020 ). However, this strategy is flawed as it offers incomplete IP protection and underutilizes the accelerator (cite89†Li et al., 2024b ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ).

L101: More recent PTSE approaches have evolved towards cryptographic obfuscation, which operates on a “lock-and-key” principle: The model is first obfuscated (locked) offline using a secret transformation, rendering it unusable by any party that does not possess the corresponding secret key. During online inference, the TEE’s role is twofold: (i) Model confidentiality. TEE guards the model’s secrets by securely holding the key needed to de-obfuscate computations from the GPU.
L102: A common approach to obfuscation in this domain is permutation-based locking, which secretly shuffles a model’s weights; the TEE then ensures the model only functions correctly by using the secret permutation key to properly order the data flowing into the locked model weights (cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite92†Yuan et al., 2024b ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite93†Xu and Fang, 2024 ; cite94†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ).
L103: Recent work has focused on scaling this approach to protect LLMs (cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite91†Refael et al., 2024 ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ). (ii) Computational integrity. The TEE serves to verify the integrity of computations offloaded to an untrusted hardware. This is a vital role, as an attacker with control over the host system could manipulate the GPU’s computations. Such tampering can degrade the model’s service quality, damaging the provider’s reputation and eroding user trust.
L104: More maliciously, even an unprivileged, co-located attacker could aim for targeted manipulations (cite96†Kim et al., 2014 ; cite97†Hong et al., 2019 ), altering the computation in subtle ways to bypass security filters or cause specific misclassifications that benefit them (cite98†Rakin et al., 2021 ; cite99†Zhao et al., 2019 ).
L105: To prevent this, systems like Soter (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ) and TSQP (cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ) leverage the TEE hardware to inject hidden fingerprints, known-answer challenges for which the TEE already knows the correct output, mixed in with real data. If the untrusted hardware returns a tampered result for these hidden checks, the integrity breach is detected, and the session can be determined as untrusted.
L106: To achieve these distinct security goals, a common architectural pattern has emerged across a wide range of systems: a three-step Mask-Offload-Restore protocol, which we detail and compare across several state-of-the-art systems in Table cite100†1 . The protocol proceeds as follows:

L120: The practical challenge arises because the TEE is responsible for more than just generating this randomness; it must also restore the original result by computing the precise effect of the noise. As we will demonstrate in Section cite11†3 , calculating this effect for truly random noise is computationally prohibitive. It would force the TEE to execute expensive On-the-Fly operations, like full matrix-vector multiplications, creating an unacceptable performance bottleneck.
L121: To circumvent this bottleneck, practical implementations are compelled to adopt a performance-driven shortcut: a precomputation-based pattern. At startup, the TEE generates and stores a small, fixed set of $K$ secret noise vectors and their corresponding effects, which we call the precomputed static basis. During online inference, all “random” noise is then efficiently generated by combining these precomputed static basis vectors using freshly sampled random coefficients.
L122: For TEE-based model confidentiality, this efficiency-driven design is a necessity to scale to LLMs, while for others, like the integrity-checking mechanism in Soter, TSQP, it is adopted as a tempting shortcut for efficiency.
L123: In this work, we demonstrate that this efficiency-driven reliance on a precomputed static basis is a fundamental security flaw. The key insight is that the random noise generated this way is not truly random. Instead, it is permanently confined to a predictable, low-dimensional ($K$-dimensional) subspace spanned by the static basis vectors.
L124: This creates a critical vulnerability that allows an attacker to aggregate information over multiple interactions to algebraically break the system’s security guarantees. This flaw is not an implementation bug or a side channel, but a systematic weakness inherent to the protocol’s design.
L125: We prove the severity of this flaw by developing attacks that compromise two distinct security guarantees: model confidentiality system and computational integrity framework. The success of our attacks on these two different real-world designs proves that the static basis is a versatile and fundamental vulnerability. Our primary contributions are:

While other protocol-level Partial TEE-shielded execution attacks exist, they typically rely on statistical inference and require a known public model to succeed (cite95†Wang, 2025 ; cite94†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite101†Zhang et al., 2023 ). L142: ## 2. Background and Threat Model
L143: ### 2.1. TEE-based Model Confidentiality
L144: Many TEE-based model confidentiality schemes operate on a lock-and-key authorization principle to enable secure computation on untrusted hardware (cite102†Bai et al., 2025 ; cite93†Xu and Fang, 2024 ; cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite89†Li et al., 2024b ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ). In this paradigm, the model’s weights are first “locked” offline through an obfuscation technique, such as permutation, rendering them unusable without a corresponding secret key.
L145: This locked model is then deployed to the untrusted environment. The secret key, the permutation matrix in this case, is stored securely within the TEE. During inference, TEE uses the secret key to securely perform the secret transformation necessary for the correct functioning of the model.
L146: As a concrete example of this mechanism, Figure cite103†1 illustrates a single layer of the Llama architecture secured using TLG (cite83†Li et al., 2024a ). In the secured model, the weights are locked offline using layer-specific permutation matrices, $\pi_{l}$ and $\rho_{l}$. To an attacker, these locked weights appear random; they are unusable without the secret permutations, which are stored securely in the TEE.
L147: We denote secured activations with a prime (e.g., $x_{l}^{\prime}$) to distinguish them from their plaintext counterparts ($x_{l}$).
L148: The inference process works by applying corresponding permutations to the activations. An incoming activation $x^{\prime}_{l}$, already permuted by the previous layer ($x^{\prime}_{l}=x_{l}\pi_{l}$), is processed by the attention block. Due to the block’s structure, the permutations on the weights and activations cancel out at step , ensuring the intermediate outputs are correct (e.g., $Q^{\prime}=x^{\prime}_{l}\pi_{l}^{T}W_{q}=(x_{l}\pi_{l})\pi_{l}^{T}W_{q}=Q$).
L149: The computed values $Q^{\prime}$, $K^{\prime}$, $V^{\prime}$ are then combined to form the aggregated context, $s^{\prime}$ (), which then gets multiplied by the output projection matrix, $W_{o}\pi_{l}$ . This gives the output of the attention block, $o^{\prime}$. A residual connection is added to create $h^{\prime}$ (), the input to the feed-forward network. At this stage, $h^{\prime}=h\pi_{l}$ is permuted correctly for the first half of the feedforward network to compute $a^{\prime}$ .
L150: Since $h^{\prime}$ cancels out the permuted weights of the first half of the feed-forward network, $a^{\prime}$ is the same as its plaintext version $a$. Therefore, $a^{\prime}$ is not correctly permuted for the locked weight $W^{\prime}_{3}=\rho_{l}^{T}W_{3}$.
L151: To securely apply the required permutation $\rho_{l}$ without leaking it to an observer, the system uses a three-step ENCRYPT-LINEAR-DECRYPT sequence: the TEE first encrypts the activation $a^{\prime}$ with a random one-time pad ($m$) before permuting it (), the GPU performs the linear computation , and TEE decrypts the result by removing the noise effect ($mW_{3}$) to recover the correct output ().
L152: To ensure the cryptographic security of one-time pad masking, all computations are performed over a finite field. Following standard practice, all floating-point activations and weights are first quantized to fixed-point integers. These integers are then treated as elements of the finite field $\mathbb{F}_{P}$, where $P$ is a large prime chosen to prevent wrap-around errors.
L153: This step is what allows the random mask m to function as a secure one-time pad, providing a formal basis for the system’s security guarantees (cite81†Tramèr and Boneh, 2019 ).
L154: ### 2.2. TEE-based Computational Integrity
L155: 
L156: cite104†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2. Soter’s mechanism for generating integrity fingerprints from a static basis of precomputed cornerstones.
L157: Model’s computational integrity is sometimes protected using a technique called oblivious fingerprinting (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ). The core idea is to mix known-answer fingerprints, challenges for which the TEE already knows the correct output, with the genuine user inputs sent to the GPU. To prevent an attacker from simply detecting and ignoring these checks, the fingerprints are designed to be statistically indistinguishable from normal inputs.
L158: By verifying the GPU’s output for these hidden fingerprints, the TEE can detect any tampering.
L159: Figure cite105†2 exemplifies this mechanism used in Soter (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ) and TSQP (cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ). Beginning with a one-time offline setup where the TEE creates and stores a static basis of $K=10$ cornerstone fingerprint pairs, $\{m_{i},F(m_{i})\}$ (). During each online inference, the TEE generates a dynamic challenge, $m^{\prime}$, as a random linear combination of the cornerstone inputs ($m^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}m_{i}$) ().
L160: This challenge is then shuffled with genuine activations and sent to the GPU (), which applies the operator $F$ to the entire batch . Upon receiving the results, the TEE unshuffles them () and verifies the GPU’s work by comparing the returned fingerprint output, $F(m^{\prime})$, against the expected result it computes locally: $F(m^{\prime})_{expected}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}F(m_{i})$ (). A mismatch signifies an integrity breach and aborts the process.
L161: ### 2.3. Threat Model
L162: 
L163: We consider a setting where a defender (model provider) deploys a proprietary model on a user’s device, which is controlled by a powerful adversary. The defender’s goal is to guarantee two security properties for the deployed model: model confidentiality, to protect the intellectual property of its weights and parameters, and computational integrity, to verify the correctness of any computation offloaded to untrusted hardware.
L164: We assume the TEE itself is a secure black box, free from physical or hardware side-channel attacks. All components outside the TEE including the host operating system, system memory, and the GPU accelerator are considered untrusted and are fully controlled by the adversary. The adversary’s capabilities are therefore formidable: they can observe, intercept, and modify all communication between the TEE and the untrusted GPU.
L165: They know the complete architecture of the deployed model and the full details of the security protocol being used, but they do not have access to the secret keying material (e.g., secret permutations, static noise basis) held within the TEE. The adversary’s objective is to break the defender’s security guarantees.
L166: Aligned with our two primary case studies, we define two concrete goals for the adversary: (1) to compromise confidentiality by recovering the secret keys needed to fully reconstruct the original model (as in a TLG-like system), or (2) to compromise integrity by identifying and bypassing the verification mechanism to tamper with results without detection (as in Soter).
L167: ## 3. Key Observations on the Trade-off between Security vs Computation Cost
L168: ### 3.1. Key Observations
L169: Analyzing the workflow of TLG, we observe that the computations inside the TEE ( and in Figure cite103†1 ) can lead to a bottleneck. In particular, the calculation of the noise effect, $mW_{3}$, should be efficient since it has the largest computational footprint.
L170: In the meantime, as the model continues to grow (e.g., LLM), and the deployment of these LLMs on mobile devices becomes prevalent (cite106†Sorensen and Khlaaf, 2024 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ), the memory cost mainly surrounding $W_{3}$ could also be a concern (Table cite107†3 ).
L171: This observation highlights a fundamental tension in Soter’s design. For the integrity mechanism to be secure, the dynamically generated fingerprints must be statistically indistinguishable from genuine activations. However, for the mechanism to be efficient, Soter relies on this static, precomputed basis to enable rapid integrity checks, a point acknowledged in the original paper (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ).
L172: As we show later in the paper, this reliance on a static basis forces all generated fingerprints into a predictable, low-dimensional subspace, making them computationally distinguishable from genuine inputs and undermining the system’s security guarantee.
L173: In summary, the mechanisms in our two case studies, TLG and Soter, share a critical dependency: the need for an efficient TEE-based calculation of a noise effect ($mW_{3}$, $F(m^{\prime})$). As we will detail in Section cite13†3.2 , there are two main strategies to compute this effect: an on-the-fly method that generates fresh randomness for each query, versus a more performant precomputation-based approach that relies on a static, pre-loaded table of noise vectors.
L174: However, the prohibitive memory and latency costs of the on-the-fly method force designers to adopt the precomputation-based approach, as shown in our subsequent detailed analysis of a TLG-like system.
L175: ### 3.2. Detailed Analysis
L176: 
L177: This section uses TLG to explain the on-the-fly vs precomputed approaches with the memory and latency constraints in consideration.
L178: On-the-Fly Approach employs an ENCRYPT-LINEAR-DECRYPT sequence to authorize computations on the untrusted platform. As illustrated in Figure cite108†3 (a), the TEE first ENCRYPTs () an activation by masking it with a random One-Time Pad (OTP), $m$. After the LINEAR computation is performed on the GPU (), the result re-enters the TEE. The DECRYPT function () then recovers the correct output by subtracting the effect of the initial noise, $mW_{3}$.
L179: The method used to obtain this noise effect, $mW_{3}$, is the most time-consuming operation across the entire sequence.
