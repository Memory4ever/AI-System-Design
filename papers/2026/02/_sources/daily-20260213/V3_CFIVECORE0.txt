L71: While approaches such as [cite58†18 , cite59†19 , cite60†20 ] achieve theoretical end-to-end differentiability, they essentially rely on numerical approximation and lack analytical gradients, and hence are unsuitable for the learning and application of high-quality world models.
L72: Moreover, due to the structural decoupling of perception and dynamics, these methods are often compelled to maintain two distinct representations: one for visual rendering (3DGS [cite61†21 ] or Nerf [cite62†22 ]) and another for physical simulation (e.g., explicit meshes or particles).
L73: In practice, enforcing precise geometric alignment between these dual representations is non-trivial, causing the back-propagated gradients from visual reconstruction errors to be indirect and spatially misaligned with the underlying physical parameters.
L74: In this work, we introduce a novel differentiable framework called ContactGaussian-WM for learning a physics-grounded world model from a small number of interactive videos. This framework enables visually realistic and physically plausible predictions and bridges the sim-to-real gap in robot planning tasks.
L75: Specifically, we unify visual and geometric representations, where a decoupled optimization approach is used to train 3DGS [cite61†21 ] to initialize the scene and leads to a representation that balances visual and geometric aspects. To ensure the differentiability of collision detection, we force the 3DGS [cite61†21 ] to be isotropic spheres.
L76: To adapt to contact-rich scenarios, we select a lightweight yet capable contact dynamics model [cite63†23 ] that is effective enough to handle different contact modes (skip, separation, stick) [cite64†24 , cite65†25 ]. This model, combined with a 3DGS differentiable renderer, is used to construct ContactGaussian-WM, enabling differentiable learning, prediction, and planning control of the world model.
L77: Extensive experiments, in both simulated and real-world environments, demonstrate that our proposed framework is capable of learning a physics-grounded world model from sparse observations in complex contact-rich scenarios. The resulting model facilitates long-horizon, accurate, and physically consistent predictions, effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap. Furthermore, we showcase its capability for real-time planning when integrated with Model Predictive Control (MPC).

L86: ## II Related Works
L87: 
L88: ### II-A World model
L89: #### II-A 1 Data-driven world model
L90: Recent generative AI has revitalized pixel-based approaches, where foundation models like  [cite66†26 , cite42†2 , cite43†3 , cite67†27 , cite44†4 , cite45†5 ] serve as generalist video simulators. To improve control efficiency, latent-state-based methods [cite68†28 , cite46†6 , cite47†7 , cite48†8 , cite49†9 , cite50†10 ] compress observations into compact states. However, both paradigms treat dynamics as black-box transitions of probability distributions.
L91: Lacking explicit physical constraints, they suffer from physical hallucinations, e.g., object morphing or vanishing, since they essentially mimic data statistics rather than enforcing causal physical laws.
L92: #### II-A 2 Physics-grounded world models
L93: 
L94: To improve physical plausibility, some works incorporate structural priors using graph neural networks on particle representations [cite69†29 , cite70†30 ]. While these offer better generalization, they remain ”black-box” and lack strict guarantees for non-penetration or friction. Alternatively, some methods integrate explicit physics engines with renderers [cite53†13 , cite54†14 ], but rely on gradient-free optimization which scales poorly with search space.
L95: Consequently, recent research explores differentiable world models. PIN-WM [cite57†17 ] achieves local differentiability via implicit differentiation of an LCP solver, while others [cite58†18 , cite59†19 , cite60†20 ] use numerical approximations. However, these often suffer from gradient discontinuities at contact boundaries and visibility changes, leading to unstable training.
L96: Furthermore, non-differentiable collision detection in models like PIN-WM prevents geometry optimization, making them unsuitable for contact-rich scenarios where geometric errors cannot be corrected. In contrast, ContactGaussian-WM achieves true end-to-end differentiability, enabling the simultaneous optimization of physical parameters and collision geometry.
L97: ### II-B Rigid Body Dynamics Simulation
L98: #### II-B 1 Collision detection
L99: 
L100: Mainstream physics engines (e.g., Bullet, MuJoCo, DART, and Drake) predominantly rely on mesh-based discrete representations and algorithms like GJK [cite71†31 ] and EPA [cite72†32 ] to compute separation distances. While precise for forward simulation, these methods are non-differentiable and ill-suited for gradient-based optimization.
L101: To address this, recent frameworks have shifted toward continuous geometric representations. Methods like gradSim [cite60†20 ], D-REX [cite59†19 ], and ContactSDF [cite73†33 ] utilize volumetric fields (SDFs or density grids) to support gradient propagation through field penetration. Similarly, recent work [cite58†18 ] employs 3DGS [cite61†21 ] to model geometry for physical interactions. However, these methods often rely on spatial and temporal discretization and approximation during contact resolution.
L102: These numerical approximations introduce gradient discontinuities or inaccuracy that limit detection accuracy and fall short of the fidelity provided by exact analytical solutions.
L103: cite74†Image: Refer to caption Fig. 2: Overview of ContactGaussian-WM. We first initialize a scene with a unified spherical Gaussian representation, then jointly refine physics and geometry: given the current physical state and action, the differentiable collision detector uses Gaussian geometry to compute contact points, which are fed into the complementarity-free contact dynamics model to compute the next physical state, and the 3DGS renderer generates the next image.
L104: The pipeline is fully differentiable for end-to-end learning.
L105: #### II-B 2 Contact Models
L106: Traditional rigid-body contact models are often formulated as Nonlinear Complementarity Problems (NCP) [cite75†34 , cite76†35 , cite77†36 ], which is computationally inefficient for high dimensions. One common approach to simplify its computation is to approximate Coulomb friction cones by polyhedral cones [cite77†36 , cite51†11 ], allowing the formulation to be transformed into Linear Complementarity Problems (LCP) solvable by efficient solvers.
L107: Another approach is to relax the NCP formulation [cite78†37 ] by constraining the contact velocity within a dual friction cone, transforming the problem into Convex Complementarity Problems (CCP). This practice offers significant computational benefits, such as fast convergence and theoretical solution guarantees [cite79†38 , cite80†39 ].
L108: Furthermore, the aforementioned CCP can be formulated as the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions for a second-order cone-constrained convex optimization problem [cite78†37 , cite81†40 , cite82†41 , cite51†11 ]. To endow the learnability for the model, several approaches [cite83†42 , cite84†43 , cite85†44 , cite86†45 , cite87†46 ] have proposed differentiable approximations as alternatives to complementarity-based models.
L109: These methods achieve differentiability through various operations, such as introducing penalty functions [cite83†42 , cite86†45 , cite87†46 ], relaxing complementarity constraints [cite84†43 ], or employing implicit complementarity formulations [cite85†44 ]. A shared feature of these methods is that they ultimately require iteratively solving a residual equation for contact constraint resolution, with differentiability typically being obtained via the Implicit Function Theorem [cite88†47 ].
L110: Consequently, some researchers in [cite89†48 , cite90†49 , cite86†45 , cite87†46 , cite63†23 ] have explored closed-form contact dynamics models. While some works [cite89†48 , cite90†49 , cite86†45 , cite87†46 ] seek a closed-form solution by decoupling contact normal forces from Coulomb friction, this often introduces additional hyperparameters and potential non-physical artifacts. In contrast, the approach in [cite63†23 ] utilizes an approximation within the dual friction cone.
L111: This allows for a unified treatment of contact normal and frictional forces within a single term, ensuring flexible capture of different contact modes and automatic satisfaction of Coulomb’s friction law.
L112: In this work, our rigid body simulator implements differentiable collision detection and integrates a closed-form contact model [cite63†23 ], creating a more efficient differentiable simulation and providing stable analytical gradients.
L113: ## III Methods
L114: ### III-A Overview of our framework
L115: Figure cite91†2 outlines the ContactGaussian-WM framework, which mainly comprises two stages: scene initialization and phys-geo refinement. Scene initialization uses decoupled optimization to obtain a unified spherical Gaussian representation, balancing visual and geometric aspects. Phys-geo refinement is achieved through the ContactGaussian-WM world model, which mainly consists of a differentiable collision detector, an explicit contact dynamics model, and a 3DGS renderer.
L116: All those components are discussed in detail as follows.
L117: ### III-B Problem formulation
L118: Given an RGB video of a contact-rich scene, our goal is to build a world model that predicts the future images of the scene and captures the underlying physical laws. Denote the image of the scene at time step $t$ as $\mathbf{I}_{t}$. Our goal is to minimize the difference between the image $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_{t}$ predicted by our world model and the actual image $\mathbf{I}_{t}$.
L119: In our ContactGaussian-WM, we represent the contact-rich scene by Gaussians, denoted $\mathcal{G}:=\{\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}},\mathcal{G}_{\text{vis}}\}=\{\{\mathbf{c},s\},\{\alpha,f\}\}$, where $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}$ (resp. $\mathcal{G}_{\text{vis}}$) encodes the geometry (resp. appearance) of all objects in the scene with the spatial center and the isotropic scale (opacity $\alpha$ and color features $f$) of the Gaussian primitives.
L120: ContactGaussian-WM predicts future images of the scene by transforming the Gaussian $\mathcal{G}$ by the physical state $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}$, and rendering them. We denote this process using a function $\mathbb{R}$. The evolution of the physical state $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ over time is realized using our differentiable simulator (Section cite18†III-D ). Therefore, the optimization problem is formulated as:
L121:  | $\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{G},\bm{\theta}}$  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G},\bm{\theta})=\sum_{t}\left(\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mathbf{I}}_{t},\mathbf{I}_{t})\right)$  |  | (1)
L122:  | $\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}$  | $\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{f}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t},\bm{\theta},\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}),\quad\hat{\mathbf{I}}_{t}=\mathbb{R}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\mathcal{G}),$  |
L123: where $\mathbf{f}$ is our differentiable simulator with control input $\mathbf{a}_{t}$ and being parameterized by physical properties $\bm{\theta}$ (detailed later) and Gaussian geometry attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}$. This cost function is designed as $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Loft}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{L1}}$, which includes Loft feature loss [cite92†50 ] and L1 loss to help us better capture differences at both the feature and pixel levels.
L124: Jointly learning Gaussian parameters $\mathcal{G}$ and physical parameters $\bm{\theta}$ from a single video segment by Eq.(cite93†1 ) is very challenging due to the high nonlinearity and ambiguity. To address this, we divide the entire learning framework into two stages. The first stage focuses on optimizing the Gaussians $\mathcal{G}$, while the second stage is to optimize the physical parameters $\bm{\theta}$, and simultaneously fine-tunes the geometry attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}$.
L125: ### III-C Stage I: Scene Initialization with Sphere Geometry Gaussian Splatting (SG-GS)
L126: 
L127: We first obtain multi-view observations $\{\mathbf{I}_{i}\}$ of the scene from single or multiple calibrated cameras. Based on that, we use SAM2 [cite94†51 ] to extract geometry features $\mathbf{G}_{i}$. Then, we propose an SG-GS method to obtain a Gaussian representation of the scene from the observation, which has two key designs:
L128: Isotropic spherical primitives. Unlike traditional 3DGS  [cite61†21 ], we enforce isotropic scaling and frozen rotation updates of Gaussians to ensure that each Gaussian maintains a spherical shape, which helps us achieve closed-form differentiable collision detection in Stage II.
L129: Decoupled optimization. To reconcile the conflict between high-frequency visual details and stable physical geometry, we adopt a decoupled optimization strategy. First, we focus exclusively on learning the geometric attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}$ while keep the visual appearance attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{vis}}$ frozen. This compels the primitives to strictly align with the geometrical structure of the object rather than compensating for photometric errors.
L130: Consequently, the optimization minimizes the reconstruction error between the rendered and observed geometric maps:
L131:  | $$\min_{\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}}:=\sum\nolimits_{i}\|\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}})-\mathbf{G}_{i}\|_{1}$$  |  | (2)
L132: 
L133: where $\hat{\mathbf{G}_{i}}$ and $\mathbf{G}_{i}$ denote the rendered and ground-truth geometric feature maps, respectively.
L134: Second, we refine the visual fidelity by optimizing the visual appearance attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{vis}}$, while freezing the geometric attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{geo}}$ to preserve the collision proxies. Following the standard 3DGS protocol [cite61†21 ], the objective combines L1 and D-SSIM losss [cite95†52 ]:
L135:  | $$\min_{\mathcal{G}_{\text{vis}}}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\text{vis}}:=\sum\nolimits_{i}((1-\lambda)\|\hat{\mathbf{I}}_{i}-\mathbf{I}_{i}\|_{1}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}}(\hat{\mathbf{I}}_{i},\mathbf{I}_{i}))$$  |  | (3)
L136: 
L137: where $\lambda$ is a weighting factor, and $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_{i}$ is the rendered image.
L138: These modifications yield a unified SG-GS representation that ensures both visual accuracy and geometric robustness. By differentiably extracting Gaussian geometry parameters $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}$ as explicit collision geometries, we transform Gaussians from mere rendering kernels into active participants in physical simulation. This ensures strict alignment between the visual appearance and the physical model, realizing a “what you see is what you get” framework.
L139: ### III-D Stage II: Phys-Geo Refinement
L140: With the optimized Gaussian $\mathcal{G}^{*}$, we next optimize the physical parameters $\bm{\theta}$ and fine-tune geometric attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}$, based on the gradient flow from visual observations. To fully represent the dynamics in Eq.(cite93†1 ) by a 3D rigid-body simulation system, we define the environment state as $\mathbf{x}_{t}=\{\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{v}_{t}\}$, representing the system’s generalized coordinates and velocity of all objects at time $t$.
L141: Furthermore, the physical properties that govern the state transitions are incorporated into the system learnable physical parameters $\bm{\theta}=($ $\mathbf{M}$, $\mu$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{D})$, where $\mathbf{M}$, $\mu$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{D}$ denotes the mass-inertia matrix, friction coefficient, stiffness, and damping coefficient, respectively.
L142: In this work, We jointly optimize its intrinsic physical properties and geometrical attributes by back-propagating through the following three core processes: a differentiable collision detection based on spherical Gaussians, an explicit contact dynamics model, and a differentiable 3DGS rendering.
L143: #### III-D 1 Differentiable collision detection.
L144: Leveraging the current predicted states $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ and initialized geometric attributes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}^{*}$ which represent the collision geometry as a union of $n$ spherical primitives. For each primitive $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, its center $\mathbf{c}_{i}$ is derived from the Gaussian’s position, and its radius is defined as $r_{i}=2s$ (twice the scale radius is a reasonably conservative value).
L145: At this point, we can use a simple formula to represent the shortest distance from the query point $\mathbf{p}$ to the Gaussian collision geometry:
L146:  | $$\phi_{\mathcal{G}_{\text{geo}}}(\mathbf{p})=\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\left(\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{c}_{i}\|_{2}-r_{i}\right).$$  |
L147: 
L148: To ensure differentiability, we first approximate the shortest distances using the LogSumExp (LSE) smoothed minimum:
L149: 
L150:  | $$\phi_{\text{soft}}(\mathbf{p})\approx-\frac{1}{\beta}\log( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \exp\left( -\beta(\| \queryP- \cen_i \|_2 - r_i) \right) ).$$  |
L151: where $\beta$ is the smoothing factor. However, applying the above formula to calculate the distance inside an object often reflects the proximity to the nearest individual sphere rather than the object’s actual surface, leading to inaccurate penetration estimates. To address this, a significant negative penalty is assigned to ensure a robust correction of inter-penetration when the distance is negative.
L152: To preserve differentiability for end-to-end learning, we design a smoothing transition with the Sigmoid function $\sigma(\cdot)$:
L153:  | $$\phi(\mathbf{p})\approx\sigma(\gamma\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{p}))\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{p})+\left(1-\sigma(\gamma\cdot\phi_{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{p}))\right)\cdot(-\delta),$$  |
L154: 
L155: where $\gamma$ controls how sharp $\sigma$ is, and $\delta$ is the positive constant penalty used for negative distances inside-object cases. Using the calculated distance $\phi(\mathbf{p})$, the nearest surface point $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{c}}$ is obtained via gradient-based projection:
L156:  | $$\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}-\phi(\mathbf{p})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\phi(\mathbf{p})/\|\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\phi(\mathbf{p})\|_{2},$$  |
L157: 
L158: with the normalized gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\phi(\mathbf{p})$ being the surface normal. Based on this collision information, the contact Jacobian can be computed based on rigid body kinematics [cite96†53 ].
L159: #### III-D 2 Differentiable complementarity-free contact dynamics.
L160: 
L161: We adopt the full-dynamic complementarity-free contact model [cite63†23 ] for contact-rich scenarios, which is given by:
L162:  | $\displaystyle$  | $\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{t+1}=\overbrace{(\mathbf{v}_{t}+h\mathbf{M}^{-1}\bm{\tau}(\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{v}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t}))}^{:=\mathbf{b}_{t}}+h\mathbf{M}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{\top}_{t}\bm{\lambda}_{t}$  |
L163:  |  | $\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{t}:=\softplus\Big(\underbrace{-\mathbf{K}\big(h\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}\mathbf{b}_{t}+\tilde{\bm{\phi}}_{t}\big)}_{\text{stiffness of dual cone}}\underbrace{-\mathbf{D}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}\mathbf{b}_{t})}_{\text{damping of dual cone}}\Big),$  |
L164: Here, $\mathbf{M}$ represents the generalized mass matrix of the rigid body. The force vector $\bm{\tau}(\mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{v}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})$ is the sum of non-contact forces in the system, including actuation force, gravity, and Coriolis forces. $\tilde{\bm{\phi}}_{t}$ is the signed collision distance of each collision pair. $\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t}(\mu)$ is the Jacobian of the faces of the polyhedral dual cone (of the friction cones) at each collision pair.
L165: These are computed from the raw collision information ($\mathbf{J}_{t}$, ${\bm{\phi}}_{t}$) and frictional coefficient $\mu$ (See Appendix for detailed form). Instead of iteratively solving the contact constraint, the complementarity-free contact model approximates the contact force $\mathbf{\lambda}$ based on an impedance mechanism defined on the dual cones, with $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ representing the learnable stiffness and damping coefficients.
L166: This leads to a closed-form solution for the contact forces $\bm{\lambda}_{t}$ on the dual cones of the Coulomb friction cones. Moreover, different contact modes, e.g., sticking, sliding, and separation, can be captured by different penetration regimes across facets of the dual cones, and Coulomb friction laws are automatically satisfied.
L167: With the computed $\mathbf{v}_{t+1}$, the system’s next generalized position $\mathbf{q}_{t+1}$ can be obtained by $\mathbf{q}_{t+1}{=}\mathbf{q}_{t}\oplus h\mathbf{v}_{t+1}$, where $\oplus$ stands for the integration of system pose (position, orientation) with velocities.
L168: #### III-D 3 3DGS rendering
L169: Up to now, we have a differentiable simulator $\mathbf{f}$, which helped us successfully construct the state transition function in Eq.(cite93†1 ). Using the current predicted state $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}$ and action $\mathbf{a}_{t}$, we can explicitly obtain the next predicted state $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}$, thus creating the computational graph at the state space. In order to perform optimization in the image space, we need to map the system states to that space.
L170: This can be done by transforming all Gaussians based on forward kinematics with the updated state $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}$, which can be written as
L171:  | $$\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\mathrm{TF}}(\hat{\mathbf{q}})=\mathbb{FK}(\mathcal{G}^{*},\hat{\mathbf{q}}).$$  |  | (4)
L172: 
L173: where $\mathbb{FK}$ is the forward kinematics transformation that applies a rigid body transformation to the properties of all Gaussians in the scene based on the current state $\hat{\mathbf{q}}$, obtaining the Gaussians in the current predicted state. Then, based on the camera pose $\mathbf{T}_{cw}$ and the 3DGS renderer, we implement the mapping from the state to the image by
L174:  | $$\hat{\mathbf{I}}=\mathbb{R}(\mathcal{G}^{*}_{\mathrm{TF}},\mathbf{T}_{cw}).$$  |  | (5)
L175: #### III-D 4 End-to-End Gradient Flow
L176: At this point, we have presented all of the components in our ContactGaussian-WM framework. This design allows for the end-to-end optimization of Eq. (cite93†1 ). Gradients from the image loss are backpropagated through the renderer $\mathbb{R}$ to the state, and subsequently propagated through the differentiable simulator $\mathbf{f}$ to optimize the physical parameters $\bm{\theta}$ and geometric parameters $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{geo}}$, which are collectively denoted as $\Theta$.
L177: Mathematically, the gradient flow representing the backpropagation process through time can be written as follows:
L178: 
L179:  | $$\frac{\differential\mathcal{L}}{\differential\Theta}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{\differential\mathcal{L}}{\differential\hat{\mathbf{I}}}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{I}}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}\frac{\differential\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}{\differential\Theta},$$  |  | (6)
L180: where $\frac{\differential\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{(i)}}{\differential\Theta}$ is recursively computed via the differentiable simulator $\mathbf{f}$:
L181: 
L182:  | $$\frac{\differential\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{(i)}}{\differential\Theta}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\Theta}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}^{(i)}}\frac{\differential\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}^{(i)}}{\differential\Theta}.$$  |  | (7)
L183: Simultaneously optimizing the physical process and visually aligned images presents significant ambiguity, and images in motion are too blurry to adequately optimize our initial Gaussian; thus, as the formulation explicitly describes, the image loss does not update the geometry parameters in Gaussians directly through the rendering layer. Instead, these parameters are fine-tuned exclusively through the physical collision interactions propagated from the differentiable simulator.

L200: ### IV-A Evaluations in simulation
L201: 
L202: Experimental Setup: We initialized the experiment by converting the object’s precise mesh into a 3DGS representation using our SG-GS method. Two interaction scenarios:
L203: 
L204:   * •
L205: 
L206: Push-slide-settle: various objects pushed by a virtual hand with images, ground-truth trajectory, and end-effector velocity being recorded.
L207: 
L208:   * •
L209: 
L210: Fall-and-rebound: the complete free-fall and rebound process of various objects with images and ground-truth trajectories being recorded.
L211: are designed within MuJoCo [cite51†11 ] to generate trajectories along with their RGB renderings. These two scenarios represent two distinct modes of motion: the former involves quasi-dynamic, continuous contact, while the latter features high-dynamic, discrete contact. To verify the framework’s generalization across various geometries and physical properties, we employ a diverse set of objects (e.g., Camera, Stanford Bunny, Piggy Bank, and Rubber Duck).
L212: The model is trained on a short interaction trajectory and evaluated across multiple unseen test sets. Specifically, for the falling scenario, the objects are released from various initial poses and positions; for the pushing scenario, the manipulator operates at different end-effector velocities. To assess long-horizon prediction stability, the test sequences are notably longer than those used during training, while maintaining a consistent sampling rate.
L213: Experimental metrics: Our evaluation focuses on the model’s learning capability and predictive accuracy in an open-loop manner (i.e., only the true state of the first frame is provided). To assess learning performance, we track the PSNR of the rendered images during the training to monitor the model’s ability to fit training data. Furthermore, we quantify predictive accuracy by averaging the translation and rotation errors between the ground-truth and predicted poses across the entire test set.
L214: Baseline methods: We compare our proposed world model against various existing baselines, categorized as follows:
L215: Data-driven world models. We select DreamerV3 [cite47†7 ] as a representative baseline for this category. As a general-purpose world model, DreamerV3 learns latent dynamics purely from visual observations but typically requires substantial data to converge. We train it using a dataset of 100 trajectories that are distinct from the test set, at the same time, we also follow its closed-loop training mode (conditioning on ground-truth states at every time step).
L216: Physics-informed world models. We select two representative approaches to cover the spectrum of physics-grounded optimization. The first is essentially a gradient-free system identification baseline. We integrate the standard MuJoCo simulator with our 3DGS renderer $\mathbb{R}$ and employ the gradient-free optimization strategy CEM from TwinTrack [cite97†54 ] to minimize L1 image error, which we call it CEM+MuJoCo+$\mathbb{R}$ in the sequel.
L217: The second approach is PIN-WM [cite57†17 ], representing differentiable physics-based methods, which constructs a physical-grounded world model with Linear Complementarity Problems (LCP) and utilizes implicit differentiation for efficient gradient-based optimization.
L218: Notably, we provide realistic meshes as collision geometry for CEM+MuJoCo+$\mathbb{R}$ and PIN-WM, and utilize a high-fidelity physics simulator for accurate collision detection. We also trained high-quality 2DGS objects for PIN-WM to prevent rendering artifacts from becoming a performance bottleneck. For a fair comparison, we used the official implementations with default hyperparameters for PIN-WM and DreamerV3.
L219: For CEM+MuJoCo+$\mathbb{R}$, we implemented 100 sets of parallel parameters using MJX [cite51†11 ], with the rest of the settings remaining the same as TwinTrack. All physics-grounded baselines are trained and evaluated on the same trajectory dataset as our proposed model, with each method trained until convergence to report its optimal performance.
L220: Comparisons on training performance: Fig. cite98†3 presents the learning performance curves. It can be observed that all four models converge in both scenarios. As DreamerV3 employs a closed-form training paradigm, it is reasonable that its PSNR significantly outperforms the other models.
L221: However, its convergence in the Fall-and-rebound scenario is slightly lower than in the Push-slide-settle scenario due to the higher visual complexity of the former, despite its lack of explicit consideration of contact dynamics.
L222: For the three physics-based models, learning performance is comparable in the Push-slide-settle scenario. The reason is that this quasi-dynamic task provides smooth and continuous gradients, and the motion trajectories are predominantly governed by the friction coefficient. Such a constrained search space and stable constraints allow both the gradient-free CEM+MuJoCo+$\mathbb{R}$ and the gradient-based methods (PIN-WM and ours) to easily locate the optimal solutions.
L223: In contrast, the Fall-and-rebound scenario presents a greater challenge. Collisions occur only within a few frames, providing limited useful information. Furthermore, discrete contacts lead to gradient discontinuities. PIN-WM, which already struggles with discrete and inaccurate gradients, fails to reach a satisfactory convergence under these conditions. For CEM+MuJoCo+$\mathbb{R}$, the parallel search allows them to fit the image loss very well.
L224: In comparison, our model achieves a more stable and accurate gradient flow. By effectively utilizing sparse and discontinuous collision information, our framework can identify precise gradient directions with less computational resources, ultimately converging to a better value.
L225: Comparisons on test performance: To evaluate the predictive accuracy of the four models, we record the cumulative translation and orientation errors on the test set (Tables cite99†I and  cite100†II ), with qualitative results being presented in the Appendix. Based on the performance, we draw the following conclusions:
L226: 1) The purely data-driven DreamerV3 suffers from a catastrophic decline in performance when generalizing to novel states and actions, failing to maintain essential scene consistency or physical plausibility during long-horizon forecasting.
L227: 2) Although CEM+MuJoCo+$\mathbb{R}$ performs well in both Push-slide-settle and Fall-and-rebound scenarios, its generalization performance drops significantly in highly dynamic and discontinuous fall scenarios, indicating that the model is unable to capture the underlying physical laws in these scenarios when the scene changes greatly, and instead overfits the training loss.
L228: 3) Frameworks like PIN-WM, which rely on implicit function differentiation and non-differentiable collision detection, are ill-suited for high-dynamic scenarios. The inherent inaccuracy and lack of smoothness in their gradient estimation make it difficult to derive reliable update directions from sparse and highly dynamic signals. Consequently, such methods may function in quasi-dynamic scenarios with continuous contacts (e.g., pushing).
L229: 4) In contrast, our proposed ContactGaussian-WM framework enables end-to-end differentiable learning. This allows the model to accurately capture underlying physical laws from images, even under challenging conditions such as inaccurate geometry, imperfect dynamics, sparse contact information, and complex collision interactions. Consequently, our framework achieves stable generalization performance.
