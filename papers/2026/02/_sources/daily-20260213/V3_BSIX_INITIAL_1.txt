L49: # VESPO: Variational Sequence-Level Soft Policy Optimization
L50: for Stable Off-Policy LLM Training
L51: 
L52: Guobin Shen Affiliation: Xiaohongshu Inc Correspondence to: floyed_shen@outlook.com    Chenxiao Zhao Affiliation: Xiaohongshu Inc    Xiang Cheng Affiliation: Xiaohongshu Inc    Lei Huang Affiliation: Xiaohongshu Inc    Xing Yu Affiliation: Xiaohongshu Inc Correspondence to: yuanshan2@xiaohongshu.com
L53: ###### Abstract
L54: Training stability remains a central challenge in reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs). Policy staleness, asynchronous training, and mismatches between training and inference engines all cause the behavior policy to diverge from the current policy, risking training collapse.
L55: Importance sampling provides a principled correction for this distribution shift but suffers from high variance; existing remedies such as token-level clipping and sequence-level normalization lack a unified theoretical foundation. We propose V ariational s E quence-level S oft P olicy O ptimization (VESPO).
L56: By incorporating variance reduction into a variational formulation over proposal distributions, VESPO derives a closed-form reshaping kernel that operates directly on sequence-level importance weights without length normalization. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that VESPO maintains stable training under staleness ratios up to $64\times$ and fully asynchronous execution, and delivers consistent gains across both dense and Mixture-of-Experts models.
L57: Code is available at cite37†https://github.com/FloyedShen/VESPO†github.com .
L58: ## 1 Introduction
L59: 
L60: Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a key technique for tackling complex problem-solving tasks with large language models (LLMs), enabling capabilities such as multi-step mathematical reasoning and code generation (cite38†OpenAI, 2024 ; cite39†Anthropic, 2025 ; cite40†DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ; cite41†Yang et al., 2025 ).
L61: In practice, off-policy updates arise naturally in RL pipelines for LLMs. A common source is that systems split large rollout batches into mini-batches for sequential updates (cite41†Yang et al., 2025 ), causing later batches to become stale relative to the evolving policy. Asynchronous systems (cite42†Fu et al., 2025 ; cite43†Zhong et al., 2025 ; cite44†Noukhovitch et al., 2025 ) amplify this by decoupling rollout from training entirely.
L62: Training-inference mismatches introduce further discrepancies, especially in MoE models where routing decisions compound through layers. To stabilize training under such distribution mismatch, existing works adopt truncated importance sampling (TIS) (cite45†Liu et al., 2025 ), mask out off-policy samples from training (cite46†Hu, 2025 ), or replay expert routing for target policy inference (cite47†Zheng et al., 2025b ; cite48†Ma et al., 2025 ).
L63: While PPO (cite49†Schulman et al., 2017 ) enforces trust region constraints via clipping, it does not directly address the variance challenge of sequence-level importance sampling (IS).
L64: Figure 1: Left: VESPO reformulates IS weight reshaping as finding a proposal $Q^{*}$ that balances proximity to $\mu$ and $\pi$ under a variance constraint. Right: Training reward (gbs/mbs=4) on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base.
L65: Existing methods address this challenge through various importance weight transformations. Most operate at the token level: GRPO (cite50†Shao et al., 2024 ; cite40†DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ) applies PPO-style clipping to per-token ratios; other methods such as SAPO (cite51†Gao et al., 2025 ) design heuristic transformations for off-policy importance weights (cite52†Xi et al., 2025 ; cite53†Zheng et al., 2025c ; cite54†Dwyer et al., 2025 ; cite55†Roux et al., 2025 ; cite56†Hu et al., 2025 ).
L66: However, token-level transformations are a compromise to avoid the multiplicative variance explosion of sequence-level weights, and have been shown to be merely a first-order approximation to their sequence-level counterparts (cite47†Zheng et al., 2025b ). Sequence-level approaches (cite47†Zheng et al., 2025b ; cite57†Zhao et al., 2025 ) introduce length normalization to control variance, but this normalization makes the IS estimator biased; hard clipping is still required on top.
L67: Despite these efforts, principled guidance for designing importance weight transformations remains limited.
L68: We propose V ariational s E quence-level S oft P olicy O ptimization (VESPO), which takes a fundamentally different approach: rather than designing reshaping heuristics, we explicitly incorporate variance reduction for off-policy importance sampling into a variational formulation, yielding a principled closed-form solution (cite58†Figure 1 ).
L69: Additionally, we find that the resulting transformation is particularly friendly to sequence-level optimization: unlike previous methods that rely on length normalization to avoid variance explosion, VESPO operates directly on sequence-level importance weights without approximation or normalization.
L70: Our contributions are summarized as follows:
L71: 
L72:   * •
L73: 
L74: We explicitly incorporate variance reduction into a variational formulation for importance weight reshaping and derive a principled closed-form solution.
L75: 
L76:   * •
L77: 
L78: The resulting algorithm, VESPO, operates directly on sequence-level importance weights, preserving inter-token dependencies while remaining free from length-dependent bias.
L79: 
L80:   * •
L81: Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that VESPO remains stable under staleness ratios up to 64$\times$ and in fully asynchronous training, delivering consistent improvements across both dense and MoE architectures.
L82: ## 2 Preliminaries
L83: 
L84: Notation. We consider an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ as a policy $\pi_{\theta}$. Let $x$ denote a query sampled from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$. In off-policy settings, responses $y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})$ are sampled from a behavior policy $\mu$ (e.g., an earlier checkpoint or a different inference engine). The likelihood of generating $y$ given $x$ factorizes as:
L85: 
L86:  | $$\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}).$$  |  | (1)
L87: We write $\tau=(x,y)$ for a query-response pair, and $R(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}$ for the sequence-level reward assigned to the complete response.
L88: 
L89: Policy Gradient with Off-Policy Correction. The goal is to maximize the expected reward under the current policy:
L90: 
L91:  | $$\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\bigl[R(\tau)\bigr].$$  |  | (2)
L92: When samples are drawn from a behavior policy $\mu$ instead, importance sampling provides an unbiased correction. Taking the gradient yields the off-policy policy gradient:
L93: 
L94:  | $$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[W(\tau)\cdot R(\tau)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\tau)\right],$$  |  | (3)
L95: where $W(\tau)=\pi_{\theta}(\tau)/\mu(\tau)$ is the importance weight. This classical policy gradient view (cite59†Sutton et al., 1999 ) reveals that the importance weight $W$ serves as a gradient weighting factor: it determines how much each sample contributes to the parameter update. More generally, any modification to the importance weight can be understood as defining a reshaping function $\phi(W)$ that reweights the gradient:
L96:  | $$\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[\phi(W(\tau))\cdot R(\tau)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\tau)\right].$$  |  | (4)
L97: 
L98: This gradient-centric view will be central to our analysis: the practical effect of any weight transformation must ultimately be understood through how it reweights the policy gradient.
L99: The Variance Challenge of Sequence-Level IS. Expanding the importance weight in terms of token-level ratios reveals a fundamental structural tension. Define the token-level importance ratio as $\rho_{t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{\mu(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}$. The sequence-level weight is then a product:
L100: 
L101:  | $$W(\tau)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{t},$$  |  | (5)
L102: 
L103: while the log-policy gradient is a sum:
L104:  | $$\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}).$$  |  | (6)
L105: This product-sum structure creates a tension: the gradient contribution of each token is weighted by a global factor $W(\tau)$ that compounds across all $T$ positions. Even small per-token deviations accumulate multiplicatively, causing $W(\tau)$ to exhibit extreme values for long sequences. The variance of $W$ grows exponentially with $T$, rendering naive importance sampling impractical.
L106: To tame this variance, existing methods define specific reshaping functions $\phi$ that modify the gradient weighting. GRPO (cite50†Shao et al., 2024 ) operates at the token level with a PPO-style clipped surrogate. From the gradient perspective, the effective weight function depends on the sign of the advantage $A$:
L107:  | $$\phi_{\text{GRPO}}(\rho_{t};A)=\begin{cases}\rho_{t},&\text{if }A>0\text{ and }\rho_{t}\leq 1{+}\varepsilon,\\
L108: \rho_{t},&\text{if }A<0\text{ and }\rho_{t}\geq 1{-}\varepsilon,\\
L109: 0,&\text{otherwise (gradient zeroed)}.\end{cases}$$  |  | (7)
L110: 
L111: This breaks the product structure and treats each token update independently, yielding only a first-order approximation (cite60†Zheng et al., 2025a ).
L112: GSPO (cite47†Zheng et al., 2025b ) operates at the sequence level, defining the gradient weight as the geometric mean of token-level ratios (i.e., normalizing by sequence length):
L113: 
L114:  | $$\phi_{\text{GSPO}}(W)=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{t}\right)^{\!1/T}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\rho_{t}\right),$$  |  | (8)
L115: followed by a clipping mechanism similar to GRPO. This normalization introduces a length-dependent bias: the implicit proposal distribution varies with $T$, and sequences with identical per-token statistics but different lengths receive identical weights despite having different true importance weights (see cite30†Appendix C for a formal analysis).
L116: These approaches all define $\phi$ heuristically; the question of what constitutes a principled choice of $\phi$ motivates the variational framework we develop next.
L117: ## 3 VESPO: Variational Sequence-Level Soft Policy Optimization
L118: 
L119: We develop a principled framework for designing importance weight transformations. We first show that any reshaping function $\phi(W)$ implicitly defines a proposal distribution, then formulate the design of $\phi$ as a variational problem with variance constraints, and finally derive a closed-form solution.
L120: ### 3.1 Weight Reshaping as Measure Change
L121: 
L122: Standard importance sampling performs an unbiased measure change $\mu\to\pi$, while any transformation $\phi(W)$ induces a different measure change $\mu\to Q$ for some implicit proposal $Q$. We now formalize this perspective. For any function $G(\tau)$:
L123: 
L124:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[\phi(W(\tau))\cdot G(\tau)\bigr]=Z\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim Q}\bigl[G(\tau)\bigr],$$  |  | (9)
L125: where $Z=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\phi(W)]$ is a normalization constant, and $Q$ is defined by
L126: 
L127:  | $$Q(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}\mu(\tau)\cdot\phi(W(\tau)).$$  |  | (10)
L128: 
L129: The reshaped gradient (cite61†Equation 4 ) is thus equivalent, up to the scalar $Z$, to an on-policy gradient under the proposal distribution $Q$.
L130: This is the key insight: any sequence-level reshaping function $\phi(W)$ implicitly defines a proposal distribution $Q$. This perspective provides a unified lens to analyze existing importance weight transformations (see cite23†Appendix A for detailed analysis of specific methods). Rather than handcrafting $\phi$ directly, we can specify desirable properties of the proposal $Q$ and derive the corresponding $\phi$.
L131: A good proposal should remain close to $\mu$ for sampling efficiency, incorporate $\pi$ to guide optimization, and control variance. The following subsections formalize these desiderata as a variational objective and derive a closed-form $\phi^{*}$.
L132: ### 3.2 Variational Objective
L133: 
L134: Dual Proximity. We seek a proposal $Q$ that remains close to $\mu$ for sample efficiency, while also approaching $\pi$ to reduce estimation bias. We formalize this via a mixed KL objective that encourages $Q$ to lie between $\mu$ and $\pi$ in the space of distributions:
L135: 
L136:  | $$\mathcal{J}(Q)=(1-\alpha)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\mu)+\alpha\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\pi),$$  |  | (11)
L137: where $\alpha$ controls the trade-off. The first term penalizes deviation from the sampling distribution, ensuring that samples from $\mu$ remain informative for estimating expectations under $Q$. The second term penalizes deviation from the target policy, reducing bias in the gradient estimate.
L138: Variance Constraint. Proximity alone is insufficient: finite sample sizes demand variance control. In importance sampling, the variance of the estimator scales with the second moment $\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[W^{2}]$, a classical result that also underlies the effective sample size (ESS) diagnostic.
L139: 
L140: Under the measure-change view, this second moment can be related to an expectation under $Q$. By cite62†Equation 10 , we have $Q(\tau)\propto\mu(\tau)\phi(W(\tau))$, so
L141:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim Q}[W(\tau)]\propto\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}[\phi(W)\cdot W].$$  |  | (12)
L142: 
L143: When $\phi(W)\approx W$ (approaching unbiased IS), this recovers $\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[W^{2}]$. Bounding $\mathbb{E}_{Q}[W]$ therefore provides a principled way to control the variance induced by the measure change:
L144: 
L145:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim Q}[W(\tau)]\leq C.$$  |  | (13)
L146: 
L147: Constrained Optimization. Combining the dual-proximity objective with the variance constraint:
L148:  | $\displaystyle\min_{Q}\;$  | $\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\mu)+\alpha\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\pi)$  |
L149:  | s.t.   | $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[W]\leq C,\quad\textstyle\int Q=1.$  |  | (14)
L150: 
L151: Introducing Lagrange multipliers $\lambda\geq 0$ for the moment constraint and $\gamma$ for normalization, we obtain the Lagrangian:
L152:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Q,\lambda,\gamma)=\;$  | $\displaystyle(1{-}\alpha)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\mu)+\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|\pi)$  |
L153:  |  | $\displaystyle+\lambda\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{Q}[W]{-}C\bigr)+\gamma\bigl(\textstyle\int Q{-}1\bigr).$  |  | (15)
L154: ### 3.3 Closed-Form Solution
L155: 
L156: Taking the functional derivative $\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta Q}=0$ yields (see cite28†Appendix B ):
L157: 
L158:  | $$Q^{*}(\tau)\propto\mu(\tau)^{1-\alpha}\,\pi(\tau)^{\alpha}\,\exp(-\lambda W(\tau)).$$  |  | (16)
L159: 
L160: Using $W=\pi/\mu$, this simplifies to $Q^{*}(\tau)\propto\mu(\tau)\cdot W(\tau)^{\alpha}\cdot\exp(-\lambda W(\tau))$. Comparing with cite62†Equation 10 , we identify the reshaping function:
L161: 
L162:  | $$\phi(W)=W^{\alpha}\cdot\exp(-\lambda W).$$  |  | (17)
L163: This kernel has two components: the power term $W^{\alpha}$ and the exponential term $\exp(-\lambda W)$ for soft suppression. It is smooth and differentiable everywhere, avoiding the discontinuities of hard clipping.
L164: Surrogate Objective Interpretation. Recall that our goal is to maximize the expected reward $\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R(\tau)]$ (cite63†Equation 2 ). With off-policy samples from $\mu$, the reshaped gradient (cite61†Equation 4 ) can be viewed as optimizing a surrogate objective. Observe that $\nabla_{\theta}W=W\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}$, so the gradient estimator can be rewritten as
L165:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}[\phi(W)\cdot A\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[\tfrac{\phi(W)}{W}\cdot A\cdot\nabla W\bigr].$$  |  | (18)
L166: 
L167: Defining $f(W)$ such that $f^{\prime}(W)=\phi(W)/W$, the right-hand side equals $\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}[f(W)\cdot A]$, since $A(\tau)$ does not depend on $\theta$. Thus VESPO implicitly maximizes a surrogate objective:
L168:  | $$\mathcal{J}_{\text{VESPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[f(W(\theta))\cdot A(\tau)\bigr].$$  |  | (19)
L169: 
L170: For $\phi(W)=W^{\alpha}\exp(-\lambda W)$, we have $f^{\prime}(W)=W^{\alpha-1}\exp(-\lambda W)$, which integrates to the lower incomplete gamma function:
L171: 
L172:  | $$f(W)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\,\gamma(\alpha,\lambda W),\quad\text{where }\gamma(a,x)=\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}e^{-t}\,dt.$$  |  | (20)
L173: This form is smooth and infinitely differentiable, and saturates as $W\to\infty$, providing a principled soft alternative to hard clipping.
L174: 
L175: Figure 2: Surrogate objectives $f(w)$ (top) and gradient scaling factors $\phi(w)=w\cdot f^{\prime}(w)$ (bottom) for positive and negative advantages. Hard clipping zeros $\phi$ abruptly at the boundary; VESPO peaks near $w{=}1$ and decays smoothly.
L176: ### 3.4 The VESPO Algorithm
L177: In practice, we use the shifted form $\phi(W)=W^{c_{1}}\exp(c_{2}(1-W))$ to ensure $\phi(1)=1$, so that on-policy samples receive unit weight and the update magnitude aligns with standard on-policy methods under the same learning rate. We adopt different hyperparameters $(c_{1},c_{2})$ for positive and negative advantages, mirroring the asymmetric clipping in PPO.
L178: Recent work (cite64†Tang et al., 2025 ) has shown that positive and negative samples exhibit different gradient dynamics during training; accordingly, we apply stronger suppression for $A<0$ with $W<1$ to prevent excessive penalization of samples the policy already dislikes, as shown in cite65†Figure 2 . We treat $(c_{1},c_{2})$ as tunable hyperparameters, which allows flexibility beyond the specific values implied by the variational derivation.
L179: Substituting the shifted kernel into cite61†Equation 4 , the VESPO gradient estimator becomes:
L180:  | $\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{J}_{\text{VESPO}}=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\bigl[$  | $\displaystyle W^{c_{1}}\exp(c_{2}(1{-}W))$  |
L181:  |  | $\displaystyle\cdot A(\tau)\cdot\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}(\tau)\bigr],$  |  | (21)
L182: 
L183: where $A(\tau)=R(\tau)-b$ is the advantage with baseline $b$ (computed as the mean reward within each prompt group, following GRPO (cite50†Shao et al., 2024 )).
L184: Connection to Existing Methods. The kernel exhibits adaptive behavior: when $W\approx 1$, $\phi(W)\approx 1$ and gradients are preserved; when $W\gg 1$, the exponential term decays rapidly; when $W\ll 1$, the power term naturally down-weights unlikely samples. Compared to hard clipping, VESPO provides a smooth approximation that gradually attenuates extreme weights (cite65†Figure 2 ).
L185: Compared to existing soft transformations that operate at the token level, VESPO applies directly to sequence-level importance weights without length normalization, and the factorized form offers flexible control: $c_{1}$ primarily governs behavior for $W<1$, while $c_{2}$ controls the decay for $W>1$.
L186: Implementation. We implement VESPO in REINFORCE style: the reshaping weight $\phi(W)$ is detached from the computation graph and serves purely as a gradient scaling coefficient. The sequence-level importance weight is computed in log-space for numerical stability:
L187: 
L188:  | $$\log W=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})-\log\mu(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})\bigr).$$  |  | (22)
L189: The reshaping $c_{1}\log W+c_{2}(1-W)$ is also computed in log-space; exponentiation is performed only at the final step, avoiding overflow from extreme importance weights. This requires storing only the per-token log-probabilities under both $\pi$ and $\mu$, with no additional memory overhead. The complete pseudocode is provided in cite34†Appendix E .
L190: ## 4 Experiments
L191: We evaluate VESPO on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, focusing on practical sources of off-policy distribution shift: (1) policy staleness from batched rollouts, where later mini-batches are updated using samples from an outdated policy, including a fully asynchronous setting where rollout and training run on separate nodes; and (2) train-inference mismatch, where different implementations between training and inference engines produce different outputs for the same input—an effect exacerbated in MoE models due to routing inconsistencies.
L192: ### 4.1 Experimental Setup
L193: Training. All experiments are conducted on 32 NVIDIA H20 GPUs using the veRL framework (cite66†Sheng et al., 2024 ), with vLLM 0.11.0 (cite67†Kwon et al., 2023 ) for inference and FSDP (cite68†Zhao et al., 2023 ) for training dense models (Megatron (cite69†Shoeybi et al., 2019 ) for MoE). The training dataset is the unfiltered version of DAPO-Math (cite70†Yu et al., 2025 ), which provides sufficient data to support extended training.
L194: For each query, we sample 8 responses and compute rewards using Math-Verify (cite71†Hugging Face, 2025 ). We set the maximum context length to 16,384 tokens for both training and evaluation. We train for 1,500 gradient steps across all methods. See cite72†Table 3 for more details.
L195: Models. We evaluate on three model scales: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (cite73†Grattafiori et al., 2024 ), Qwen3-8B-Base, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base (cite41†Yang et al., 2025 ). The MoE architecture amplifies train-inference mismatch due to routing decisions that compound across layers.
L196: Evaluation. We evaluate on four mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023, and MATH-500 (cite74†Hendrycks et al., 2021 ). We report avg@$k$ accuracy (average over $k$ sampled responses per problem), with $k=32$ for AIME 2024/2025, $k=16$ for AMC 2023, and $k=4$ for MATH-500. We select the best checkpoint based on the average performance across all four benchmarks.
L197: Baselines. We compare against three representative methods: GRPO (cite50†Shao et al., 2024 ), which applies PPO-style clipping at the token level with clip ratios $(0.2,0.28)$ following DAPO (cite70†Yu et al., 2025 ); GSPO (cite47†Zheng et al., 2025b ), which operates at the sequence level with $1/T$ length normalization and clip ratios $(3\text{e-}4,4\text{e-}4)$; and SAPO (cite51†Gao et al., 2025 ), which uses soft adaptive gating with $\tau_{\text{pos}}=1.0$ and $\tau_{\text{neg}}=1.05$.
L198: For VESPO, we use $(c_{1},c_{2})=(2.0,3.0)$ for $A>0$ and $(c_{1},c_{2})=(3.0,2.0)$ for $A<0$.
L199: To simulate policy staleness, we fix the mini-batch size (mbs) at 256 and vary the global batch size (gbs) to control the degree of off-policy updates. With gbs/mbs $=N$, each rollout of gbs samples is split into $N$ mini-batches for sequential policy gradient updates. We measure progress in policy update steps to ensure all methods process the same amount of data. Our primary experiments use $N=8$, with ablations exploring $N\in\{4,8,16,32,64\}$.
L200: We additionally evaluate under a fully asynchronous setting where rollout and training run on separate node groups. The trainer synchronizes updated weights to the rollout engine every 4 local updates (analogous to gbs/mbs${=}4$ in the synchronous setting), and in-flight rollouts generated under stale parameters are preserved across synchronization boundaries rather than discarded (cite75†Zhou et al., 2025 ).
