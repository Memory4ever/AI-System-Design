L63: # Vulnerabilities in Partial TEE-Shielded LLM Inference with Precomputed Noise
L64: 
L65: CCS: Security and privacy Key management CCS: Security and privacy Cryptanalysis and other attacks CCS: Computing methodologies Natural language processing CCS: Security and privacy Domain-specific security and privacy architectures
L66: Abhishek Saini Affiliation: Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Piscataway, NJ, USA email: abhishek.saini@rutgers.edu , Haolin Jiang Affiliation: Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Piscataway, NJ, USA email: hj462@scarletmail.rutgers.edu and Hang Liu Affiliation: Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Piscataway, NJ, USA email: hang.liu@rutgers.edu
L67: ###### Abstract.
L68: The deployment of large language models (LLMs) on third-party devices requires new ways to protect model intellectual property. While Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) offer a promising solution, their performance limits can lead to a critical compromise: using a precomputed, static secret basis to accelerate cryptographic operations. We demonstrate that this mainstream design pattern introduces a classic cryptographic flaw, the reuse of secret keying material, into the system’s protocol.
L69: We prove its vulnerability with two distinct attacks: First, our attack on a model confidentiality system achieves a full confidentiality break by recovering its secret permutations and model weights. Second, our integrity attack completely bypasses the integrity checks of systems like Soter and TSQP.
L70: We demonstrate the practicality of our attacks against state-of-the-art LLMs, recovering a layer’s secrets from a LLaMA-3 8B model in about 6 minutes and showing the attack scales to compromise 405B-parameter LLMs across a variety of configurations.

L72: 
L73: Confidential Computing, Machine Learning Security, Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs), Large Language Models (LLMs), Cryptographic Attacks, Key Reuse
L74: ## 1. Introduction
L75: The initial wave of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been dominated by centralized, API-based services (cite51†OpenAI, 2025a ; cite52†Anthropic, 2025 ; cite53†Google, 2025 ). However, this paradigm is rapidly giving way to a new frontier: local deployment of models outside the provider’s direct control.
L76: This shift is not driven by a single factor, but by three distinct and essential requirements: First, enterprise users are increasingly running models on-premises to process sensitive, proprietary data, driven by concerns over exposing market secrets to competitors and the legal and compliance risks of sharing confidential information with third-party model providers (cite54†OpenAI, 2025b ; cite55†SentinelOne, 2025 ).
L77: Second, entire classes of mission-critical applications, such as autonomous driving and robotics, are non-negotiably edge-native; their need for real-time responsiveness and constant availability precludes any reliance on a remote server (cite56†Firoozi et al., 2023 ; cite57†Cui et al., 2024 ; cite58†Tesla, 2025 ; cite59†Karumbunathan, 2022 ).
L78: Finally, the proliferation of consumer devices like smartphones and smart assistants, now equipped with powerful AI accelerators, has created a demand for a new generation of responsive, always-on applications powered by local proprietary models (cite60†Apple, 2025 ; cite61†Gupta, 2023 ; cite62†Wilhelm, 2023 ).
L79: While each requirement is distinct, they converge on a single, critical security challenge: placing a multi-million dollar model, a core intellectual property asset, onto hardware not managed by the provider exposes it to unauthorized replication and reverse engineering (cite63†Sun et al., 2021 ).
L80: This tension has led to a clear call from the research community for a “middle path” that delivers the full benefits of local execution without sacrificing the model provider’s intellectual property (cite64†Huang et al., 2025 ; cite65†Cheng et al., 2025 ).
L81: One approach to securing inference relies on purely cryptographic methods like Homomorphic Encryption (HE), which allow computation on encrypted data. However, HE is often too slow for the demands of low-latency LLM inference (cite66†Gilad-Bachrach et al., 2016 ; cite67†Juvekar et al., 2018 ).
L82: Consequently, hardware-based solutions using Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) have emerged as a more practical alternative that offers a better balance of security and performance (cite68†Hanzlik et al., 2019 ; cite69†Ohrimenko et al., 2016 ). However, the memory and performance constraints of TEEs make running an entire state-of-the-art LLM within a secure enclave impractical (cite70†Lee et al., 2019 ; cite71†Kim et al., 2020 ).
L83: While recent confidential computing architectures like Intel TDX, AMD SEV, Arm CCA, and NVIDIA’s Confidential Computing are beginning to address these limitations, their practical adoption is hindered by limited availability or the persistent challenge of securing untrusted accelerators (cite72†Kaplan et al., 2021 ; cite73†Siby et al., 2024 ; cite74†Abdollahi et al., 2025 ; cite75†Dong and Wang, 2025 ; cite76†Zhu et al., 2024 ; cite77†Hunt et al., 2020 ; cite78†Vaswani et al., 2023 ; cite79†Volos et al., 2018 ; cite80†Chrapek et al., 2024 ).
L84: This has led to the mainstream paradigm of Partial TEE-Shielded Execution (PTSE), which offloads computationally intensive operations to powerful but untrusted hardware like Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), while the TEE retains a critical role in managing the overall secure execution of the model (cite81†Tramèr and Boneh, 2019 ; cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite85†Stoica et al., 2017 ).
L85: Early PTSE strategies focused on model partitioning, where the model is split, and only the most sensitive layers or components are executed inside the TEE (cite86†Mo et al., 2020 ; cite87†Liu et al., 2023 ; cite88†Schlögl and Böhme, 2020 ). However, this strategy is flawed as it offers incomplete IP protection and underutilizes the accelerator (cite89†Li et al., 2024b ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ).

L101: More recent PTSE approaches have evolved towards cryptographic obfuscation, which operates on a “lock-and-key” principle: The model is first obfuscated (locked) offline using a secret transformation, rendering it unusable by any party that does not possess the corresponding secret key. During online inference, the TEE’s role is twofold: (i) Model confidentiality. TEE guards the model’s secrets by securely holding the key needed to de-obfuscate computations from the GPU.
L102: A common approach to obfuscation in this domain is permutation-based locking, which secretly shuffles a model’s weights; the TEE then ensures the model only functions correctly by using the secret permutation key to properly order the data flowing into the locked model weights (cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite92†Yuan et al., 2024b ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite93†Xu and Fang, 2024 ; cite94†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ).
L103: Recent work has focused on scaling this approach to protect LLMs (cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite91†Refael et al., 2024 ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ). (ii) Computational integrity. The TEE serves to verify the integrity of computations offloaded to an untrusted hardware. This is a vital role, as an attacker with control over the host system could manipulate the GPU’s computations. Such tampering can degrade the model’s service quality, damaging the provider’s reputation and eroding user trust.
L104: More maliciously, even an unprivileged, co-located attacker could aim for targeted manipulations (cite96†Kim et al., 2014 ; cite97†Hong et al., 2019 ), altering the computation in subtle ways to bypass security filters or cause specific misclassifications that benefit them (cite98†Rakin et al., 2021 ; cite99†Zhao et al., 2019 ).
L105: To prevent this, systems like Soter (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ) and TSQP (cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ) leverage the TEE hardware to inject hidden fingerprints, known-answer challenges for which the TEE already knows the correct output, mixed in with real data. If the untrusted hardware returns a tampered result for these hidden checks, the integrity breach is detected, and the session can be determined as untrusted.
L106: To achieve these distinct security goals, a common architectural pattern has emerged across a wide range of systems: a three-step Mask-Offload-Restore protocol, which we detail and compare across several state-of-the-art systems in Table cite100†1 . The protocol proceeds as follows:

L120: The practical challenge arises because the TEE is responsible for more than just generating this randomness; it must also restore the original result by computing the precise effect of the noise. As we will demonstrate in Section cite11†3 , calculating this effect for truly random noise is computationally prohibitive. It would force the TEE to execute expensive On-the-Fly operations, like full matrix-vector multiplications, creating an unacceptable performance bottleneck.
L121: To circumvent this bottleneck, practical implementations are compelled to adopt a performance-driven shortcut: a precomputation-based pattern. At startup, the TEE generates and stores a small, fixed set of $K$ secret noise vectors and their corresponding effects, which we call the precomputed static basis. During online inference, all “random” noise is then efficiently generated by combining these precomputed static basis vectors using freshly sampled random coefficients.
L122: For TEE-based model confidentiality, this efficiency-driven design is a necessity to scale to LLMs, while for others, like the integrity-checking mechanism in Soter, TSQP, it is adopted as a tempting shortcut for efficiency.
L123: In this work, we demonstrate that this efficiency-driven reliance on a precomputed static basis is a fundamental security flaw. The key insight is that the random noise generated this way is not truly random. Instead, it is permanently confined to a predictable, low-dimensional ($K$-dimensional) subspace spanned by the static basis vectors.
L124: This creates a critical vulnerability that allows an attacker to aggregate information over multiple interactions to algebraically break the system’s security guarantees. This flaw is not an implementation bug or a side channel, but a systematic weakness inherent to the protocol’s design.
L125: We prove the severity of this flaw by developing attacks that compromise two distinct security guarantees: model confidentiality system and computational integrity framework. The success of our attacks on these two different real-world designs proves that the static basis is a versatile and fundamental vulnerability. Our primary contributions are:

L140: Distinction from existing closely related attacks. While other protocol-level Partial TEE-shielded execution attacks exist, they typically rely on statistical inference and require a known public model to succeed (cite95†Wang, 2025 ; cite94†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite101†Zhang et al., 2023 ). L142: ## 2. Background and Threat Model
L143: ### 2.1. TEE-based Model Confidentiality
L144: Many TEE-based model confidentiality schemes operate on a lock-and-key authorization principle to enable secure computation on untrusted hardware (cite102†Bai et al., 2025 ; cite93†Xu and Fang, 2024 ; cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite89†Li et al., 2024b ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ). In this paradigm, the model’s weights are first “locked” offline through an obfuscation technique, such as permutation, rendering them unusable without a corresponding secret key.
L145: This locked model is then deployed to the untrusted environment. The secret key, the permutation matrix in this case, is stored securely within the TEE. During inference, TEE uses the secret key to securely perform the secret transformation necessary for the correct functioning of the model.
L146: As a concrete example of this mechanism, Figure cite103†1 illustrates a single layer of the Llama architecture secured using TLG (cite83†Li et al., 2024a ). In the secured model, the weights are locked offline using layer-specific permutation matrices, $\pi_{l}$ and $\rho_{l}$. To an attacker, these locked weights appear random; they are unusable without the secret permutations, which are stored securely in the TEE.
L147: We denote secured activations with a prime (e.g., $x_{l}^{\prime}$) to distinguish them from their plaintext counterparts ($x_{l}$).
L148: The inference process works by applying corresponding permutations to the activations. An incoming activation $x^{\prime}_{l}$, already permuted by the previous layer ($x^{\prime}_{l}=x_{l}\pi_{l}$), is processed by the attention block. Due to the block’s structure, the permutations on the weights and activations cancel out at step , ensuring the intermediate outputs are correct (e.g., $Q^{\prime}=x^{\prime}_{l}\pi_{l}^{T}W_{q}=(x_{l}\pi_{l})\pi_{l}^{T}W_{q}=Q$).
L149: The computed values $Q^{\prime}$, $K^{\prime}$, $V^{\prime}$ are then combined to form the aggregated context, $s^{\prime}$ (), which then gets multiplied by the output projection matrix, $W_{o}\pi_{l}$ . This gives the output of the attention block, $o^{\prime}$. A residual connection is added to create $h^{\prime}$ (), the input to the feed-forward network. At this stage, $h^{\prime}=h\pi_{l}$ is permuted correctly for the first half of the feedforward network to compute $a^{\prime}$ .
L150: Since $h^{\prime}$ cancels out the permuted weights of the first half of the feed-forward network, $a^{\prime}$ is the same as its plaintext version $a$. Therefore, $a^{\prime}$ is not correctly permuted for the locked weight $W^{\prime}_{3}=\rho_{l}^{T}W_{3}$.
L151: To securely apply the required permutation $\rho_{l}$ without leaking it to an observer, the system uses a three-step ENCRYPT-LINEAR-DECRYPT sequence: the TEE first encrypts the activation $a^{\prime}$ with a random one-time pad ($m$) before permuting it (), the GPU performs the linear computation , and TEE decrypts the result by removing the noise effect ($mW_{3}$) to recover the correct output ().
L152: To ensure the cryptographic security of one-time pad masking, all computations are performed over a finite field. Following standard practice, all floating-point activations and weights are first quantized to fixed-point integers. These integers are then treated as elements of the finite field $\mathbb{F}_{P}$, where $P$ is a large prime chosen to prevent wrap-around errors.
L153: This step is what allows the random mask m to function as a secure one-time pad, providing a formal basis for the system’s security guarantees (cite81†Tramèr and Boneh, 2019 ).
L154: ### 2.2. TEE-based Computational Integrity
L155: 
L156: cite104†Image: Refer to caption Figure 2. Soter’s mechanism for generating integrity fingerprints from a static basis of precomputed cornerstones.
L157: Model’s computational integrity is sometimes protected using a technique called oblivious fingerprinting (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ). The core idea is to mix known-answer fingerprints, challenges for which the TEE already knows the correct output, with the genuine user inputs sent to the GPU. To prevent an attacker from simply detecting and ignoring these checks, the fingerprints are designed to be statistically indistinguishable from normal inputs.
L158: By verifying the GPU’s output for these hidden fingerprints, the TEE can detect any tampering.
L159: Figure cite105†2 exemplifies this mechanism used in Soter (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ) and TSQP (cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ). Beginning with a one-time offline setup where the TEE creates and stores a static basis of $K=10$ cornerstone fingerprint pairs, $\{m_{i},F(m_{i})\}$ (). During each online inference, the TEE generates a dynamic challenge, $m^{\prime}$, as a random linear combination of the cornerstone inputs ($m^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}m_{i}$) ().
L160: This challenge is then shuffled with genuine activations and sent to the GPU (), which applies the operator $F$ to the entire batch . Upon receiving the results, the TEE unshuffles them () and verifies the GPU’s work by comparing the returned fingerprint output, $F(m^{\prime})$, against the expected result it computes locally: $F(m^{\prime})_{expected}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}F(m_{i})$ (). A mismatch signifies an integrity breach and aborts the process.
L161: ### 2.3. Threat Model
L162: 
L163: We consider a setting where a defender (model provider) deploys a proprietary model on a user’s device, which is controlled by a powerful adversary. The defender’s goal is to guarantee two security properties for the deployed model: model confidentiality, to protect the intellectual property of its weights and parameters, and computational integrity, to verify the correctness of any computation offloaded to untrusted hardware.
L164: We assume the TEE itself is a secure black box, free from physical or hardware side-channel attacks. All components outside the TEE including the host operating system, system memory, and the GPU accelerator are considered untrusted and are fully controlled by the adversary. The adversary’s capabilities are therefore formidable: they can observe, intercept, and modify all communication between the TEE and the untrusted GPU.
L165: They know the complete architecture of the deployed model and the full details of the security protocol being used, but they do not have access to the secret keying material (e.g., secret permutations, static noise basis) held within the TEE. The adversary’s objective is to break the defender’s security guarantees.
L166: Aligned with our two primary case studies, we define two concrete goals for the adversary: (1) to compromise confidentiality by recovering the secret keys needed to fully reconstruct the original model (as in a TLG-like system), or (2) to compromise integrity by identifying and bypassing the verification mechanism to tamper with results without detection (as in Soter).
L167: ## 3. Key Observations on the Trade-off between Security vs Computation Cost
L168: ### 3.1. Key Observations
L169: Analyzing the workflow of TLG, we observe that the computations inside the TEE ( and in Figure cite103†1 ) can lead to a bottleneck. In particular, the calculation of the noise effect, $mW_{3}$, should be efficient since it has the largest computational footprint.
L170: In the meantime, as the model continues to grow (e.g., LLM), and the deployment of these LLMs on mobile devices becomes prevalent (cite106†Sorensen and Khlaaf, 2024 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ), the memory cost mainly surrounding $W_{3}$ could also be a concern (Table cite107†3 ).
L171: This observation highlights a fundamental tension in Soter’s design. For the integrity mechanism to be secure, the dynamically generated fingerprints must be statistically indistinguishable from genuine activations. However, for the mechanism to be efficient, Soter relies on this static, precomputed basis to enable rapid integrity checks, a point acknowledged in the original paper (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ).
L172: As we show later in the paper, this reliance on a static basis forces all generated fingerprints into a predictable, low-dimensional subspace, making them computationally distinguishable from genuine inputs and undermining the system’s security guarantee.
L173: In summary, the mechanisms in our two case studies, TLG and Soter, share a critical dependency: the need for an efficient TEE-based calculation of a noise effect ($mW_{3}$, $F(m^{\prime})$). As we will detail in Section cite13†3.2 , there are two main strategies to compute this effect: an on-the-fly method that generates fresh randomness for each query, versus a more performant precomputation-based approach that relies on a static, pre-loaded table of noise vectors.
L174: However, the prohibitive memory and latency costs of the on-the-fly method force designers to adopt the precomputation-based approach, as shown in our subsequent detailed analysis of a TLG-like system.

L176: 
L177: This section uses TLG to explain the on-the-fly vs precomputed approaches with the memory and latency constraints in consideration.
L178: On-the-Fly Approach employs an ENCRYPT-LINEAR-DECRYPT sequence to authorize computations on the untrusted platform. After the LINEAR computation is performed on the GPU (), the result re-enters the TEE. The DECRYPT function () then recovers the correct output by subtracting the effect of the initial noise, $mW_{3}$.

L242: The first stage of the attack is to characterize the $K$-dimensional subspace containing the permuted noise vectors $m\rho_{l}$ and construct a noise-canceling filter. This is possible because the noise vector $m$ is not truly random but is always a linear combination of $K$ secret basis vectors, $m=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}n_{i}$. Consequently, the output $m\rho_{l}$ is always confined to a predictable, low-dimensional subspace. The attacker proceeds with the following steps (see Figure cite111†4 ):
L243:   1. (1)
L244: 
L245: Query for noise samples: The attacker queries the ENCRYPT function $K+\delta$ times using a zero-vector input, $a^{\prime}=\vec{0}^{T}$. The function returns $K+\delta$ permuted noise samples as $1\times d$ row vectors, $m_{i}\rho_{l}$. The attacker transposes these into a set of $d\times 1$ column vectors, which we denote as $\{u_{i}=(m_{i}\rho_{l})^{T}\}_{i=0}^{K+\delta-1}$.
L246: 
L247:   2. (2)
L248: Derive orthonormal basis: Using the collected column vectors $\{u_{i}\}$, the attacker applies the Gram-Schmidt process to compute an orthonormal basis for the subspace spanned by permuted noise, $m\rho_{l}$. This results in a $d\times K$ matrix $Q=[q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{K-1}]$, whose columns span the subspace.
L249: 
L250:   3. (3)
L251: Construct noise-canceling filter: The attacker uses this basis to construct a $d\times d$ projection matrix, $C=I-QQ^{T}$. This matrix projects any vector onto the orthogonal complement of the noise subspace, acting as a perfect noise-canceling filter. For any collected noise vector $u_{j}=(m_{j}\rho_{l})^{T}$, it holds that $Cu_{j}=0$.
L252: #### 4.1.2. Stage 2: Extracting the Permutation Matrix $\rho_{l}$
L253: 
L254: In the second stage, the attacker uses the noise-canceling filter $C$ to reveal the secret permutation $\rho_{l}$ one mapping at a time. The reveal of the first mapping using the first canonical vector $\vec{e}_{0}=[1,0,0,...]^{T}$ is illustrated in Figure cite111†4 :
L255: 
L256:   1. (1)
L257: Query with a canonical vector: The attacker queries the ENCRYPT function with the first canonical basis vector as input, $a^{\prime}=\vec{e}_{0}^{T}$. The function returns the row vector $(\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\rho_{l}+m^{\prime}\rho_{l})$.
L258: 
L259:   2. (2)
L260: 
L261: Apply the filter: The attacker transposes this output into a column vector, $v=(\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\rho_{l})^{T}+(m^{\prime}\rho_{l})^{T}$, and applies the projection matrix:
L262:  | $\displaystyle Cv$  | $\displaystyle=C((\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\rho_{l})^{T}+(m^{\prime}\rho_{l})^{T})$  |
L263:  |  | $\displaystyle=C(\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\rho_{l})^{T}+C(m^{\prime}\rho_{l})^{T}.$  |
L264: 
L265: Since the noise term $C(m^{\prime}\rho_{l})^{T}$ is nullified by the filter, the result is only the projected signal:
L266: 
L267:  | $$Cv=C(\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\rho_{l})^{T}.$$  |
L268: 
L269:   3. (3)
L270: Reveal the mapping: The term $(\vec{e}_{0}^{T}\rho_{l})^{T}=\rho_{l}^{T}\vec{e}_{0}$ is itself a canonical column vector, which we can call $\vec{e}_{k}$. The final result is thus $C\vec{e}_{k}$, which is equivalent to the $k$-th column of the matrix $C$. By observing the resulting vector and matching it to the corresponding column of the matrix $C$, the attacker can determine the index $k$. This reveals one mapping of the secret permutation: $\rho_{l}^{T}$ maps index $0$ to index $k$.
L271: By repeating this process for all other canonical vectors ($\vec{e}_{1},\dots,\vec{e}_{d-1}$), the attacker can recover the entire secret permutation matrix $\rho_{l}$. This two-stage process of characterizing the noise subspace and then filtering it to isolate the signal is formalized in Appendix cite49†D - Algorithm cite112†1 .
L272: ### 4.2. Discussion on sampling strategy, $K$ and $\delta$
L273: 
L274: Even if the TEE uses a complex strategy to combine the noise vectors, such as combining a random subset of the basis vectors for each query, the output $m\rho_{l}$ is still confined to the same $K$-dimensional subspace, making the system vulnerable. We experimentally demonstrate this in Section cite32†6.5 .
L275: $K$ is a protocol parameter chosen by the model provider during the offline noise table precomputation step. Our threat model assumes the attacker has full knowledge of the protection protocol, including $K$, except for the secret keys and permutations. However, even if the attacker does not know $K$, it can be found empirically by collecting outputs and observing the rank of the resulting vector set until it stabilizes.
L276: We confirm the practicality of the attack under unknown $K$ (Section cite30†6.3 ), demonstrating that the attack succeeds even when $K$ is initially unknown. Discovery of $K$ is possible because the rank is guaranteed to stabilize at $K$ since $m\rho$ is generated from a fixed $K$-dimensional subspace.
L277: Our protocol assumes that a linear combination of all $K$ vectors is used to generate the noise mask. Therefore, by querying $K+\delta$ times, the attacker observes enough samples of the permuted noise subspace to derive its basis, $Q$ ( in Figure cite111†4 ). The small constant $\delta>0$ is chosen to avoid the pathological case when some of the random combinations are linearly dependent by pure chance.
L278: For a $K\times K$ matrix whose entries are chosen uniformly at random from a finite field with $P$ elements, the probability of it being rank deficient (linearly dependent) is:
L279: 
L280:  | $$P(\text{rank deficient})=1-\prod_{i=1}^{K}\left(1-\frac{1}{P^{i}}\right).$$  |
L281: 
L282: A derivation for this result has been provided in Appendix cite40†A . In our implementation of LLaMA-3-8B model, we used $P=2^{31}-1$ for which this probability is negligible. So $\delta$ can be something small like 1 or 2.
L283: ## 5. Attack on TEE-based Computational Integrity
L284: 
L285: In this section, we show an attack that fully compromises a TEE-based integrity checking mechanism. In particular, we use Soter (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ) as an example to explain the attack, although the same mechanism is also used in TSQP (cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ).
L286: cite113†Image: Refer to caption Figure 5. An illustration of the two-stage computation integrity attack on Soter. Stage 1: Subspace Recovery. The attacker collects two independent sets of vectors from the TEE’s output, Set A and Set B. By computing the intersection of the vector spaces they span, the attacker recovers the secret cornerstone fingerprint subspace ($V_{C}$). Stage 2: Integrity Bypass.
L287: The attacker intercepts a new query (4), uses the recovered subspace as a filter to identify the challenge fingerprints.
L288: ### 5.1. Attack Formalization
L289: Soter protects computational integrity by mixing hidden, known-answer challenge fingerprints among genuine activations. By verifying the GPU’s computation on these challenges, the TEE can detect any tampering. The attacker’s goal, therefore, is to distinguish these challenge fingerprints from the genuine data. Doing so would allow the attacker to pass the integrity check by computing the challenges correctly while selectively tampering with genuine activations.
L290: The attack works in two stages as illustrated in Figure cite114†5 :
L291: ##### Stage 1: Revealing the subspace for $m_{i}$.
L292: 
L293: In the first stage, the adversary learns a basis for the $K$-dimensional cornerstone fingerprint subspace, which we denote as $V_{C}$.
L294: 
L295: ##### Stage 2: Identifying the fingerprint to bypass the integrity check.
L296: 
L297: Armed with a basis for $V_{C}$, the adversary can now distinguish the fingerprints from genuine activations. Using this knowledge, the attacker only tampers the genuine activations while correctly computing the integrity checks.
L298: #### 5.1.1. Stage 1: Revealing the subspace for $m_{i}$
L299: The attacker begins by passively monitoring the TEE-GPU interface to collect two independent sets of outputs, Set A () and Set B (). Each set itself is generated from $K+\delta$ independent inference calls. For Soter, $K=10$. Because each challenge is generated from a fresh, random linear combination of all cornerstone fingerprints, $K+\delta$ calls for each set ensure that the cornerstone fingerprint subspace is a subspace of the vector space spanned by each set.
L300: The inference calls are kept independent of each other to avoid any linear dependence within the genuine activations. Therefore, the only structure systematically shared between the two sets is the underlying static cornerstone subspace, $V_{C}$. The adversary recovers a basis for $V_{C}$ by computing the intersection of the vector spaces spanned by set $A$ and set $B$ (). Note that the individual fingerprint vectors within set $A$ will be different from those within set $B$.
L301: We can still calculate the intersection subspace because even though these fingerprint vectors are distinct, those within set $A$ and those within set $B$ each span the cornerstone fingerprint subspace $V_{C}$. Calculating the subspace intersection reduces to solving a system of linear equations (Appendix cite41†B ).
L302: #### 5.1.2. Stage 2: Identifying the fingerprint to bypass the integrity check.
L303: 
L304: Once the attacker has uncovered the fingerprint subspace, $V_{C}$, they use it to bypass the integrity checks as follows:
L305: 
L306:   * •
L307: 
L308: If a vector lies inside the subspace $V_{C}$, it is identified as an integrity challenge. To remain undetected, the attacker allows it to be processed correctly, passing the integrity check.
L309: 
L310:   * •
L311: If a vector lies outside the subspace $V_{C}$, it is identified as a genuine user query. The attacker is now free to return a tampered result to the application .
L312: 
L313: This attack fully compromises Soter’s integrity mechanism, demonstrating that the efficiency-driven static basis design pattern is insecure even in sophisticated protocols.

L315: 
L316: In this section, we empirically validate the feasibility and effectiveness of our proposed attacks. Our evaluation is structured to answer six key questions:
L317: 
L318: ### 6.1. Experimental Setup
L319: ##### Setup.
L320: 
L321: Our experiments are conducted on a server with a dual-socket Intel Xeon Silver 4309Y CPU (2.80 GHz, SGXv2 enabled with a 4 GB EPC, of which 512 MB is allocated as heap space for our enclave) and a colocated NVIDIA A100-80GB GPU. The software stack consists of Rocky Linux 9.5, Intel SGX SDK v2.25, and PyTorch 2.5.1.
L322: ##### Implementation.
L323: Our implementation consists of two components. First, for the real-world performance benchmarks (RQ1, RQ6), we built a lightweight, single-threaded C++ library to execute the TEE-based cryptographic protocols on our physical SGX hardware. This library implements standard optimizations like fast modular reduction and memory tiling. Second, for a controlled analysis of the protocol-level vulnerabilities (RQ2–RQ6), we implemented faithful simulations of the core protocols from TLG and Soter.
L324: All finite field $\mathbb{F}_{P}$ computations are done with the prime $P=2^{31}-1$ unless stated otherwise. For the integrity attack simulation, we assume activations are drawn from a normal distribution.
L325: ### 6.2. The Performance Imperative for Precomputation
L326: Our central claim is that the static basis vulnerability arises from a severe security-performance trade-off. To validate this premise, we first benchmark the performance of a TEE-shielded LLaMA-3 8B model. We implement the TEE-based model confidentiality protocol presented in TLG, as detailed in Section cite8†2.1 .
L327: To compute the noise, $m$, and its noise effect, $mW_{3}$ ( and in Figure cite108†3 ), we compare two cryptographic strategies analyzed in Section cite11†3 : a secure on-the-fly method that generates fresh randomness for each operation, and the precomputation method that uses a static basis.
L328: As shown in Figure cite115†6 , the secure on-the-fly noise generation method is 37x - 55x slower than the precomputation strategy. For a typical interactive scenario (batch size 1, 128-token prompt), the on-the-fly method has a time-to-first-token (TTFT) of 187 seconds, compared to 3.61 seconds for the precomputation strategy. This is a 52x slowdown, making it unsuitable for real-world applications. The impact on throughput is also severe.
L329: At a batch size of 8, the on-the-fly method achieves only 0.5 tokens/sec (TPS), while the precomputation strategy delivers 18.6 TPS. This prohibitive performance gap makes the secure on-the-fly method impractical for any system requiring low-latency inference.

L331: ### 6.3. Discoverability of Secret Noise Dimension $K$
L332: A key artifact of the static basis vulnerability is that the secret noise dimension, K, is empirically observable. While our threat model grants the attacker knowledge of this parameter, we first demonstrate it can be discovered empirically. This initial step showcases how the core algebraic principles of our attack serve as a versatile method to snoop for information leakage inherent to the precomputation-based pattern. We show this for both of our target systems.
L333: For a TLG-like system, an attacker can perform the first stage of the attack described in Section cite14†4 by sending zero-vector queries ( in Figure cite111†4 ) and observing the rank of the resulting output vectors. As shown in Figure cite116†7 (a), the rank grows linearly with each new observation until it stabilizes precisely at $K$, which reveals the system parameter.
L334: For a more complex system like Soter, an attacker can passively execute the first part of the subspace recovery stage of the attack detailed in Section cite19†5 . As the attacker observes batches of mixed data ( in Figure cite114†5 ), the rank of the collected vectors initially grows linearly. However, as shown in Figure cite116†7 (b), the rank growth soon deviates from the expected trajectory, revealing a distinct change in slope.
L335: This occurs because the low-dimensional fingerprints, all drawn from the same precomputed basis, contribute progressively less new information. This deviation allows an attacker to infer the dimension of the fingerprint subspace, $K$.

L337: ### 6.4. Efficacy: A Deterministic and Total System Break
L338: 
L339: Having established that an attacker can discover $K$, we now demonstrate the attack’s efficacy. The success of the final attack is critically dependent on the initial stage of learning the complete noise subspace. To show this, we measure the success rate of the full attack (Stage 1 + Stage 2) while varying the number of queries used to learn the subspace (Stage 1).

L375: ## 7. Related Work
L376: ### 7.1. TEE-Shielded Model Protection
L377: Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) are a hardware-based solution for protecting proprietary machine learning (ML) models on third-party devices. Early solutions in this area focused on executing the entire model within a secure enclave (cite68†Hanzlik et al., 2019 ; cite69†Ohrimenko et al., 2016 ; cite126†Kunkel et al., 2019 ; cite127†Bayerl et al., 2020 ).
L378: While this provides strong security, traditional CPU-based TEEs face severe memory and performance constraints that make this impractical for large models (cite70†Lee et al., 2019 ; cite71†Kim et al., 2020 ; cite128†VanNostrand et al., 2019 ; cite129†Gangal et al., 2020 ; cite130†Islam et al., 2023 ; cite131†Li et al., 2023 ; cite132†Hu et al., 2023 ; cite133†Quoc et al., 2020 ).
L379: Newer confidential computing architectures on GPUs and CPUs, such as NVIDIA Hopper Confidential Computing and Arm’s CCA, are beginning to address these limitations by enabling the secure execution of entire large models (cite74†Abdollahi et al., 2025 ; cite73†Siby et al., 2024 ; cite75†Dong and Wang, 2025 ; cite76†Zhu et al., 2024 ).
L380: To leverage powerful but untrusted hardware accelerators like GPUs, Partial TEE-Shielded Execution (PTSE) approaches have been introduced. A crude PTSE strategy involves model partitioning, where the model is split and only the most sensitive layers are executed within the TEE (cite86†Mo et al., 2020 ; cite88†Schlögl and Böhme, 2020 ; cite87†Liu et al., 2023 ; cite134†Liu et al., 2024 ; cite135†Narra et al., 2019 ; cite136†Mo et al., 2024 ).
L381: Sophisticated PTSE strategies involve cryptographic obfuscation (cite81†Tramèr and Boneh, 2019 ; cite137†Hou et al., 2022 ; cite138†Bruhns et al., 2024 ; cite139†Hashemi et al., 2022 ; cite93†Xu and Fang, 2024 ; cite140†Sun et al., 2025a ; cite102†Bai et al., 2025 ), where weights are decomposed (cite91†Refael et al., 2024 ), transformed, masked, or permuted inside the TEE before being offloaded for computation (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ).
L382: The performance demands of modern LLMs mean these obfuscation schemes either explicitly adopt a precomputed, static secret basis to accelerate cryptographic operations (cite82†Shen et al., 2022 ; cite90†Sun et al., 2025b ; cite93†Xu and Fang, 2024 ), or would require such a design to scale efficiently, making them susceptible to the same vulnerability (cite94†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite84†Sun et al., 2023 ; cite83†Li et al., 2024a ; cite101†Zhang et al., 2023 ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ; cite89†Li et al., 2024b ).
L383: Our work is the first to formally identify this performance-driven design pattern as a fundamental flaw and demonstrate its exploitability through a deterministic, cross-query algebraic attack.
L384: ### 7.2. Attack Landscape for Secure Inference
L385: #### Orthogonal Attack Vectors
L386: A significant body of work targets secure inference through vectors orthogonal to our protocol-level analysis.
L387: Side-channel attacks exploit hardware-level information leakage like cache timing (cite141†Bernstein, 2005 ; cite142†Kocher, 1996 ), memory access patterns (cite143†Yarom and Falkner, 2014 ), or power consumption (cite144†Kocher et al., 1999 ) to infer secrets, and have been used to leak user inputs (cite145†Yang et al., 2024 ; cite106†Sorensen and Khlaaf, 2024 ; cite146†Adiletta and Sunar, 2025 ; cite147†Yuan et al., 2024a ; cite148†Banerjee et al., 2023 ; cite149†Grover et al., 2019 ) and model secrets (cite150†Hua et al., 2018 ; cite151†Yan et al., 2020 ; cite152†Wei et al., 2020 ; cite153†Gao et al., 2024 ; cite154†Rakin et al., 2022 ).
L388: Meanwhile, model extraction aims to steal a model’s functionality by training a substitute on its API outputs (cite155†Tramèr et al., 2016 ; cite156†Sanyal et al., 2022 ; cite157†Rolnick and Kording, 2019 ; cite158†Oliynyk et al., 2025 ; cite159†Nayan et al., 2024 ; cite160†Jagielski et al., 2020 ; cite161†Finlayson et al., 2024 ; cite162†Carlini et al., 2020 ; cite163†Carlini et al., 2024 ).
L389: Our attack is fundamentally different: we assume a perfect TEE free from side channels and exploit a mathematical flaw in the security protocol itself, rather than attacking its physical implementation or stealing its learned function.
L390: #### Protocol-Level Flaws
L391: Prior work has also identified protocol-level flaws, but these typically exploit statistical properties of the obfuscated weights to infer shielded components by matching them against a known public model (cite94†Zhang et al., 2024 ; cite95†Wang, 2025 ; cite101†Zhang et al., 2023 ). Consequently, the effectiveness of this class of attacks is limited, as they are predicated on the assumption that the victim model is fine-tuned from a public model. Our attack is fundamentally different.
L392: It is algebraic and deterministic; we do not infer secrets from noisy correlations but construct a system of linear equations from multiple queries to solve for the exact secret basis. Our contribution is thus more closely aligned with the well-understood category of vulnerabilities stemming from the reuse of secret keying material (cite164†Stinson and Paterson, 2018 ; cite165†Vanhoef and Piessens, 2017 ; cite166†Schneier and Mudge, 1998 ; cite167†Böck et al., 2016 ).
L393: We identify the static secret basis pattern as a new manifestation of this flaw within TEE-shielded ML and are the first to demonstrate how it enables an adversary to aggregate information across queries to algebraically break the system’s security.
L394: ## 8. Conclusion
L395: This work identifies the use of a precomputed static basis, an appealing optimization that performance-bound TEE-shielded systems are often forced to adopt, as a fundamental vulnerability. We prove its severity through two distinct algebraic attacks that defeat both confidentiality and integrity guarantees: one fully recovers secret permutations in a TLG-like system, while the other completely bypasses the integrity checks in Soter.
L396: Our findings reveal a critical tension between this optimization and provable security, highlighting the need for new protocols that achieve high performance without reusing secret material.
