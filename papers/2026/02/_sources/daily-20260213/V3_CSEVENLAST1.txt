L429: 
L430: cite94†Song et al. (2021) showed that the same model admits a deterministic implicit sampler (DDIM) obtained by setting the variance term to zero:
L431: 
L432:  | $$\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\underbrace{\Bigl(\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\Bigr)}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t).$$  |  | (11)

L434: ##### Classifier-free guidance.
L435: Both architectures employ classifier-free guidance (CFG) (cite246†Ho and Salimans, 2022 ). CFG biases the denoising direction toward a user condition without requiring an external classifier. For a current latent $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ and the shared noise predictor $\epsilon_{\theta}$, we obtain two estimates at the same step $t$: the unconditional prediction $\epsilon_{\!\text{uncond}}$ (with the condition omitted) and the conditional prediction $\epsilon_{\!\text{cond}}$ (under the desired condition).
L436: We then form a guided estimate
L437:  | $$\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon_{\!\text{uncond}}+s\bigl(\epsilon_{\!\text{cond}}-\epsilon_{\!\text{uncond}}\bigr),\qquad s\geq 0,$$  |
L438: 
L439: and substitute $\tilde{\epsilon}$ in place of $\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)$ in the DDPM/DDIM updates (Eqs. (cite243†10 )–(cite244†11 )). Setting $s=1$ at inference-time ignores updates from the unconditional stream, i.e., $\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon_{\!\text{cond}}$.
L440: ### A.2 Transformer
L441: The noise–prediction objective (cite245†9 ) only specifies what to learn but leaves open how the denoiser $\epsilon_{\theta}$ is parameterised. Classical DDPMs adopt a convolutional U-Net encoder–decoder (cite247†Ronneberger et al., 2015 ; cite242†Ho et al., 2020a ), whereas modern large‑scale models (cite248†Rombach et al., 2022 ; cite249†Bao et al., 2023 ; cite250†Karras et al., 2022 ) replace the convolutional blocks with transformer layers, yielding the U-ViT (U-shaped Vision Transformer) backbone.
L442: Both architectures, WaLa and Make-A-Shape, implement a diffusion generative model via transformer layers.
L443: #### A.2.1 Overview
L444: 
L445: Both methods adopt a wavelet–latent diffusion pipeline in which 3D shapes are represented as multiscale wavelet coefficients and a U‑ViT‑style denoising backbone (cite101†Hoogeboom et al., 2023 ) is trained in the DDPM (cite95†Ho et al., 2020b ) framework. The key difference lies in how the wavelet data are fed to the diffusion core.
L446: 
L447:   1. 1.
L448: WALA first compresses the full wavelet tree with a convolutional VQ‑VAE (stage 1), mapping the diffusible wavelet tree to a latent grid. The latent grid is then modeled by a 32-layer U-ViT (stage 2), where each transformer layer runs self-attention and cross-attention, totaling 32 cross-attention calls.
L449: 
L450:   2. 2.
L451: Make-A-Shape skips the auto‑encoder and instead packs selected wavelet coefficients into a compact grid. The U‑ViT backbone then downsamples this tensor to a bottleneck volume. The bottleneck is traversed by a 16-layer U-ViT core—8 self-attention layers immediately followed by 8 cross-attention layers— before up-sampling restores the packed grid.
L452: #### A.2.2 Conditioning pathway (point-cloud)
L453: In general, both Make-A-Shape and WaLa share a common pipeline for conditioning on point clouds: a PointNet (cite251†Qi et al., 2017 ) encoding followed by aggregation and injecting the resulting latent vectors into the U-ViT generator via  (i) affine modulation of normalization layers and  (ii) cross-attention.
L454: In particular, Make-A-Shape injects the conditioning latent vectors into the U-ViT generator at three stages:  (1) concatenation: the latent vectors are aggregated and concatenated as additional channels of the input noise coefficients,  (2) affine modulation: the latent vectors are aggregated and subsequently utilized to condition the convolution (down-sampling) and de-convolution (up-sampling) layers via modulating the affine parameters of the group normalization layers,  (3) cross-attention: each condition latent vector is augmented with an element-wise positional encoding and then fed into a cross-attention module alongside the bottleneck volume.
L455: WaLa injects the conditioning latent vectors into the U-ViT generator at two stages:  (1) affine modulation: the latent vectors are linearly projected via a global projection network and used to modulate the scale and bias parameters of GroupNorm layers in both the ResNet and attention blocks (AdaGN) (cite103†Esser et al., 2024 ),  (2) cross-attention: each latent vector, augmented with an element-wise positional encoding, is employed as the key and value in cross-attention modules interleaved within each transformer block.

L515: ### B.1 General
L516: 
L517: This section provides a general overview on the experimental setup for all results reported in this work.
L518: 
L519: ##### Restrict analysis to conditional stream.
L520: 
L521: As the failure behavior, Meltdown, is independent of the unconditional stream, we exclusively investigate the conditional prediction stream. That is, we set the CFG scale $s=1.0$ and restrict our mechanistic analysis (e.g., activations) and diffusion dynamics analysis (e.g., latents) to the conditional stream.
L522: ##### Seeding.
L523: 
L524: Randomness regarding a diffusion trajectory is controlled globally by seeding Python, NumPy, and PyTorch (torch.backends.cudnn.deterministic=True, benchmark=False) so that every evaluation at a given $\rho$ starts from the same terminal noise $x_{T}$.

L527:

L529: #### B.2.1 Setup
L530: 
L531: We detail the minimal, fully reproducible setup used to produce the sphere experiments for WaLa and Make-A-Shape. Throughout, the control parameter is $\rho\in[0,1]$, and the phenomenological order parameter is the number of connected components $C(\rho)$ in the generated mesh.
L532: ##### Conditioning clouds on the sphere.
L533: 
L534: We work on $\mathcal{S}=\{x:\|x\|_{2}=1\}$ and fix $N=400$ points for WaLa and $N=1200$ for Make-A-Shape. The base cloud $\mathcal{P}(0)$ uses a golden-angle (Fibonacci) sphere distribution:
L535: 
L536:  | $$g=\pi(3-\sqrt{5}),\quad i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\},\quad y_{i}=1-\frac{2(i+0.5)}{N},\quad r_{i}=\sqrt{1-y_{i}^{2}},\quad\theta_{i}=g\,i,$$  |
L537:  | $$p_{i}(0)=\bigl(r_{i}\cos\theta_{i},~y_{i},~r_{i}\sin\theta_{i}\bigr).$$  |
L538: A second target cloud $\mathcal{P}(1)$ is produced by jittering each $p_{i}(0)$ with i.i.d. Gaussian noise $n_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^{2}\mathbf{I}_{3})$ and renormalizing to the unit sphere:
L539: 
L540:  | $$\tilde{p}_{i}=\frac{p_{i}(0)+n_{i}}{\|p_{i}(0)+n_{i}\|_{2}},\qquad\mathcal{P}(1)=\{\tilde{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}.$$  |
L541: 
L542: We then move each point along the surface via per-point spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) between corresponding pairs:
L543:  | $$p_{i}(\rho)=\operatorname{slerp}\bigl(p_{i}(0),\tilde{p}_{i};\rho\bigr)=\frac{\sin((1-\rho)\omega_{i})}{\sin\omega_{i}}\,p_{i}(0)+\frac{\sin(\rho\,\omega_{i})}{\sin\omega_{i}}\,\tilde{p}_{i},\quad\omega_{i}=\arccos\!\bigl(\langle p_{i}(0),\tilde{p}_{i}\rangle\bigr).$$  |
L544: 
L545: This yields the cloud path $\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\{p_{i}(\rho)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ used throughout.
L546: ##### Decoding and component counting.
L547: 
L548: Given $\mathcal{P}(\rho)$, we compute a conditioning code via the model’s encoder and sample a latent with the diffusion sampler to yield $G(\mathcal{P}(\rho))$, i.e. a mesh. We report
L549: 
L550:  | $$C(\rho)=\texttt{len}(\texttt{trimesh.split(mesh)}),$$  |
L551: 
L552: i.e., the number of connected components in trimesh.
L553: 
L554: ##### Grid over the control parameter.
L555: 
L556: We sweep a uniform grid of $\rho$ values, i.e., $\rho\in\{0,0.05,\dots,1.0\}$.
L557: ##### Connectivity curve $C(\rho)$.
L558: 
L559: We evaluate $C(\rho)$ on the uniform $\rho$ grid. For each $\rho$, we reseed the RNGs to reproduce the identical terminal noise $x_{T}$. The curve reported is the set
L560: 
L561:  | $$\{(\rho,\,C(\rho))\}_{\rho\in\{0,0.05,\dots,1.0\}},$$  |
L562: 
L563: from which the observed plateau at $C(\rho)=1$ and the subsequent jump to $C(\rho)>1$ over a narrow $\rho$-interval (a connectivity bifurcation) are directly obtained.
L564: ##### Spectral entropy curve $H(\rho)$.
L565: 
L566: We evaluate the spectral entropy of the localized cross–attention write on the same uniform control grid $\rho\in\{0,0.05,\dots,1.0\}$ and with identical terminal noise across $\rho$.
L567: For each $\rho$, we encode the cloud $\mathcal{P}(\rho)$, run a single sampling trace, and read out the token-wise cross-attention write at the chosen site, $\mathbf{Y}(\rho)$. Let $\{\sigma_{i}(\rho)\}_{i}$ be the singular values of $\mathbf{Y}(\rho)$ (SVD of the matrix with shape tokens $\times$ features). We form normalized directional energies

L570: using the natural logarithm. The reported curve is the set
L571: 
L572:  | $$\bigl\{\,(\rho,\;H(\rho))\,\bigr\}_{\rho\in\{0,0.05,\dots,1.0\}}.$$  |

L663: ### B.3 Dataset Evaluation
L664: 
L665: This section provides (i) evidence that Meltdown exists across a variety of shapes, i.e., across the GSO (cite91†Downs et al., 2022 ) and SimJEB (cite92†Whalen et al., 2021 ) corpora, and diffusion transformers, i.e., WaLa and Make-A-Shape. Furthermore it details our setup to evaluate our method PowerRemap on GSO and SimJEB as well as the results of these evaluations.
L666: ##### General.
L667: 
L668: We evaluate PowerRemap on the WaLa and Make-A-Shape architectures, using DDIM sampling (cite94†Song et al., 2021 ). For each object, we load the corresponding mesh as the ground‑truth surface $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}$, center it and scale it to the unit cube.
L669: ##### Find Meltdown.
L670: 
L671: We reuse the notation of §cite7†2 . Given a mesh $\mathcal{S}$ and generator $G$, we first determine a sparse point budget by searching the smallest $N$ over a grid for which a Poisson‑disk sample $A=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\!\subset\!\mathcal{S}$ yields a healthy output $C(G(A))=1$. We then define a surface‑constrained Meltdown path by jittering $A$ and projecting back to $\mathcal{S}$ to obtain $B=\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$, and interpolate on‑manifold
L672:  | $$\mathcal{P}_{\rho}\;=\;\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}\!\big((1-\rho)A+\rho B\big),\quad\rho\in[0,1],$$  |
L673: 
L674: where $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}$ is nearest‑point projection. With a fixed random seed (reseeded before every inference), we sweep $\rho$ on a geometric grid to bracket a jump in connectivity, then refine by bisection to the smallest $\varepsilon$ such that $C\!\left(G(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})\right)\gg 1$.
L675: 
L676: Algorithm 2 Adversarial Meltdown Search
L677: 0:  surface $\mathcal{S}$, generator $G$, component counter $C(\cdot)$, seed $s=0$
L678: 
L679: 1:  normalize $\mathcal{S}$; find smallest $N$ s.t. $A\!\sim\!\text{Poisson}(\mathcal{S},N)$ gives $C(G(A)){=}1$ (reseed $s$)
L680: 
L681: 2:  $B\leftarrow\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(A+\xi)$ {jitter & project}
L682: 
L683: 3:  sweep $\rho$ on a geometric grid; find $\rho_{\mathrm{lo}}{<}\rho_{\mathrm{hi}}$ with $C(\rho_{\mathrm{lo}}){=}1$, $C(\rho_{\mathrm{hi}}){>}1$
L684: 4:  $\varepsilon\leftarrow$ bisection$(\rho_{\mathrm{lo}},\rho_{\mathrm{hi}})$ with reseeding to $s$
L685: 
L686: 5:  Return $(N,\varepsilon,\;C_{0}{=}C(G(A)),\;C_{\varepsilon}{=}C(G(\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon})))$
L687: ##### Evaluate PowerRemap.
L688: The task of reconstructing a global surface from a sparse point cloud has only two possible outcomes: success or failure. Thus, we assess the effectiveness PowerRemap by counting the number of times it succeeded in reducing $C_{\varepsilon}$ to 1, i.e., turning a speckle into a shape. Hence, we treat each shape as a Bernoulli trial under our adversarial search (Algorithm cite258†2 ).
L689: Each trial has an outcome $p\in\{0,1\}$, where $p=1$ iff the reconstruction meets the criterion $C_{\varepsilon}=1$; otherwise $p=0$. We first identify baseline failures as those with $C_{0,\text{baseline}}=1$ and $C_{\varepsilon,\text{baseline}}>1$. We then apply PowerRemap only to these failures and count a remedy when $C_{\varepsilon,\texttt{PowerRemap}}=1$.
L690: #### B.3.1 GSO
L691: 
L692: This section provides a quantitative evaluation of the Meltdown phenomenon on the GSO dataset (cite91†Downs et al., 2022 ) and assesses the effectiveness of PowerRemap as a mitigation strategy. GSO (cite91†Downs et al., 2022 ) is a diverse corpus of 1,030 scanned household objects and was not used to train either WaLa or Make-a-Shape. All results in this section are obtained by applying the protocol described in Appendix cite61†B.3 to the GSO dataset.
L693: ##### Evaluate PowerRemap.
L694: 
L695: For WaLa, we found Meltdown in 926/1,030 ($89.9\%$) shapes. Our method PowerRemap remedies failure in 910/926 (98.3%) for $\gamma=100$. Table cite121†1 (top) depicts the performance of our method across all shape categories. For Make-A-Shape, we consider a category-representative subset of 130 GSO shapes and find Meltdown in 130/130 ($100\%$) shapes. We evaluate PowerRemap over a $\gamma$-grid for the given subset, where
