L80: ###### Abstract
L81: Agentic theorem provers—pipelines that couple a mathematical reasoning model with library retrieval, subgoal-decomposition/search planner, and a proof assistant verifier—have recently achieved striking empirical success, yet it remains unclear which components drive performance and why such systems work at all despite classical hardness of proof search.
L82: We propose a distributional viewpoint and introduce statistical provability, defined as the finite-horizon success probability of reaching a verified proof, averaged over an instance distribution, and formalize modern theorem-proving pipelines as time-bounded MDPs.
L83: Exploiting Bellman structure, we prove existence of optimal policies under mild regularity, derive provability certificates via sub-/super-solution inequalities, and bound the performance gap of score-guided planning (greedy/top-$k$/beam/rollouts) in terms of approximation error, sequential statistical complexity, representation geometry (metric entropy/doubling structure), and action-gap margin tails.
L84: Together, our theory provides a principled, component-sensitive explanation of when and why agentic theorem provers succeed on biased real-world problem distributions, while clarifying limitations in worst-case or adversarial regimes.
L85: ## 1 Introduction
L86: Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly improved at mathematical reasoning (cite63†Barreto, 2026 ; cite64†Bryan et al., 2026 ; cite65†Epoch AI, 2026 ), and a parallel effort has produced increasingly capable agentic theorem provers: systems that combine an LLM policy, a planner for decomposition, a retriever over large libraries (such as Mathlib), and a proof assistant (such as Lean, Rocq, and Isabelle, often with SAT/SMT support) that provides rigorous feedback.
L87: Despite striking empirical progress, these pipelines are complex and highly modular, and it remains difficult to explain which components affect how—and in what sense they can overcome, or fail to overcome, the classical hardness barriers surrounding proof search. This gap between practice and theory is now a bottleneck: many design choices are guided by heuristics and ablations, while a principled understanding of provability under realistic distributions and finite compute is still missing.
L88: Formalization itself has a long history, from the early foundations of logic (Frege) through Hilbert’s program and Gödel’s incompleteness results. It is also well known that even very simple fragments of logic already exhibit computational hardness: for example, $3$-SAT belongs to propositional logic yet is NP-complete, and decision problems for first-order logic are undecidable in general.
L89: Meanwhile, contemporary reasoning models can solve not only competition-style mathematics but also problems that look “modern” in flavor, and agentic systems can sometimes translate such reasoning into machine-checked proofs. This creates a tension.
L90: A formal proof is a discrete object—a finite sequence of symbolic transformations—so why should a stochastic generator plus a search loop succeed at all? Has the emergence of LLM-based reasoning somehow transcended complexity theory? The answer is no: classical lower bounds and undecidability results remain valid. The key question is therefore more subtle:
L91: > What changes when we replace worst-case, unbiased settings by the highly structured, biased distributions of theorem statements and proof patterns that appear in practice?
L92: 
L93: We propose the working hypothesis that resolves the apparent paradox:
L94: > Statistical provability. Real-world theorem instances are not distributed uniformly over “all statements of a given size.” Instead, they concentrate on a highly structured, repetitive subset of mathematics (libraries, idioms, canonical proof patterns). Under such biased distributions, there exist regimes where statistical methods—learned policies and learned scoring functions—can outperform generic decision procedures and brute-force search, even though unbiased/uniform formulations remain hard.
L95: Traditional complexity theory has emphasized worst-case analysis (uniform norm over all instances) and, when considering average-case settings, often uses distributions designed to be unbiased or “flat.” In those settings, the implication “unbiased/uniform $\Rightarrow$ hard” is difficult to escape. Our focus is complementary: we ask when and why “typical/frequent $\Rightarrow$ easy” might hold, and how modern agentic pipelines exploit this.
L96: Of course, if an instance distribution were supported on worst cases, no such conclusion should be expected. The point is not to deny classical hardness, but to explain empirical success in the regimes where these systems actually operate.
L97: ### 1.1 Our approach: provability as a finite-horizon reachability problem
L98: To make such statements precise, we model an agentic theorem prover as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP). The state encodes the current proof obligation and verifier feedback (e.g., goals, subgoals, error messages), the action encodes the next proof step (tactic/lemma invocation, parameters, retrieval prompt, decoding controls), and the transition is induced by the proof assistant’s execution and checking.
L99: The evaluation target is time-bounded success: reach the solved state within a budget of $B$ interaction steps (or verifier calls). This leads to a canonical object:
L100:   * •
L101: 
L102: the provability of an instance $x_{0}$ under budget $B$,
L103: 
L104:  | $$V_{B}^{*}(x_{0}):=\sup_{\pi}\Pr^{\pi}(\exists t\leq B:X_{t}\in G\mid X_{0}=x_{0}),$$  |
L105: 
L106: where $G$ is the goal/success set and $\pi$ ranges over policies.
L107: 
L108: We then define statistical provability as a distributional notion—provability averaged over an instance distribution $Q$:
L109: 
L110:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}[V_{B}^{\pi}(x_{0})]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q}[V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})].$$  |
L111: This formalizes the central object that agentic systems implicitly optimize: not worst-case solvability, but success probability on a (highly biased) stream of problems under a fixed compute budget.
L112: ### 1.2 Why Bellman structure matters: certificates and component-wise effects
L113: 
L114: A major advantage of the MDP view is that it exposes the Bellman structure of the objective. This enables two kinds of results that are difficult to obtain from purely heuristic narratives:
L115: 
L116:   1. 1.
L117: 
L118: Existence and optimality: under mild regularity (compactness/Feller-type continuity), the optimal finite-horizon value $V_{B}^{*}$ exists and is attained by a deterministic Markov policy.
L119: 
L120:   2. 2.
L121: Provability certificates: rather than only predicting success rates, we construct upper and lower bounds on $V_{B}^{*}$ using Bellman inequalities (sub-/super-solutions). This yields a certificate interval
L122: 
L123:  | $$L_{B}(x_{0})\leq V_{B}^{*}(x_{0})\leq U_{B}(x_{0}),$$  |
L124: 
L125: whose gap quantifies uncertainty about provability given an approximate planner/value surrogate.
L126: To connect these certificates to learning and search, we treat the reasoning model as producing a score function (or a family of score functions) $h_{b}(x,a)$ that guides greedy selection, top-$k$ filtering, beam search, or rollouts.
L127: Deviations from the optimal value can then be controlled in terms of (i) approximation error of $h_{b}$ relative to the true one-step optimal value $Q_{b}^{*}$, (ii) statistical estimation error under adaptive data collection, and (iii) action-gap (“margin”) properties that determine when mis-ranking actually harms success. In a representative form, the analysis yields bounds where performance degrades roughly like
L128:  | $$\text{(effective proof length)}\times\text{(score error)}^{\text{(margin exponent)}},$$  |
L129: 
L130: making explicit how design choices change success probability through a small set of interpretable parameters.
L131: ### 1.3 From heuristics to qualitative theory: what each pipeline component buys you
L132: 
L133: This framework lets us reason qualitatively (and, under assumptions, quantitatively) about the impact of common pipeline components:
L134: 
L135:   * •
L136: 
L137: Verifier feedback shrinks the reachable region, eliminates invalid branches early, and can increase action separations; in the theory this improves effective dimension, margins, and certificate tightness.
L138: 
L139:   * •
L140: Retrieval over libraries (RAG) reduces approximation error by exposing relevant lemmas/proof schemas; in the theory this is a decrease in $\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}$ and often a reduction of the effective horizon by enabling shortcuts.
L141: 
L142:   * •
L143: 
L144: Test-time search (beam/rollout) trades compute for probability mass on good trajectories; its benefit is controlled by margin tails and by how quickly near-optimal actions remain in top-$k$ sets.
L145: 
L146:   * •
L147: Representation geometry (how proof states are embedded into a metric space) controls statistical rates through covering numbers / metric entropy; in favorable geometries, estimation error decays much faster with the number of verifier interactions.
L148: ### 1.4 Contributions
L149: 
L150: This paper develops a statistical provability theory for agentic theorem proving. Concretely:
L151: 
L152:   * •
L153: 
L154: MDP formalization and the notion of statistical provability. We formalize modern agentic theorem provers as finite-horizon reachability MDPs and define provability as optimal time-bounded success probability, together with its distributional counterpart over instance distributions.
L155: 
L156:   * •
L157: Bellman-structure evaluation and certificates. We use Bellman recursions and inequalities to derive provability certificates and to bound the deviation between the optimal success probability and that achieved by score-guided planning algorithms.
L158: 
L159:   * •
L160: A component-sensitive bound on success probability. We show how proof complexity, representation geometry (via metric entropy / doubling-type conditions), tool feedback, retrieval, test-time search, and sequential statistical complexity (e.g., sequential entropy / sequential Rademacher-type quantities) enter the provability bounds.
L161: 
L162:   * •
L163: A bridge between logical hardness and statistical complexity. We position these results as a step toward connecting classical complexity barriers with the statistical structure of real mathematical corpora, clarifying when “typical” instances can be tractable for learned, agentic systems.
L164: ## 2 Problem Formulation
L165: 
L166: Here we formalize interactive formal theorem proving (with an LLM agent and an exact verifier/proof assistant) as a time-bounded reachability problem in a Markov decision process (MDP). We then develop a Bellman-structure–based evaluation of (i) provability (optimal success probability), (ii) deviation from optimality of score-based planning (greedy/top-$k$/beam/rollout), and (iii) certificate-style upper/lower bounds. Finally, we discuss easy vs hard instances.
L167: ### Notation and standing conventions
L168: 
L169: $(K,d_{K})$ denotes a compact metric space used as an embedding space for individual goals, $M(K)$ denote the set of finite positive Borel measures on $K$. For $W>0$:
L170: 
L171:  | $$M_{\leq W}(K):=\{\mu\in M(K):\mu(K)\leq W\}.$$  |
L172: 
L173: For a bounded function $f:K\to\mathbb{R}$:
L174:  | $$\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in K}|f(x)|,\quad\mathrm{Lip}(f):=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d_{K}(x,y)},$$  |
L175:  | $$\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}:=\max\{\|f\|_{\infty},\mathrm{Lip}(f)\}.$$  |
L176: 
L177: The bounded Lipschitz metric on $M(K)$ is
L178: 
L179:  | $$d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1}\int_{K}fd(\mu-\nu).$$  |
L180: 
L181: “Goal solved” is represented by the zero measure $0\in M(K)$.
L182: All probabilities are defined on an underlying probability space on which the agent’s randomness (LLM sampling, exploration) and any environment randomness live.
L183: ## 3 From formal proof search to a time-bounded reachability MDP
L184: 
L185: ### 3.1 Informal picture
L186: 
L187: At each step the agent observes a proof assistant state (goals + local context + error messages) and chooses an action (e.g., a tactic/lemma invocation, or an LLM query template with retrieval parameters). The proof assistant deterministically (or stochastically, if external tools are randomized) returns the next state. We are interested in the probability of reaching a solved state within a fixed time/step budget.
L188: ### 3.2 Measure-valued encoding of “a finite set of goals”
L189: 
L190: Let $\mathcal{G}_{0}$ denote the (discrete) set of syntactic goals (or goal+context objects). Fix an embedding map
L191: 
L192:  | $$\phi:\mathcal{G}_{0}\to K.$$  |
L193: 
L194: If a proof state $x$ contains goals $g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}$ (allowing multiplicity), define the goal measure
L195: 
L196:  | $$\mu(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\phi(g_{i})}\in M(K).$$  |
L197: We note: (1) The solved state (“no goals”) corresponds to $\mu(x)=0$. (2) The number of goals is the total mass: $\mu(x)(K)=n$. (3) If we enforce (or truncate to) $\mu(x)(K)\leq W$, then $\mu(x)\in M_{\leq W}(K)$.
L198: 
L199: Especially, we use the following compactness in cite17†Section 4 to guarantee the existence of optimal policies via dynamic programming with maximizers.
L200: ###### Theorem 1 (Compactness).
L201: 
L202: If $(K,d_{K})$ is compact, then $(M_{\leq W}(K),d_{\mathrm{BL}})$ is a compact metric space.
L203: 
L204: A full proof is given in cite49†Appendix B .
L205: 
L206: This encoding is permutation-invariant and naturally supports variable goal counts.
L207: ### 3.3 MDP
L208: 
L209: We define a finite-horizon MDP:
L210: 
L211: State space: $\mathcal{X}:=M_{\leq W}(K)$ (or a truncated version; see cite27†Section 8 ).
L212: 
L213: Action space: a compact metric space $(\mathcal{A},d_{\mathcal{A}})$ representing admissible control choices (tactic calls, parameters, retrieval settings, decoding strategy, etc.).
L214: 
L215: Transition kernel: $P(\cdot\mid\mu,a)$ is a Borel probability measure on $\mathcal{X}$.
L216: 
L217: Goal set: $G:=\{0\}\subset\mathcal{X}$ (closed in $d_{\mathrm{BL}}$).
L218: We assume the proof assistant acts as a verifier: it produces correct successor states, so that the MDP’s transition is a faithful abstraction of actual interaction.
L219: ### 3.4 Provability as a time-bounded reachability objective
L220: 
L221: ###### Definition 1 (Time-bounded success / provability).
L222: 
L223: Fix a horizon $B\in\mathbb{N}$. For a policy $\pi$ and initial state $\mu\in\mathcal{X}$, define the time-bounded success probability
L224: 
L225:  | $$V_{B}^{\pi}(\mu):=\Pr^{\pi}_{\mu}\left(\exists t\in\{0,1,\dots,B\}\ \text{such that}\ \mu_{t}\in G\right).$$  |
L226: 
L227: The optimal provability is
L228: 
L229:  | $$V_{B}^{*}(\mu):=\sup_{\pi}V_{B}^{\pi}(\mu).$$  |
L230: ###### Definition 2 (Finite-horizon value functions).
L231: 
L232: For $b=0,1,\dots,B$ define
L233: 
L234:  | $$V_{b}^{*}(\mu):=\sup_{\pi}\Pr^{\pi}_{\mu}\left(\exists t\in\{0,1,\dots,b\}\ \text{s.t.}\ \mu_{t}\in G\right).$$  |
L235: 
L236: Then $V_{0}^{*}(\mu)=\mathbf{1}[\mu\in G]$.
L237: ###### Definition 3 (One-step optimal action-value).
L238: 
L239: For $b\geq 1$ define
L240: 
L241:  | $$Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}V_{b-1}^{*}(\mu^{\prime})P(d\mu^{\prime}\mid\mu,a).$$  |
L242: 
L243: We have Bellman recursion (finite-horizon reachability). For $b\geq 0$:
L244: 
L245:  | $$V_{b+1}^{*}(\mu)=\mathbf{1}[\mu\in G]\ \vee\ \sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{b+1}^{*}(\mu,a).$$  |
L246: 
L247: ###### Lemma 1 (Bounds and monotonicity).
L248: 
L249: For all $b$ and $\mu$, $0\leq V_{b}^{*}(\mu)\leq 1$ and $V_{b}^{*}(\mu)\leq V_{b+1}^{*}(\mu)$.
L250: ###### Proof.
L251: 
L252: Immediate from the definition of reachability probabilities. ∎

L254: ## 4 Existence of optimal value functions and optimal Markov policies
L255: 
L256: We state mild continuity assumptions ensuring that the supremum over actions is attained.
L257: ###### Assumption 1 (Weak continuity / Feller-type condition).
L258: 
L259: For every bounded continuous $f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$, the map
L260: 
L261:  | $$(\mu,a)\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(\mu^{\prime})P(d\mu^{\prime}\mid\mu,a)$$  |
L262: 
L263: is continuous on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}$.
L264: 
L265: This implies that $(\mu_{n},a_{n})\to(\mu,a)$ entails $P(\cdot\mid\mu_{n},a_{n})\Rightarrow P(\cdot\mid\mu,a)$ weakly.
L266: ###### Theorem 2 (Existence of maximizers and optimal Markov policies).
L267: 
L268: Assume: (1) $\mathcal{X}=M_{\leq W}(K)$ is compact under $d_{\mathrm{BL}}$ (cite66†Theorem 1 ), (2) $\mathcal{A}$ is compact, (3) the kernel $P$ satisfies cite67†Assumption 1 , (4) $G=\{0\}$ is closed (hence $\mathbf{1}[\mu\in G]$ is upper semicontinuous).
L269: Then for each $b=1,\dots,B$: (a) The Bellman recursion defines a Borel-measurable $V_{b}^{*}$, (b) For each $\mu$, the supremum $\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a)$ is attained by some $a_{b}^{*}(\mu)\in\mathcal{A}$, (c) hence there exists an optimal deterministic Markov policy $\pi^{*}=(a_{b}^{*})_{b=1}^{B}$ achieving $V_{B}^{*}$.
L270: 
L271: The proof is based on backward induction. See cite50†Appendix C for the full proof.
L272: ## 5 Bellman inequalities and provability certificates
L273: 
L274: The following monotonicity principle is central: it yields upper/lower bound certificates without solving the full MDP.
L275: 
L276: ###### Definition 4 (Bellman operator).
L277: 
L278: Define $T$ on bounded measurable $V:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]$ by
L279: 
L280:  | $$(TV)(\mu):=\mathbf{1}[\mu\in G]\ \vee\ \sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}V(\mu^{\prime})P(d\mu^{\prime}\mid\mu,a).$$  |
L281: 
L282: Then $V_{b+1}^{*}=TV_{b}^{*}$ with $V_{0}^{*}=\mathbf{1}[\cdot\in G]$.
L283: ###### Lemma 2 (Monotonicity).
L284: 
L285: If $V\leq W$ pointwise then $TV\leq TW$ pointwise.
L286: 
L287: ###### Proof.
L288: 
L289: Supremum and expectation preserve order. ∎
L290: ###### Theorem 3 (Sub-/super-solution certificates).
L291: 
L292: Let $(U_{b})_{b=0}^{B}$ and $(L_{b})_{b=0}^{B}$ be sequences of functions $\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]$.
L293: 
L294: If $U_{0}\geq\mathbf{1}[\cdot\in G]$ and
L295: 
L296:  | $$U_{b+1}\geq TU_{b}\quad\text{for all }b=0,\dots,B-1,$$  |
L297: 
L298: then $U_{b}\geq V_{b}^{*}$ for all $b$.
L299: 
L300: If $L_{0}\leq\mathbf{1}[\cdot\in G]$ and
L301: 
L302:  | $$L_{b+1}\leq TL_{b}\quad\text{for all }b=0,\dots,B-1,$$  |
L303: 
L304: then $L_{b}\leq V_{b}^{*}$ for all $b$.
L305: 
L306: ###### Proof.
L307: 
L308: Induction using cite68†Lemma 2 . ∎
L309: ###### Definition 5 (provability certificate and certificate gap).
L310: 
L311: For an initial state $\mu_{0}$, any pair $(L_{B}(\mu_{0}),U_{B}(\mu_{0}))$ such that
L312: 
L313:  | $$L_{B}(\mu_{0})\leq V_{B}^{*}(\mu_{0})\leq U_{B}(\mu_{0})$$  |
L314: 
L315: is a provability certificate, and
L316: 
L317:  | $$\mathrm{Gap}(\mu_{0}):=U_{B}(\mu_{0})-L_{B}(\mu_{0})$$  |
L318: 
L319: is the certificate gap.

L321: ## 6 Score-based planning: deviation from optimal provability
L322: 
L323: We now connect LLM-driven planning to provability via score functions.
L324: ### 6.1 Score functions and induced policies
L325: 
L326: Let $(h_{b})_{b=1}^{B}$ be score functions
L327: 
L328:  | $$h_{b}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1].$$  |
L329: 
L330: Define the greedy policy
L331: 
L332:  | $$\pi_{h}(\mu,b)\in\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}h_{b}(\mu,a).$$  |
L333: 
L334: Similarly, define top-$k$ candidate sets $\mathrm{Top}\text{-}k(h_{b}(\mu,\cdot))$ and beam-search variants (beam introduces a tree policy, but for analysis we track how often near-optimal actions remain in the candidate set).
L335: ### 6.2 Uniform score approximation and worst-case regret
L336: 
L337: ###### Assumption 2 (Uniform approximation of $Q_{b}^{*}$ on a domain).
L338: 
L339: Let $\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}$ be a compact “relevant” domain (e.g., reachable pairs). Assume that for each $b$,
L340: 
L341:  | $$\sup_{(\mu,a)\in\mathcal{D}}\big|h_{b}(\mu,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a)\big|\leq\varepsilon_{b}.$$  |
L342: 
L343: (How to obtain such bounds with high probability is addressed in cite24†Section 7 )
L344: ###### Lemma 3 (One-step suboptimality bound).
L345: 
L346: Under cite69†Assumption 2 , for every $\mu$ such that $(\mu,a)\in\mathcal{D}$ for all relevant $a$,
L347: 
L348:  | $$\sup_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,\pi_{h}(\mu,b))\leq 2\varepsilon_{b}.$$  |
L349: 
L350: ###### Proof.
L351: 
L352: Let $a^{*}\in\arg\max_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a)$ and $\hat{a}\in\arg\max_{a}h_{b}(\mu,a)$. Then $Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a^{*})\leq h_{b}(\mu,a^{*})+\varepsilon_{b}\leq h_{b}(\mu,\hat{a})+\varepsilon_{b}\leq Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,\hat{a})+2\varepsilon_{b}$. Rearrange. ∎
L353: ###### Theorem 4 (Worst-case provability regret bound).
L354: 
L355: Under cite69†Assumption 2 , for any initial $\mu_{0}$ whose relevant trajectories remain inside $\mathcal{D}$,
L356: 
L357:  | $$0\leq V_{B}^{*}(\mu_{0})-V_{B}^{\pi_{h}}(\mu_{0})\leq 2\sum_{b=1}^{B}\varepsilon_{b}.$$  |
L358: ###### Proof.
L359: 
L360: At each step $b$, the greedy action loses at most $2\varepsilon_{b}$ in the optimal one-step value (cite70†Lemma 3 ). Since finite-horizon reachability accumulates these losses additively under dynamic programming, summing yields the bound. ∎
L361: 
L362: ### 6.3 Certificate construction from score bounds
L363: ###### Corollary 1 (Score-based certificate).
L364: 
L365: Under cite69†Assumption 2 , define for $b\geq 1$:
L366: 
L367:  | $$U_{b}(\mu):=\mathbf{1}[\mu\in G]\ \vee\ \mathrm{clip}\Big(\sup_{a}h_{b}(\mu,a)+\varepsilon_{b}\Big),$$  |
L368:  | $$L_{b}(\mu):=\mathbf{1}[\mu\in G]\ \vee\ \mathrm{clip}\Big(\sup_{a}h_{b}(\mu,a)-\varepsilon_{b}\Big),$$  |
L369: 
L370: with $\mathrm{clip}(t)=\min\{1,\max\{0,t\}\}$. Then
L371: 
L372:  | $$L_{b}(\mu)\leq V_{b}^{*}(\mu)\leq U_{b}(\mu),\qquad U_{b}(\mu)-L_{b}(\mu)\leq 2\varepsilon_{b}.$$  |
L373: ###### Proof.
L374: 
L375: For each $\mu$, $\sup_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a)\in[\sup_{a}h_{b}(\mu,a)-\varepsilon_{b},\ \sup_{a}h_{b}(\mu,a)+\varepsilon_{b}]$, then apply cite71†Theorem 3 . ∎
L376: 
L377: ### 6.4 Top-$k$/beam and a margin condition (fast rates)
L378: 
L379: Define the $(k+1)$-gap
L380: 
L381:  | $$\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(\mu):=Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a_{1}^{*}(\mu))-Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,a_{k+1}^{*}(\mu)),$$  |
L382: 
L383: where $a_{i}^{*}(\mu)$ are actions sorted by $Q_{b}^{*}(\mu,\cdot)$.
L384: ###### Assumption 3 (Small-margin tail under occupancy).
L385: 
L386: Let $\nu_{b}$ be an occupancy distribution over states at depth $b$ (induced by the algorithm or a reference policy). Assume there exist $C_{\Delta},\beta>0$ such that for small $t$,
L387: 
L388:  | $$\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(\mu)\leq t)\leq C_{\Delta}t^{\beta}.$$  |
L389: ###### Theorem 5 (Expected fast-rate regret under margin).
L390:
