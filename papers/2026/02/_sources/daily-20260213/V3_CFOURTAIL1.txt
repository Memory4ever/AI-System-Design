L341: ## Appendix A Learning Rate Selection Details
L342: 
L343: For all direct fine-tuning experiments on text tasks, we first determined the optimal learning rate for each LoRA rank and model size combination to ensure that our baseline comparisons were as strong as possible. We used the Adafactor optimizer with a linear warmup of 1000 steps and no subsequent learning rate decay.
L344: The optimal learning rate was selected via a grid search over the set {0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1}. For each (model, rank) pair, we chose the learning rate that yielded the highest average performance across all 12 of our text-only tasks. This process revealed a consistent trend: lower-rank LoRA configurations (e.g., $r\leq 4$) benefited from a higher learning rate, whereas higher-rank configurations achieved better performance with a more conservative rate.

L406: ## Appendix D Derivation of Memory-Efficient LoRA-Squeeze
L407: 
L408: Here, we provide further details on the derivation of the memory-efficient variant of LoRA-Squeeze, described in Algorithm cite56†2 in the main paper. We seek to compute SVD of $\Delta W_{src}$ (i.e., the full model update as approximated by the source-rank LoRA) to truncate the least-contributing dimensions efficiently without explicitly constructing the (typically large) dense $m\times n$ matrix.
L409: For simplicity of notation, in the following derivation we assume that $r=r_{src}$, $\Delta W=\Delta W_{src}$, $A=A_{src}$, and $B=B_{src}$. We first perform QR decomposition on the factor matrices $A$ and $B$ to isolate their orthonormal bases:
L410: 
L411:  |  | $\displaystyle A=Q_{A}R_{A}\quad\text{where }Q_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r},R_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$  |  | (1)
L412:  |  | $\displaystyle B^{\top}=Q_{B}R_{B}\quad\text{where }Q_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},R_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$  |  | (2)
L413: By definition of the QR decomposition, $Q_{A}$ and $Q_{B}$ have orthonormal columns:
L414: 
L415:  | $$Q_{A}^{\top}Q_{A}=I_{r},\quad Q_{B}^{\top}Q_{B}=I_{r}$$  |  | (3)
L416: 
L417: We then substitute the decompositions back into the expression for $\Delta W$:
L418: 
L419:  | $\displaystyle\Delta W$  | $\displaystyle=(Q_{A}R_{A})(R_{B}^{\top}Q_{B}^{\top})$  |  | (4)
L420:  | $\displaystyle\Delta W$  | $\displaystyle=Q_{A}(R_{A}R_{B}^{\top})Q_{B}^{\top},$  |  | (5)
L421: and we can then define the core interaction matrix $M\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$ as:
L422: 
L423:  | $$M=R_{A}R_{B}^{\top}$$  |  | (6)
L424: 
L425: Thus, $\Delta W=Q_{A}MQ_{B}^{\top}$.
L426: 
L427: We then perform (full or randomized) SVD on the small matrix $M^{r\times r}$:
L428: 
L429:  | $$M=U_{M}S_{M}V_{M}^{\top}$$  |  | (7)
L430: 
L431: where $U_{M},V_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$ are orthogonal matrices and $S_{M}$ is the diagonal matrix of singular values.
L432: 
L433: Substituting this back into $\Delta W$:
L434:  | $$\Delta W=Q_{A}(U_{M}S_{M}V_{M}^{\top})Q_{B}^{\top}$$  |  | (8)
L435: 
L436: If we regroup the factor matrices above, we can write:
L437: 
L438:  | $$\Delta W=\underbrace{(Q_{A}U_{M})}_{\text{New }U}S_{M}\underbrace{(V_{M}^{T}Q_{B}^{T})}_{\text{New }V^{T}}$$  |  | (9)
L439: 
L440: Since $Q_{A}$ is orthogonal and $U_{M}$ is orthogonal, their product $(Q_{A}U_{M})$ is also orthogonal. The same applies to the right side. Therefore, $S_{M}$ is exactly equal to $S$ derived from directly applying SVD on $\Delta W$.
L441: If we then prune the rank of matrices in Eq. (cite152†9 ) to $r_{tgt}$, we then create an $r_{tgt}$-rank approximation of $\Delta W_{src}$, which is exactly the central mechanism of LoRA-Squeeze.
L442: ## Appendix E On (Coarsely Approximated) Computational Complexity of Different Decompositions
L443: Doing 1) full SVD decomposition on matrix $\Delta W^{m\times n}$ has a total complexity of $O(mn\penalty\ \text{min}(m,n))$, while 2) using Randomized SVD instead decreases it to $O(mn\penalty\ (r_{tgt}+k_{o}))$. Finally, 3) the efficient variant from Algorithm cite56†2 decreases it further to $O((m+n)\penalty\ r_{src}^{2})$. Let’s assume that $m=2,048,n=2,048,r_{src}=64,r_{tgt}=8,k_{o}=10$.
L444: In this case, the total, theoretically estimated number of FLOPs required for version 1 (full SVD on $\Delta W$) is $\sim$ $8.6B$ FLOPs, it is $\sim$ $75.5M$ FLOPs for version 2 (randomized SVD on $\Delta W$), and it is only $\sim$ $16.8M$ FLOPs for the memory-efficient LoRA-Squeeze variant.^{8}^{8} 8 Note that versions 1 and 2 also need to spend additional $m\times n\times r_{src}$ FLOPs on creating $\Delta W$, which is bypassed by the memory-efficient variant.
L445: ## Appendix F On Task Knowledge Retention
L446: 
L447: We hypothesise that the collapse observed with some tasks when the Post-Squeeze transformation step is too large (e.g., 128$\rightarrow$1, see Figures cite74†4(a) and cite75†4(b) in the main paper) is due to discarding too much information. We quantify the amount of kept task-related information from $r_{src}$ to $r_{tgt}$ as variance/energy retention, and compute it based on sorted singular values:
L448:  | $\displaystyle V_{r}(r_{src}\rightarrow r_{tgt})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r_{tgt}}s_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{r_{src}}s_{i}^{2}},$  |  | (10)
L449: 
L450: where $s_{i}$ denotes the $i$-th singular value from matrix $S$ obtained via RSVD. By design, $V_{r}=100\%$ when $r_{src}=r_{tgt}$ and then it starts decreasing with the decrease of $r_{tgt}$.

L481: ## Appendix G Further Discussion: On Theoretical Motivation and Connections
L482: Connections to Intrinsic Task Dimensionality. As established in prior work, the main motivation for the LoRA-style task adaptation relies on the fact that the adaptation lies on a low-dimensional manifold within the high-dimensional parameter space (cite52†Aghajanyan et al., 2021 ; cite169†Ansuini et al., 2019 ). This implies that the task vector $\Delta W$ has a low intrinsic rank. The objective of LoRA training is to find a good low-rank approximation of this vector.
L483: However, directly optimizing within a low-rank space (i.e., training LoRA with a small, fixed rank $r_{tgt}$ from the start) is a non-convex and highly constrained optimization problem. Such a process can easily converge to a suboptimal local minimum, failing to fully capture the essential structure of the task adaptation.
L484: Fine-tuning with higher ranks also bypasses the issue of setting a LoRA rank lower than the intrinsic task dimensionality before fine-tuning which can yield suboptimal performance for more complex tasks; intrinsic task dimensionality can be encountered during fine-tuning or post-hoc as proposed by LoRA-Squeeze.
L485: Connections to Overparameterization and the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis. By training with a higher source rank $r_{src}>r_{tgt}$, we essentially perform the optimization in a less constrained, overparameterized space. This larger search space provides more degrees of freedom, making it easier to navigate the complex loss landscape and find a high-quality solution that effectively captures the low-dimensional manifold corresponding to the task.
L486: This principle may be seen as analogous to the findings of cite170†Frankle and Carbin (2019) , termed the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, where an overparameterized network provides a richer substrate from which an efficient, high-performing subnetwork can be identified.
L487: In our case, RSVD serves as a principled, data-driven, and efficient pruning mechanism; SVD guarantees the best low-rank approximation of a matrix with respect to the Frobenius norm. By retaining the top $r_{tgt}$ singular values and their corresponding singular vectors, we are preserving the directions of greatest variance in the learned delta matrix $\Delta W_{src}$.
L488: This effectively isolates the most significant, principal components of the task adaptation while filtering out potential noise or less impactful components that may have been learned due to overparameterization.
