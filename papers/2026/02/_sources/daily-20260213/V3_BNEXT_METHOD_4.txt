L143: Modern neural network workflows typically involve training families of models at multiple scales. In the early, exploratory stage, smaller models enable rapid prototyping and scaling laws analysis, helping guide architecture choices and forecast the performance of larger variants.
L144: Later, for a final release, researchers devote substantially more compute to train much larger models of varying sizes, which are often shipped as suites to meet diverse downstream hardware, latency, and cost constraints (as, e.g., for LLaMA or GPT). As the models of varying scale are typically trained from scratch, this workflow involves a wasteful cycle of disposal and re-training.
L145: A natural question is whether smaller trained models can be upscaled to warm-start the training of larger ones, transferring information across scales to reduce the total compute required for the final suite.

L97: Principled Warm Starts and Hyperparameter Transfer

L102: ###### Abstract
L103: Modern large-scale neural networks are often trained and released in multiple sizes to accommodate diverse inference budgets. To improve efficiency, recent work has explored model upscaling: initializing larger models from trained smaller ones in order to transfer knowledge and accelerate convergence. However, this method can be sensitive to hyperparameters that need to be tuned at the target upscaled model size, which is prohibitively costly to do directly.
L104: It remains unclear whether the most common workaround—tuning on smaller models and extrapolating via hyperparameter scaling laws—is still sound when using upscaling. We address this with principled approaches to upscaling with respect to model widths and efficiently tuning hyperparameters in this setting.
L105: First, motivated by $\mu$P and any-dimensional architectures, we introduce a general upscaling method applicable to a broad range of architectures and optimizers, backed by theory guaranteeing that models are equivalent to their widened versions and allowing for rigorous analysis of infinite-width limits.
L106: Second, we extend the theory of $\mu$Transfer to a hyperparameter transfer technique for models upscaled using our method and empirically demonstrate that this method is effective on realistic datasets and architectures.

L328: At the same time, their updates also stay at $\Theta(1)$ scale updates, ensuring the model stays in the rich “feature learning” regime and allowing it to achieve lower loss. For upscaling specifically, this ensures that both the initialization and the injected noise make non-trivial contributions to the widened model’s training dynamics, a desirable regime that maintains some signal from the base model while exploiting the benefits of increased width.
L329: Finally, using the Tensor Program framework of cite89†Yang and Littwin (2023) , we can explicitly characterize the infinite-width limit of the entire training dynamics involving upscaling. To streamline future work, we introduce a modified Tensor Program tailored to upscaled training, which we present in Appendix cite47†E.1 . Without upscaling, the Tensor Program analysis shows that pre-activations converge to i.i.d. Gaussian random variables in the infinite-width limit.
L330: With upscaling by a factor $k$ (i.e., increasing the width from $n$ to $kn$), pre-activations instead converge to i.i.d. blocks, each a $k$-dimensional Gaussian random vector that may have non-trivial covariance structure. Our modified framework tracks these vectors explicitly—through their evolving covariance structure—across the entire training trajectory. We illustrate the resulting characterization on two simple MLPs trained with SGD in Appendices cite50†E.3 and cite55†E.4 .

L143: Modern neural network workflows typically involve training families of models at multiple scales. In the early, exploratory stage, smaller models enable rapid prototyping and scaling laws analysis, helping guide architecture choices and forecast the performance of larger variants.
L144: Later, for a final release, researchers devote substantially more compute to train much larger models of varying sizes, which are often shipped as suites to meet diverse downstream hardware, latency, and cost constraints (as, e.g., for LLaMA or GPT). As the models of varying scale are typically trained from scratch, this workflow involves a wasteful cycle of disposal and re-training.
L145: A natural question is whether smaller trained models can be upscaled to warm-start the training of larger ones, transferring information across scales to reduce the total compute required for the final suite.
L146: Several strategies have been proposed (e.g. cite84†Chen et al. (2015) ; cite85†Gong et al. (2019) ; cite86†Chen et al. (2022) ; cite87†Kim et al. (2024) ), typically employing function-preserving weight transformations that expand a pre-trained smaller model into a larger one so that the larger model produces identical outputs for any input, thereby allowing it to immediately inherit the smaller model’s performance before further optimization.
L147: However, the training dynamics of upscaled models could be quite different from those of large models trained from scratch. As a result, to use upscaling effectively requires finding good choices of several hyperparameters that are highly dependent on the model architecture and optimizer. In practice, hyperparameter tuning at the scale of modern large models is infeasible, so hyperparameters for training large models are typically chosen by extrapolating from smaller models using scaling laws.
L148: Unfortunately, upscaling appears a priori to be incompatible with this approach, since training a single model with upscaling involves training both at small and then at large scales. Previous work has relied only on informal heuristics for hyperparameter tuning in the presence of upscaling to address this issue.
L149: As our first contribution, presented in Section cite8†2 , we extend the theory of function-preserving model expansions (cite84†Chen et al., 2015 ). We introduce a procedure that combines weight transformations with a coordinated rescaling of the learning rate and other relevant optimizer hyperparameters, and we prove that the resulting widened model both is functionally identical at initialization and remains equivalent to the original model throughout the entire training trajectory.
L150: We further show that this procedure is closely connected to the Maximal Update Parametrization ($\mu$P) proposed in cite88†Yang and Hu (2020) ; cite89†Yang and Littwin (2023) ; see Section cite11†2.3 . Using this connection, we extend the $\mu$P software package to support the widening procedure, facilitating its implementation across standard architectures including multi-layer perceptron (MLP), ResNets, and transformers.
L151: For our second contribution, presented in Section cite13†3 , we propose an upscaling algorithm grounded in the above theory. Intuitively, this theory implies that the training dynamics of the narrower model evolve within a lower-dimensional subspace of the wider model’s ambient parameter space. Therefore, once we widen a model, we also inject a small symmetry-breaking perturbation into the parameters so that the upscaled dynamics can escape this lower-dimensional subspace; see Figure cite90†1(a) .
L152: We leverage the connection with $\mu$P to show that our upscaling algorithm—with the right amount of noise—induces training dynamics that are provably stable and exhibit “optimal” feature learning in the infinite-width limit. In the same vein as cite91†Yang et al.
L153: (2022) , our method aligns training dynamics across widths and enables zero-shot hyperparameter transfer: we can now easily tune the hyperparameters on narrow models using the same upscaling procedure, and the best hyperparameters found there can then directly be used for large model upscaling; see Figure cite92†1(b) . Our implementation based on the $\mu$P software package also enables straightfoward implementation of hyperparameter transfer.
L154: As our third contribution, presented at the end of Section cite15†3.2 with additional details in Appendix cite46†E , we use the Tensor Programs machinery to characterize the infinite-width limit of upscaled training for common network architectures, opening the door to further theoretical analysis of training dynamics involving upscaling.
L155: While here we focus on upscaling with respect to “width”, extensions to upscaling with respect to depth are a natural next step whose treatment we leave to future work (see for example cite93†Yang et al. 2023b ; cite94†Bordelon et al. 2023 for hyperparameter transfer across depths). In Section cite18†4 , we provide experiments showing the performance of the proposed upscaling algorithm across various architectures and datasets, and illustrating the hyperparameter transfer numerically.
L448: Our upscaling algorithm extends Net2WiderNet [cite84†Chen et al., 2015 ] from function preservation to dynamical equivalence—functional equivalence throughout the training trajectory—by rescaling learning rates and other hyperparameters. This connects our method to $\mu$P and the Tensor Program literature on infinite-width analysis and, in turn, enables principled hyperparameter transfer. By contrast, Net2WiderNet provides no tuning guidance: cite84†Chen et al.

Such shifts necessitate costly retuning, which our framework mitigates through hyperparameter transfer. Additionally, although cite84†Chen et al. L450: Motivated by the function-preserving philosophy, recent work has empirically explored model upscaling for large language models (LLMs) [cite85†Gong et al., 2019 , cite86†Chen et al., 2022 , cite195†Samragh et al., 2024 , cite194†Du et al., 2024 , cite87†Kim et al., 2024 , cite196†Zhang et al., 2024 ] and vision transformers [cite197†Hao et al., 2025 ]. Several studies [cite86†Chen et al., 2022 , cite196†Zhang et al., 2024 ] propose alternative symmetry breaking by mixing parameters from upper layers.
L451: In contrast to known function-preserving transformations, [cite192†Wang et al., 2023 , cite193†Pan et al., 2023 ] develops data-driven upscaling strategies that learn mappings from smaller pretrained models to larger ones, without guarantees on equivalence or training dynamics. Across these approaches, hyperparameter tuning for upscaled models is ad hoc: practitioners either perform costly retuning at the upscaled dimension, reuse base-model hyperparameters (often suboptimal), or apply heuristic adjustments.
L452: ##### Any-dimensional learning.
L453: The motivation of our work—particularly the analysis of equivalence across neural-network weight spaces with varying hidden widths in Section cite8†2 —originates from a parallel line of research: any-dimensional learning. In our work, we examine the “equivalence” between narrower and wider architectures achieved by expanding a narrow network’s weights through “duplication” of entries followed by suitable rescaling.
L454: We note a related form of “cross-dimensional equivalence” that we do not pursue here: expanding a narrow model’s weights by zero-padding also yields a wider model that is functionally equivalent. This type of “cross-dimensional” equivalence, via duplication or zero padding, has been used to study convex sets [cite198†Levin and Chandrasekaran, 2023 ] and polynomial optimization [cite199†Levin and Chandrasekaran, 2025 ] over varying dimensions.
L455: Similar ideas have been used for any-dimensional learning, in which inputs and outputs can be objects of arbitrary size. Architectures operating on sets, graphs, or point clouds are examples of “any-dimensional neural networks:” they use a fixed number of parameters while processing inputs of arbitrary size. Correspondingly, the underlying learning tasks are likewise defined to arbitrary sizes and sometimes require cross-size equivalence via duplication or zero-padding.
L456: Along these lines, cite200†Levin and Díaz [2024] provides a general framework for constructing equivariant, any-dimensional neural networks; cite201†Díaz et al. [2025] explores these ideas in the context of kernel methods; and cite202†Levin et al. [2025] investigates size generalization for any-dimensional neural networks. Our work extends this line of research by moving from the input/output space to the weight space, establishing cross-dimensional equivalence among network parameters.
