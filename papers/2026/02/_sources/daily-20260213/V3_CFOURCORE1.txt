L38: # The emergence of numerical representations
L39: in communicating artificial agents
L40: 
L41: Daniela Mihai    Lucas Weber Affiliation: Department of Translation and Language Sciences, Universitat Pompeu Fabra, Barcelona, Spain    Francesca Franzon Affiliation: Department of Translation and Language Sciences, Universitat Pompeu Fabra, Barcelona, Spain Affiliation: Corresponding Author: a-d.mihai@soton.ac.uk Affiliation: School of Electronics and Computer Science, University of Southampton, United Kingdom
L42: ###### Abstract
L43: Human languages provide efficient systems for expressing numerosities, but whether the sheer pressure to communicate is enough for numerical representations to arise in artificial agents, and whether the emergent codes resemble human numerals at all, remains an open question. We study two neural network-based agents that must communicate numerosities in a referential game using either discrete tokens or continuous sketches, thus exploring both symbolic and iconic representations.
L44: Without any pre-defined numeric concepts, the agents achieve high in-distribution communication accuracy in both communication channels and converge on high-precision symbol–meaning mappings. However, the emergent code is non-compositional: the agents fail to derive systematic messages for unseen numerosities, typically reusing the symbol of the highest trained numerosity (discrete), or collapsing extrapolated values onto a single sketch (continuous).
L45: We conclude that the communication pressure alone suffices for precise transmission of learned numerosities, but additional pressures are needed to yield compositional codes and generalisation abilities.
L46: ## 1 Communicating and representing numerosities
L47: Number words, such as two, ten, sixteen, allow humans to encode any numerosity up to infinity, relying on finite memory. Their encoding efficiency lies in several properties. First, they exhibit precision: each word maps one-to-one onto a numerosity; this mapping can be extended indefinitely through extrapolation, assigning a new word to each added element.
L48: They also follow transparent compositional rules, reducing the number of forms to memorise: for example, knowing the words for units and tens allows speakers to interpolate intermediate numerosities.
L49: These properties enable learnability, making concept–word mappings easy to infer and generalise to novel words and novel numerosities: in humans, understanding the structure of number words unlocks precise representation of any numerical concept cite26†Barner (2017) , whereas without number words, quantity representation has been argued to be only approximate.
L50: Finally, the form of number words is arbitrary, as no property of the word suggests the numerosity they refer to; however, as commonly in language, most frequently occurring number words are shorter cite27†Pajot et al. (2025) . However, written representations of numbers vary in arbitrariness and can start as iconic representations, where the visual properties of number symbols directly reproduce the numerosity to encode cite28†Overmann (2025) .
L51: Although numerical systems emerged independently in different cultures cite29†Coolidge and Overmann (2012) , number words across languages largely share these properties cite30†Comrie (2013) , suggesting that they reflect optimisation to similar encoding pressures cite31†Xu et al. (2020) ; cite32†Denić and Szymanik (2024) .
L52: Language evolution theories propose that structured systems arise under pressures for transmission, economy, and generalisation cite33†Kirby et al. (2008) . Across cultures, numbers originated as a cultural invention for unambiguous communication, used to record stored goods and track quantities in transactions cite34†Menninger (2013) . We therefore ask whether communicative pressures shape number words and, in turn, enable the representation of number concepts.
L53: Specifically, we ask (i) whether the properties observed in natural language numerals also arise in a code self-optimised through communication, (ii) which aspects of communication support them and (iii) whether the resulting code supports generalisation to unseen numerosities, bootstrapping their conceptual representation.
L54: To this end, we implement a referential game  cite35†Lewis (1969) ; cite36†Boldt and Mortensen (2024) ; cite37†Brandizzi (2023) , in which a communication protocol is shaped solely through collaborative interaction between two neural-network agents without prior knowledge of numerals. We test whether the pressure to communicate is sufficient for agents to develop a code, and whether it shows precision, compositionality, generalisation, and arbitrariness as emerging properties.
L55: We also assess whether frequency and learning strategy (interpolation vs extrapolation) affect communication success and code structure.
L56: ## 2 Environmental Setup
L57: ### Communication Task.
L58: We used a referential game setup (Fig. cite38†1(a) ), where two neural-network agents communicate about numerosities. In each trial, a sender agent is presented with an image displaying a certain number of dots and sends a message to a receiver agent. The receiver, based on the message, must identify the target image showing the same number of dots, among distractors with different numerosities.
L59: Upon target selection, both agents receive feedback; in this way, the communication protocol emerges while solving the task. The setup has a training phase, where agents receive feedback and therefore develop their code, and a generalisation phase, where they communicate about novel numerosities.

L65: ### Stimuli.
L66: We designed the stimuli to ensure that agents rely only on numerosity, preventing confounds of other visual properties, like shape, colour, area or perimeter occupied by the dots. Stimuli images are 256 x 256 pixels in size, each depicting a different number of black dots on a white background (e.g. Fig. cite41†1(b) ).
L67: In order to make information on numerosity independent of other visual variables such as the area occupied by black pixels, we constrain the total black area occupied by dots to 5-10% of the canvas for all numerosities.
L68: ### Conditions.
L69: For the purpose of this work, the main training set is mostly limited to numerosities between 1 and 5 (although we experimented with larger training sets, such as 1 to 20, and observed similar results). The extrapolation/interpolation evaluation can include one other numerosity. The exact training and testing numerosities are specified for each experiment.
L70: To test if representational abilities of numerosity emerge independently of the low-level visual properties, we test two target setups, Same and Diff, which indicate whether the agents have the exact same target image, or two different instances from the same numerosity class.
L71: ### Agents.
L72: Both agents use a shared pretrained ViT encoder for processing the dot images cite42†Radford et al. (2021) . We model two types of communication channels. In the discrete setup, the sender is equipped with an LSTM encoder cite43†Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) , which outputs a message as a sequence of discrete tokens chosen over a predefined vocabulary; the receiver’s LSTM reads the message and accordingly selects an image as a possible target.
L73: In the continuous setup, the sender draws a fixed number of straight lines on a blank canvas, i.e. a sketch cite44†Mihai and Hare (2021) . The receiver reads the sketch through the same ViT encoder used for the candidate target images. A multi-class hinge loss is employed to maximise the probability that the correct target image is selected. Training happens on a set of numerosities as specified per experiment, and the testing is both in-range and on unseen values.
L74: ### Metrics.
L75: We assess communication success via task accuracy, defined as the proportion of trials in which the receiver selects the numerosity presented to the sender.
L76: Additionally, we assess the properties of the emerging communication code: Efficiency via message length (tokens) or sketch strokes/span; Precision as conditional entropy of numerosity given message (sketches are discretised via clustering); Generalisation by accuracy on unseen numerosities and where their messages map among seen classes; and Arbitrariness by skewing training frequencies and checking if more frequent numbers get shorter or simpler codes.
L77: ## 3 Results
L78: ### Communication success and code precision
L79: First, we observe that agents can reliably communicate trained numerosities, even in the Diff condition, where the sender and receiver see different instances of the same numerosity (Fig. cite45†2(a) and cite46†2(c) ). The system becomes more precise in the different setups indicated by the lower entropy (Fig. cite47†2(b) and cite48†4(a) ).
L80: Under length regularisation (Fig. cite49†3 ), we observe that messages become shorter with minimal accuracy loss and lower entropy, indicating the pressure toward efficient bijective encodings. This shows that even without language-internal conventions, precision emerges from the joint pressure of coordination and information bottleneck.

L95: ### Arbitrariness outweighs frequency
L96: We next vary the training context to assess whether more frequently occurring numerosities are encoded more economically compared to the least frequent ones. We find that manipulating class frequency (Uniform/Increase/Decrease setups detailed in supplementary) barely changes accuracy (Fig. cite50†4(b) ) and does not induce shorter codes for frequent numerosities. The sketch channel tends to scale with the spatial span of the input image elements, rather than with frequency (Fig. cite51†4(c) ).
L97: Human numerals often show mixed arbitrariness with some high-frequency lexicalisation. In this set of experiments, the absence of transmission costs beyond accuracy likely weakens Zipf-like simplification, and the arbitrariness survives because it is good enough for coordination.

L104: ### Generalisation and compositionality
L105: We further investigate whether the code developed by the agents can generalise to unseen numerosities (Discrete setup shown in Fig. cite53†5 Top). We find that under out-of-distribution testing, agents typically reuse the message or symbol for the largest trained numerosity. The interpolation analysis, testing within-the-range numerosities, also indicates a lack of systematic structure.
L106: Sketch-based communication behaves similarly, although on average, extrapolations with a continuous channel are better than chance. However, agents do not develop a compositional sketch but rather map everything larger than in training onto a single sketch, which usually resembles the highest seen numerosity (e.g.Fig. cite53†5 -Bottom, and supplementary).
L107: The emerging signals stabilise as holistic labels, with a separate message/sketch for each trained numerosity and no subparts are reused to compose new messages.

L110: ## 4 Implications for Language Evolution
L111: 
L112: ### Coordination yields precision and numerical representations.
L113: 
L114: The agents are able to quickly learn low-entropy codes, precisely communicating about trained numerosities. Numerosities are communicated independently of visual features, indicating the acquisition of abstract numerical representations.
L115: ### Iconicity helps, but arbitrariness wins.
L116: 
L117: The sketch communication channel shows some size and span cues, with sketch size capturing the spread of the dots, rather than the quantity; but both discrete and continuous communication channels settle into mostly arbitrary, class-specific signals.
L118: ### Frequency alone doesn’t yield efficient codes.
L119: 
L120: Human languages often make frequent words shorter, but here, without explicit costs for longer messages, skewed input distributions do not result in more efficient codes. Adding production and perception costs could aid the brevity of the emergent codes.
L121: ### Lack of compositionality, lack of generalisation
L122: . When faced with higher, untrained numerosities, agents reuse the message associated with the largest trained numerosity. This limitation may relate to the lack of compositionality in the emergent communication system: agents use holistic messages, and neither reuse interpretable message parts nor develop rule-based compositions.
L123: While holistic messages encoding non-decomposable bundles of features commonly emerge in referential games cite55†LaCroix (2025) ; cite56†Freeborn (2025) , compositionality plays a crucial role in bootstrapping numerical representations, as evidenced by human learning. Inducing such compositionality in this context remains a key challenge. Unlike referents defined by multiple features (e.g., colour and shape), numbers vary along a single ordered dimension, offering no inherently decomposable features to encode.
L124: Successful generalisation, therefore, requires the emergence of an arbitrary but systematic partitioning of this dimension, rather than the transparent mapping of perceptual features. How such a structure can be induced remains an open question.
L125: Alternative learning setups may help, such as population training (<)e.g.¿mahaut2025referential or other mechanisms that promote compositionality in both human communication and neural networks, such as iterated learning, vocabulary constraints, and biases toward symbol reuse cite57†Lake and Baroni (2023) ; cite33†Kirby et al. (2008) . Exploring such mechanisms may therefore be key to enabling systematic numerical generalisation.

L149: ## Appendix A Manipulating Class Frequency - Experimental Setup
L150: We vary the training context to assess whether more frequent, and thus varied, numerosities trigger a more efficient code, and the least varied correspond to a more complex code. While we use a Uniform distribution in most of our experiments and with an equal number of training samples for each numerosity (700), we also define Increase and Decrease setups.
L151: Therefore, we define Increase setup to have the following distribution of samples per class: $100,200,300,400,700$ increasing from numerosity 1 to numerosity 5; and the reverse for the Decrease setup.
L152: The model used for this experiment has variable message length, up to 5 tokens, a vocabulary of size 3 and a message regularisation loss with a coefficient of 0.005. Figure cite69†6 shows the discrete channel test accuracies for different training data distributions, and Figure cite70†7 illustrates the exact mappings for the Increase and Decrease setups across 3 seeds.
L153: The continuous channel accuracies across seeds are reported in Figure 4b of the main paper and are similar in trend to those of the discrete channel (Figure cite69†6 ). Further examples of sketches produced at test time for each of the numerosity classes present in the training set are shown in Figure cite71†10 . The different rows indicate results from agents being trained with more or less diverse training samples, some classes having more or fewer instances.
L154: The pairwise dissimilarity of sketches in the three varying frequency setups is shown in Figure cite72†8 .
L155: The results indicate the messages are arbitrary and there is no concrete correlation between message complexity and the number of training samples. The only consistent trend is observed in the accuracy across the different training setups, Decreasing being the most difficult to solve with both a discrete and continuous channel.
L156: An observation from this experiment is that while the symbols do not vary in complexity based on the frequency, nor are they compositional (in the sense of combining more frequent numerosities’ codes), they all seem to correlate with the area filled by elements. For example, numerosity 1 is usually represented through a symbol whose lines are concentrated at the centre of the sketch and do not cover much of the available space.
L157: As the numerosity increases and the elements start spreading across the target image space, the lines start increasing in length and are positioned in such a way as to cover as much of the available sketching space.
