L395: ## Appendix A Detailed proof of Section cite18†3.3 L396: 
L397: ### A1. Assumptions and Notation Definitions
L398: 
L399: To rigorously complete the proof, we first clarify the definitions of core symbols and basic assumptions in this section:
L400: ##### Basic Assumptions:
L401: 
L402: 1. The matrix differential satisfies the product rule: For any differentiable matrices $A,B$, $d(AB)=AdB+dAB$;
L403: 2. The learning rate $\eta$ is sufficiently small, and the higher-order infinitesimal term $\eta^{2}$ can be neglected;
L404: 3. The loss function $L(W)$ is twice differentiable with respect to $W$, and the gradient $\nabla_{W}L$ exists and is continuous.
L405: ### A2. Update Rule of $W$ in the Ideal Case
L406: 
L407: The core idea of gradient descent is to update parameters along the negative gradient direction of the loss function to minimize the loss. For the projection matrix $W$, the ideal update rule is:
L408: 
L409:  | $$W^{\prime}=W-\eta\nabla_{W}L$$  |
L410: 
L411: , where $W^{\prime}$ denotes the updated matrix. Defining the parameter update amount $\Delta W=W^{\prime}-W$, we have in the ideal case:
L412: 
L413:  | $$\Delta W=-\eta\nabla_{W}L.$$  |  | (A1)
L414: At this point, the differential of the loss is $dL=\text{tr}(\nabla_{W}^{T}\Delta W)=-\eta\text{tr}(\nabla_{W}^{T}\nabla_{W})$. Since $\text{tr}(\nabla_{W}^{T}\nabla_{W})=\|\nabla_{W}\|_{F}^{2}\geq 0$, the loss can decrease with maximum efficiency.
L415: ### A3. Derivation of the Actual $\Delta W$ Under Low-Rank Decomposition
L416: 
L417: When $W=PQ$, the actual objects of parameter update are the low-rank factors $P$ and $Q$, rather than $W$ itself. Assume the update rules for $P$ and $Q$ are:
L418: 
L419:  | $$P^{\prime}=P-\eta\nabla_{P}L,\quad Q^{\prime}=Q-\eta\nabla_{Q}L,$$  |  | (A2)
L420: 
L421: where $\nabla_{P}L$ and $\nabla_{Q}L$ are the gradients of the loss $L$ with respect to $P$ and $Q$, respectively.
L422: #### A3.1 Expansion of $\Delta W$
L423: 
L424: The updated $W^{\prime}=P^{\prime}Q^{\prime}$, thus:
L425: 
L426:  | $\displaystyle\Delta W$  | $\displaystyle=W^{\prime}-W=(P-\eta\nabla_{P}L)(Q-\eta\nabla_{Q}L)-PQ$  |
L427:  |  | $\displaystyle=PQ-\eta\nabla_{P}L\cdot Q-\eta P\cdot\nabla_{Q}L+\eta^{2}\nabla_{P}L\cdot\nabla_{Q}L-PQ$  |
L428:  |  | $\displaystyle=-\eta\left(\nabla_{P}L\cdot Q+P\cdot\nabla_{Q}L\right)+\eta^{2}\nabla_{P}L\cdot\nabla_{Q}L.$  |
L429: Since the learning rate $\eta\ll 1$, the contribution of the higher-order term $\eta^{2}\nabla_{P}L\cdot\nabla_{Q}L$ to $\Delta W$ is negligible. Therefore:
L430: 
L431:  | $$\Delta W\approx-\eta\left(\nabla_{P}L\cdot Q+P\cdot\nabla_{Q}L\right).$$  |  | (A3)
L432: #### A3.2 Derivation of Gradients $\nabla_{P}L$ and $\nabla_{Q}L$
L433: 
L434: To solve $\nabla_{P}L$ and $\nabla_{Q}L$, we need to use the trace trick for matrix derivatives, which relies on two key properties of the trace operation:
L435: 1. Cyclic permutation property: For any compatible matrices $A,B$, $\text{tr}(AB)=\text{tr}(BA)$;
L436: 2. Linearity: For any matrices $A,B$ and constant $c$, $\text{tr}(A+B)=\text{tr}(A)+\text{tr}(B)$ and $\text{tr}(cA)=c\text{tr}(A)$.
L437: ##### Step 1: Definition of Loss Differential
L438: 
L439: For the loss function $L(W)$, its differential can be expressed as the inner product of the gradient and the parameter differential, in matrix form:
L440: 
L441:  | $$dL=\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}dW\right),$$  |  | (A4)
L442: 
L443: where $dW$ is the differential matrix of $W$.
L444: cite102†Image: Refer to caption Figure 4: Impact of Orthogonal Loss on Matrices of Different Sizes. All iterations range from 0 to 2000. The difference among the three figures lies in the second dimension size of the matrices. As the size increases, the benefit of adding orthogonal loss becomes greater.
L445: ##### Step 2: Substitute the Low-Rank Decomposition $W=PQ$
L446: 
L447: According to the product rule of matrix differentials, $dW=PdQ+dPQ$ (where $dP$ and $dQ$ are the differential matrices of $P$ and $Q$, respectively). Substituting this into Equation (A4):
L448: 
L449:  | $$dL=\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}(PdQ+dPQ)\right)=\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}PdQ\right)+\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}dPQ\right).$$  |
L450: ##### Step 3: Differentiate with Respect to $dQ$ (Derive $\nabla_{Q}L$)
L451: 
L452: Applying the cyclic permutation property of the trace to the first term above:
L453: 
L454:  | $$\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}PdQ\right)=\text{tr}\left((P^{T}\nabla_{W})^{T}dQ\right).$$  |
L455: 
L456: By the definition of matrix gradient: If $dL=\text{tr}(G^{T}dX)$, then $\nabla_{X}L=G$. Here, $X=Q$, so:
L457: 
L458:  | $$\nabla_{Q}L=P^{T}\nabla_{W}.$$  |  | (A5)
L459: ##### Step 4: Differentiate with Respect to $dP$ (Derive $\nabla_{P}L$)
L460: 
L461: Applying the cyclic permutation property of the trace to the second term above:
L462: 
L463:  | $$\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}dPQ\right)=\text{tr}\left(Q\nabla_{W}^{T}dP\right)=\text{tr}\left((\nabla_{W}Q^{T})^{T}dP\right).$$  |
L464: 
L465: Similarly, by the definition of matrix gradient, $X=P$, so:
L466: 
L467:  | $$\nabla_{P}L=\nabla_{W}Q^{T}.$$  |  | (A6)
L468: #### A3.3 Simplification of $\Delta W$ After Substituting Gradients
L469: 
L470: Substituting Equations (A5) and (A6) into Equation (A3):
L471: 
L472:  | $\displaystyle\Delta W$  | $\displaystyle\approx-\eta\left((\nabla_{W}Q^{T})\cdot Q+P\cdot(P^{T}\nabla_{W})\right)$  |  | (A7)
L473:  |  | $\displaystyle=-\eta\left(\nabla_{W}(QQ^{T})+(P^{T}P)\nabla_{W}\right).$  |
L474: Due to the associativity of matrix multiplication, $\nabla_{W}(QQ^{T})=\nabla_{W}\cdot QQ^{T}$ (where $QQ^{T}$ is a $\beta\times\beta$ matrix, compatible with the left multiplication of $\nabla_{W}$), and $(P^{T}P)\nabla_{W}=P^{T}P\cdot\nabla_{W}$. Thus, the common factor $\nabla_{W}$ can be factored out:
L475: 
L476:  | $$\Delta W\approx-\eta\nabla_{W}\left(QQ^{T}+P^{T}P\right).$$  |  | (A8)
L477: ### A4. Impact of Orthogonality on Optimization Efficiency
L478: #### A4.1 Core Criterion for Optimization Efficiency
L479: 
L480: The efficiency of loss reduction is determined by $dL=\text{tr}(\nabla_{W}^{T}\Delta W)$: When $\Delta W$ is aligned with $-\nabla_{W}$, $dL$ reaches its minimum (the loss decreases the fastest). Combining Equation (A8):
L481: 
L482:  | $$dL\approx-\eta\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}\nabla_{W}(QQ^{T}+P^{T}P)\right).$$  |
L483: 
L484: If $QQ^{T}+P^{T}P=cI_{\beta}$ (where $c>0$ is a constant), then:
L485: 
L486:  | $$dL\approx-\eta c\text{tr}\left(\nabla_{W}^{T}\nabla_{W}\right).$$  |
L487: At this point, $\Delta W$ is completely aligned with $-\nabla_{W}$, and the optimization efficiency is maximized (the constant $c$ only affects the step size, which can be adjusted via the learning rate $\eta$).
L488: #### A4.2 Optimality of Orthogonality
L489: 
L490: When $P$ and $Q$ satisfy the orthogonality condition:
L491: 
L492:  | $$P^{T}P\approx I_{\beta},\quad QQ^{T}\approx I_{\beta},$$  |  | (A10)
L493: 
L494: we have $QQ^{T}+P^{T}P\approx I_{\beta}+I_{\beta}=2I_{\beta}$. Substituting into Equation (A8):
L495: 
L496:  | $$\Delta W\approx-2\eta\nabla_{W}.$$  |  | (A11)
L497: At this point, $\Delta W$ is completely consistent with the ideal update direction (Equation (A1)) (only the step size coefficient is 2, which does not affect the direction), and the loss reduction efficiency is maximized.
L498: Conversely, if $P$ or $Q$ is non-orthogonal (e.g., $P^{T}P=3I_{\beta}$, $QQ^{T}=0.5I_{\beta}$), then $QQ^{T}+P^{T}P=3.5I_{\beta}$, leading to $\Delta W\approx-3.5\eta\nabla_{W}$, where the step size deviates from the optimal value. More critically, if $QQ^{T}+P^{T}P$ is a non-diagonal matrix, the direction of $\Delta W$ will deviate from $-\nabla_{W}$, significantly reducing optimization efficiency and even causing training oscillations.
L499: ### A6. Conclusion
L500: The detailed proof above demonstrates that the low-rank decomposition $W=PQ$ causes the parameter update direction to deviate from the ideal negative gradient direction. The orthogonality of $P$ and $Q$ is the key to solving this problem—orthogonality enables $\Delta W$ to approximate the ideal update direction, maximizing optimization efficiency.
L501: By introducing the orthogonal regularization loss, this orthogonality can be maintained during training, ultimately improving the convergence speed and performance of the low-rank adapter.

L505: ## Appendix C Limitation
L506: We aim to discuss the limitations herein to identify promising avenues for future exploration in this domain. To the best of our knowledge, there is no method has been shown to outperform full fine-tuning across a wide range of visual recognition tasks with extra latency. While adapter-based approaches yield impressive performance on visual tasks, they inevitably introduce extra modules and inference latency.
L507: We observe that LoRA-like methods often outperform full fine-tuning in large language models (LLMs) without incurring additional inference costs (cite104†Ding et al., 2023 ). This is primarily because the input and output distributions of LLMs are nearly homogeneous. LoRA excels at LLMs due to its ability to enable efficient domain transfer for homogeneous data.
L508: For visual recognition tasks, however, the input and output distributions of foundation models during pretraining (e.g., classification, unsupervised learning) differ significantly from those of downstream tasks (e.g., detection, segmentation). Thus, fine-tuning visual foundation models for downstream tasks imposes more stringent requirements on tuning methods than fine-tuning LLMs does.
L509: Going forward, research on fine-tuning for visual recognition tasks should strive toward the goal of zero inference latency—a direction that we also intend to pursue in our future work.

L511: ## Appendix D Adapter Tuning Paradigm
L512: 
L513: For dataset $D=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}^{N}_{i=1}$, fine-tuning calculates the loss between inference results and labels according to the formula:
L514: 
L515:  | $$L\left(D,\theta\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{loss(f_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\right),y_{i})},$$  |
L516: 
L517: where $f_{\theta}$ denotes the network forward function and $loss$ represents the loss function. After that, $\theta$ is optimized through
L518: 
L519:  | $$\theta\leftarrow\underset{\theta}{{\arg\min}\,L(D,\theta)}.$$  |
L520: In adapter tuning paradigm, parameters consist of two parts, including parameters in adapter $\theta_{A}$ and parameters in the original architecture $\theta$. Here, $\theta$ is further divided into frozen part $\theta_{F}$ and trainable part $\theta_{T}$, noted as $\theta=\left\{\theta_{F},\theta_{T}\right\}$. Let $\Omega$ be all the trainable parameters, then $\Omega=\left\{\theta_{A},\theta_{T}\right\}$. The loss function and optimization formula in adapter can be written as:
L521:  | $$L\left(D,\theta_{F},\Omega\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{loss(f_{\theta_{F},\Omega}\left(x_{i}\right),y_{i})},$$  |
L522: 
L523:  | $$\Omega\leftarrow\underset{\Omega}{{\arg\min}\,L(D,\theta_{F},\Omega).}$$  |
