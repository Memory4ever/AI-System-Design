L61: Collectively, these intertwined issues of computational cost, expert coupling, and routing stability make successful and localized knowledge editing in MoEs substantially more difficult than in their dense counterparts.
L62: To address this tripartite challenge, we introduce MoEEdit, an expert-aware editor that reframes MoE knowledge editing as a principled, block-structured optimization problem where each expert constitutes a block. L63: To solve this, we develop a novel per-expert null-space projection that constrains parameter updates to preserve the input to subsequent routers, thereby safeguarding model stability. Our integrated approach sets a new state-of-the-art on standard benchmarks (COUNTERFACT, zsRE), decisively outperforming dense-model editors adapted for MoEs. This result underscores the necessity of expert-aware, routing-stable interventions tailored to the unique architectural properties of MoE models.

L70: ## 2 Related Work
L71: KE in dense Transformers. KE seeks to revise specific factual associations in LLMs while preserving general capabilities (cite34†Zhang et al., 2024c ; cite35†Zhong et al., 2023 ). Methods for dense Transformers fall into two families: parameter-modifying and parameter-preserving.
L72: Within the former, locate-then-edit approaches such as ROME (cite40†Meng et al., 2022 ) and MEMIT (cite41†Meng et al., 2023 ) directly modify a small set of FFN down-projection weights identified via causal analysis, enabling single-fact and batched edits, respectively. Gradient-based editors such as MEND (cite42†Mitchell et al., 2022a ) learn hypernetworks that transform fine-tuning gradients into localized weight updates.
L73: To improve locality and robustness under sequential edits, AlphaEdit (cite48†Fang et al., 2025 ) projects updates into the null space of a preservation set. Other strands explore instruction-based editing (cite54†Zhang et al., 2024a ), hybrid neural–symbolic mechanisms (cite55†Zhang et al., 2024b ), and in-context editing (cite56†Zheng et al., 2023 ).
L74: Complementing these, parameter-preserving or semi-parametric methods (e.g., SERAC (cite43†Mitchell et al., 2022b )) store edits in external memories and perform inference-time routing, trading parametric locality for reversibility and capacity. Notably, LEMoE (cite57†Wang & Li, 2024 ) introduces a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture within the adaptor itself to manage lifelong editing.
L75: However, LEMoE functions as a parameter-preserving framework that attaches external modules to a frozen backbone (typically dense). It addresses routing consistency within the added adaptor rather than the routing distribution shift of the base model itself.
L76: MoE architectures. MoE layers scale capacity by activating only a sparse subset of experts per token through a learned router, thereby increasing representational power while keeping FLOPs nearly constant (cite49†Shazeer et al., 2017 ; cite50†Lepikhin et al., 2021 ; cite51†Du et al., 2022 ). Each expert is a gated feed-forward network with its own parameters; the router selects the top-$K$ experts using input-dependent logits and produces a gate-weighted sum of their outputs.
L77: This conditional computation yields strong expert specialization but also creates editing complications: edits must respect the router’s distribution, multiple experts jointly determine the output, and perturbations at one layer can alter downstream routing.
L78: Knowledge editing for MoE LLMs. However, KE for MoE architectures remains largely unexplored. While methods like LEMoE (cite57†Wang & Li, 2024 ) utilize MoE structures externally, they do not tackle the challenge of modifying intrinsic MoE parameters.
L79: Existing techniques, which assume fully active parameters, are ill-suited for the conditional computation in MoEs and present an intractable trade-off: updating all experts is computationally prohibitive, while updating a subset is unreliable due to stochastic routing. This leaves a critical gap for an editing framework explicitly designed for the sparse and modular nature of MoE models.
L80: ## 3 Preliminaries
L81: 
L82: Locate-then-Edit Paradigm (Dense Models). An autoregressive LLM updates the layer-$l$ hidden state as ${\bm{h}}^{l}={\bm{h}}^{l-1}+{\bm{a}}^{l}+{\bm{v}}^{l}$, where ${\bm{a}}^{l}$ and ${\bm{v}}^{l}$ are the outputs of the attention and feed-forward (FFN) blocks at layer $l$, respectively. The FFN output can be written as
L83:  | $\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{l}={\bm{W}}_{\text{out}}^{l}\underbrace{\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{\text{in}}^{l}\,\gamma({\bm{h}}^{l-1}+{\bm{a}}^{l})\big)}_{\text{"keys" }{\bm{k}}}={\bm{W}}_{\text{out}}^{l}\cdot{\bm{k}},$  |  | (1)
L84: with layer norm $\gamma(\cdot)$ and nonlinearity $\sigma(\cdot)$. Following cite45†Geva et al. (2021) , $\bm{W}_{\mathrm{out}}^{l}$ can be viewed as a linear associative memory that maps post-gate features (“keys”: ${\bm{k}}=\sigma\big({\bm{W}}_{\text{in}}^{l}\,\gamma({\bm{h}}^{l-1}+{\bm{a}}^{l})\big)$) to outputs (“values”: ${\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}^{l}$).
L85: If factual knowledge is formalized as triples $(s,r,o)$ (subject, relation, object), one can view ${\bm{k}}$ as encoding $(s,r)$ and ${\bm{v}}$ as encoding $o$ (cite40†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite46†Dai et al., 2022 ).
L86: We adopt a locate-then-edit formulation: given keys ${\bm{K}}_{1}=\left[{\bm{k}}_{1}\left|{\bm{k}}_{2}\right|\cdots\mid{\bm{k}}_{n}\right]$ for the new associations and targets ${\bm{V}}_{1}=\left[{\bm{v}}_{1}\left|{\bm{v}}_{2}\right|\cdots\mid{\bm{v}}_{n}\right]$, find a small perturbation $\mathbf{\Delta}$ to a single FFN projection ${\bm{W}}_{\text{out}}$ such that $({\bm{W}}_{\text{out}}+\mathbf{\Delta})\bm{K}_{1}\approx\bm{V}_{1}$ while preserving behavior on a preservation set with keys $\bm{K}_{0}$.
L87: This yields the regularized least-squares objective
L88:  | $$\mathbf{\Delta}=\arg\min_{\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}}\big\|({\bm{W}}_{\text{out}}+\tilde{\Delta}){\bm{K}}_{1}-{\bm{V}}_{1}\big\|^{2}+\big\|\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}{\bm{K}}_{0}\big\|^{2}+\lambda\|\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}\|^{2},$$  |  | (2)
L89: 
L90: where $\lambda\!\geq\!0$ controls locality and conditioning. For clarity, layer indices are omitted below since the formulation applies identically to each layer.
L91: KE in MoE LLMs. Modern LLMs increasingly adopt MoE layers to scale capacity with near-constant FLOPs (cite49†Shazeer et al., 2017 ; cite50†Lepikhin et al., 2021 ). Given an input representation ${\bm{u}}$, a trainable router produces logits $s_{n}={\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{e}}_{n}$ (${\bm{e}}_{n}$ is the routing embedding for expert n) and selects a small set $S=\operatorname{TopK}(s_{1:N},K)$ ($\operatorname{TopK}$ selects K biggest element of $s_{1:N}$, and typically $K\!\ll\!N$ for MoE models).
L92: Let $g_{n}$ denote the router weight for expert $n$. The MoE block output is a router-weighted mixture
L93:  | $${\bm{v}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}\,E_{n}({\bm{u}}),\qquad g_{n}=\begin{cases}\exp(s_{n})\big/\sum_{j\in S}\exp(s_{j}),&n\in S,\\[4.0pt]
L94: 0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$  |  | (3)
L95: 
L96: where each expert is a gated FFN
L97: 
L98:  | $$E_{n}({\bm{u}})={\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{down}}\!\left[\big({\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{up}}{\bm{u}}\big)\odot\sigma\!\big({\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{gate}}{\bm{u}}\big)\right].$$  |  | (4)
L99: Thus, every expert realizes its own key–value memory: the post-gate feature ${\bm{k}}_{n}=\big({\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{up}}{\bm{u}}\big)\odot\sigma\!\big({\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{gate}}{\bm{u}}\big)$ serves as a key and the linear map ${\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{down}}$ returns a value ${\bm{v}}_{n}={\bm{W}}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{down}}{\bm{k}}_{n}$, which the router aggregates into ${\bm{v}}=\sum_{n}g_{n}\,{\bm{v}}_{n}$.
L100: Extending Eqn. cite58†2 to MoE, we edit expert-specific projections $\{{\bm{W}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}$ (For brevity in the editing objective, we denote ${\bm{W}}_{n}\equiv{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{down}}^{(n)}$). For edit request $i$, let ${\bm{k}}_{i,n}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}$ be the post-gate key of expert $n$, $g_{i,n}\!\geq\!0$ its router weight, and ${\bm{v}}_{i}$ the target output.
L101: We consider disjoint sets: an edit set $\mathcal{E}$ where outputs should change and a preservation set $\mathcal{P}$ to remain stable. The MoE KE objective seeks small perturbations $\{\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\}$ that (i) match targets on $\mathcal{E}$ and (ii) preserve behavior on $\mathcal{P}$:
L102:  | $$\{\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\}=\arg\min_{\{\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{i,n}\big({\bm{W}}_{n}+\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\big){\bm{k}}_{i,n}-{\bm{v}}_{i}\Big\|^{2}\;+\;\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{i,n}\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}\Big\|^{2}\;+\;\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|^{2}.$$  |  | (5)
L103: Compared with the dense case, the router weights $\{g_{i,n}\}$ couple experts: each example influences up to $K$ experts (Top-$K$ gating), and a naive closed-form solve would require inverting a $(Nd_{k})\!\times\!(Nd_{k})$ system, computationally prohibitive for large $N$. This coupling and scale motivate the expert-aware optimization strategy developed in Section cite9†4 .
L104: ## 4 Method
L105: In this section, we present MoEEdit, a purpose-built, expert-aware knowledge editor for MoE models.
L106: Our approach tackles the unique challenges of MoE editing head-on: (i) it mitigates the routing distribution shift: a key instability when naively apply KE to MoE models by reparameterizing expert updates through per-expert null-space projections , and (ii) solves the resulting objective efficiently with a randomized block coordinate descent (BCD) procedure that scales with the expert hidden size, making large-scale MoE editing tractable.
L107: ### 4.1 Routing Distribution Shift in MoE Editing
L108: Editing expert parameters changes the MoE block outputs and, after subsequent normalization and attention, perturbs the input ${\bm{u}}$ to the router in the following MoE layers. Following the definition in Section cite8†3 , let ${\bm{E}}_{\ell}=[{\bm{e}}^{\ell}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{e}}^{\ell}_{N}]$ collect the router embeddings at layer $\ell$.
L109: The router computes logits and mixture weights as ${\bm{g}}_{\ell}=\operatorname{softmax}({\bm{s}}_{\ell})$^{2}^{2} 2 For clarity of analysis, we use the full softmax distribution and omit the Top-$K$ selection, so that ${\bm{g}}_{\ell}$ remains differentiable for the Jacobian-based first-order analysis., where ${\bm{s}}_{\ell}={\bm{E}}_{\ell}^{\top}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}$.
L110: A perturbation applied in layer $(\ell-1)$ changes ${\bm{u}}_{\ell}$ by $\delta{\bm{u}}_{\ell}$, thus ${\bm{s}}_{\ell}$ by $\delta{\bm{s}}_{\ell}=E_{\ell}^{\top}\delta{\bm{u}}_{\ell}$ and produces new routing weights ${\bm{g}}_{\ell}^{\prime}=\operatorname{softmax}({\bm{s}}_{\ell}+\delta{\bm{s}}_{\ell})$. We define the routing distribution shift as $\delta{\bm{g}}_{\ell}={\bm{g}}_{\ell}^{\prime}-{\bm{g}}_{\ell}$.
L111: Its magnitude can be quantified over a set of prompts using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence or the Routing Similarity (RS), which is defined as the Jaccard similarity between the pre- and post-edit Top-$K$ expert sets:
L112:  | $$\mathrm{RS}_{\mathrm{route}}^{\ell}=\frac{\lvert S_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pre}}\cap S_{\ell}^{\mathrm{post}}\rvert}{\lvert S_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pre}}\cup S_{\ell}^{\mathrm{post}}\rvert}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{KL}({\bm{g}}_{\ell}\|{\bm{g}}_{\ell}^{\prime}),$$  |  | (6)
L113: 
L114: where $S_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pre}}$ and $S_{\ell}^{\mathrm{post}}$ denote the expert sets before and after editing, respectively. To characterize $\delta{\bm{g}}_{\ell}$ analytically, we linearize the softmax around ${\bm{s}}_{\ell}$:
L115:  | $${\bm{g}}_{\ell}^{\prime}\approx{\bm{g}}_{\ell}+J_{\mathrm{sm}}({\bm{s}}_{\ell})\delta{\bm{s}}_{\ell}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta{\bm{g}}_{\ell}\approx J_{\mathrm{sm}}({\bm{s}}_{\ell}){\bm{E}}_{\ell}^{\top}\delta{\bm{u}}_{\ell},$$  |  | (7)
L116: Where $\approx$ denotes a first-order Taylor approximation of the softmax function around ${\bm{s}}_{\ell}$, when the perturbation $\delta{\bm{s}}_{\ell}$ is small, and $J_{\mathrm{sm}}({\bm{s}})=\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{sm}({\bm{s}}))-\operatorname{sm}({\bm{s}})\operatorname{sm}({\bm{s}})^{\top}$ is the Jacobian of softmax and sm is the softmax function.
L117: This relation highlights a crucial observation: only the component of $\delta{\bm{u}}_{\ell}$ that lies in the span of ${\bm{E}}_{\ell}$ influences the routing probabilities, and the Jacobian can amplify such components, potentially destabilizing expert selection. This insight motivates our design, suppressing the projection of perturbations onto $\operatorname{span}({\bm{E}}_{\ell})$ is key to preventing routing drift.
L118: ### 4.2 Per-Expert Null-Space Projection Reparameterization
L119: As established in Section cite10†4.1 , suppressing routing drift amounts to ensuring that $\delta{\bm{u}}_{\ell}\approx\mathbf{0}$ on a preservation set. To achieve this by construction, we reparameterize each expert update so that its effect vanishes along directions spanned by preservation features. Inspired by the null-space constrained approach of AlphaEdit for dense models, we generalize the idea to the MoE setting by computing a per-expert projector that filters out harmful update components.
L120: Concretely, recall from Eqn. cite59†4 that expert $n$ produces output ${\bm{W}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}$ for post-activation key ${\bm{k}}_{i,n}$. Let $\mathcal{P}$ denote the set of preservation prompts, and collect their features for expert $n$ into the matrix ${\bm{K}}^{0}_{n}=\big[{\bm{k}}_{i,n}\big]_{i\in\mathcal{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times|\mathcal{P}|}$. The covariance ${\bm{K}}^{0}_{n}{\bm{K}}^{0\top}_{n}$ captures the subspace of activations we wish to keep invariant.
L121: We compute its eigendecomposition, ${\bm{K}}^{0}_{n}{\bm{K}}^{0\top}_{n}={\bm{U}}_{n}\Lambda_{n}{\bm{U}}_{n}^{\top}$, and select indices $\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{p:\lambda_{n,p}<\tau\}$ corresponding to (near-)null eigenvalues under a small threshold $\tau>0$. Let ${\bm{U}}_{n}^{0}={\bm{U}}_{n}[:,\mathcal{I}_{0}]$ and define the orthogonal projector onto the complement of $\operatorname{span}({\bm{K}}^{0}_{n})$ by ${\bm{P}}_{n}={\bm{U}}_{n}^{0}{\bm{U}}_{n}^{0\top}$.
L122: Intuitively, ${\bm{P}}_{n}$ preserves only those directions orthogonal to all preservation features, so any update projected by ${\bm{P}}_{n}$ is guaranteed not to alter expert outputs on $\mathcal{P}$.
L123: We then reparameterize the expert update as $\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}{\bm{P}}_{n}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}$ is the free variable to be optimized. Because ${\bm{P}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}=\mathbf{0}$ for all $i\in\mathcal{P}$ (up to numerical error), the preservation outputs are unaffected: $\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}{\bm{P}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}=\mathbf{0}$.
L124: Consequently, for every $i\in\mathcal{P}$ we have $\delta{\bm{u}}_{\ell}(i)=\mathbf{0}$, and by Eqn. cite60†7 the induced routing shift satisfies $\delta{\bm{g}}_{\ell}(i)\approx\mathbf{0}$, minimizing Eqn. cite61†6 on the preservation set.
L125: Projected editing objective. Let $\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}={\bm{P}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}$ denote the projected key. Substituting $\Delta_{n}=\hat{\Delta}_{n}P_{n}$ into the MoE objective (Eqn. cite62†5 ) yields
L126:  | $\displaystyle\{\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}$  | $\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\{\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{i,n}\big({\bm{W}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}+\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}\big)-{\bm{v}}_{i}\Big\|^{2}+\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|^{2},$  |  | (8)
L127: where $\lambda\geq 0$ controls the update magnitude and improves locality. No separate preservation term is needed, as ${\bm{P}}_{n}$ removes all preservation components by construction.
L128: ### 4.3 Randomized Block Coordinate Descent Solver
L129: A naive step is to solve the projected objective in Eqn. cite63†8 in one shot, just like what we do in dense model KE (cite40†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite41†Meng et al., 2023 ; cite48†Fang et al., 2025 ). In this subsection, we (i) expose the structure of the global closed-form solution, (ii) explain why the direct route is computationally impractical in MoE, and (iii) arrive at an efficient randomized block coordinate descent (BCD) procedure that scales with the expert hidden size.
L130: The global closed-form (one shot). Let $\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{k}}$ be the projected free variable for expert $n$, and stack all expert updates horizontally as $\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}=[\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{1}\ \cdots\ \hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times(Nd_{k})}$.
L131: For edit example $i$, define the base residual (excluding any edits) ${\bm{r}}_{i}={\bm{v}}_{i}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{i,n}{\bm{W}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}$, and the design vector $\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}=[g_{i,1}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,1}^{\top}\ \cdots\ g_{i,N}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,N}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd_{k}}$, where $\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}={\bm{P}}_{n}{\bm{k}}_{i,n}$.
L132: Then Eqn. cite63†8 becomes a regularized multi-output linear regression: $\min_{\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}}\ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}-{\bm{r}}_{i}\big\|^{2}+\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|^{2}.$ Vectorizing with ${\bm{\theta}}=\operatorname{vec}(\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}Nd_{k}}$ and using $\operatorname{vec}(\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i})=(\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d_{m}}){\bm{\theta}}$, the normal equations take the compact form
L133:  | $$\Bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}^{\top})\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d_{m}}+\lambda{\bm{I}}_{d_{m}Nd_{k}}\Bigg){\bm{\theta}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d_{m}}){\bm{r}}_{i},$$  |  | (9)
L134: 
L135: with unique minimizer
L136: 
L137:  | $${\bm{\theta}}^{\star}={\bm{M}}_{\mathrm{glob}}^{-1}{\bm{b}}_{\mathrm{glob}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}^{\star}=\operatorname{unvec}({\bm{\theta}}^{\star}),$$  |  | (10)
L138: where ${\bm{M}}_{\mathrm{glob}}=\sum_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}^{\top})\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d_{m}}+\lambda{\bm{I}}$ and ${\bm{b}}_{\mathrm{glob}}=\sum_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d_{m}}){\bm{r}}_{i}$. A proof is provided in Appendix cite25†B.2 .
L139: Although Eqn. cite64†9 is elegant, it is not a practical editing primitive at MoE scale. First, even exploiting the Kronecker structure, the system decomposes into $d_{m}$ independent problems of size $(Nd_{k})\times(Nd_{k})$ each. For typical MoE layers, $N$ can be 8–128 and $d_{k}$ in the thousands, so factorizing $d_{m}$ such systems (and re-factorizing as $\mathcal{E}$ changes) is prohibitively expensive in both time $O\big(d_{m}(Nd_{k})^{3}\big)$ and memory $O\big(d_{m}(Nd_{k})^{2}\big)$.
L140: Second, while Top-$K$ routing makes each $\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}$ $K$-block sparse, the accumulated Gram matrix $\sum_{i}\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\tilde{\bm{\psi}}_{i}^{\top}$ quickly densifies, yielding substantial fill-in under Cholesky/LDL^{⊤}. These realities make the one-shot solve not suitable for fast, iterative MoE editing.
L141: From global to block: randomized BCD. Since the insight that every expert is a natural chunk. The structure of Eqn. cite63†8 suggests a block strategy: treat each expert as a block and optimize one block while holding the rest fixed. This reduces the problem to a sequence of well-conditioned, small ridge least-squares solves of size $d_{k}\times d_{k}$.
L142: 
L143: Fix $\{\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\neq n}$ and define the residual that excludes expert $n$:
L144:  | $${\bm{r}}_{i}^{(-n)}={\bm{v}}_{i}-\sum_{\ell\neq n}g_{i,\ell}\big({\bm{W}}_{\ell}{\bm{k}}_{i,\ell}+\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\ell}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,\ell}\big).$$  |  | (11)
L145: 
L146: The subproblem in $\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}$ becomes the ridge-regularized least squares
L147: 
L148:  | $$\min_{\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}\big\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(-n)}-g_{i,n}\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}\big\|^{2}+\lambda\|\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|^{2}.$$  |  | (12)
L149: 
L150: Its normal equations
L151:  | $$\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}g_{i,n}^{2}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}^{\top}+\lambda{\bm{I}}\Big)}_{{\bm{M}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d_{k}}}=\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}g_{i,n}{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(-n)}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}^{\top}\Big)}_{{\bm{B}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{k}}},$$  |  | (13)
L152: 
L153: admit the closed-form update
L154:  | $$\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}^{\star}={\bm{B}}_{n}{\bm{M}}_{n}^{-1}=\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}g_{i,n}{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(-n)}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}^{\top}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}g_{i,n}^{2}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}^{\top}+\lambda{\bm{I}}\Big)^{-1}.$$  |  | (14)
L155: 
L156: We then write to parameters via the projection $\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\star}=\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}^{\star}{\bm{P}}_{n}$ and move to the next block.
L157: Practicalities and complexity. We traverse experts in randomized order and update only those active in the current minibatch, which further reduces cost. For each updated expert, forming ${\bm{M}}_{n}$ costs $O(|\mathcal{E}|d_{k}^{2})$ and inverting it costs $O(d_{k}^{3})$, typically modest since $d_{k}\ll d_{m}$. We stream-accumulate ${\bm{B}}_{n}$ and ${\bm{M}}_{n}$, cache $\tilde{{\bm{k}}}_{i,n}$, and use Cholesky with diagonal loading for numerical stability.
L158: Because Eqn. cite63†8 is a strictly convex quadratic in $\{\hat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\}$, (randomized) BCD with exact block solves converges globally under standard conditions (cite65†Tseng, 2001 ; cite66†Richtárik & Takáč, 2014 ). Empirically we see fast decrease within a few passes ($\leq 10$).
L159: ## 5 Experiments
L160: ### 5.1 Baselines, Datasets, and Metrics
L161: We evaluate two modern MoE LLMs on standard factual-editing benchmarks: Qwen3-30B-A3B (cite67†Yang et al., 2025 ) (128 experts; top-8 per token) and GPT-OSS-20B (cite68†Agarwal et al., 2025 ) (32 experts; top-4 per token). As baselines, we adapt parameter-editing methods originally designed for dense Transformers but directly applicable to MoE models: Fine-Tuning (FT) (cite69†Zhu et al., 2020 ), FT-L (FT with a norm constraint), AdaLoRA (cite70†Zhang et al., 2023 ), and UnKE (cite71†Deng et al., 2025 ).
L162: Following prior work, we use COUNTERFACT (single-hop counterfactual edits introduced with MEMIT) (cite41†Meng et al., 2023 ) and ZsRE (zero-shot relation extraction) (cite72†Levy et al., 2017 ). Visualized dataset examples are provided in Appendix cite29†D for unfamiliar readers.
L163: We report the standard editing metrics (cite40†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite41†Meng et al., 2023 ; cite42†Mitchell et al., 2022a ): (i) Efficacy (edit success on edited prompts), (ii) Generalization (success on paraphrases and lightly perturbed contexts), and (iii) Specificity (locality on unrelated controls). Unless stated otherwise, we perform sequential batched edits. And to summarize the overall trade-off, we additionally report Utility as the mean of the three metrics.
L164: See Appendix cite20†A for full calculation details.

L166: 
L167: We perform 1,000 sequential edits on each dataset (COUNTERFACT and ZsRE) with a batch size of 50 edits for all methods. Table cite73†1 summarizes results on Qwen3-30B-A3B and GPT-OSS-20B.
