L656: #### A.4.4 Which TTS strategy works depends on problem structure
L657: Recent empirical studies argue that test-time compute does not translate into uniform gains: different tasks benefit from different allocations across sampling, self-correction, verifier-guided selection, or adaptive procedures. cite120†Wu et al. (2024) motivates one of our central modeling choices: the “difficulty” of a theorem should not be summarized solely by a single scalar (e.g., length), but also by structure—including decomposability and availability of useful lemmas in the library.
L658: #### A.4.5 Verifiers can change scaling laws
L659: Verifier-guided selection is often the mechanism that makes TTS effective in high-stakes settings. Several authors (cite121†Beirami et al., 2025 ; cite122†Setlur et al., 2025 ; cite123†Botta et al., 2025 ) formalize Best-of-$N$ (BoN) selection and characterize how performance depends on verifier quality. They argue for separations between verifier-based and verifier-free approaches under comparable compute/data budgets.
L660: In theorem proving, the proof assistant kernel plays the role of a near-perfect verifier for syntactic correctness, but “semantic” verifiers (e.g., premise relevance, lemma usefulness, intermediate progress) may be imperfect—so the verifier-quality dependence remains central.
L661: ### A.5 Statistical theory with computational constraints
L662: Classical computational learning theory already highlights the role of interaction and queries. A canonical example is Angluin’s $L^{\ast}$ algorithm (cite124†Angluin, 1987 ) for learning regular languages from membership and equivalence queries to a “teacher,” connecting sample efficiency to an interactive protocol rather than passive i.i.d. data.
L663: This viewpoint is highly suggestive for theorem proving: the proof assistant and solver feedback constitute an interactive information channel, and the agent’s objective is to adaptively extract the right information to complete a proof.
L664: More recently, several directions attempt to incorporate computational constraints into information and generalization theory. For instance, cite125†Finzi et al. (2026) proposes “epiplexity”, an information-like quantity intended to capture what computationally bounded observers can extract from data, explicitly motivated by tensions between classical information theory and modern practice.
L665: Separately, several authors (cite126†Strobl et al., 2024 ) relate transformer expressivity to formal languages and circuit classes, offering a vocabulary to discuss which forms of reasoning are representationally plausible under given architectural and precision constraints.
L666: Positioning. Our work sits at the intersection: we analyze theorem proving as an interactive sequential decision process, where success depends on both (i) computational/proof-theoretic structure (e.g., the availability of short proofs via cut/lemmas) and (ii) statistical/learning-theoretic quantities suited to adaptive interaction (sequential complexity, covering numbers).
L667: We do not claim a full unification, but we aim to put these components into a single framework where their contributions to proof success probability can be compared on the same scale.
L668: ## Appendix B Proof of cite66†Theorem 1 : Compactness of $M_{\leq W}(K)$ under $d_{\mathrm{BL}}$
L669: 
L670: We prove cite66†Theorem 1 .
L671: 
L672: ###### Lemma 6 (Arzelà–Ascoli compactness of the BL unit ball).
L673: 
L674: Let
L675: 
L676:  | $$\mathcal{F}:=\{f:K\to\mathbb{R}:\ \|f\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq 1\}.$$  |
L677: 
L678: Then $\mathcal{F}$ is compact in $(C(K),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})$.
L679: 
L680: ###### Proof.
L681: 
L682: Functions in $\mathcal{F}$ are uniformly bounded ($\|f\|\leq 1$) and equicontinuous (Lipschitz constant $\leq 1$). Since $K$ is compact, Arzelà–Ascoli applies. ∎
L683: ###### Lemma 7 ($d_{\mathrm{BL}}$ metrizes weak convergence on $M_{\leq W}(K)$).
L684: 
L685: Let $(\mu_{n})$ and $\mu$ be in $M_{\leq W}(K)$. Then
L686: 
L687:  | $$\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu\ \text{(weakly)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0.$$  |
L688: ###### Proof.
L689: 
L690: we show
L691: 
L692:   * •
L693: 
L694: ($\Leftarrow$) If $d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0$, then $\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\to 0$ for all bounded Lipschitz $f$. Bounded Lipschitz functions are dense in $C(K)$ on compact metric spaces, so convergence holds for all continuous $f$, i.e., $\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu$.
L695: 
L696:   * •
L697: ($\Rightarrow$) Assume $\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu$. Suppose $d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\not\to 0$. Then there exists $\varepsilon>0$ and a subsequence (still indexed by $n$) and $f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}$ such that
L698: 
L699:  | $$\left|\int f_{n}d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }n.$$  |
L700: 
L701: By Lemma A.1, $(f_{n})$ has a further subsequence converging uniformly to some $f\in\mathcal{F}$. Then
L702:  | $\displaystyle\left|\int f_{n}d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|$  | $\displaystyle\leq\left|\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|+\left|\int(f_{n}-f)d(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|$  |
L703:  |  | $\displaystyle\leq\left|\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\right|+\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.$  |
L704: Since $\mu_{n},\mu$ are positive with mass $\leq W$, $\|\mu_{n}-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mu_{n}(K)+\mu(K)\leq 2W$, and $\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty}\to 0$. Also, $f$ is continuous so $\int fd(\mu_{n}-\mu)\to 0$ by weak convergence. Hence the right-hand side tends to $0$, contradicting $\geq\varepsilon$.
L705: 
L706: Therefore $d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0$. ∎
L707: ###### Lemma 8 (Compactness).
L708: 
L709: $M_{\leq W}(K)$ is compact under $d_{\mathrm{BL}}$.
L710: 
L711: ###### Proof.
L712: 
L713: $M_{\leq W}(K)$ is tight and weakly sequentially compact (by Prokhorov; on compact $K$, tightness is automatic). Any sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence; by Lemma A.2 that subsequence is Cauchy and convergent in $d_{\mathrm{BL}}$. Hence sequential compactness, hence compactness. ∎
L714: 
L715: This completes the proof of cite66†Theorem 1 .

L737: ## Appendix D Occupancy distribution, margin condition, fast rates, and what if margin fails
L738: ### D.1 Occupancy distribution $\nu_{b}$: definition and meaning
L739: 
L740: In finite-horizon control, the occupancy distribution captures where the algorithm actually visits, and is the correct object for distributional assumptions (like margins).
L741: 
L742: For a (possibly randomized) policy $\pi$ and an initial distribution $Q$ over states, define the depth-$b$ state occupancy as the law of $X_{b-1}$:
L743: 
L744:  | $$\nu_{b}^{\pi}(\cdot):=\Pr^{\pi}(X_{b-1}\in\cdot\mid X_{0}\sim Q).$$  |
L745: For reachability tasks, it is often more appropriate to condition on “not solved yet”:
L746: 
L747:  | $$\tilde{\nu}_{b}^{\pi}(\cdot):=\Pr^{\pi}(X_{b-1}\in\cdot\mid X_{0}\sim Q,\ T^{\pi}\geq b),$$  |
L748: 
L749: because decisions after termination are irrelevant.
L750: 
L751: For beam search, one can analogously define an occupancy over expanded nodes at depth $b$ induced by the beam’s node-selection rule; the key point is the same: $\nu_{b}$ is the distribution of states that matter at depth $b$ under the algorithm.
L752: ### D.2 Margin condition: what it says
L753: 
L754: Let $Q_{b}^{*}(x,\cdot)$ be the optimal one-step value at depth $b$. For top-$k$ analysis, define the $(k+1)$-gap
L755: 
L756:  | $$\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(x):=Q_{b}^{*}(x,a_{1}^{*}(x))-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a_{k+1}^{*}(x)),$$  |
L757: 
L758: where actions are ranked by $Q_{b}^{*}$.
L759: 
L760: The margin condition (cite128†Arlot and Bartlett, 2011 , Mammen-Tsybakov-style, see e.g.) states that near-ties are rare:
L761: 
L762:  | $$\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(k)}(X)\leq t)\ \leq\ C_{\Delta}t^{\beta}\quad\text{for small }t.$$  |
L763: Interpretation:
L764: 
L765:   * •
L766: 
L767: $\beta$ large $\Rightarrow$ “clear separation”: ambiguous states are rare.
L768: 
L769:   * •
L770: 
L771: $\beta$ small (or $0$) $\Rightarrow$ “many near-ties”: small score errors often change rankings.
L772: 
L773: This is exactly the condition under which plug-in decision rules achieve fast rates in classification, transplanted here to action selection.
L774: ### D.3 Proof of cite129†Theorem 5 : fast-rate proof (for the plug-in greedy policy)
L775: 
L776: To make the argument transparent, we first present the $k=1$ (greedy) case; the top-$k$ version is the same with $\Delta_{b}^{(k)}$.
L777: 
L778: Assume a uniform score approximation at depth $b$:
L779: 
L780:  | $\displaystyle\sup_{x,a\in\mathcal{D}}|h_{b}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)|\leq\varepsilon.$  |  | (1)
L781: Let $\hat{a}(x)\in\arg\max_{a}h_{b}(x,a)$ and $a^{*}(x)\in\arg\max_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)$. Define the gap $\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x):=Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*}(x))-\max_{a\neq a^{*}(x)}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)$.
L782: ###### Lemma 9 (Mis-selection implies small gap).
L783: 
L784: Under cite130†Equation 1 , if $\hat{a}(x)\neq a^{*}(x)$ then $\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon$.
L785: ###### Proof.
L786: 
L787: If $\hat{a}\neq a^{*}$, then
L788: 
L789:  | $$Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*})\leq h_{b}(x,a^{*})+\varepsilon\leq h_{b}(x,\hat{a})+\varepsilon\leq Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a})+2\varepsilon.$$  |
L790: 
L791: Thus $Q_{b}^{*}(x,a^{*})-Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a})\leq 2\varepsilon$. Since $\hat{a}$ is not optimal, the left side is at least the gap $\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)$, giving $\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon$. ∎
L792: ###### Lemma 10 (Pointwise regret bound).
L793: 
L794: Define the one-step regret at $x$:
L795: 
L796:  | $$r_{b}(x):=\max_{a}Q_{b}^{*}(x,a)-Q_{b}^{*}(x,\hat{a}(x)).$$  |
L797: 
L798: Then under cite130†Equation 1 ,
L799: 
L800:  | $$r_{b}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon\ \mathbf{1}{\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon}.$$  |
L801: ###### Proof.
L802: 
L803: If $\hat{a}=a^{*}$, then $r_{b}(x)=0$. Otherwise, by cite131†Lemma 9 , $\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon$ and also $r_{b}(x)\leq\Delta_{b}^{(1)}(x)\leq 2\varepsilon$. Combine. ∎
L804: 
L805: Now take expectation under occupancy $\nu_{b}$:
L806: 
L807:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{b}}[r_{b}(X)]\leq 2\varepsilon\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(1)}\leq 2\varepsilon)\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot C_{\Delta}(2\varepsilon)^{\beta}=O(\varepsilon^{\beta+1}).$$  |
L808: This is the fast rate: the expected regret is smaller than linear in $\varepsilon$ when $\beta>0$.
L809: 
L810: Finally, summing over depths (and propagating through Bellman recursion) yields an overall provability gap bound of the form
L811: 
L812:  | $$\mathbb{E}[V_{B}^{*}(X_{0})-V_{B}^{\pi_{h}}(X_{0})]\ \lesssim\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{b}}[r_{b}(X)]\ =\ O\left(B\varepsilon^{\beta+1}\right),$$  |
L813: 
L814: up to constants and technical details about termination (see Section 4 below).
L815: ###### Remark 2 (Top-$k$ / beam version).
L816: 
L817: Replace $\Delta_{b}^{(1)}$ by $\Delta_{b}^{(k)}$. The same logic shows:
L818: 
L819:   * •
L820: 
L821: if the optimal action is pruned from top-$k$, then $\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon$;
L822: 
L823:   * •
L824: 
L825: the loss incurred by missing the optimal action is at most $\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon$ on that event;
L826: 
L827:   * •
L828: 
L829: thus expected loss scales as $\varepsilon\cdot\nu_{b}(\Delta_{b}^{(k)}\leq 2\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{\beta+1})$.
L830: ### D.4 If the margin condition fails: what can we still say?
L831: 
L832: If the margin tail does not decay (effectively $\beta=0$), then the above argument collapses to the worst-case linear behavior:
L833: 
L834:  | $$\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{b}}[r_{b}(X)]\ \lesssim\ O(\varepsilon).$$  |
L835: 
L836: More pessimistically, if there is non-negligible mass of exact ties (e.g., $\nu_{b}(\Delta=0)>0$), then ranking-based selection can be intrinsically unstable: arbitrarily small perturbations can flip the argmax on that set. In that case:
L837: 
L838:   * •
L839: fast rates are not information-theoretically available from plug-in ranking alone,
L840: 
L841:   * •
L842: 
L843: and one must rely on search (keeping multiple candidates), additional structure (tie-breaking information, richer feedback), or accept slower rates.
L844: Beam/top-$k$ can still help when margins fail, but then the analysis must track set coverage rather than “gap-based stability.” A common alternative is to bound success in terms of the probability that at least one of the retained actions is “good enough,” which leads to different regularity assumptions (e.g., mass of $\epsilon$-optimal actions) rather than gap tails.
L845: ## Appendix E Proof of cite73†Lemma 4 (Adaptive uniform deviation via covering numbers)
L846: 
L847: Statement. Let $(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}$ be a filtration and let $Z_{t}$ be $\mathcal{F}_{t}$-measurable random variables taking values in a set $\mathcal{Z}$. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a class of functions $f:\mathcal{Z}\to[0,1]$. For $f\in\mathcal{F}$, define the martingale difference
L848: 
L849:  | $$D_{t}(f):=f(Z_{t})-\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].$$  |
L850: Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and let $\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{M}\}$ be an $\varepsilon$-net of $\mathcal{F}$ in $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$ (so $M=\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon;\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})$). Then for any $\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
L851: 
L852:  | $$\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq 2\varepsilon+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}}.$$  |
L853: ###### Proof.
L854: 
L855: we show
L856: 
L857:   1. 1.
L858: 
L859: Reduction to a finite net. For any $f\in\mathcal{F}$, choose $f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}$ such that $\|f-f_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon$. Then for each $t$,
L860:  | $\displaystyle|D_{t}(f)-D_{t}(f_{i})|$  | $\displaystyle=\left|\big(f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})\big)-\mathbb{E}\big[f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\right|$  |
L861:  |  | $\displaystyle\leq|f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})|+\mathbb{E}\big[|f(Z_{t})-f_{i}(Z_{t})|\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]$  |
L862:  |  | $\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\varepsilon=2\varepsilon.$  |
L863: 
L864: Averaging over $t$ gives
L865: 
L866:  | $$\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|+2\varepsilon.$$  |
L867: Hence
L868: 
L869:  | $$\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq M}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|+2\varepsilon.$$  |
L870: 
L871:   2. 2.
L872: 
L873: Concentration for each net element. For fixed $i$, the sequence $D_{t}(f_{i})$ is a martingale difference adapted to $\mathcal{F}_{t}$ and satisfies $D_{t}(f_{i})\in[-1,1]$ because $f_{i}(Z_{t})\in[0,1]$ and its conditional expectation lies in $[0,1]$. By Azuma–Hoeffding,
L874:  | $$\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|\geq n\gamma\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\gamma^{2}}{2}\right).$$  |
L875: 
L876:   3. 3.
L877: 
L878: Union bound. By union bound over $i=1,\dots,M$,
L879: 
L880:  | $$\Pr\left(\max_{i\leq M}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f_{i})\right|\geq\gamma\right)\leq 2M\exp\left(-\frac{n\gamma^{2}}{2}\right).$$  |
L881: 
L882: Set the right-hand side to $\delta$ and solve:
L883: 
L884:  | $$\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}}.$$  |
L885: 
L886:   4. 4.
L887: Combine steps. With this choice of $\gamma$, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
L888: 
L889:  | $$\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}(f)\right|\leq 2\varepsilon+\gamma,$$  |
L890: 
L891: which is the desired bound.
L892: 
L893: ∎
L894: ## Appendix F Proof of cite132†Theorem 6 (High-probability uniform approximation)
L895: 
L896: Technically, this theorem implicitly mixes two issues:
L897: 
L898:   1. 1.
L899: 
L900: controlling estimation error under adaptive sampling, and
L901: 
L902:   2. 2.
L903: 
L904: converting that to a uniform-in-domain sup norm bound.
L905: ### F.1 Setting
L906: 
L907:   * •
L908: 
L909: Let $\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}$ be compact.
L910: 
L911:   * •
L912: 
L913: Let $Q:\mathcal{D}\to[0,1]$ denote the target function (in the main text, $Q=Q_{b}^{*}$).
L914: 
L915:   * •
L916: 
L917: Assume $Q$ is $L_{Q}$-Lipschitz on $\mathcal{D}$.
L918: 
L919:   * •
L920: 
L921: Let $\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathrm{Lip}_{L_{H}}(\mathcal{D},[0,1])$ be the hypothesis class.
L922: 
L923:   * •
L924: 
L925: Define approximation error
L926: 
L927:  | $$\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}:=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\ \|h-Q\|_{\infty}.$$  |
L928: ### F.2 Sampling assumption (coverage / generative access)
L929: 
L930: There exist an $\eta$-net $\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}$ of $\mathcal{D}$ (with $N=\mathcal{N}(\eta;\mathcal{D})$) and, for each net point $z_{i}$, we can collect $m$ conditionally unbiased samples
L931: 
L932:  | $$Y_{i,1},\dots,Y_{i,m}\in[0,1],\qquad\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,j}\mid z_{i}]=Q(z_{i}),$$  |
L933: 
L934: independent across $j$ given $z_{i}$. (In practice, $Y_{i,j}$ can be a rollout success indicator from $(\mu,a)=z_{i}$.)
L935: Let $\hat{q}_{i}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}Y_{i,j}$.
L936: ### F.3 Constructing an estimator
L937: 
L938: Define $\hat{h}$ as a Lipschitz extension of the values $\{\hat{q}_{i}\}$ onto $\mathcal{D}$ with Lipschitz constant at most $L_{H}$ (e.g., a McShane extension). Then $\hat{h}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{L_{H}}(\mathcal{D},[0,1])$. If we further project $\hat{h}$ onto $\mathcal{H}$ (or assume $\mathcal{H}$ contains such extensions), we can ensure $\hat{h}\in\mathcal{H}$ up to $\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}$.
L939: 
L940: ### F.4 Statement (Uniform sup norm bound; explicit form)
L941: ###### Theorem 7 (cite132†Theorem 6 , refined).
L942: 
L943: Under the setting above, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
L944: 
L945:  | $\displaystyle\|\hat{h}-Q\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+(L_{H}+L_{Q})\eta+\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2m}}.$  |  | (2)
L946: 
L947: In particular, if $\mathcal{D}$ has doubling dimension $d$ so that $N\lesssim(C/\eta)^{d}$ and $n:=mN$ is the total number of samples, optimizing $\eta$ yields (up to polylog factors)
L948:  | $$\|\hat{h}-Q\|_{\infty}\ \lesssim\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}+(L_{H}+L_{Q})^{\frac{d}{d+2}}\left(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+2}}.$$  |
L949: ###### Proof.
L950: 
L951: Fix an $\eta$-net $\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ of $\mathcal{D}$. For each $i$, by Hoeffding’s inequality,
L952: 
L953:  | $$\Pr\left(|\hat{q}_{i}-Q(z_{i})|\geq\tau\right)\leq 2\exp(-2m\tau^{2}).$$  |
L954: 
L955: Union bound over $i=1,\dots,N$ gives: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
L956: 
L957:  | $\displaystyle\max_{i\leq N}|\hat{q}_{i}-Q(z_{i})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2m}}.$  |  | (3)
L958: 
L959: Now fix any $z\in\mathcal{D}$. Let $z_{i}$ be a net point with $d(z,z_{i})\leq\eta$. Then
L960:  | $\displaystyle|\hat{h}(z)-Q(z)|$  | $\displaystyle\leq|\hat{h}(z)-\hat{h}(z_{i})|+|\hat{h}(z_{i})-Q(z_{i})|+|Q(z_{i})-Q(z)|$  |
L961:  |  | $\displaystyle\leq L_{H}d(z,z_{i})+|\hat{q}_{i}-Q(z_{i})|+L_{Q}d(z,z_{i})$  |
L962:  |  | $\displaystyle\leq(L_{H}+L_{Q})\eta+\max_{j\leq N}|\hat{q}_{j}-Q(z_{j})|.$  |
L963: Taking supremum over $z\in\mathcal{D}$ and using cite133†Equation 3 yields the bound for the extension estimator. If $\hat{h}$ is then projected into $\mathcal{H}$, an additional $\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}$ term appears by triangle inequality, giving cite134†Equation 2 . ∎
L964: ###### Remark 3 (Relation to cite73†Lemma 4 / sequential complexity.).
L965: 
L966: cite135†Theorem 7 is a clean sufficient condition relying on explicit coverage. cite73†Lemma 4 is more general: it controls deviations under adaptive sampling and can be used to bound on-policy or occupation-weighted errors when explicit net coverage is unavailable. In this paper, both perspectives are useful:
L967: 
L968:   * •
L969: 
L970: net-coverage yields uniform sup norm bounds (strongest),
L971: 
L972:   * •
