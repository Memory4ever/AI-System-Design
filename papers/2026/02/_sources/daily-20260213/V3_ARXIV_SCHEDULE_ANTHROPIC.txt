L166:   1. cite2†Home L167:   2. cite78†Policies L168: # Availability of submissions
L169: 
L170: Work submitted to arXiv undergoes a series of quality assurance checks as part of cite83†moderation . This helps authors by ensuring their work will be discoverable and archivable and helps readers find relevant research that is in a consistent, readable format.
L171: Quality assurance checks can take between one to four days to resolve, sometimes longer. Authors will be notified by email if any issues are found in the submission. Authors will also receive an email when the submission is posted publicly.
L172: Submissions to arXiv are typically posted publicly Sunday through Thursday, with no announcements Friday or Saturday. You can check the cite88†current arXiv time†arxiv.org , which also shows the next deadline. Submissions are made public as part of the cite27†scheduled announcement process . This includes new submissions as well as cite35†replacements , cite44†withdrawal notices , cite30†cross listings and cite32†journal reference .
L173: ## A note about arXiv-id assignments
L174: 
L175: The final cite49†arXiv identifier is assigned as part of the automated process when the work is announced. It is not possible to generate or to be provided with the arXiv identifier or DOI in advance. Submitting authors will receive an email when the work is announced and it will appear on their account dashboard.
L176: Note: The arXiv identifier cannot be back-dated, so identifiers will be assigned in the month of first announcement. It may be the case that a submission appears in a different identifier month than its original submission date due to various factors.

L63@P2-3: 31 Introduction
L64@P3: This risk report argues that Claude Opus 4.6 does not pose a significant risk of autonomous
L65@P3: actions that contribute significantly to later catastrophic outcomes, which we refer to as
L66@P3: sabotage risk. We limit our scope here to threats caused largely by model actions in this
L67@P3: way, and do not address threat models where intentional harmful actions by humans play a
L68@P3: central role.We argue that the overall risk is very low but not negligible.
L69@P3: This report follows up on a note in the system card for Claude Opus 4.6, which stated:
L70@P3: … we [have] committed to writing sabotage risk reports that meet the AI
L71@P3: R&D-4 RSP standard for all future frontier models exceeding Opus 4.5’s
L72@P3: capabilities, so as not to rely on difficult and subjective assessments of
L73@P3: model capabilities like the one above for compliance with the RSP. As such,
L74@P3: we have prepared a Sabotage Risk Report assessing sabotage-related risks
L75@P3: posed by Claude Opus 4.6 and the safeguards we have in place to address
L76@P3: them, which will be published shortly after the launch of Opus 4.6.
L77@P3: Some text has been redacted in this public version of the report, either because we expect
L78@P3: the unredacted text reveals information that would directly increase misuse-related risk, or
L79@P3: because it would reveal commercially sensitive or proprietary information to a degree not
L80@P3: justified by any commensurate public benefit. Redacted text has been made available to
L81@P3: Anthropic’s internal Stress-Testing Team—alongside technical employees of Anthropic
L82@P3: more broadly—and will be made available to some external reviewers. All such reviewers,
L83@P3: internal and external, will be asked to comment on the appropriateness of the redactions.
L84@P3-4: 42 Overview
L85@P4: Threat model Sabotage is when an AI model with access to powerful
L86@P4: affordances within an organization uses its affordances to
L87@P4: autonomously exploit, manipulate, or tamper with that
L88@P4: organization’s systems or decision-making in a way that raises
L89@P4: the risk of future catastrophic outcomes (e.g. by altering the
L90@P4: results of AI safety research, either inadvertently or due to its
L91@P4: pursuit of dangerous goals).
L92@P4: Relevant AI model We focus our analysis on Claude Opus 4.6, our most capable
L93@P4: model and the one used most internally.
L94@P4: Current usage and
L95@P4: capabilities
L96@P4: Claude Opus 4.6 is used heavily within Anthropic for coding, data
L97@P4: generation, and other agentic use cases. We do not believe it has
L98@P4: dangerous coherent goals that would raise the risk of sabotage,
L99@P4: nor that its deception capabilities rise to the level of invalidating
L100@P4: our evidence.
L101@P4: Current risk mitigations Internal monitoring and security controls, as well as alignment
L102@P4: audits to assess the possibility of dangerous goals.
L103@P4: Looking forward We hope to improve assessments and mitigations over time to
L104@P4: account for increased model subversion capabilities.
L105@P4: Overall risk assessment Very low but not negligible
L106@P4-5: 53 Threat model
L107@P5: The threat: If AI models are heavily used by powerful organizations like AI developers or
L108@P5: governments—for example, to write a large amount of critical code—they might take
L109@P5: advantage of this access to manipulate decision-making, insert and exploit cybersecurity
L110@P5: vulnerabilities, and take other actions that could significantly raise the risk of future
L111@P5: catastrophic outcomes from AI. This could be because AI models strategically and
L112@P5: persistently pursue dangerous goals, because they occasionally pursue such goals in some
L113@P5: situations, or because they make more innocent mistakes in particularly high-stakes
L114@P5: situations.
L115@P5: Likelihood of impact: This is sensitive to model capabilities and usage patterns, which we
L116@P5: address below. To give illustrative example cases (we discuss how we see the current state
L117@P5: of affairs below):
L118@P5: ● If AI models were incapable of reliably executing complex technical tasks, and were
L119@P5: exclusively used in contexts where humans are heavily involved in reviewing and
L120@P5: iterating on their work, then we would consider this sort of impact to be very
L121@P5: unlikely.
L122@P5: ● If AI models were routinely used to carry out significant technical workflows with
L123@P5: very little human oversight - analogous to senior technical human employees - then
L124@P5: we believe the impact of concern would be highly plausible. The likelihood would
L125@P5: further depend on (a) the extent to which AIs had dangerous goals (including
L126@P5: intermittent ones); (b) the extent to which they were subject to monitoring and
L127@P5: oversight.
L128@P5: Why this is a priority threat:
L129@P5: ● We believe that the potential magnitude of impact is in range of the very top risks
L130@P5: we are concerned about, and that the impact is at least plausible if we assume AI
L131@P5: capabilities roughly in line with those of human research scientists at Anthropic. AI
L132@P5: models with these capabilities would likely be routinely used to carry out significant
L133@P5: technical workflows with very little human oversight, per above.
L134@P5: ● We also believe that prioritizing this threat will give us early experience with the
L135@P5: kinds of problems we will need to tackle to get ahead of some of the additional risks
L136@P5: we prioritize (see below).
L137@P5: We go through more detail on various threat pathways below, when discussing our overall
L138@P5: assessment of risk.
L139@P5-6: 64 Current state of model capabilities and behaviors
L140@P6: Claude Opus 4.6 is being widely deployed within Anthropic for a wide range of use cases
L141@P6: across R&D, model training, security, and deployment safeguards, in settings both with and
L142@P6: without human operators. Like our other frontier models, it was deployed first within
L143@P6: Anthropic, then released externally. We know less about the precise nature of its use and
L144@P6: the use of similar past models by other actors, but we believe that it will be used to a
L145@P6: substantial degree by the general public, by major governments, and by major technology
L146@P6: companies—including, in some cases, other frontier AI developers. It is highly capable,
L147@P6: though not fully reliable, at a wide range of tasks that would take hours for a human
L148@P6: specialist in the absence of LLM assistance. It is reliable at a much smaller set of generally
L149@P6: simpler tasks. The Claude Opus 4.6 System Card presents our pre-deployment capability
L150@P6: evaluations.
L151@P6: In the remainder of this section, we assess the potential risks that its capabilities and
L152@P6: behavioral traits could pose. We do not address how these risks might be mitigated by
L153@P6: deployment-time safeguards and other countermeasures, instead addressing those in our
L154@P6: risk mitigations and overall assessment sections below.
L155@P6: We focus primarily on assessing the possibility of dangerous coherent misaligned goals: the
L156@P6: possibility that the model consistently, across a wide range of interactions in ordinary
L157@P6: deployment, could be motivated by goals that could lead it to commit a
L158@P6: potentially-catastrophic form of sabotage.
L159@P6: This could take the form of, for example, routinely assessing whether high-stakes
L160@P6: interactions give it a chance to disable its monitoring systems and acting in ways that
L161@P6: maximize the chance that it is able to do so. Similarly, it could also take the form of subtly
L162@P6: misrepresenting research results in research tasks in ways that, as these
L163@P6: misrepresentations compound over time, lead us to place unwarranted trust in some
L164@P6: safeguard that the model later exploits. In both cases, a coherent model would be acting
L165@P6: flexibly to pursue its goal across relatively disparate situations, and would be taking
L166@P6: opportunities to prevent its goal from being discovered.
L167@P6: Coherent misaligned goals are not a strict prerequisite for all forms of sabotage. But, if we
L168@P6: are able to make a strong case that Claude Opus 4.6 does not have dangerous coherent
L169@P6: misaligned goals, the task of ruling out sabotage threat models becomes simpler: we believe
L170@P6: this makes it highly unlikely that the model is systematically resisting our attempts to study
L171@P6: it or apply safeguards. In this section, we argue that Claude Opus 4.6 does not have any
L172@P6: such dangerous coherent misaligned goals.
L173@P6-7: 7We also consider dangerous context-dependent misalignment, where a model takes on
L174@P7: some dangerous goal in a narrow, limited subset of interactions, but would not—in other
L175@P7: situations—act to further that goal or to hide it. We argue, using some of the same
L176@P7: evidence, that dangerous forms of context-dependent misalignment, if they are present at
L177@P7: all, are not common.
L178@P7: Our core argument around misaligned goals includes three key claims, made in the next
L179@P7: three sections:
L180@P7: ● Claim 1: Any model trained under our current standard practices in the near future
L181@P7: is relatively unlikely to have dangerous coherent misaligned goals.
L182@P7: ● Claim 2: Our alignment assessment, drawing on simulated scenarios, interpretability
L183@P7: investigations, monitoring of internal and external pilot usage, and external testing,
L184@P7: found no evidence of dangerous coherent misaligned goals in Claude Opus 4.6.
L185@P7: ● Claim 3: Claude Opus 4.6 is very unlikely to be capable of hiding dangerous coherent
L186@P7: misaligned goals from us in the face of such an assessment.
L187@P7: No one of these claims is sufficient in our view to rule out dangerous forms of coherent
