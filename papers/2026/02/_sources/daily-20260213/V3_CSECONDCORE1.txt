L85: When only the biases of the neural network are initialized as $\equiv 0$, individual neurons still behave differently, allowing effective learning. For this reason, it is standard practice to set all the biases $\equiv 0$ at initalization. The most common initialization methods (cite63†Glorot and Bengio, 2010 ; cite64†He et al., 2015 ) therefore only randomly initialize the weight matrices while keeping the biases $\equiv 0$.
L86: In this paper, we establish novel theoretical results for random unbiased Leaky ReLU networks leading to better behaved initializations especially in low dimensions and when the depth of the neural network is larger than its width.
L87: To introduce notation, suppose we have an unbiased neural network of fixed width $d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}$ where a depth-$\ell$ layer is defined iteratively for $\ell\geq 1$ as
L88: 
L89:  | $$X_{\ell}=\phi(W_{\ell}X_{\ell-1})\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad X_{0}=x_{0},$$  |  | (1)
L90: where $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the input, $W_{\ell}\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$ is a $d\times d$ weight matrix and $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is an activation function applied component-wise. The question addressed is from which distribution the initial weight matrices $W_{\ell}$ should be chosen.
L91: This paper establishes unique and remarkable properties of Leaky ReLU networks that do not extend to other activation functions, making a strong case for their use in specific applications. We proceed with exposing our theoretical results and then outline the resulting novel initialization method.

L93: #### Law of Large Numbers.
L94: 
L95: Throughout the rest of this paper, we consider a Leaky ReLU activation function defined for a fixed $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$ as
L96: 
L97:  | $$\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$$  |  | (2)
L98: 
L99: for $x\in\mathbb{R}$. A main insight of this article is that while it is difficult to study the distribution of $|X_{\ell}|$ directly, we can instead study $\log|X_{\ell}|$, where $|\circ|$ is the euclidean norm.
L100: Indeed, we will show in Theorem cite66†3.3 the following analogue of the Law of Large Numbers: If $\mu$ is a distribution on the space of matrices satisfying suitable assumptions and the weight matrices $W_{\ell}$ are sampled independently from $\mu$, then there exists a number $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}$ such that almost surely
L101: 
L102:  | $$\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}|\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\lambda_{\mu,\phi}.$$  |  | (3)
L103: In the case where $\phi$ is the identity, $X_{\ell}$ is simply a product of independent random matrices applied to a vector. In the latter case, (cite67†3 ) is well-know and was first established under certain assumptions by (cite68†Furstenberg and Kesten, 1960 ). The novelty in our work lies in the extension to random Leaky ReLU neural networks, which is, to the authors’ knowledge, the first non-linear Law of Large Numbers and is therefore also of mathematical significance.
L104: When $\phi$ is the identity, the limit in (cite67†3 ) is called the Lyapunov exponent and therefore we call $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}$ the Lyapunov exponent of the neural network.
L105: The Law of Large Numbers (cite67†3 ) states that as a first approximation $|X_{\ell}|\!\approx\!e^{\ell\cdot\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}.$ This implies that when $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\!\!>\!\!0$, then $|X_{\ell}|$ explodes as $\ell$ increases while $|X_{\ell}|$ vanishes when $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\!\!<\!\!0$. Therefore, the Lyapunov exponent governs the exponential growth rate of the activations.
L106: We will show that in low dimensions all the previous initialization methods have a negative Lyapunov exponent and therefore lead to vanishing activations and inefficient learning. For example, when $d=2$ and $\alpha=0.1$, He inizialization (see Section cite12†2 ) has a Lyapunov exponent of $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\approx-0.81$. As the dimension increases however, He initialization leads to a zero Lyapunov exponent.
L107: #### Central Limit Theorem.
L108: 
L109: The Law of Large Numbers gives a rough estimate on the size of the values of $\log|X_{\ell}|$. We also establish a version of the Central Limit Theorem (Theorem cite66†3.3 ): If $\mu$ is again a matrix distribution satisfying suitable assumptions, the matrix weights are sampled independently from $\mu$ and $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}$ is the Lyapunov exponent, then there exists $\gamma_{\mu,\phi}>0$ such that
L110:  | $$\frac{\log|X_{\ell}|-\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\overset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{\mu,\phi}),$$  |  | (4)
L111: 
L112: where the convergence holds in distribution.
L113: 
L114: The Central Limit Theorem (cite69†4 ) provides a finer description of $\log|X_{\ell}|$ than the Law of Large Numbers, stating that $\log|X_{\ell}|$ roughly behaves like a Gaussian with mean $\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{\ell\gamma_{\mu,\phi}}$.
L115: #### Lyapunov Initialization.
L116: 
L117: Building on these theoretical insights, we propose a novel initialization strategy, referred to as Lyapunov initialization. By choosing the weight distribution such that the Lyapunov exponent is exactly zero, we ensure that activations are as stable as possible for deep networks. We provide explicit formulas for calculating the optimal scaling factors for both Gaussian and uniformly sampled orthogonal weight matrices.
L118: According to the Central Limit Theorem, setting the Lyapunov exponent to zero prevents exponential explosion or vanishing in expectation, but it nonetheless establishes stochastic variation. In particular, the logarithm of the activation norm, $\log|X_{\ell}|$, behaves as a Gaussian variable with a standard deviation of $O(\sqrt{\ell})$, causing the activation magnitude $|X_{\ell}|$ to fluctuate between $e^{-O(\sqrt{\ell})}$ and $e^{O(\sqrt{\ell})}$.
L119: To counter these depth-dependent fluctuations, we propose a refinement to our method termed sampled Lyapunov initialization: We suggest to generate $O(\sqrt{\ell})$ many candidate initializations prior to the training phase. By doing so, we ensure with high probability that at least one candidate yields an output norm $|X_{\ell}|$ close to one, allowing us to select the most stable configuration for training.
L120: #### Experiments.
L121: We provide experimental evidence that our new Lyapunov methods outperform previous standard initialization strategies for deep networks of low width and also that sampling further improves Lyapunov initialization. From our viewpoint, the reason why the previous methods are not optimal, is because their Lyapunov exponent is negative and far from zero. Therefore, not even small changes in their coefficients lead to a regime where the activations have roughly the right norm, preventing efficient learning.

L141: ## 2 Related Work
L142: #### Glorot and He initilization
L143: In their seminal work, Glorot and Bengio (cite63†Glorot and Bengio, 2010 ) proposed choosing all weights i.i.d. from distributions with mean zero and a variance of $\frac{1}{d}$, such as $\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d})$ or $\text{Unif}[-\sqrt{\frac{3}{d}},\sqrt{\frac{3}{d}}]$. Known as Glorot (or Xavier) initialization, this method aims to keep the mean and variance of activations constant across layers.
L144: However, this approach relies on the simplifying assumption that the activation function is linear and therefore only works well in practice for activation functions that are linear near zero (i.e., $\phi(x)\approx x$), which holds for functions like $\tanh$ but fails for ReLU and Leaky ReLU.
L145: To address this, He initialization (cite64†He et al., 2015 ) was developed specifically for ReLU-based networks. For Leaky ReLU with a slope parameter $\alpha$, it samples weights with a variance of $\frac{2}{d(1+\alpha^{2})}$. While this choice successfully preserves the second moment of the activations, that is ensuring $\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell}|^{2}]\!=\!\mathbb{E}[|X_{\ell^{\prime}}|^{2}]$ for all $\ell,\!\ell^{\prime}\!\geq\!0$, it does not preserve the mean or variance of $|X_{\ell}|$.
L146: In this paper, we demonstrate that in low dimensions, He initialization results in a negative Lyapunov exponent that is far from zero, establishing quantitatively how quickly the activations vanish rather than stabilize. On the other hand, our results prove that He initialization has a zero Lyapunov exponent in high dimensions. We believe that the specific methods and results established in this paper are essential for proving this particular result.
L147: #### Orthogonal Initialization
L148: (cite70†Saxe et al., 2014 ) proposed initializing weights with random orthogonal matrices rather than Gaussian entries. Orthogonal matrices preserve the norm vectors. While effective for deep linear networks, applying them to non-linear networks requires a rescaling of the orthogonal matrices to account for the activation function’s contraction or expansion (see also (cite71†Mishkin and Matas, 2016 )).
L149: In this paper, we establish the optimal scaling factor for orthogonal initializations of infinite-depth Leaky ReLU networks.
L150: In a complementary direction, using mean field theory for the infinite width case, (cite72†Pennington et al., 2017 ) established the optimal scaling rate for certain networks with orthogonal initialization. (cite73†Xiao et al., 2018 ) further extended these mean field limit based ideas to CNNs, allowing the training of a 10,000-layer CNN without batch normalization.
L151: #### Lyapunov exponents and random matrix products
L152: From a theoretical perspective, our work is inspired by classical results on random matrix products (cite68†Furstenberg and Kesten, 1960 ; cite74†Le Page, 1982 ). Lyapunov exponents arising from such products play a central role in probability theory and dynamical systems. Our work differs from these classical results by analyzing compositions of random matrices and nonlinear activation functions. This difference fundamentally changes the dynamics, meaning that existing linear results do not directly apply.
L153: To our knowledge, our work provides the first concrete non-linear Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem.

L166: #### Edge of Chaos
L167: In the literature, the edge of chaos refers to a critical phase transition of neural network, where the parameters are calibrated to a regime that maximizes stability and learning performance. Early papers studying this phenomenon were (cite78†Bertschinger et al., 2004 ) and (cite79†Boedecker et al., 2011 ). More recently, edge of chaos has been analyzed in the context of infinite-width networks using mean field theory (cite80†Poole et al., 2016 ; cite81†Schoenholz et al., 2017 ).
L168: In contrast, the Lyapunov exponent studied in this paper quantifies the edge of chaos for the regime of infinite-depth neural networks.

L170: #### Probabilistic Setup and Notation
L171: 
L172: For our results to be valid, we require that the weight matrices $W_{\ell}$ are independent and identically distributed. With this assumption, the stochastic process $(X_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}$ is a Markov chain in the sense that the transition probabilities do not depend on the layer.
L173: 
L174: Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$, the space of real $d\times d$ matrices. We work with the probability space
L175:  | $$(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})=(M_{d}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{N}},\mathscr{B}(M_{d}(\mathbb{R}))^{\mathbb{N}},\mu^{\mathbb{N}}),$$  |  | (5)
L176: that is the space of $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$-sequences endowed with the the natural product $\sigma$-algebra and the product measure coming from $\mu$. So every $\omega\in\Omega$ is a sequence of matrices $\omega=(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\ldots)$ and for a given $\omega\in\Omega$ we denote by $W_{n}(\omega)$ the projection of $\omega$ to the n-th coordinate. So (cite82†1 ) formally means that for $\omega\in\Omega$ we have for a fixed $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,
L177:  | $$X_{\ell}(\omega)=\phi(W_{\ell}(\omega)X_{\ell-1}(\omega))\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{0}=x_{0}.$$  |  | (6)
L178: 
L179: While our methods require $W_{\ell}$ to be independent and identically distributed, the choice of the underlying distribution is flexible. In particular, our theorems hold provided $\mu$ satisfies either of the following assumptions.
L180: ###### Assumption 3.1.
L181: 
L182: Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on the space of $d\times d$ real matrices $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$ with independent entries. For each $1\leq i,j\leq d$, assume the $(i,j)$-coordinate distribution has a density $p_{ij}$ satisfying:
L183: 
L184:   1. (1)
L185:

L187: 
L188:   2. (2)
L189: 
L190: Finite Second Moment: Each distribution has a finite second moment: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u^{2}p_{ij}(u)\,du<\infty.$
L191: 
L192:   3. (3)
L193: Local Positivity: The density is strictly positive in a neighborhood of the identity matrix. That is, there exists $\epsilon>0$ such that for each $i,j$ it holds that $\inf_{|u|\leq\epsilon}p_{ij}(\delta_{ij}+u)>0,$ where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta defined as $\delta_{ij}=1$ if $i=j$ and otherwise $\delta_{ij}=0$.
L194: For example, when $p_{ij}$ is $\mathcal{N}(m_{ij},\sigma_{ij})$ with $m_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\sigma_{ij}>0$ or $\mathrm{Unif}([-a_{ij},b_{ij}])$ for $a_{ij},b_{ij}>0$, then Assumption cite83†3.1 is satisfied. L209: #### Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem
L210: 
L211: We are now ready to state our version of the law of large numbers.
L212: ###### Theorem 3.3.
L213: 
L214: (Law of large numbers for $|X_{\ell}|$) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$ satisfying either Assumption cite83†3.1 or Assumption cite86†3.2 . Let $(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})$ be as in (cite87†5 ), $(X_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}$ be as in (cite88†6 ) and let $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ be a Leaky ReLU activation function with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$.
L215: Then there exists a real number $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}$ depending on $\mu$ and $\phi$ such that for all $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}$ and for $\mathbb{P}_{\mu}$-almost all $\omega\in\Omega$ it holds that
L216: 
L217:  | $$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}\log|X_{\ell}(\omega)|=\lambda_{\mu,\phi}.$$  |  | (9)
L218: 
L219: The convergence is moreover in $L^{1}$, uniformly for $x_{0}$ of constant modulus, i.e.,
L220:  | $$\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu}}\left[\bigg|\frac{1}{\ell}\log\frac{|X_{\ell}(\omega)|}{x_{0}}-\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\bigg|\right]=0.$$  |
L221: 
L222: We now formally define the Lyapunov exponent of the neural network (cite82†1 ) as the number $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}$ from (cite89†9 ). Next, we state a version of the central limit theorem in this setting.
L223: ###### Theorem 3.4.
L224: 
L225: (Central Limit Theorem for $|X_{\ell}|$) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$ satisfying either Assumption cite83†3.1 or Assumption cite86†3.2 . Let $(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})$ be as in (cite87†5 ), $(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}$ be as in (cite88†6 ) and let $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ be a Leaky ReLU activation function with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$.
L226: Then there exists $\gamma_{\mu,\phi}>0$ such that for any $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\}$ it holds that as $\ell\to\infty$,
L227: 
L228:  | $$\frac{\log|X_{\ell}|-\ell\lambda_{\mu,\phi}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{\mu,\phi}),$$  |
L229: 
L230: where the convergence holds in distribution and $\lambda_{\mu,\phi}\in\mathbb{R}$ is from Theorem cite66†3.3 .
L231: 
L232: Theorem cite66†3.3 is visualized in Figure cite90†1 and Theorem cite77†3.4 with Figure cite91†2 .
L233: We remark that with the employed methods, as discussed in Appendix cite33†C , we can establish a Law of Large Numbers and a Central Limit Theorem for more general measures $\mu$ and for any activation function of the form $\phi(x)=\max(\alpha_{1}x,\alpha_{2}x)$ with $\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$. However, the results do not carry over to other standard activation functions.
L234: The proof idea is explained in Appendix cite34†C.1 , also exposing what part of the argument fails for other activation functions. In particular, we show in Lemma cite92†3.5 that the Law of Large numbers (Theorem cite66†3.3 ) fails for ReLU activations and the Central Limit Theorem (Theorem cite77†3.4 ) is false for $\tanh$, as we demonstrate in Figure cite93†5 in the appendix.
L235: #### Counterexamples to Law of Large Numbers
L236: 
L237: The Law of Large Numbers and especially the Central Limit Theorem as stated above are subtle results. Indeed, we observe that the conclusion of our Law of Large Numbers (Theorem cite66†3.3 ) is not valid in the stated form for ReLU activation functions or for the Leaky ReLU case when all the matrices in the support of $\mu$ have positive entries. We state the following precise lemma.
L238: ###### Lemma 3.5.
L239: 
L240: (Counterexample to Law of Large Numbers for $|X_{\ell}|$) Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $M_{d}(\mathbb{R})$. Let $(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})$ be as in (cite87†5 ), $(X_{\ell})_{\geq 0}$ be as in (cite88†6 ). Then the conclusion of Theorem cite66†3.3 is false under either of the following assumptions:
L241: 
L242:   1. (1)
L243: 
L244: (ReLU activation function) When $\phi(x)=\max(x,0)$ and the entries of $\mu$ are independent and all distributed as $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})$ for $\sigma>0$.
L245:   2. (2)
L246: 
L247: (Measure supported on matrices with positive entries) When $\phi(x)=\max(x,\alpha x)$ for $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$ and the entries of $\mu$ are independent and all distributed as $\mathrm{Unif}[0,a]$ for some $a>0$.
