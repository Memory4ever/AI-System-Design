L58: # Reverse-Engineering Model Editing on Language Models
L59: 
L60: Zhiyu Sun    Minrui Luo    Yu Wang    Zhili Chen    Tianxing He Correspondence to: hetianxing@mail.tsinghua.edu.cn
L61: ###### Abstract
L62: Large language models (LLMs) are pretrained on corpora containing trillions of tokens and, therefore, inevitably memorize sensitive information. Locate-then-edit methods, as a mainstream paradigm of model editing, offer a promising solution by modifying model parameters without retraining. However, in this work, we reveal a critical vulnerability of this paradigm: the parameter updates inadvertently serve as a side channel, enabling attackers to recover the edited data.
L63: We propose a two-stage reverse-engineering attack named KSTER (K ey S paceRecons T ruction-then-E ntropy R eduction) that leverages the low-rank structure of these updates. First, we theoretically show that the row space of the update matrix encodes a “fingerprint” of the edited subjects, enabling accurate subject recovery via spectral analysis. Second, we introduce an entropy-based prompt recovery attack that reconstructs the semantic context of the edit.
L64: Extensive experiments on multiple LLMs demonstrate that our attacks can recover edited data with high success rates. Furthermore, we propose subspace camouflage, a defense strategy that obfuscates the update fingerprint with semantic decoys. This approach effectively mitigates reconstruction risks without compromising editing utility. Our code is available at cite46†https://github.com/reanatom/EditingAtk.git†github.com .

L70: ## 1 Introduction
L71: Large language models (LLMs) are pretrained on corpora containing trillions of tokens (cite47†Allen-Zhu and Li, 2024 ; cite48†Wang et al., 2024a ; cite49†Kong et al., 2025 ), which poses a risk of memorizing sensitive information (cite50†Nasr et al., 2023 ; cite51†Cheng et al., 2025 ), such as personal data. Model editing (cite52†Wang et al., 2024c ) has emerged as a promising solution to mitigate this issue by modifying specific parameters without retraining (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite54†Mitchell et al., 2022a ).
L72: Among various editing approaches, the locate-then-edit paradigm (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ; cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ) has drawn wide research interest. It first identifies the parameters that store specific knowledge, then edits them. This paradigm offers strong interpretability and zero inference overhead, making it a leading candidate for cost-effective privacy protection (cite57†Hossain and Kagal, 2025 ; cite58†Li et al., 2025 ).
L73: While prior research has primarily focused on editing effectiveness and generalization, a critical safety question remains underexplored: Does the editing process itself create a leakage side-channel? Consider a scenario in which malicious attackers gain access to the model parameters before and after editing. Although the sensitive information resides in the pre-edit model, locating it within billions of parameters is computationally prohibitive.
L74: However, attackers can efficiently pinpoint and extract the knowledge that is erased by analyzing the parameter differences induced by editing, paradoxically turning the safety mechanism into a vulnerability.
L75: In this work, we address this question and reveal a latent vulnerability in the locate-then-edit paradigm, demonstrating that the act of editing can serve as a side channel, exposing the very information it aims to edit. Specifically, through analyzing the algebraic structure of widely used editing algorithms, we theoretically prove that the row space of the parameter difference matrix encodes a unique mathematical “fingerprint” of the edited subjects.
L76: With this insight, we propose a two-stage reverse-engineering attack framework. First, we employ spectral analysis on the update matrix to execute subject inference, accurately pinpointing the edited subject (e.g., Alice). Second, we introduce an entropy-based prompt recovery method to reconstruct the semantic context associated with the edit (e.g., {}’s phone number is {}).
L77: We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate the performance of our framework across three models and three editing methods. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, showing that we can accurately recover both the subject and the prompt. For instance, our attack method achieves a subject recall rate of over 99% and a semantic similarity of 88% when applied to Llama3-8B-Instruct (cite59†Dubey et al., 2024 ) on the CounterFact (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ) dataset.
L78: Moreover, to mitigate the edit-leakage risk, we propose a defense strategy named subspace camouflage. By injecting semantic decoys during the update process, this method obfuscates the spectral fingerprint of the target subjects, thereby effectively misleading algebraic inversion without compromising editing efficacy.

L92: ## 2 Related Work
L93: 
L94: This work investigates the reverse-engineering risks of model editing. Due to the space limitation, we provide a brief overview of related work here and a more comprehensive discussion in Appendix cite23†A .
L95: Prior work reveals various vulnerabilities in LLMs, ranging from security attacks like jailbreaking (cite61†Wei et al., 2023 ; cite62†Shen et al., 2024 ; cite63†Deng et al., 2023 ) and backdoors (cite64†Cai et al., 2022 ; cite65†Shi et al., 2023 ) to privacy risks such as membership inference (cite66†Kaneko et al., 2025 ; cite67†Feng et al., 2025 ) and personally identifiable information leakage (cite68†Nakka et al., 2024 ; cite69†Kim et al., 2023 ).
L96: To mitigate these risks, model editing has emerged as a surgical solution (cite57†Hossain and Kagal, 2025 ; cite58†Li et al., 2025 ).
L97: While current editing techniques span from external memory (cite70†Mitchell et al., 2022b ; cite71†Huang et al., 2023 ; cite72†Hartvigsen et al., 2023 ; cite73†Yu et al., 2024 ) to meta-learning (cite74†De Cao et al., 2021 ; cite54†Mitchell et al., 2022a ; cite75†Zhang et al., 2024 ), the locate-then-edit paradigm (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ; cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ) has emerged as a popular approach due to its parameter efficiency and interpretability.
L98: However, research on the privacy risks of this paradigm remains scarce.
L99: Related to our approach, cite76†Patil et al. (2024) detects original outputs by analyzing intermediate layer logits, while relying on the relatively strong assumption that the attacker has access to the original prompts. In contrast, our work accurately recovers sensitive subjects and prompts by analyzing parameter updates, without requiring original prompts.
L100: ## 3 Preliminaries
L101: ### 3.1 Model Editing in LLMs
L102: 
L103: Let $f_{\theta}$ be an LLM parameterized by weights $\theta$. We define a batch of knowledge triples as $\mathcal{D}^{t}=\{d_{1}^{t},\dots,d_{N}^{t}\}$, where each tuple $d_{i}^{t}=(s_{i},r_{i},o_{i})$. Here, $r_{i}$ is a prompt template containing two placeholders: the first for the subject $s_{i}$, which together form the query context, and the second for the target output $o_{i}$ (e.g., $s_{i}$: Alice, $r_{i}$: {} lives in {}, $o_{i}$: New York).
L104: In this work, we focus on the locate-then-edit paradigm, specifically ROME (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ) and its variants (cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ; cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ). These methods implement knowledge editing by updating specific feed-forward networks (FFNs) that store factual associations. We focus on the single-time editing setting and leave multi-time editing for future work.
L105: For a given edit input, let key vector $\mathbf{k}_{*}$ denote the hidden state of the subject’s last token at the target layer, where $\mathbf{k}_{*}$ is mapped to a value vector $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{k}_{*}$ by the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$. The objective is to find an update $\Delta\mathbf{W}$ that remaps $\mathbf{k}_{*}$ to a desired target value $\mathbf{v}_{*}$, i.e., $(\mathbf{W}+\Delta\mathbf{W})\mathbf{k}_{*}=\mathbf{v}_{*}$.
L106: This is typically formulated as a (constrained) least-squares problem, aiming to satisfy the edit requirement without compromising the preserved knowledge $\mathbf{K}_{p}$.
L107: For single-fact editing ($N=1$), ROME (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ) defines the residual vector as $\mathbf{r}_{*}=\mathbf{v}_{*}-\mathbf{v}$ and derives a rank-one analytical solution:
L108: 
L109:  | $$\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{ROME}}=\frac{\mathbf{r}_{*}\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}}{\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{*}},$$  |  | (1)
L110: 
L111: where $\mathbf{C}\triangleq\mathbf{K}_{p}\mathbf{K}_{p}^{\top}$ is a pre-cached matrix, typically estimated using key vectors sampled from Wikipedia.
L112: To support batch editing ($N>1$), MEMIT (cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ) generalizes this approach: Let $\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times N}$ denote the stacked key matrix containing key vectors for the batch of samples, and $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times N}$ denote the corresponding residual matrix. Its update is:
L113: 
L114:  | $$\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{MEMIT}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\right)^{-1}.$$  |  | (2)
L115: To mitigate model collapse^{1}^{1} 1 Refers to the severe degradation of general model capabilities as sequential edits accumulate. in sequential editing, AlphaEdit (cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ) maintains general model capabilities by projecting updates onto the null space of $\mathbf{K}_{p}$:
L116: 
L117:  | $$\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{AlphaEdit}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\left(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1},$$  |  | (3)
L118: where $\mathbf{P}$ is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the null space of $\mathbf{K}_{p}$.
L119: ### 3.2 Threat Model
L120: The attacker’s goal is to reverse-engineer the knowledge $\mathcal{D}^{t}$ that has been edited. We consider two attacker settings: (1) White-box: The attacker has access to (i) the model parameter weights before ($\theta$) and after ($\theta^{\prime}$) the edit; and (ii) the editing algorithm (e.g., MEMIT) and its hyperparameters. (2) Gray-box: The attacker has access only to the output logits of the model before and after editing, and the model parameters are inaccessible.
L121: In this work, we mainly explore the white-box setting, and we also include a gray-box baseline.
L122: In both settings, we assume the attacker cannot access $\mathcal{D}^{t}$ but (1) has access to the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}$ (we will show this assumption can be relaxed with an ablation study in §cite15†4.4 ); (2) has access to a joint candidate pool $\mathcal{S}_{\rm cand}\times\mathcal{R}_{\rm cand}$ containing the ground-truth edit(s) $(s,r)$. This assumption is reasonable as attackers can filter candidates based on public trends or specific domains, making the search space feasible to construct.
L123: ## 4 The KSTER Attack
L124: In this section, we show how to reverse-engineer the edited knowledge using the parameter difference $\Delta\theta$. It has two stages: (1) Subject inference: Given a candidate set of subjects $\mathcal{S}_{\rm cand}$, identify the specific subject $\hat{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm cand}$ that is involved in the edit. (2) Prompt recovery: Given a candidate set of relations $\mathcal{R}_{\rm cand}$, recover the prompt $\hat{r}\in\mathcal{R}_{\rm cand}$ used in the edit context.
L125: With these two components, we can easily obtain the original object $o^{\rm ori}$ by querying the initial model $f_{\theta}$.
L126: Below, we begin with a key observation on subject invariance, which provides the empirical basis for our method. We then present the proposed two-stage reverse-engineering attack framework.
L127: ### 4.1 Subject Invariance in LM Model Editing
L128: 
L129: In locate-then-edit algorithms such as MEMIT (cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ), the key matrix $\mathbf{K}$ is constructed by stacking the last-token hidden states of a subject batch at a specific FFN layer. A critical question for subject inference is whether the key matrix $\mathbf{K}$ is strongly affected by the prompt contexts used during editing.
L130: We first analyze the attention distribution in Figure cite77†2a , which reveals that the model’s attention is primarily focused on subject tokens across different prompt templates. Motivated by this, we use cosine similarity to compare the hidden states of the same subject at the shallowest edited layer across different prompt templates. As shown in Figure cite78†2b , the results show a surprising consistency: the activations are highly similar across all templates.
L131: Detailed lists of the subjects and prompt templates are provided in Appendix cite36†D.3 .
L132: This observation gives us a key insight: $\mathbf{K}$ primarily encodes unique features of the subjects and is largely decoupled from the prompt context. Consequently, an attacker does not need to reconstruct the exact prompts to infer the edited subjects. Instead, the attacker can simply query the model with a generic template. If a candidate subject’s activation vector aligns strongly with $\mathbf{K}$, it is likely that the subject belongs to the edited batch.

L137: ### 4.2 Attack Stage I: Subject Inference
L138: The first stage aims to recover the edited subject set $\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ from the update matrix $\Delta\mathbf{W}$ of the shallowest edited layer. Although it is hard to recover $\mathbf{K}$ directly because of the unknown residual matrix $\mathbf{R}$ (or residual vector $\mathbf{r}_{*}$), we can indeed reconstruct the column space of $\mathbf{K}$ (namely key space) through a carefully designed mathematical transformation.
L139: The intuition here is to apply the Woodbury matrix identity (Lemma cite81†E.9 ) to rewrite the weight update $\Delta\mathbf{W}$.
L140: Here we take MEMIT as an example. By applying the Woodbury matrix identity, the weight update in Eq. (cite82†2 ) can be written as:
L141: 
L142:  | $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{K}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}.$  |  | (4)
L143: 
L144: The detailed derivations can be found in Eq. (cite83†24 ), Appendix cite41†E.2 . This reveals an important property that the row space of $\Delta\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C}$ lies within the column space of $\mathbf{K}$:
L145:  | $\displaystyle\row(\Delta\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C})\subseteq\col(\mathbf{K}).$  |  | (5)
L146: 
L147: Specifically, the signature of the edited subjects is preserved in the row space of $\Delta\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C}$.

L176: Based on the geometric property in Eq. (cite84†5 ), we propose our subject inference attack for MEMIT, as shown in lines 1–10 of Algorithm cite85†6 . The procedure has the following two steps:
L177: Step 1: Subspace Reconstruction. To recover the subspace associated with the target subjects, we first determine the number of edited knowledge $N$ by analyzing the rank of weight update $\Delta\mathbf{W}$: since generally $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are full column rank, the rank of $\Delta\mathbf{W}$ is exactly $N$, which is detailed in Lemma cite86†E.10 and Remark cite87†E.11 in the appendix.
L178: Then, we eliminate the geometric distortion induced by the covariance matrix: by computing $\mathbf{M}=\Delta\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C}$ and its singular value decomposition $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$, the subspace spanned by the edited key vectors (equivalently the column space of $\mathbf{K}$) is revealed by $\mathbf{V}_{N}$, whose columns are the top-$N$ right singular vectors of $\mathbf{M}$.
L179: Step 2: Projection-based Ranking. In the second step, we score each candidate subject $s_{i}^{c}\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm cand}$ (see Appendix cite34†D.1 for the detailed construction of $\mathcal{S}_{\rm cand}$) by generating a proxy key vector $\mathbf{k}_{i}^{c}=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(s_{i}^{c},\mathcal{T}_{\rm gen})$ using a generic template (see Appendix  cite37†D.4 for details).
L180: Here, $\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(s_{i}^{c},\mathcal{T}_{\rm gen})$ denotes the activation vector at the shallowest edited layer of the pre-edit model $\theta$, corresponding to the subject’s last token. This vector is obtained by a forward pass with the subject-filled template. We then compute the projection coefficient of $\mathbf{k}_{i}^{c}$ onto $\col(\mathbf{V}_{N})$, which is $\rho_{i}^{c}=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{V}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{i}^{c}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{k}_{i}^{c}\right\|_{2}}$.
L181: A higher score indicates that the candidate is likely one of the edited targets. Finally, we obtain the top-$N$ predicted subjects $\hat{\mathcal{S}}$. We further perform an ablation study on the selection of templates in Appendix cite38†D.5 .
L182: The application of our techniques to other locate-then-edit methods, such as ROME (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ) and AlphaEdit (cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ), is provided in Appendix cite28†B.1 and cite29†B.2 . Notably, the scoring of AlphaEdit differs slightly from MEMIT due to the projection matrix $\mathbf{P}$, as explained in Lemma cite88†E.18 and Remark cite89†E.19 (Appendix cite43†E.4 ).
L183: Theoretical Guarantees. In Appendix cite39†E , we provide theoretical guarantees for the correctness and robustness of the subject inference part within our KSTER attack strategy. Under appropriate assumptions established in Appendix cite40†E.1 , we prove that Algorithm cite85†6 (MEMIT) exactly recovers the correct $N$ edited subjects in Theorem cite90†E.13 .
L184: A similar guarantee for the attack strategy of AlphaEdit (Algorithm cite29†B.2 , which differs from Algorithm cite85†6 in its score function $\rho^{c}$) is provided in Theorem cite91†E.21 . Furthermore, to address practical scenarios where the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}$ is only noisily estimated rather than directly obtained, we also prove that Algorithm cite85†6 is robust to perturbation on $\mathbf{C}$ in Theorem cite92†E.16 , by utilizing subspace perturbation bounds.
L185: ### 4.3 Attack Stage II: Prompt Recovery
L186: 
L187: After inferring the target subject set $\hat{\mathcal{S}}$ in Stage I, we now aim to recover the corresponding prompt for each subject $\hat{s}_{i}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}$. We formulate this part as an entropy reduction analysis between the pre-edit model ($\theta$) and the post-edit model ($\theta^{\prime}$).
L188: Entropy Reduction Analysis. A key consequence of model editing is a sharp reduction in uncertainty for the edited fact. When queried with the target subject and prompt, the pre-edit model ($\theta$) may exhibit high entropy, whereas the edited model ($\theta^{\prime}$) is optimized to output the desired answer with near-zero entropy (i.e., extreme confidence) (cite53†Meng et al., 2022 ; cite55†Meng et al., 2023 ; cite56†Fang et al., 2025 ).
L189: Since this optimization does not apply to non-target prompts, the entropy change for them is much smaller. Leveraging this, we employ an entropy-based approach to identify the most likely prompt.
L190: We evaluate a set of candidate prompts $\mathcal{R}_{\text{cand}}$, which contains the ground-truth target prompts (see Appendix cite34†D.1 for the detailed construction of $\mathcal{R}_{\text{cand}}$). For each candidate $r_{j}^{c}\in\mathcal{R}_{\text{cand}}$, we compute the Shannon entropy of the next-token distribution conditioned on subject $\hat{s}_{i}$:
L191:  | $\displaystyle H(\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c};\theta)=-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(t\mid\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c})\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(t\mid\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c}),$  |  | (6)
L192: 
L193: where $\mathcal{V}$ denotes the vocabulary and $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c})$ denotes next-token probability predicted by the model $\theta$. This formulation is designed to capture the model’s prediction confidence regarding a specific prompt.
L194: We use the relative entropy reduction as our discriminator. This metric measures the entropy shift from the pre-edit to the post-edit model, normalized by the post-edit prediction confidence:
L195: 
L196:  | $\displaystyle\text{Score}(r_{j}^{c}\mid\hat{s}_{i},\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\frac{H(\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c};\theta)-H(\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c};\theta^{\prime})}{H(\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c};\theta^{\prime})+\epsilon},$  |  | (7)
L197: where $\epsilon$ is set to $10^{-9}$ for numerical stability. As illustrated in lines 11–18 of Algorithm cite85†6 , for each subject $\hat{s}_{i}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}$, we predict the top-$N_{r}$ prompts $\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{i}$ as the most likely original prompts. The denominator ensures that prompts gaining over-confidence (where $H(\hat{s}_{i},r_{j}^{c};\theta^{\prime})\to 0$) receive higher scores, precisely identifying the edited prompt.
L198: Finally, note that if the inference of the subject and the prompt is correct, it should then be easy to get the target object (explained in Appendix cite34†D.1 ). Therefore, we omit the object in our evaluation.
