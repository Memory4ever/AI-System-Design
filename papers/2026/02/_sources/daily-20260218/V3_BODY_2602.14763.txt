Abstract
Reasoning-oriented large language models (RLMs) achieve strong gains on tasks such as mathematics and coding by generating explicit intermediate reasoning. However, their impact on machine translation (MT) remains underexplored. We systematically evaluate several open- and closed-weights RLMs on the WMT24++ benchmark and find that enabling explicit reasoning consistently degrades translation quality across languages and models. Analysis reveals that MT reasoning traces are highly linear, lacking revision, self-correction and exploration of alternative translations, which limits their usefulness. Furthermore, injecting higher-quality reasoning traces from stronger models does not reliably improve weaker models’ performance. To address this mismatch, we propose a structured reasoning framework tailored to translation, based on multi-step drafting, adequacy refinement, fluency improvement, and selective iterative revision. We curate a synthetic dataset of dynamic structured reasoning traces and post-train a large reasoning model on this data. Experiments show significant improvements over standard translation fine-tuning and injected generic reasoning baselines. Our findings demonstrate that reasoning must be task-structured to benefit MT.

1 Introduction
Reasoning-oriented language models (RLMs), that is, large language models (LLMs) trained to spend more compute generating intermediate reasoning before answering, have recently delivered large performance gains on reasoning-heavy benchmarks, with especially strong improvements in mathematics and coding tasks ( Kimi Team, 2025 ; Mistral-AI, 2025 ; Qwen Team, 2025 ; NVIDIA, 2025 ) .
This paradigm is increasingly viewed as a general strategy for improving model reliability, controllability, and performance via test-time compute scaling, making it a central design axis for the next generation of foundation models ( Wang et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2025c ) .
As a result, these improvements have sparked interest in whether explicit reasoning can also help on other challenging generative tasks, including machine translation (MT).
At the same time, relative to the depth of work on math and programming evaluations, MT has seen fewer systematic studies of RLMs and reasoning-style prompting as a first-class design axis ( Liu et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ) , leaving open whether reasoning-based scaling transfers to high-stakes real-world generative tasks.
This work bridges the gap by investigating whether and how reasoning capabilities contribute to machine translation performance.
We begin by evaluating a diverse set of open- and closed-weight RLMs on the WMT24++ benchmark ( Deutsch et al., 2025 ) , comparing RLMs performance with and without using thinking tokens.
Contrary to findings on most reasoning-demanding tasks ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2024 ) , and contrary to the common assumption that explicit reasoning is broadly beneficial across domains, our results show that enabling explicit reasoning does not improve translation quality.
In fact, we even observe a slight decline in performance when RLMs generate reasoning traces for MT.
To better understand this behavior, we conduct a qualitative analysis of the generated reasoning trajectories in Section 3.1 . We find that these traces exhibit a linear structure lacking exploration of alternative translations, self-correction, and revision.
This contrasts with math and code problems, where effective reasoning involves hypothesis exploration, backtracking, and verification ( Marjanovic et al., 2026 ; Cuesta-Ramirez et al., 2025 ) , behaviors that align well with search-based problem structures but appear mismatched with the incremental, constraint-driven nature of translation.
We further examine whether reasoning traces generated by a stronger RLM can benefit a weaker model, enhancing the performance.
More specifically, in Section 3.2 , we inject reasoning traces generated by a stronger RLM (called a injecting model) into another RLM (called a receiving model), allowing the receiving model to generate final translations conditioned on the injected reasoning. Surprisingly, even when the injected reasoning traces are of high quality and accompanied by strong translations, we do not observe an improvement in the receiving model’s performance.
This suggests that simply increasing test-time compute or transferring generic reasoning traces is insufficient for improving translation, raising questions about the universality of current reasoning paradigms.
Based on these observations, we hypothesize that the limitation is not the absence of reasoning per se, but a misalignment between generic reasoning formats and the structural requirements of translation.
Our observation is furthermore reinforced by the success of Chain-of-thoughts reasoning that improves MT quality ( Nguyen & Xu, 2025 ; Wu et al., 2025b ) .
To test our hypothesis, in Section 4 we design a synthetic data generation pipeline that enforces a structured reasoning style for MT. Our proposed reasoning template explicitly incorporates iterative refinements using a multi-turn translation setup ( Briakou et al., 2024 ) and a dynamic format that adapts to the difficulty of the given translation examples, enhancing the efficiency of reasoning length.
We show that reasoning can be beneficial for MT when it is explicitly shaped around error revision, constraint satisfaction, and iterative refinement, rather than leaving it up on model for generic reasoning.
Looking into the reason behind the effectiveness of the structured reasoning, in Section 6.1 , and through controlled ablation studies with varying reasoning trace quality and target translation quality, we show that having higher quality reasoning traces (e.g., proposed structure reasoning) leads to performance gain on translation quality, even when paired with weaker final outputs. However, the quality of the final translation has a more positive role on the overall performance.
We summarize our main contributions as follows:
•
We present a comprehensive evaluation and analysis of reasoning large language models for machine translation. We show that existing reasoning formats in RLMs, without revision, self-correction, or exploration, do not benefit MT. We also demonstrate that injecting reasoning traces with the same format, but higher-quality translations, does not constantly improve RLM’s performance in MT ( Section 3 ).
•
We propose a structured reasoning framework tailored to machine translation, a designed synthetic reasoning generation pipeline that enforces iterative revision and self-correction. Training on the structured reasoning traces leads to a significant improvement in translation performance ( Sections 4 and 6 ).
•
We disentangle the roles of reasoning trace quality and final translation quality through controlled ablation studies on RLMs, showing that while high-quality structured reasoning traces provide performance improvements, the quality of the final translation is the more important factor in determining MT performance ( Section 6.1 )
Together, these findings challenge the assumption that test-time scaling via longer generic reasoning traces will reliably improve performance across tasks, and instead point to the need for task-specific structural priors when generating synthetic reasoning data.
More generally, they motivate viewing reasoning not as a single transferable skill, but as a family of task-dependent deliberation strategies that must be learned and evaluated in a task-aware manner, with consequences for other generative tasks.

2 Related Work
Reasoning Language Models
Prompting language models to generate intermediate steps (commonly referred to as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) ( Wei et al., 2022 ) ) has yielded significant performance gains across complex, reasoning-demanding tasks like mathematics ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) , logic reasoning ( Sprague et al., 2025 ) , and programming ( Min et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025d ) .
In a similar direction, it has been shown that reasoning capabilities can also emerge through pure reinforcement learning (RL) training ( DeepSeek Team, 2025 ; Mistral-AI, 2025 ) .
Beyond performance gains, recent studies have also looked into reasoning trajectories generated by RLMs, investigating their structures and features and validating the intermediate steps ( Marjanovic et al., 2026 ; Liao et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) .
Reasoning for MT
Several studies have explored CoT prompting for machine translation, reporting remarkable gains on the performance ( Nguyen & Xu, 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025b ) . Moreover, it has been that multi-step translation focusing on different aspect of translations in each step (e.g., drafting or fluency) ( Briakou et al., 2024 ) , as well as simple iterative translation refinements ( Wu et al., 2025a ) , boost the translation quality.
More recently, a similar work to ours has evaluated RLMs on MT showing that explicit thinking tokens do not improve translation quality, a finding that aligns with our observations ( Zebaze et al., 2025 ) . Furthermore, they have also reported that fine-tuning with synthetic CoT explanations does not outperform standard input-output fine-tuning on MT. This conclusion that is not supported by our experimental results.
In a nutshell, our work differs from prior studies in a few key aspects: (1) we conduct a systematic evaluation and in-depth analyses on the role of reasoning on machine translation performance in RLMs showing the ineffectiveness of thinking tokens on MT and investigating the reasons behind it. (2) We design a dynamic reasoning generation pipeline to synthetically generate helpful reasoning traces for MT, and we show a solid performance improvement using our structured reasoning framework. (3) We carry out an ablation study to investigate the contribution of reasoning traces quality compared to the final translation quality in the synthetic dataset, showing that both positively affect the performance while the final translation quality have a stronger role on RLMs performance.
3 Reasoning Capability Inspection
Reasoning skills have gained special attention in recently developed large language models. It has been shown that explicit reasoning, also known as thinking tokens , through decomposition, self-verification, and iterative refinement substantially improves LLMs’ performance across complex tasks, such as mathematical problems or code generation. However, the extent to which the reasoning capabilities benefit open-ended generation tasks like machine translation (MT) remains less-explored.
To investigate the role of reasoning on MT, we begin with evaluating four open and closed-weights reasoning language models, Command-A-Reasoning ( Cohere Team, 2025 ) , Claude-4-Opus ( Anthropic, 2025 ) , DeepSeek-R1 ( DeepSeek Team, 2025 ) , and Gemini-2.5-Flash ( Gemini Team, 2025 ) . We evaluate these models on nine language pairs from the WMT24++ benchmark and compare the quality of generated translation with and without reasoning using the XCOMET-XL metric ( Guerreiro et al., 2024 ) , a state-of-the-art metric for automatic evaluation ( Freitag et al., 2024 ) . Additional experimental details are provided in Appendix A .
Table 1 summarizes the results.
At a high-level glance and looking into the average performance, we observe that all the reasoning models consistently have a significantly higher performance without reasoning.
The pattern holds for all the individual language pairs as well, except for Farsi in Claude-4-Opus where we see that reasoning improves the translation quality.
Our findings show that while it has been widely reported in previous studies that reasoning abilities help LLMs to improve their performance on a variety of tasks ( Pham et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ; Son et al., 2026 ) , it has an opposite effect on the translation performance.
To further investigate the characteristics of reasoning traces in the machine translation task, we conduct a series of qualitative and quantitative analyses, focusing on the open-weights models, in the following sections.

To better understand why having reasoning traces fails to improve MT performance, we analyze the structure of reasoning traces for the translation task.
Appendix B shows an example of a generated reasoning trajectory by DeepSeek-R1 .
Looking into the generated reasoning traces, we notice that, unlike other reasoning-demanding tasks (like math problems), where the RLM explores multiple scenarios, evaluates intermediate steps, and can backtrack ( An et al., 2025 ) , MT reasoning style exhibits a linear structure. The RLMs split the source text into segments, translate each segment in isolation without considering possible alternative translations, and proceed sequentially without revisiting earlier translations.
To put this in a numerical perspective, we calculate the number of distinct reasoning paths (i.e., exploring different possible translations) that an RLM explores in its generated reasoning traces for MT. Following previous studies ( An et al., 2025 ; Cuesta-Ramirez et al., 2025 ) , we use linguistic cues, Wait and Alternatively as a signal for a reasoning path and count their occurrences.
We report the average number of different reasoning paths within the whole reasoning trace in Table 2 . The numerical results confirm our qualitative observation that MT reasoning traces are highly linear, lacking exploration or self-correction in the reasoning process. Among the selected models, Command-A-Reasoning has the highest number of reasoning paths in the reasoning traces on average, meaning that the model explores alternative translation reformulation for 1.56 1.56 number of segments in the given text on average. It is worth mentioning that a given text could have multiple segments.
3.2 Reasoning Injection
Figure 1: The performance of open-weights RLMs when their reasoning traces are generated and injected from other models. The dash-line represents the baseline performance of the receiving models, and the injecting models are presented at the top. As can be seen, the strength of the injecting model used to generate reasoning traces does not correlate with final translation quality across different injecting and receiving models.
In our primary experiment, we have shown that having reasoning does not bring performance gain on the machine translation task for RLMs, and this pattern is consistent across all the selected models. Nevertheless, the absolute translation performance varies across models, with Claude-4-Opus achieving the highest overall performance. In this part, we investigate whether a higher-quality reasoning trace generated by a stronger model on MT can provide other models with a hint to improve their performance.
We conduct a controlled reasoning injection experiment. For each translation example, we generate a reasoning trace using an injecting model (e.g., Claude-4-Opus ), insert it into another receiving model (e.g., Command-A-Reasoning ), and let the receiving model generate the final translation using the provided reasoning trace. To have full control over the reasoning traces between thinking tokens , we limit ourselves to the open-weights models, namely Command-A-Reasoning and DeepSeek-R1 , as the receiving models, while using all four models as injecting ones to generate reasoning traces.
The results are presented in Figure 1 . First, we don’t observe a notable performance drop in the receiving models, indicating that RLMs are not biased toward their own reasoning style for machine translation. Secondly, although reasoning traces from stronger injecting models, such as Claude-4-Opus , lead to performance improvement of DeepSeek-R1 , this pattern does not hold for Command-A-Reasoning . In other words, reasoning traces generated by stronger models do not consistently yield higher translation quality across different receiving models.
In summary, we find that the reasoning styles for machine translation differ from other reasoning-demanding tasks. In their current format, translation reasoning trajectories follow a linear process and rarely include translation revision or exploration of possible translation options, and even higher-quality reasoning traces from stronger models cannot benefit other RLMs to enhance their translation performance, suggesting the ineffectiveness of the existing reasoning format in the MT task.
Building on our findings and inspired by previous studies on the effectiveness of step-wise translation methods, in the next section, we propose a framework for creating dynamic, step-by-step reasoning traces for machine translation.
4 Structured Reasoning Trace
We design a synthetic data generation pipeline to train reasoning skills beneficial for machine translation in reasoning language models. Building on our findings in the previous section showing that the state-of-the-art reasoning language models do not generate useful reasoning traces for machine translation and the success of chain-of-thoughts techniques used in MT ( Nguyen & Xu, 2025 ; Wu et al., 2025b ; Briakou et al., 2024 ) , we aim to create structured reasoning traces, teaching the model how to do step-by-step reasoning, and improve translation through successive refinements.
In the following subsections, we describe our data generation process, quality check of reasoning traces, and the construction of reasoning templates.
4.1 Multi-turn Translation Annotation
Dynamic Structured Reasoning Template
Step 1 --- Initial Draft
Let me start by producing an initial, faithful translation of the source text into {target_language}, which will serve as the foundation for later refinements.
{initial_draft}
Step 2 --- Adequacy
In this step, I focus on adequacy, ensuring that the full meaning of the challenging sentences is conveyed accurately and completely in {target_language}.
{challenging_sentences_adequacy}
Step 3 --- Fluency
At this stage, I refine the challenging sentences, ensuring they read smoothly and naturally in {target_language}, as if originally written in it.
{challenging_sentences_fluency}
Step 4 --- Final Translation
Finally, I bring together all the work from the previous steps, addressing any remaining issues or translation errors to produce a polished final version in {target_language}.
Figure 2: An example of the dynamic template used to generate structured reasoning traces. Curly-braced fields denote placeholders. Steps 2 and 3 are applied only to selected challenging sentences, those sentences that benefited the most from steps 2 and 3 with the defined MetricX margin; all other segments are discarded from the initial draft.
We begin with a high-quality monolingual corpus of documents spanning 23 source languages, prepared to be translated. All the documents pass quality control and difficulty sampling ( Proietti et al., 2025 ; Kocmi et al., 2025 ) . Following Briakou et al. (2024) , for each source document, we generate a sequence of four translation refinements using an offline, step-wise procedure employing the Command-A-Translate ( Kocmi et al., 2025 ) . Each step focuses on a distinct and important aspect of translation.
1.
Initial Draft: Command-A-Translate is prompted to generate an initial draft intended to be rough and as a foundation for the next steps of translation.
2.
Adequacy Enhancement: Given the draft translation, the model is asked to revise the translation with an explicit emphasis on semantic accuracy.
3.
Fluency Refinement: In this step, the LLM improves the readability and fluency of the adequate translation in the target language.
4.
Final Translation: In this final stage, Command-A-Translate considers all the previous translations, resolving remaining errors, and generates its best possible translation.
We consider this four-step trajectory as a structured approximation of a useful reasoning process for translation. In this step, we discard samples whose translated documents are not line-aligned with the source documents, a requirement for the follow up quality estimation, resulting in 320 ​ k 320k high-quality samples. Detailed prompts used for each step of translations are presented in Appendix C .
4.2 Quality-based Data Selection
Having the multi-turn translations for all 320 ​ k 320k examples, we notice that only a small subset shows meaningful improvements across the refinement steps. We quantify the extent of improvement for each intermediate translation using MetricX ( Juraska et al., 2024 ) .
Since the MetricX has limited context window, we evaluate all lines in isolation, averaging them into the document-level score.
We observe that less than 10 % 10\% of examples show substantial quality gains (e.g., more than 0.5 0.5 MetricX improvement, technically meaning half a minor error in the MQM terminology ( Lommel et al., 2013 ) ) across different steps. This is expected as SOTA MT systems like Command-A-Translate have remarkably high performance and often translate well in a single pass, meaning most of the translation examples are not challenging for them. Therefore, we calculate the MetricX for each segment of a given document and consider the average MetricX score as the whole document score. As stated before, we keep only examples where the improvement between the initial draft and the final translation is at least 0.5 0.5 MetricX score. We find the filtering step critical for improving RLMs performance.
This filtering yields a collection of 28k high-quality samples, each demonstrating a non-trivial translation. A breakdown of language-pair distribution for this subset is provided in Figure 3 .
4.3 Constructing Reasoning Traces
Having instances where step-by-step translation is beneficial, we next convert the multi-step outputs into reasoning traces. We hypothesize that training the model with a step-by-step style of reasoning helps it use this structure for reasoning in the machine translation task. We later verify our hypothesis through empirical experiments.
Static Template
The first way to create the reasoning trajectories is to chain the four translations into a single trace. We treat a set of human-generated sentences as connectives to connect four translations. To diversify the reasoning structure, for each step translation, we randomly insert one of the predefined sentences into the reasoning trace reflecting a natural thinking flow in the model.
An illustration of this template is provided in Appendix D . A concern raised by this template is that the trace length scales with document size, which may introduce inefficiencies for long inputs.
Dynamic Template
As mentioned above, having all four steps for the whole document is not efficient, as not all the segments of a document need translation through iterative reasoning. To enhance the efficiency of the reasoning traces, we develop a dynamic template that, after the initial draft, includes reasoning only for challenging segments. We consider sentences challenging that the translation quality between the initial drafting step and the adequacy or fluency stage improves by at least 1 MetricX point.
More specifically, in this template, we include the initial draft translation of the entire document. Then, we drop the translation of segments without meaningful improvement in the fluency and adequacy steps from the reasoning trace. We also discard the final translation from the trace to reduce repetition. Figure 2 shows an example of the resulting dynamic template strategy. More details on dynamic templates are presented in Appendix E .
4.4 Final Training Examples
Each training sample in our dataset consists of the triple (source text, reasoning trace, target text) in which reasoning traces are constructed using the explained dynamic template, and the target text is adopted as the Command-A-Translate final-step translation.
The resulting 28k data provides explicit, structured reasoning for translation, which we use to train reasoning models to carry out step-by-step reasoning and improve their reasoning capability for machine translation.

5 Experimental Setup
As a post-training stage, we do supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the Command-A-Reasoning model with 111B parameters using our curated 28k translation-reasoning examples. We pick this model as it is among the strongest open-weights reasoning models. All the experiments are conducted with a maximum sequence length of 32768 32768 tokens, and we evaluate the impact of different data selection strategies by training models on subsets of 1 ​ k 1k , 10 ​ k 10k , and the full 28 ​ k 28k dataset.
Unless otherwise noted, models trained on 28 ​ k 28k samples are finetuned for 4 4 epochs. Models trained on 1 ​ k 1k and 10 ​ k 10k subsets are trained for 8 8 and 6 6 epochs, respectively. We use a peak learning rate of 1 × 10 − 5 1\times 10^{-5} , decayed to 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} , with additive weight decay of 0.1 0.1 . All the SFT runs are done on 64 64 first class GPUs.
5.1 Baselines
To isolate the contribution of the designed structured reasoning traces, we compare our approach to the following baselines:
Direct Translation
To disentangle the effect of additional MT supervision from explicit reasoning, we finetune the same base model using only direct translations without reasoning traces. This setup is the strong baseline as it shows the impact of training off high quality MT system. We employ a set of ten templates within the thinking tokens, instructing the model to skip step-by-step reasoning. The used templates are available in Appendix F .
Injected Reasoning Traces
As a contrastive ablation, we train the same base model on reasoning traces generated for the same 28k examples using Command-A-Reasoning and DeepSeek-R1 . Note that only the reasoning traces are generated from these models, and the target text translation is kept from the Command-A-Translate as in the other experiments. This allows us to assess the role of structured reasoning traces compared to linear reasoning trajectories.
To ensure a fair comparison, we use the same hyperparameters as the structured reasoning for the baselines.
6 Results
We evaluate all models on the WMT24++ benchmark ( Deutsch et al., 2025 ) , focusing on nine diverse language pairs. Translation quality is evaluated using the XCOMET-XL metric ( Guerreiro et al., 2024 ) . For all evaluations, we set the decoding temperature to 0 0 to minimize variance and maximize reproducibility.
Table 3 summarizes the overall results. In the first block of the table, we compare the performance of our structured reasoning approach against both the base model and the direct translation finetuning setup. Direct Translation is a strong baseline as we ensure the performance improvement is not solely due to additional high-quality training data.
Across all language pairs, structured reasoning shows substantial performance improvements over these baselines, indicating that the structured dynamic template is effective in developing a reasoning process that meaningfully supports translation quality. It is worth mentioning that 0.5 0.5 XCOMET-XL score improvement is considered as noticeable by human as +1 BLEU improvement ( Kocmi et al., 2024 ) .
The middle part of the table compares our synthetic reasoning traces with the models trained on the trajectories generated by DeepSeek-R1 and Command-A-Reasoning . Comparing these models against the direct translation baseline, we see that the injected reasoning does not offer additional benefits. This finding is consistent with our earlier analysis in Section 3 , where we show that the existing reasoning capabilities of reasoning models are beneficial for the machine translation task.
Finally, in the bottom part of Table 3 , we conduct an ablation on the size of the training data to investigate the relation between the training size and performance improvement. We see a monotonically increasing trend as we use more data for training, aligning with Li et al. (2025) observations on positive effect of training size. 6.1 Reasoning vs. Translation Quality
To further examine the relative contribution of reasoning trace quality and target translation quality, we design a controlled ablation study using our 28 ​ k 28k samples.
As we mentioned earlier, each training example includes a triple of (source text, reasoning trace, target text) . Therefore, the quality of both the reasoning trace and the target text affects the model performance.
In this analysis, we explicitly vary these two factors independently. We consider three sources of reasoning traces: reasoning trajectories generated from Command-A-Reasoning , DeepSeek-R1 , and our proposed structured reasoning. Separately, we consider three sources of target translations: translations generated by Command-A-Reasoning , DeepSeek-R1 , and the stronger model Command-A-Translate . By pairing each reasoning trace source with each target translation source, we construct multiple training datasets in which the reasoning traces and target translations may originate from different models. We then run SFT on the same base model using each dataset and evaluate the resulting model’s translation performance.
The results are reported in Table 4 . Each block is dedicated to a reasoning trace source, and each row within a block presents the target translation source model.
Focusing on the top two blocks, we observe that for a fixed reasoning source, using target translations generated by the stronger model (i.e., Command-A-Translate ) consistently leads to substantially higher performance than using target translations from the other models. Interestingly, for the Command-A-Translate ’s target translation set, we do not observe meaningful performance differences between reasoning traces generated by Command-A-Reasoning and those by DeepSeek-R1 .
In the bottom block of Table 4 , we evaluate structured reasoning paired with target translations from Command-A-Reasoning . This configuration yields higher performance than using DeepSeek-R1 translations.
This is expected, as in Table 1 , we have shown that Command-A-Reasoning is a better model than DeepSeek-R1 on the translation task.
However, the gains remain notably smaller than those obtained when using Command-A-Translate as the target translation ( Table 3 ).
Overall, these results indicate that target translation quality has a substantially larger impact on downstream performance than the quality of the associated reasoning traces in machine translation. This suggests that improvements in reasoning traces alone are insufficient to compensate for weaker target translations.
7 Conclusions
In this paper, we conduct an in-depth study on the role of reasoning capabilities of reasoning language models in machine translation. Despite the substantial benefits that reasoning has provided for most reasoning-intensive tasks, our experiments show that current reasoning degrades translation quality across RLMs and languages. Through detailed analyses, we notice that the structure of current reasoning traces is highly linear, lacking key features such as revision, self-correction, and exploration of possible alternatives.
Building on our findings, we propose a structured reasoning framework specifically designed for MT that dynamically adapts the length of reasoning to the difficulty of translation. We show that post-training an RLM on a small set of structured reasoning substantially improves the translation performance.
Finally, we investigate the underlying factors behind the effectiveness of structured reasoning by disentangling the contributions of reasoning trace quality and final translation quality. Our results indicate that while both factors affect MT performance, the quality of final translations plays a dominant role.
Impact Statement
In this work, we present in-depth analyses on the reasoning capabilities of RLMs for MT. We propose a structured reasoning generation pipeline to enhance translation performance in RLMs. We do not foresee any significant negative societal impacts directly resulting from this work.
Limitations
In this work, we use WMT24++ as our primary evaluation benchmark, focusing on 9 language pairs. While we have tried to select a diverse set of language pairs, adding more could strengthen our conclusions and findings. Moreover, we solely employ automatic metrics for evaluation and to investigate the role of reasoning on the translation quality. While costly, incorporating human evaluation could provide additional support for our findings.

Let me start by producing an initial, faithful translation of the source text into {target_language}, which will serve as the foundation for later refinements.
{initial_draft}
Step 2 --- Adequacy
In this step, I focus on adequacy, ensuring that the full meaning of the challenging sentences is conveyed accurately and completely in {target_language}.
{adequacy_focused_translation}
Step 3 --- Fluency
At this stage, I refine the challenging sentences, ensuring they read smoothly and naturally in {target_language}, as if originally written in it.
{fluency_focused_translation}
Step 4 --- Final Translation
Finally, I bring together all the work from the previous steps, addressing any remaining issues or translation errors to produce a polished final version in {target_language}.
{final_translation}
Appendix E Structured Reasoning Templates
To make a diverse training set and avoid overfitting on the predefined sentences in the structured reasoning template, we consider a diverse set of transitional sentences for each step. We randomly pick a sentence for each step to generate a reasoning trace. Below, you find the set of sentences we considered.
