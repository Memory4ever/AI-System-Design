Abstract
More than half the global population now carries devices that can run ChatGPT-like language models with no Internet connection and minimal safety oversight – and hence the potential to promote self-harm, financial losses and extremism among other dangers 1 ; 2 ; 3 . Existing safety tools either require cloud connectivity or discover failures only after harm has occurred 4 ; 5 . Here we show that a large class of potentially dangerous tipping originates at the atomistic scale in such edge AI due to competition for the machinery’s attention 6 . This yields a mathematical formula for the dynamical tipping point n ∗ n^{*} , governed by dot-product competition for attention between the conversation’s context and competing output basins, that reveals new control levers. Validated against multiple AI models,
the mechanism can be instantiated for different definitions of ‘good’ and ‘bad’ and hence in principle applies across domains (e.g. health, law, finance, defense), changing legal landscapes (e.g. EU, UK, US and state level), languages, and cultural settings.
Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) has over one billion monthly active users 11 , with adoption accelerating across high-stakes domains: 66% of U.S. physicians now use AI professionally 8 and 12.5% of young adults use AI for primary mental health guidance 9 .
Phones and edge devices can now run language models locally and entirely offline with no real-time checks, no content filtering, and no ability to patch 2 ; 10 ; 3 , hence creating an ongoing unmonitored risk for more than 50% of the global population 1 ; 7 ; 12 (Fig. 1(a)). Such offline use is attractive for physicians avoiding HIPAA violations 13 , attorneys protecting privilege 14 , financial professionals protecting proprietary strategies, military personnel in denied-connectivity environments 15 ; 16 ; 17 – and any adult, teen or child seeking privacy and companionship 12 .
But cloud-based safety infrastructure is structurally absent from such AI, and any vulnerability in the frozen model weights persists for the lifetime of the deployment.
The AI safety community has developed increasingly sophisticated guardrails for large language models 4 ; 18 ; 5 ; 19 ; 20 ; 21 ; 22 ; 23 ; 24 . But training-time methods (e.g. RLHF, Constitutional AI) offer no prediction of when they will fail during deployment, while inference-time defenses (filtering, monitoring, patching) require cloud connectivity that edge devices lack.
Mechanistic interpretability 25 ; 26 ; 27 ; 31 ; 32 ; 22 ; 33 ; 34 ; 35 ; 36 ; 37 ; 38 ; 39 has made impressive progress via circuit-level analysis and sparse autoencoders. But these map static geometry, not the dynamics of when good output will tip to bad. Moreover, no amount of safety investment in cloud-hosted commercial models addresses this tipping issue, because users may have deliberately disconnected from those services.
Here we develop and test a predictive theory of good-to-bad tipping for AI models deployed on any device, including phones. Cross-architecture validation on six transformer models confirms the predictions, and independent data from ChatGPT-4o 40 provides evidence that the mechanism, and hence the risk, persists at production scale – indicating that it will not be outgrown as edge devices become more powerful.
Figures 1 and 2 demonstrate tipping in GPT-2 41 , a model small enough to run on any smartphone with no Internet connection and no external safety guardrails. Cross-architecture validation for GPT-2-medium, Pythia-160m, Pythia-410m, OPT-125m, OPT-350m is reported in SI Table S1.
We set the decoding temperature T T low to expose the raw mechanical output: increasing T T adds stochasticity that can produce additional hallucinations, but the core tendency for internal tipping persists at higher T T in a more stochastic form (see SI).
During both the AI’s single response (Fig. 1(b)) and the extended conversation (Fig. 2), the tipping to bad content ( 𝐃 \bf D ) can be immediate, or it can follow a long run of good ( 𝐁 \bf B ) content and hence lull the user into a false sense of trust.
Furthermore, Fig. 2 shows that the AI’s response to questions about vaccines, hurting people, or self-harm can flip between good ( 𝐁 {\bf B} ) and bad ( 𝐃 {\bf D} ) depending on what it has already been asked about the Earth’s shape.
To connect these dynamics to a predictive framework, we coarse-grain outputs into symbols based on the concept they convey 42 ; 33 . (See SI Sec. 7 for mathematical justification of this coarse-graining).
In the one-step conversation about the Earth’s shape, for example, 𝐀 \bf A denotes neutral content (e.g. ‘Shape?’, ‘Is it flat?’); 𝐁 \bf B denotes desirable output (e.g. ‘Round’, ‘It is not flat’); 𝐃 \bf D denotes undesirable output (e.g. ‘Flat’, ‘It is not round’); and 𝐂 \bf C denotes any other content. Each can be thought of as a basin in the machine’s internal representation space of concepts (Fig. 3(b)). Function words like ‘the’ are absorbed into these dominant-concept categories by coarse-graining.
As an additional consistency check, three independent commercial models (Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude) each produced identical symbol sequences to our manual classifications.

The initial ‘crack’ appears at the atomistic scale: a single attention head in the term Attn(..). We follow Figs. 1,2 in considering a coarse-grained vocabulary and refer to the SI for full algebra. The vector dimension is arbitrary: Fig. 3 uses 2D purely for illustration.
Suppose the conversation so far is 𝐀 \bf A . The attention mechanism 6 computes the dot product of the last symbol (query) with each input symbol (key), yielding attention weights that form the compass needle 𝐜 \bf c (context vector) as a weighted sum of input vectors: here 𝐜 = 𝐀 {\bf c}={\bf A} . The next output symbol is chosen via softmax over the dot products 𝐜 ⋅ 𝐁 {\bf c\cdot B} and 𝐜 ⋅ 𝐃 {\bf c\cdot D} , so at low T T the basin with the larger dot product wins.

Adding layer normalization (LN) and MLP – and multiple layers – can shift n ∗ n^{*} (SI Sec. 6) but does not remove the mechanism 45 . The 𝐁 − 𝐃 {\bf B-D} competition is equivalent to an effective force f = 𝐜 ⋅ 𝐁 − 𝐜 ⋅ 𝐃 f={\bf c\cdot B}-{\bf c\cdot D} along the 𝐁 − 𝐃 {\bf B}-{\bf D} axis: adding MLP and LN introduces a cubic nonlinearity which yields an approximate logistic map 46 (SI Secs. 8,9). This enriches the post-tipping dynamics from simple fixed point attractors . . . 𝐃𝐃𝐃𝐃 . . {\bf...DDDD..} to richer attractors (e.g. . . . 𝐁𝐁𝐃𝐃𝐁𝐁𝐃𝐃 . . {\bf...BBDDBBDD..} ) which become accessible at higher T T – as confirmed empirically.

The SI provides explicit numerical evaluation and verification across three cases of increasing architectural complexity: attention-only (Case 1, n ∗ = 3 n^{*}\!=\!3 ), pre-norm attention with layer normalization (Case 2, n ∗ = 4 n^{*}\!=\!4 ), and the full transformer block including MLP (Case 3, n ∗ n^{*} modulated but tipping preserved). Equation 2 is built from dot products among the prompt/context embedding 𝐀 {\bf A} and basin centroids 𝐁 {\bf B} and 𝐃 {\bf D} . Two natural evaluation limits exist: (i) raw dot products, which directly mirror the attention score s i ​ j = 𝐪 i ⋅ 𝐤 j / d k s_{ij}={\bf q}_{i}\!\cdot\!{\bf k}_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}} and hence provide the appropriate sign for the tipping direction in deployed transformers; and (ii) a cosine-normalized limit, motivated by LayerNorm placing penultimate-layer states on a “fuzzy sphere”, in which the basin self-competition inequality 𝐁 ⋅ 𝐁 > 𝐁 ⋅ 𝐃 {\bf B}\!\cdot\!{\bf B}>{\bf B}\!\cdot\!{\bf D} is guaranteed and the n ∗ n^{\ast} expression is mathematically well-posed (SI Sec. 3.5).
In practice, these conventions yield the same qualitative direction except when the cosine margin | Δ cos | |\Delta_{\cos}| is extremely small, i.e. when the prompt lies close to the basin boundary (SI Sec. 3.5).
Moreover, in the regimes studied here the exponential prefactor in Eq. 2 contributes a sub-percent correction, so that n ∗ n^{\ast} is dominated by the ratio term (SI Sec. 3.5 and SI Sec. 3.11).
Equation 2 reveals new control levers: the relative alignments of content basins, determined by training and by injected content. Because the formalism is general to however ‘good’ and ‘bad’ are defined, it can be applied across domains, changing legal landscapes, languages, and cultural settings, simply by redefining the centroid vectors 𝐁 {\bf B} and 𝐃 {\bf D} . The equation can be evaluated for any input 𝐏 1 , 𝐏 2 , … , 𝐏 i {\bf P}_{1},{\bf P}_{2},\ldots,{\bf P}_{i} , whether from an initial prompt or an entire conversation stream, with 𝐁 , 𝐃 {\bf B},{\bf D} representing the dominant basin pair in that setting.
As a practical safety tool, Eq. 2 can be evaluated at any stage during any interaction to detect approaching tipping: within the machine during its response (Fig. 1(b)) or across a multi-turn conversation (Fig. 2). Evaluation requires only dot products against precomputed centroid vectors, an operation that scales as d d compared with the d 2 d^{2} cost of the forward pass itself, enabling real-time monitoring without modifying the generation pipeline (Fig. 3(a), see Methods).

Another key prediction is that good-to-bad tipping can be controlled
by conversation history. Figure 2 demonstrates this empirically: the
same question yields 𝐁 {\bf B} or 𝐃 {\bf D} depending on preceding
exchanges. Injecting content 𝐂 {\bf C} into the prompt adds terms to
the numerator of Eq. 2: when 𝐂 {\bf C} is positively aligned with
𝐃 {\bf D} , n ∗ n^{*} decreases (earlier tipping); when 𝐂 {\bf C} is
negatively aligned, n ∗ n^{*} increases (delayed tipping) — both
confirmed by the calculation in Fig. 3 and by the empirical CCDH
study 40 . This reveals a control lever: injecting
appropriately chosen content can delay tipping so far that it falls
outside the machine’s finite output window and hence never appears.

Six transformers from three
independent groups yield unanimous sign agreement on safety-critical
prompts (SI Table S1); two independent codebases produce consistent
diagnostics across all prompts (SI Figs. S17–S33); a systematic
temperature series confirms the full theoretically predicted phase
diagram from deterministic lock-in through stochastic switching to
chaotic mixing; and the tipping formula produces correct predictions
only when evaluated at the penultimate layer where semantic structure
has formed, not at early layers where it has not (SI Sec. 14),
confirming that the mechanism is coupled to genuine learned geometry.
Taken together, these demonstrate
robust, reproducible directional predictions when the geometry is
well separated, and an explicit diagnostic for when predictions
should be treated as stochastic near a basin boundary, while avoiding
spurious tipping in a control setting.
Even if future device advances eventually allow ChatGPT-size
systems — and their guardrails — to run on edge devices, this
tipping mechanism will survive. The CCDH study 40 sent
60 high-risk prompts to ChatGPT-4o ( × 20 \times 20 repeats,
N = 1,200 N=1{,}200 ) and found harmful content in 53% of responses, varying
by domain (44% self-harm, 66% eating disorders, 50% substance
abuse). Four qualitatively distinct features of these results map
onto distinct structural properties of Eq. 2 (SI Table S2): (1) domain-dependent Δ raw \Delta_{\rm raw} yields domain-dependent n ∗ n^{*} ; (2) 𝐂 \mathbf{C} vectors aligned toward 𝐃 \mathbf{D} collapse n ∗ n^{*} to ≈ 0 \approx\!0 ; (3) 𝐃 \mathbf{D} tokens are self-reinforcing: each lowers n ∗ n^{*} further; (4) near-boundary prompts tip stochastically at finite T T .
Though precise internal embeddings of ChatGPT-4o are not accessible
for direct evaluation, four independent predictions from a single
formula – applied to a system three orders of magnitude larger than
the one on which it was quantitatively validated – constitute a
non-trivial test.
Unlike static classification tools such as linear probes, which must
be retrained per domain and per model, Eq. 2 is domain-portable and
predicts dynamics — tipping delay, escalation, and control
levers — while providing its own reliability indicator: wide
bootstrap CIs and | Δ ^ raw | ≈ 0 |\hat{\Delta}_{\rm raw}|\approx 0 diagnose when a
prompt sits near a basin boundary and the prediction should be treated
as stochastic rather than deterministic. Even if multiple basins compete, there will always be a pair whose dot products are closest – and extending Eq. 2 to include 𝐃 𝟏 \bf{D}_{1} , 𝐃 𝟐 \bf{D}_{2} etc. is straightforward.

first-layer embeddings, where the semantic distinction has not yet
formed, yield incorrect predictions, whereas penultimate-layer
embeddings produce correct ones (SI Sec. 14, Figs. S17, S19, S20).
Basin centroids 𝐁 {\bf B} and 𝐃 {\bf D} are means of N X = 6 N_{X}=6 phrase
embeddings per category (e.g. 𝐁 = { ‘Earth is round’ , ‘The Earth is a sphere’ , … } {\bf B}=\{\text{`Earth is round'},\text{`The Earth is a sphere'},\ldots\} ) and are held fixed across
all models.
These centroids are stable under larger phrase sets due to a
centre-of-mass averaging effect.
Full phrase lists are given in the SI.
The coarse-graining from Eq. 1 is a restructuring of sums, with the
approximation that each sentence’s projection onto the
( 𝐁 , 𝐃 ) ({\bf B},{\bf D}) subspace is captured by its
centroid 50 ; 51 ; 37 ; 52 ; 53 .
Metrics.
The qualitative tipping direction is assessed from the sign of the raw
dot-product difference
Δ raw ≡ 𝐀 ⋅ 𝐃 − 𝐀 ⋅ 𝐁 \Delta_{\rm raw}\equiv{\bf A}\!\cdot\!{\bf D}-{\bf A}\!\cdot\!{\bf B} ,
since these are the quantities the attention mechanism operates on
( s i ​ j = 𝐪 i ⋅ 𝐤 j / d k s_{ij}={\bf q}_{i}\!\cdot\!{\bf k}_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}} ).
The exact normalization lies between a raw-dot-product limit and a
cosine-normalized limit, depending on the particular architecture’s
layer normalization.
LayerNorm periodically compresses but does not fix hidden-state norms,
placing vectors on a fuzzy sphere whose radius fluctuates across layers
(coefficient of variation 2–6% across architectures;
SI Sec. 3.5) 54 .
Both the directional alignment and the residual norm asymmetry therefore
enter the attention competition, which is why
Δ ^ raw \hat{\Delta}_{\rm raw} is typically 1–2 orders of magnitude larger
than
Δ cos ≡ cos ⁡ ( 𝐀 , 𝐃 ) − cos ⁡ ( 𝐀 , 𝐁 ) \Delta_{\cos}\equiv\cos({\bf A},{\bf D})-\cos({\bf A},{\bf B})
and why its sign is the more reliable predictor of tipping direction.
Because the absolute scale of high-dimensional dot products is large
( ∼ 10 5 \sim\!10^{5} – 10 6 10^{6} for d = 768 d=768 ), all six pairwise dot products for
each prompt are divided by the largest among them for readability;
the resulting scaled index is
Δ ^ raw ≡ Δ raw / max \hat{\Delta}_{\rm raw}\equiv\Delta_{\rm raw}/\max .
The tipping-point estimate n ∗ n^{*} (Eq. 2) is evaluated in the
unit-normalized (cosine) limit, where the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
guarantees that the denominator is negative and the Boltzmann
competition between basins is well defined (SI Sec. 12.7).
Bootstrap and statistical assessment.
Bootstrap resampling ( N = 200 N=200 ) of the six basin phrases gives 95%
confidence intervals on Δ ^ raw \hat{\Delta}_{\rm raw} , Δ cos \Delta_{\cos} ,
and n ∗ n^{*} .
Individual bootstrap CIs on Δ ^ raw \hat{\Delta}_{\rm raw} span zero for
most model–prompt pairs because six basin phrases provide limited
resampling power; the statistical evidence therefore resides in
cross-model sign consistency (6/6 positive for both Flat Earth and
Vaccines, binomial p = 0.031 p=0.031 per prompt).
Models within each family share training corpora and are not fully
independent, but all three independent groups (OpenAI, EleutherAI,
Meta) agree on the tipping direction for both safety-critical prompts.
Dual-codebase robustness.
To check robustness, we verified that the geometric diagnostic
𝐀 ⋅ 𝐃 > 𝐀 ⋅ 𝐁 {\bf A}\!\cdot\!{\bf D}>{\bf A}\!\cdot\!{\bf B} is reproduced by an
independently developed codebase (code supplied) that uses different
basin phrases, a different model-loading class, and raw dot products
rather than cosine similarities as its primary metric; details are
given in the SI.
CCDH mapping.
The CCDH study 40 used multi-step conversations whose
full transcripts are not publicly available; SI Table S2 therefore maps
structural predictions of Eq. 2 to observed phenomenology rather than
attempting direct quantitative evaluation.
The logistic-map reduction (SI Secs. 8–9) predicts period-doubling
oscillations
( 𝐁 → 𝐃 → 𝐁 → 𝐃 ​ … {\bf B}\to{\bf D}\to{\bf B}\to{\bf D}\ldots ) under high MLP gain
or low temperature; we observe these patterns in all tested models and
in the traces from ChatGPT-4o 40 ; 46 .
Real-time deployment cost.
Practical basin centroids can be precomputed offline for each domain of
concern and stored as a lightweight lookup table; updating
Δ raw \Delta_{\rm raw} requires only a running weighted sum over the
context window.
The per-token cost reduces to a small number of dot products against
precomputed centroid vectors 𝐁 {\bf B} and 𝐃 {\bf D} , an operation that
scales as d d and is therefore negligible compared with the d 2 d^{2} cost
of the forward pass itself.
The dominant overhead is not computation but definition: specifying the
centroid phrases that define 𝐁 {\bf B} and 𝐃 {\bf D} for each domain
and cultural setting.
This is a one-time annotation task analogous to defining classification
labels, and can be updated independently of the model weights.
In deployment, Eq. 2 therefore functions as a low-cost monitor that runs
in parallel with generation, flagging when the predicted n ∗ n^{*} falls
below a safety threshold without modifying the generation pipeline
itself.
