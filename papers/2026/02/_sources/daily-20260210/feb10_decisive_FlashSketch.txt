Additionally, we provide a fast but fragile block-row sampling alternative in cite65†Appendix C .\nL282: ## 6 Theory: Permutation-Coupled Localization\nL283: We build our theoretical understanding of BlockPerm-SJLT on the framework of localized sketching developed in (cite106†Srinivasa et al., 2020 ). Localized sketching studies block-diagonal (or block-local) sketches whose guarantees depend on how the target subspace distributes across the chosen blocks. Our sketch BlockPerm-SJLT follows a very similar locality principle with the key difference that a single output block mixes information from multiple input blocks via the union-of-permutations wiring.\nL284: We formalize this via a new neighborhood coherence quantity that is a natural generalization of the block coherence in (cite106†Srinivasa et al., 2020 ).\nL285: ### 6.1 Coherence for Permutation-Coupled Localization\nL286: \nL287: We begin by defining our generalization of block-coherence cite124†Definition 3.2 , the neighborhood coherence.\nL288: ###### Definition 6.1 (Neighborhood coherence for permutation wiring).\nL289: \nL290: Fix $\\kappa$ edge-disjoint permutations $\\pi_{1},\\ldots,\\pi_{\\kappa}$ on $[M]$ and define the neighborhood of output block $g\\in[M]$ by\nL291: \nL292:  | $$\\mathcal{N}(g)\\;:=\\;\\{\\pi_{\\ell}(g)\\}_{\\ell=1}^{\\kappa}.$$  |  | (3)\nL293: \nL294: Let $U_{\\mathcal{N}(g)}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{(\\kappa B_{c})\\times r}$ denote the matrix obtained by stacking the blocks $U^{(h)}$ for $h\\in\\mathcal{N}(g)$. The neighborhood coherence of $U$ under $\\pi$ is\nL295:  | $$\\mu_{\\mathrm{nbr}}(U;\\pi)\\;:=\\;\\frac{M}{\\kappa}\\max_{g\\in[M]}\\,\\|U_{\\mathcal{N}(g)}\\|_{2}^{2}.$$  |  | (4)\nL296: \nL297: Recall that the edge-disjoint condition ensures that each output block $g$ connects to $\\kappa$ distinct input blocks.\nL298: ### 6.2 Oblivious Subspace Embedding Guarantee\nL299: \nL300: We now state our main theoretical result cite125†Theorem 6.2 , which is an OSE guarantee for BlockPerm-SJLT controlled by neighborhood coherence. The flavor is very similar to the localized SJLT result of (cite106†Srinivasa et al., 2020 ), but with block coherence replaced by neighborhood coherence.\nL301: ###### Theorem 6.2 (OSE for BlockPerm-SJLT).\nL302: \nL303: Fix $U\\in\\mathbb{R}^{d\\times r}$ with orthonormal columns. Let $S\\sim\\textsc{BlockPerm-SJLT}$ with parameters $(M,B_{r},\\kappa,s)$ and embedding dimension $k=MB_{r}$. Let $t:=r+\\log\\frac{1}{\\delta}$. There exist absolute constants $C,c>0$ such that, if we have\nL304: \nL305:  | $$k\\;\\geq\\;C\\,\\frac{\\mu_{\\mathrm{nbr}}(U;\\pi)}{\\varepsilon^{2}}\\,t\\qquad\\text{and}\\qquad\\kappa s\\;\\geq\\;C\\,\\frac{1}{\\varepsilon}\\,t,$$  |  | (5)\nL306: \nL307: then with probability at least $1-\\delta$ over $S$,\nL308:  | $$\\bigl\\|U^{\\top}S^{\\top}SU-I_{r}\\bigr\\|_{2}\\leq\\varepsilon.$$  |  | (6)\nL309: The proof follows the localized sketching blueprint closely. For a fixed vector $w$, we decompose $\\|SUw\\|_{2}^{2}$ into a sum of independent block contributions and combine a fixed-vector tail bound with a net argument. The union-of-permutations wiring enters through a simple energy identity that ensures the local neighborhoods collectively cover the input without bias. A detailed proof appears in cite46†Appendix A .\nL310: Note that for $\\kappa=1$, cite125†Theorem 6.2 recovers the localized SJLT regime controlled by $\\mu_{\\mathrm{blk}}(U)$ from (cite106†Srinivasa et al., 2020 ).\nL311: ### 6.3 Controlling Neighborhood Coherence with Randomized Permutations\nL312: \nL313: We now discuss how neighborhood coherence interpolates between block coherence and fully mixed coherence, and how random permutations can improve it in practice.\nL314: ##### Worst-case comparison.\nL315: \nL316: First, a simple worst case comparison between neighborhood coherence and block coherence shows that permutations can reduce coherence by up to a factor of $\\kappa$, but in general we cannot do better than block coherence. For any fixed set of edge-disjoint permutations $\\pi$,\nL317: \nL318:  | $$\\frac{1}{\\kappa}\\,\\mu_{\\mathrm{blk}}(U)\\;\\leq\\;\\mu_{\\mathrm{nbr}}(U;\\pi)\\;\\leq\\;\\mu_{\\mathrm{blk}}(U).$$  |  | (7)\nL319: The upper bound is a triangle inequality and the lower bound follows because every block appears in at least one neighborhood. A detailed derivation appears in cite46†Appendix A .\nL320: ##### Randomized Permutations.\nL321: \nL322: Now, if we model the permutations $\\pi_{1},\\ldots,\\pi_{\\kappa}$ as independent and uniformly random, then we can show a stronger upper bound that improves with $\\kappa$ and pushes $\\mu_{\\mathrm{nbr}}(U;\\pi)$ toward one. A precise statement appears as cite126†Proposition A.11 in cite46†Appendix A . Informally, we have the following smoothing bound. Let $L:=\\log\\!\\bigl(\\tfrac{Mr}{\\delta}\\bigr)$. With probability at least $1-\\delta$ over the permutations, we have\nL323:  | $$\\mu_{\\mathrm{nbr}}(U;\\pi)\\;\\leq\\;1+C\\left(\\sqrt{\\frac{\\mu_{\\mathrm{blk}}(U)\\,L}{\\kappa}}+\\frac{\\mu_{\\mathrm{blk}}(U)\\,L}{\\kappa}\\right)$$  |  | (8)\nL324: \nL325: for an absolute constant $C>0$.\nL326: \nL327: This rigorously justifies the benefit of using multiple permutations to improve mixing. When $\\kappa$ is large enough, the neighborhood coherence approaches one, which is the optimal coherence for any $U$, improving the OSE guarantee in cite125†Theorem 6.2 .\nL328: ###### Remark 6.3 (Independent permutations in practice).\nL329: \nL330: Our analysis models $\\pi_{1},\\ldots,\\pi_{\\kappa}$ as independent uniformly random permutations. In FlashSketch, we generate $\\kappa$ distinct permutations using a lightweight family for efficiency, see cite66†Appendix D . When $\\kappa\\ll M$, sampling distinct permutations is close to sampling independently, and collisions are rare. This justifies our theoretical model.\nL331: ## 7 Experiments\nL332: We empirically evaluate FlashSketch at two levels. First, we treat sketching as a primitive and measure the runtime and distortion of computing $SA$ for dense $A$ across a broad range of shapes. Second, we integrate FlashSketch into end-to-end workloads where sketching sits on the critical path. As part of this, we consider a suite of standard RandNLA tasks as well as GraSS, an end-to-end ML application for data attribution (cite98†Hu et al., 2025 ) in which sketching is a core component.\nL333: We time using CUDA events and report the mean over 10 iterations (after warm-up iterations). We restrict our evaluation to FP32 arithmetic for both sketching and downstream tasks for consistency, since all our baselines support FP32. Our main kernel idea extends naturally to other precisions as well, but we leave concrete exploration of precision-specific optimizations to future work.\nL334: ### 7.1 Baselines\nL335: \nL336: We compare against dense and sparse GPU baselines.\nL337: \nL338:   1. 1.\nL339: \nL340: A dense JL transform with a standard Gaussian projection using cuBLAS.\nL341: \nL342:   2. 2.\nL343: \nL344: A sparse JL transform using cuSPARSE SpMM.\nL345: \nL346:   3. 3.\nL347: \nL348: A sparse JL transform using the GraSS SJLT kernel (cite98†Hu et al., 2025 ).\nL349: \nL350:   4. 4.\nL351: \nL352: A subsampled randomized Hadamard transform (SRHT) using the Fast Hadamard Transform (FHT) kernel from Dao-AILab (cite118†Dao-AILab, 2024 ).\nL353: ### 7.2 Sketching\nL354: \nL355: For the primitive sketching evaluation, we use the relative error in the computed Gram matrix $\\|A^{\\top}A-(SA)^{\\top}(SA)\\|_{F}/\\|A^{\\top}A\\|_{F}$ as our quality metric. At a high level, GraSS constructs a feature cache for the training set by compressing per-example gradients using a sparsification followed by a random projection (or sketch).\nL404: In the attribution phase, GraSS computes an analogous representation for a test/query example and produces an attribution vector using the cached features and a small downstream solve. In GraSS, the random projection step is a key bottleneck: it is invoked for every training example during cache construction (and for every query at inference time), so kernel efficiency directly impacts end-to-end wall time.\nL405: We replace GraSS’s sparse projection (its SJLT CUDA kernel) with FlashSketch while keeping all other pipeline components fixed. We adapt the code from their open-source repository directly (cite132†TRAIS-Lab, 2025 ). We focus our evaluation on the time spent in the projection/sketching step during feature cache construction.\nL406: ##### Attribution quality via the linear datamodeling score (LDS).\nL407: \nL408: We evaluate attribution quality using the linear datamodeling score (LDS) introduced in TRAK (cite133†Park et al., 2023 ), which GraSS also adopts. LDS is a counterfactual metric: it measures how well an attribution method predicts how the model’s output on a fixed example $z$ would change if we retrained on different subsets of the training set. Note that higher LDS is better.\nL409: cite134†Figure 4 shows the quality–speed Pareto frontier for end-to-end GraSS on an MLP trained on MNIST (cite135†LeCun et al., 2002 ). In the low $k$ regime, low occupancy limits speedups. We resolve this to an extent using a split-$B_{c}$ approach discussed in cite63†Section B.1 . Additionally, while $\\kappa$ can be tuned to trade off mixing and memory efficiency, very large $\\kappa$ increases input reads, which can limit speedups.\nL417: ##### Statistics–hardware tradeoffs.\nL418: \nL419: While a primary contribution of FlashSketch is the tunable parameter $\\kappa$ that directly trades off sketch quality and GPU efficiency, the optimal choice of $\\kappa$ depends on the input data statistics and the hardware characteristics. In this work, we empirically choose the optimal $\\kappa$ on the Pareto frontier. An interesting future direction is to more deeply explore this tradeoff and develop a principled, perhaps adaptive approach to select $\\kappa$.\nL420: ##### Theoretical limitations.\nL421: \nL422: Our theoretical analysis in cite28†Section 6 focuses on OSE guarantees for BlockPerm-SJLT using independent uniform permutations. In practice, we sample edge-disjoint permutations which introduce dependence between neighborhoods. While this is mild in the $\\kappa\\ll M$ regime we focus on, it is an interesting open question to analyze BlockPerm-SJLT with such dependent permutations.\nL423: ##### Beyond OSE.\nL424: \nL425: We focus on OSE-style guarantees because they capture the geometric fidelity needed in many RandNLA algorithms. An appealing direction is to derive tighter guarantees tailored to the Gram-matrix metrics we evaluate, and to connect those directly to end-to-end downstream performance in learning pipelines.\nL426: ## References\nL427:   * Agarwal et al.
