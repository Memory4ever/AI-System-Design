69: Cross-Architecture Merging for Large Language Models
70: Abstract
71: Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong capabilities by scaling model capacity and training data, yet many real-world deployments rely on smaller models trained or adapted from low-resource data. This gap motivates the need for mechanisms to transfer knowledge from large, high-resource models to smaller, resource-constrained targets.
72: While model merging provides an effective transfer mechanism, most existing approaches assume architecture-compatible models and therefore cannot directly transfer knowledge from large high-resource LLMs to heterogeneous low-resource targets.
73: In this work, we propose a cross-architecture merging framework based on optimal transport (OT) that aligns activations to infer cross-neuron correspondences between heterogeneous models.
74: The resulting transport matrices are then used to guide direct weight-space fusion, enabling effective high-resource to low-resource transfer using only a small set of inputs.
75: Extensive experiments across low-resource languages and specialized domains demonstrate consistent improvements over target base models. The code is available at https://github.com/chenhangcuisg-code/Cross-Architecture-Merging-for-\Large-Language-Models/.

90: 1 Introduction
91: Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success in general language understanding and generation (Achiam et al., 2023; Grattafiori et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2023), enabling a wide range of downstream applications, e.g., in medicine (Xie et al., 2024), finance (Konstantinidis et al., 2024), and education (Wen et al., 2024). This success is largely driven by scaling model capacity and pretraining on massive, high-quality, and diverse corpora.
92: In practice, many real-world deployments rely on models with limited parameter budgets trained or adapted from low-resource data. Typical examples include low-resource languages such as Malaysian languages (Hew et al., 2025) and Cantonese dialects (Liu, 2022). Due to constraints on both model capacity and data availability, models trained from low-resource languages are unable to acquire specific knowledge comparable to large and data-rich models.
93: A natural direction to address this challenge is high-resource to low-resource transfer (Adimulam et al., 2022; Myakala & Naayini, 2023; Cao et al., 2025), which leverages representations learned by large, well-trained models to improve performance in low-resource domains.
94: Among existing approaches, model merging (Wortsman et al., 2022a; Jin et al., 2022; Imfeld et al., 2023a) has emerged as an alternative to gradient-based adaptation. It directly aggregates parameters from multiple expert models, enabling capability fusion without access to massive training data or repeated optimization.
95: With the rise of large language models (LLMs) (Yang et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2024; Perin et al., 2024), this idea naturally extends to LLM merging.
96: Despite its appeal, many current techniques (Tao et al., 2024; Perin et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2025) assume that LLMs being merged share the same architecture, enabling direct weight-space operations. This assumption restricts their applicability in low-resource transfer scenarios, where the source model is often a large high-resource LLM, while the target model is a smaller architecture designed for deployment efficiency or domain-specific constraints (Liu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023a; Kolomeitsev, 2025). Direct parameter merging between heterogeneous architectures becomes challenging.
97: Although some recent methods attempt cross-architecture merging or knowledge fusion for LLMs (Wan et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025), they typically rely on distillation-based training (Hinton et al., 2015; Hsieh et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2025) to first distill knowledge into the parameter space of a target model, followed by merging among architecture-compatible models.
98: These limitations naturally raise the following question:
99: Can we enable high-resource to low-resource knowledge transfer across heterogeneous model architectures through direct model merging?
100: Motivated by this question, we propose a cross-model merging framework based on optimal transport (Peyré & Cuturi, 2019; Rout et al., 2021) to enable parameter transfer between heterogeneous architectures.
101: Our approach is motivated by the observation that, despite differences in architecture and model capacity, language models often exhibit correlated internal activations when processing the same inputs (Huh et al., 2024; Shah & Khosla, 2025).
102: Building on this insight, we establish a structured correspondence between heterogeneous models by aligning their internal activations using an optimal transport formulation.
103: Given a small set of inputs, we extract intermediate activations from both the source and target models and compute cross-model similarity signals at the feature level.
104: These signals are used to infer cross-neuron relationships between the two models, yielding a global transfer plan that captures transferable structure across layers and neurons.
105: The resulting transport relationships are then converted into weight-space fusion operators, enabling direct parameter fusion across architectures.
106: In summary, our contribution is a cross-architecture model merging framework that enables high-resource to low-resource knowledge transfer.
107: By aligning heterogeneous language models in activation space through an optimal transport formulation, our method infers structured neuron-level correspondences across models with different architectures and capacities using only a small set of inputs.
108: Crucially, we show that these activation-space correspondences admit a principled weight-space interpretation, allowing them to be converted into direct fusion for parameter transfer across heterogeneous models.
109: Extensive experiments on low-resource languages and domains demonstrate consistent improvements, establishing our approach as a practical alternative to distillation-based transfer.
110: As shown in Figure 1, our method consistently improves performance across different settings under multiple strategies.

117: 2 Related Work
118: Model Merging.
119: Model merging seeks to combine multiple expert models into a single model, typically without access to the original training data and without costly training (Ilharco et al., 2022; Wortsman et al., 2022b; Ainsworth et al., 2022).
120: Existing approaches primarily involve two stages: pre-merging and during-merging, covering both foundation models (LLMs and MLLMs) (Yang et al., 2024) and other deep learning settings (Li et al., 2023).
121: Pre-merging methods focus on preparing models for subsequent fusion by modifying their parameters or representations in advance.
122: Typical approaches include adaptation schemes designed to reduce parameter interference (Jacot et al., 2018; Ortiz-Jimenez et al., 2023) and alignment techniques that map different checkpoints into compatible parameter spaces prior to merging (Singh & Jaggi, 2020; Imfeld et al., 2023b).
123: During-merging methods perform the actual fusion, ranging from simple parameter averaging (Wortsman et al., 2022a) to more structured strategies such as subspace-based merging (Yadav et al., 2023), as well as optimization-based merging (Wei et al., 2025).
124: Although some methods attempt cross-architecture merging or knowledge fusion in LLMs (Wan et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025), they generally rely on distillation-based training to first transfer knowledge into the parameter space of a target model.
125: Optimal Transport and Applications.
126: Optimal transport (OT) provides a principled framework for comparing probability measures by
127: finding a minimum-cost plan that transports mass from a source distribution to a target
128: distribution (Peyré & Cuturi, 2019).
129: Building on solid theory, OT has become a versatile tool across a broad range of applications.
130: In machine learning, OT is commonly used for distribution matching in generative modeling (Rout et al., 2021), domain adaptation under distribution shift (Liu et al., 2023b; Redko et al., 2019), imitation learning (Luo et al., 2023), and clustering (Del Barrio et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2023).
131: More recently, OT has been explored as a mechanism for model merging, where a transport plan provides a soft correspondence between neurons and enables weight merging across models (Imfeld et al., 2023a; Singh & Jaggi, 2020).
132: However, while recent studies have used optimal transport (OT) to reveal cross-model feature similarity even between heterogeneous LLMs (Shah & Khosla, 2025), how to effectively leverage OT for model merging with different architectures remains underexplored.

134: 3.1 Optimal Transport
135: Optimal transport (OT) provides a principled way to compute a soft correspondence between
136: two sets of objects under a pairwise cost.
137: Given a cost matrix C∈ℝn×mC\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m} and two discrete distributions
138: a∈Δna\in\Delta_{n}, b∈Δmb\in\Delta_{m}, OT solves
139: minQ∈ℝ+n×m⁡⟨C,Q⟩s.t.Q​𝟏=a,Q⊤​𝟏=b.\min_{Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times m}}\ \langle C,Q\rangle\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Q\mathbf{1}=a,\ \ Q^{\top}\mathbf{1}=b.
140: (1)
141: We adopt the entropically regularized variant,
142: minQ∈ℝ+n×m⁡⟨C,Q⟩−ε​H​(Q)s.t.Q​𝟏=a,Q⊤​𝟏=b,\min_{Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times m}}\ \langle C,Q\rangle-\varepsilon H(Q)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Q\mathbf{1}=a,\ \ Q^{\top}\mathbf{1}=b,
143: (2)
144: where H(Q)=−∑i,jQi​j(logQi​j−1)H(Q)=-\sum_{i,j}Q_{ij}(\log Q_{ij}-1) and ε>0\varepsilon>0.
145: The solution admits the Sinkhorn scaling form (Cuturi, 2013)
146: Q=diag⁡(u)​K​diag​(v)Q=\mathrm{diag}(u)\,K\,\mathrm{diag}(v) with K=exp(−C/ε)K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon), and can be computed
147: by alternating updates on uu and vv.
148: 3.2 Neurons and Features in Transformers
149: In this paper, we adopt a structural view of neurons in Transformer models.
150: Specifically, we use the term “neuron” to refer to a unit in the weight space of a linear
151: sublayer, corresponding to a single row or column of the associated weight matrix.
152: The values propagated through the network are
153: referred to as activated features.
154: Specifically, consider a linear transformation
155: y=W​x,y=Wx,
156: (3)
157: where W∈ℝdout×dinW\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}.
158: We distinguish two neuron spaces induced by WW:
159: (i) input-side neurons correspond to the columns of WW (indexed by dind_{\mathrm{in}}), and
160: (ii) output-side neurons correspond to the rows of WW (indexed by doutd_{\mathrm{out}}).
161: Accordingly, x∈ℝdinx\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}} and y∈ℝdouty\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}} represent
162: features in the input and output spaces, respectively.
163: In a Transformer block, the primary linear sublayers include the attention projections
164: Q=WQ​h,K=WK​h,V=WV​h,Q=W_{Q}h,\quad K=W_{K}h,\quad V=W_{V}h,
165: (4)
166: and the MLP projections
167: z=W1​h,h′=σ⁡(z),o=W2​h′,z=W_{1}h,\quad h^{\prime}=\sigma(z),\quad o=W_{2}h^{\prime},
168: (5)
169: where σ⁡(⋅)\sigma(\cdot) denotes an elementwise nonlinearity.
170: In all these cases, rows and columns of the projection matrices define neurons in weight space,
171: while each dimension of the activations corresponds to a feature induced by these
172: neurons.

174: Illustration of cross-architecture merging pipeline.
175: Given a small dataset 𝒟\mathcal{D}, we extract intermediate activations from a high-resource source model and a low-resource target model with heterogeneous architectures.
176: We then use optimal transport to infer layer- and feature-level correspondences, and leverage the resulting transport plans for direct parameter fusion.
177: Finally, the fused model can be optionally refined via residual-frozen adaptation, where the transferred residuals are kept fixed and only base weights are updated.

179: Problem Definition.
180: Our goal is to merge models across heterogeneous architectures.
181: We consider a target model MAM_{A} with LL layers and a source model MBM_{B} with MM layers.
182: Given a set of TT samples 𝒟={x1,…,xT}\mathcal{D}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{T}\}, we record
183: activations from both models across transformer modules:
184: Xℓ\displaystyle X_{\ell}
185: ∈ℝT×nℓ,ℓ=1,…,L,\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n_{\ell}},\quad\ell=1,\dots,L,
186: (6)
187: Ym\displaystyle Y_{m}
188: ∈ℝT×nm′,m=1,…,M,\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n^{\prime}_{m}},\quad m=1,\dots,M,
189: where each row corresponds to one input sample and each column corresponds to one feature channel.
190: Details of the data used for activation extraction and transport estimation are provided in
191: Appendix B.1.
192: 4.1 Finding Feature and Layer Relationships using Optimal Transport
193: We use optimal transport (OT) (Peyré & Cuturi, 2019) to infer transport relationship matrices from
194: activations, which reveal how activated feature channels in a source model can be recombined to
195: match those in a target model.
196: Although the ultimate goal is to merge parameters (i.e., neuron weights), directly establishing
197: neuron-to-neuron correspondences is challenging across heterogeneous architectures.
198: Instead, we first infer correspondences in the activation space and then lift these
199: activation-level relationships to the neuron level for parameter transport.
200: Concretely, for each target layer ℓ\ell and source layer mm, we quantify cross-model similarity at
201: the level of activation channels by constructing a correlation-based cost matrix
202: Cℓ​m[i,j]=dcorr(Xℓ[:,i],Ym[:,j])≜1−ρ(Xℓ[:,i],Ym[:,j]),C^{\ell m}[i,j]=d_{\mathrm{corr}}\!\left(X_{\ell}[:,i],\,Y_{m}[:,j]\right)\triangleq 1-\rho\!\left(X_{\ell}[:,i],\,Y_{m}[:,j]\right),
203: (7)
204: where ρ⁡(⋅,⋅)\rho(\cdot,\cdot) denotes the Pearson correlation coefficient.
205: Based on this cost matrix, we compute a feature relationship matrix
206: Qℓ​m∈ℝnℓ×nm′Q^{\ell m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times n^{\prime}_{m}} by solving an entropically regularized optimal transport
207: problem:
208: Qℓ​m=arg⁡minQ∈𝒯⁡(nℓ,nm′)​⟨Cℓ​m,Q⟩−ε​H​(Q),Q^{\ell m}=\arg\min_{Q\in\mathcal{T}(n_{\ell},n^{\prime}_{m})}\ \langle C^{\ell m},Q\rangle-\varepsilon H(Q),
209: (8)
210: where H(Q)=−∑i,jQi​j(logQi​j−1)H(Q)=-\sum_{i,j}Q_{ij}(\log Q_{ij}-1) and ε>0\varepsilon>0.
211: The transportation polytope enforces balanced marginals,
212: 𝒯(nℓ,nm′)={Q∈ℝ+nℓ×nm′:Q𝟏=1nℓ𝟏,Q⊤𝟏=1nm′𝟏}.\mathcal{T}(n_{\ell},n^{\prime}_{m})=\Big\{Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{\ell}\times n^{\prime}_{m}}:Q\mathbf{1}=\tfrac{1}{n_{\ell}}\mathbf{1},\ Q^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\tfrac{1}{n^{\prime}_{m}}\mathbf{1}\Big\}.
213: (9)
214: The resulting transport plan Qℓ​mQ^{\ell m} specifies how each target activation channel can be
215: represented as a weighted combination of source channels.
216: Although inferred purely from activation statistics, Qℓ​mQ^{\ell m} will later be used as a
217: neuron-level mixing operator for transporting source parameters into the target neuron coordinates.
218: To summarize the compatibility between target layer ℓ\ell and source layer mm, we aggregate the
219: feature-level correspondence into a scalar transport cost inspired by (Shah & Khosla, 2025)
220: Clayer​[ℓ,m]=⟨Cℓ​m,Qℓ​m⟩,C_{\mathrm{layer}}[\ell,m]=\langle C^{\ell m},Q^{\ell m}\rangle,
221: (10)
222: which yields a layer-to-layer cost matrix
223: Clayer∈ℝL×MC_{\mathrm{layer}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times M}.
224: Using these layer-wise costs, we further infer a global correspondence matrix
225: P∈ℝL×MP\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times M} by solving another entropically regularized optimal transport problem:
226: P=arg⁡minP∈𝒯⁡(L,M)​⟨Clayer,P⟩−η​H​(P),P=\arg\min_{P\in\mathcal{T}(L,M)}\ \langle C_{\mathrm{layer}},P\rangle-\eta H(P),
227: (11)
228: where η>0\eta>0 and
229: 𝒯(L,M)={P∈ℝ+L×M:P𝟏=1L𝟏,P⊤𝟏=1M𝟏}.\mathcal{T}(L,M)=\Big\{P\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{L\times M}:P\mathbf{1}=\tfrac{1}{L}\mathbf{1},\ P^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\tfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}\Big\}.
230: (12)
231: Each entry Pℓ​mP_{\ell m} quantifies the degree of correspondence between target layer ℓ\ell and source
232: layer mm.
233: Together, the feature-level transport plans {Qℓ​m}\{Q^{\ell m}\} and the global matrix PP
234: specify how source neurons are used to construct target neurons.
235: All optimal transport problems are solved using Sinkhorn iterations (Cuturi, 2013); implementation details about the algorithm are
236: provided in Appendix A.
237: 4.2 From Activated Features to Neurons: Weight Fusion via Feature and Layer Transport
238: Our transport matrices are estimated from activated features, while the ultimate objective
239: is to merge neurons and their associated parameters.
240: The key observation is that each feature dimension corresponds to a well-defined neuron axis in
241: the underlying weight matrix, specifically, a column in the input space or a row in the output space.
242: This correspondence allows us to lift feature-level relations into neuron-level mixing operators,
243: which are then used to transport source weights into the target neuron basis.
244: Feature- and Layer-wise Transport.
245: In attention modules, input-side (pre-projection) and output-side (post-projection) features lie in
246: different representation spaces and may induce different cross-layer alignment signals.
247: Accordingly, we estimate two feature-level transport plans,
248: Qinℓ​mQ^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{in}} from pre-activation features and
249: Qoutℓ​mQ^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{out}} from post-activation features.
250: At the layer level, we compute two compatibility costs by aggregating the corresponding feature-level
251: transport objectives, and solve the layer-level OT separately for the pre- and post-sides,
252: yielding PpreP_{\mathrm{pre}} and PpostP_{\mathrm{post}}, respectively.
253: We then combine them into an effective layer correspondence
254: Peff​[ℓ,m]=Ppre​[ℓ,m]⋅Ppost​[ℓ,m],P_{\mathrm{eff}}[\ell,m]=\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{pre}}[\ell,m]\cdot P_{\mathrm{post}}[\ell,m]},
255: (13)
256: Selective transport via top-kk neuron replacement.
257: To improve robustness across heterogeneous architectures and avoid unnecessary interference,
258: we restrict fusion to a small subset of neurons that are highly activated on the same dataset
259: 𝒟\mathcal{D} used for alignment.
260: Concretely, for each layer ℓ\ell and each projection module,
261: we run the target model on 𝒟\mathcal{D} and record neuron activations via forward hooks.
262: For each neuron, we compute an activation strength score as the mean absolute activation over
263: samples
264: sj=1T​∑t=1T|ht,j|,s_{j}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl|h_{t,j}\bigr|,
265: where ht,jh_{t,j} denotes the activation of neuron jj at sample tt.
266: We then select the top-kk neurons with the largest scores in each layer and projection module,
267: and record their indices. Details about selection can be found in Appendix A.5
268: Fusion is applied only to these selected neuron indices, while all remaining parameters
269: in the target model are left unchanged.
270: For attention projections, this corresponds to replacing the rows or columns of the weight
271: matrices associated with the selected neurons.
272: This design yields a partial weight fusion scheme that injects source knowledge only into
273: neurons that are both highly active on the target data and well-aligned across models.
274: Under this design, the fused weights at target layer ℓ\ell can be written as:
275: WAℓ,fused=WAℓ\displaystyle W_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{fused}}=W_{A}^{\ell}
276: (14)
277: +α⋅𝐌ℓ⊙(∑m=1MPeff[ℓ,m]Qℓ​moutWBm(Qℓ​min)⊤−WAℓ),\displaystyle+\alpha\cdot\mathbf{M}^{\ell}\odot\Bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{M}P_{\mathrm{eff}}[\ell,m]\,Q^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{out}}\,W_{B}^{m}\,\bigl(Q^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{in}}\bigr)^{\!\top}-W_{A}^{\ell}\Bigg),
278: where 𝐌ℓ\mathbf{M}^{\ell} is a binary neuron-level mask that is nonzero only on the selected
279: top-kk neuron indices, ⊙\odot denotes masking at the neuron level, and
280: α∈[0,1]\alpha\in[0,1] controls the strength of replacement.
281: Bias terms are handled analogously.
282: 4.3 Residual-Frozen Adaptation after Fusion
283: After fusion, only a small subset of neurons in the target model are modified by
284: transported source parameters, while the remaining parameters remain identical to the original
285: target model.
286: During this stage we freeze the transported residual parameters and train only
287: the original base parameters of the target model.
288: This design ensures that the transferred knowledge is not overwritten by subsequent optimization,
289: while still allowing the target model to adapt to the downstream task distribution.
290: Empirically, this residual-frozen adaptation consistently outperforms training the target model
291: alone under the same conditions, indicating that fusion provides a strong and stable initialization.
292: Residual Parameterization.
293: We parameterize fusion through a residual form that separates transferred neuron-level
294: knowledge from the original model parameters.
295: Specifically, weight of target layer ℓ\ell is expressed as
296: WAℓ,fused=WAℓ,base+α⋅𝐌ℓ⊙Δ​WAℓ,W_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{fused}}=W_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{base}}+\alpha\cdot\mathbf{M}^{\ell}\odot\Delta W_{A}^{\ell},
297: (15)
298: where WAℓ,baseW_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{base}} denotes the original weights of the target model MAM_{A},
299: 𝐌ℓ\mathbf{M}^{\ell} is a neuron-level mask that is nonzero only on the selected top-kk neurons,
300: ⊙\odot denotes neuron-level masking, and α∈[0,1]\alpha\in[0,1] controls the strength of replacement.
301: The transported residual Δ​WAℓ\Delta W_{A}^{\ell} aggregates source operators across layers via
302: activation-aligned transport,
303: Δ​WAℓ=∑m=1MPeff​[ℓ,m]​Qoutℓ​m​WBm​(Qinℓ​m)⊤,\Delta W_{A}^{\ell}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}P_{\mathrm{eff}}[\ell,m]\,Q^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{out}}\,W_{B}^{m}\,(Q^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{in}})^{\!\top},
304: (16)
305: where Peff​[ℓ,m]P_{\mathrm{eff}}[\ell,m] is the effective layer correspondence and
306: Qinℓ​m,Qoutℓ​mQ^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{in}},Q^{\ell m}_{\mathrm{out}} are the feature-level transport maps inferred
307: from activation statistics.
308: Only the neurons selected by 𝐌ℓ\mathbf{M}^{\ell} participate in fusion; all others
309: remain unchanged.
310: Residual-Freezing and Adaptation.
311: During post-fusion adaptation, we freeze the transported residual Δ​WAℓ\Delta W_{A}^{\ell} entirely and
312: optimize only the base parameters WAℓ,baseW_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{base}}.
313: Gradients are therefore applied uniformly to the base weights, without any neuron-level freezing:
314: ∂ℒ∂Δ​WAℓ=0,∂ℒ∂WAℓ,base≠0.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Delta W_{A}^{\ell}}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{base}}}\neq 0.
315: (17)
316: This separation preserves the transferred neuron-level representations while allowing the target
317: model to adapt globally.
318: Weight Folding.
319: After adaptation, we fold the residual back into the base weights to obtain a
320: parameter representation for inference:
321: WAℓ,final=WAℓ,base+α⋅𝐌ℓ⊙Δ​WAℓ.W_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{final}}=W_{A}^{\ell,\mathrm{base}}+\alpha\cdot\mathbf{M}^{\ell}\odot\Delta W_{A}^{\ell}.
322: (18)
323: This folding operation yields a model
324: that is architecturally identical to the target model, with transferred neuron-level
325: knowledge permanently absorbed into its parameters.

659: 6 Conclusion
660: We proposed an OT-based framework for cross-architecture model merging that aligns heterogeneous models in activation space and converts the resulting correspondences into direct weight-space fusion.
661: Across low-resource languages and expert domains, our method improves target models using only a small calibration set, and optional residual-frozen post-fusion adaptation further strengthens performance while preserving general capabilities.
662: Despite these gains, our approach still depends on extracting intermediate activations on a calibration set and may be less reliable when the source/target architectures or tokenization distributions are extremely mismatched; addressing these limitations is an important direction for future work.
663: Overall, transport-guided cross-architecture fusion provides a practical alternative to distillation when architectures differ.
664: Impact Statement
665: This work improves knowledge transfer from high-resource models to smaller low-resource or domain-specific models with heterogeneous architectures.
666: We use activation-based optimal transport to estimate cross-model correspondences from a small input set for direct parameter fusion, optionally followed by lightweight post-fusion adaptation.
667: Positive impacts include reducing data/compute requirements for low-resource languages and specialized domains.
668: Negative impacts include propagating biases, factual errors, or unsafe behaviors from the source model, and overstating robustness if evaluation is insufficient.
669: We therefore emphasize rigorous evaluation, transparent reporting, and appropriate safeguards when applying the method in practice.
