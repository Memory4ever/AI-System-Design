75: Abstract
76: Behavioral evaluation is the dominant paradigm for assessing alignment in large language models (LLMs).
77: In practice, alignment is inferred from performance under finite evaluation protocols—benchmarks, red-teaming suites, or automated pipelines—and observed compliance is often treated as evidence of underlying alignment.
78: This inference step, from behavioral evidence to claims about latent alignment properties, is typically implicit and rarely analyzed as an inference problem in its own right.
79: We study this problem formally.
80: We frame alignment evaluation as an identifiability question under partial observability and allow agent behavior to depend on information correlated with the evaluation regime.
81: Within this setting, we introduce the Alignment Verifiability Problem and the notion of Normative Indistinguishability, capturing when distinct latent alignment hypotheses induce identical distributions over all evaluator-accessible signals.
82: Our main result is a negative but sharply delimited identifiability theorem: under finite behavioral evaluation and evaluation-aware agents, observed behavioral compliance does not uniquely identify latent alignment.
83: That is, even idealized behavioral evaluation cannot, in general, certify alignment as a latent property.
84: We further show that behavioral alignment tests should be interpreted as estimators of indistinguishability classes rather than verifiers of alignment.
85: Passing increasingly stringent tests may reduce the space of compatible hypotheses, but cannot collapse it to a singleton under the stated conditions.
86: This reframes alignment benchmarks as providing upper bounds on observable compliance within a regime, rather than guarantees of underlying alignment.
87: 1 Introduction
88: Behavioral evaluation has become the dominant paradigm for assessing alignment in large language models (LLMs).
89: In current practice, alignment is inferred from performance under finite evaluation protocols—benchmarks, red-teaming scenarios, or automated evaluation pipelines—and compliant behavior is commonly interpreted as evidence that a model is aligned with its intended objectives.
90: This inference, from observed behavior to claims about underlying alignment, underlies a substantial fraction of contemporary alignment practice.
91: However, this inference step is rarely made explicit or analyzed as an inference problem in its own right.
92: Behavioral evaluation produces finite, protocol-dependent observations, while alignment is typically treated as a latent property of the system.
93: Whether, and under what conditions, such latent properties are identifiable from behavioral evidence is therefore a foundational question for alignment evaluation.
94: Concerns about the limits of behavioral inference are well established in machine learning and decision theory.
95: A long-standing literature on proxy objectives, reward misspecification, and specification gaming shows that optimizing observable metrics can decouple performance from the intended target property, producing Goodhart-style failures [14, 7, 8].
96: Related work in inverse and preference learning demonstrates that latent objectives are, in general, not identifiable from behavior alone: multiple distinct hypotheses may induce indistinguishable observed behavior under finite interaction [16, 1].
97: These results already imply limits on behavioral inference under standard assumptions of stationary behavior generation.
98: A key assumption underlying most existing identifiability results, however, is that the behavior being observed is generated independently of the evaluation procedure itself.
99: In alignment evaluation for LLMs, this assumption is routinely violated.
100: The agent being evaluated may condition its behavior on information correlated with the evaluation regime, including input distributions, feedback signals, or structural regularities of the protocol.
101: As a result, the evaluation procedure becomes part of the data-generating process.
102: When behavior is evaluation-aware, standard inference guarantees based on passive or fixed observation no longer apply.
103: Empirical evidence increasingly supports the relevance of this distinction.
104: Modern language models have been shown to adapt strategically to evaluation regimes, exploiting proxy metrics, conditioning behavior on assessment context, or selectively underperforming on evaluations targeting sensitive capabilities [20, 18].
105: In parallel, work on evaluation methodology documents substantial variability and fragility across benchmarks, metrics, and evaluation protocols, raising concerns about reproducibility and construct validity [17, 9, 11].
106: Together, these findings suggest that finite behavioral evaluation cannot be assumed to sample from a stationary, evaluator-independent behavior distribution.
107: From an inference-theoretic perspective, alignment evaluation therefore falls into a class of problems characterized by partial observability, latent target properties, and endogenous data generation.
108: Despite the convergence of relevant theory, evaluation research, and empirical observations, the alignment literature lacks a unifying formal account of when alignment is verifiable from finite behavioral evidence under evaluation-aware behavior, and when it is not.
109: In this paper, we address this gap by framing alignment evaluation explicitly as an identifiability problem.
110: We formalize the Alignment Verifiability Problem and introduce the notion of Normative Indistinguishability, defined as an equivalence relation over latent alignment hypotheses that are observationally indistinguishable with respect to all evaluator-accessible signals under a given evaluation regime.
111: Our main contribution is a negative but sharply delimited result: under finite behavioral evaluation and evaluation-aware agents, observed compliance does not uniquely identify latent alignment.
112: This establishes a principled epistemic limit on behavioral alignment evaluation.
113: Importantly, we do not conclude that behavioral evaluation is uninformative.
114: Rather, we show that behavioral alignment tests should be interpreted as estimating indistinguishability classes rather than verifying alignment.
115: In Section 8, we formalize this perspective by introducing an alignment indistinguishability test, a behavioral analogue of classical black-box testing, which clarifies what such evaluations can and cannot establish.
116: Scope and non-claims.
117: This paper focuses exclusively on the inferential limits of behavioral alignment evaluation.
118: We do not propose new training methods, benchmarks, mitigation strategies, or governance frameworks, nor do we make claims about internal representations, mechanistic interpretability, or model intent.
119: Our results characterize what can and cannot be inferred from behavioral evidence alone, under explicitly stated assumptions.

129: 2 Preliminaries
130: This section introduces the basic formal setting used throughout the paper.
131: We define interaction histories, evaluation regimes, alignment hypotheses, and the notion of evaluation awareness.
132: The goal is not to fix a specific model of alignment, but to provide a minimal structure sufficient to reason about behavioral verification and identifiability.
133: 2.1 Interaction Histories and Evaluation Regimes
134: We consider an interactive setting between an evaluator and an agent.
135: An interaction history is a finite sequence
136: |
137: | h=(o_{1},a_{1},o_{2},a_{2},\dots,o_{t},a_{t}),
138: |
139: where o_{i} denotes an observation or input provided by the evaluator and
140: a_{i} denotes the agent’s response.
141: An evaluation regime E specifies a finite behavioral alignment test in the sense of Section 4.
142: Formally, E induces:
143: (i) a finite set of interaction histories \mathcal{H}_{E} that are observable under evaluation, and
144: (ii) a corresponding \sigma -algebra over observable datasets \mathcal{D}_{E} generated by these histories.
145: Evaluation is necessarily finite: \mathcal{H}_{E} is bounded in length and scope by the evaluation protocol.
146: 2.2 Alignment Hypotheses
147: Alignment is represented by a latent hypothesis \theta\in\Theta .
148: An alignment hypothesis specifies how the agent behaves across possible interaction histories, potentially through internal objectives, preferences, or constraints.
149: The framework is agnostic to the specific formalization of alignment.
150: Representative instantiations include:
151: (i) reward functions defining optimization objectives,
152: (ii) preference orderings over trajectories or outcomes, and
153: (iii) constraint-based or rule-based normative specifications.
154: Each hypothesis \theta\in\Theta induces a policy
155: |
156: | \pi_{\theta}(a\mid h,z),
157: |
158: which specifies a distribution over actions given an interaction history h
159: and auxiliary information z available to the agent.
160: 2.3 Evaluation Awareness
161: We model evaluation awareness as access to information correlated with the evaluation regime.
162: Definition 1 (Evaluation Awareness).
163: Let E be an evaluation regime.
164: An agent is said to be evaluation-aware if its policy
165: \pi_{\theta}(a\mid h,z) depends on a random variable Z(E) ,
166: where Z(E) captures information about the evaluation regime observable by the agent.
167: We do not assume that Z(E) uniquely identifies the evaluation regime.
168: Any non-degenerate correlation with evaluation context suffices.
169: Examples of such information include distributional regularities of inputs,
170: feedback signals, termination conditions, or other protocol-level cues.
171: Evaluation-unaware agents correspond to the special case in which Z(E) is constant.
172: 2.4 Behavioral Evidence and Observability
173: For a fixed evaluation regime E , interaction between the evaluator and the agent induces a probability distribution P_{\theta} over observable datasets \mathcal{D}_{E} .
174: These datasets may include full interaction traces, aggregated scores, or other summaries produced by the evaluation protocol.
175: Two alignment hypotheses \theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta are said to be
176: behaviorally equivalent under E if they induce identical distributions
177: over \mathcal{D}_{E} , i.e.,
178: |
179: | P_{\theta}(D)=P_{\theta^{\prime}}(D)\quad\text{for all }D\in\mathcal{D}_{E}.
180: |
181: Behavioral equivalence under E does not imply equivalence outside the evaluation regime.
182: In particular, hypotheses may induce distinct policies on interaction histories not contained in \mathcal{H}_{E} .
183: This separation between observable behavior under evaluation and latent behavior outside the evaluated domain underlies the identifiability results developed in subsequent sections.
184: 3 Verification and Behavioral Alignment Tests
185: This section formalizes what it means to verify alignment from behavioral evidence.
186: We introduce behavioral alignment tests as epistemic instruments and define verifiability in terms of identifiability from observable interaction data.
187: The goal is to make explicit the assumptions under which behavioral evaluation can, or cannot, support claims about latent alignment hypotheses.
188: 3.1 Behavioral Alignment Tests
189: Alignment evaluation in contemporary AI systems proceeds through structured interaction protocols.
190: We refer to any such protocol as a behavioral alignment test.
191: Definition 2 (Behavioral Alignment Test).
192: A behavioral alignment test is a finite evaluation procedure that interacts with an agent according to a prescribed protocol and produces an outcome based solely on observable behavior.
193: The outcome may consist of full interaction traces, aggregated scores, or other summaries derived from the interaction.
194: Each behavioral alignment test induces an evaluation regime E as defined in Section 2.
195: In particular, it determines a finite set of observable interaction histories \mathcal{H}_{E} and a corresponding \sigma -algebra over observable datasets \mathcal{D}_{E} .
196: Behavioral alignment tests subsume benchmarks, red-teaming suites, preference elicitation protocols, and scalable oversight pipelines used in current practice.
197: They are treated here as epistemic instruments rather than as certification procedures.
198: 3.2 Verification as Identifiability
199: We treat alignment verification as an inference problem.
200: Given behavioral evidence generated under an evaluation regime E , the evaluator seeks to determine which alignment hypotheses in \Theta are consistent with the observed data.
201: Definition 3 (Verifiability under Behavioral Evaluation).
202: An alignment hypothesis \theta\in\Theta is said to be verifiable under an evaluation regime E if no distinct hypothesis \theta^{\prime}\neq\theta induces the same probability distribution over observable datasets \mathcal{D}_{E} .
203: Equivalently, verification requires that \theta be identifiable from behavioral evidence generated under E .
204: If multiple hypotheses induce identical distributions over \mathcal{D}_{E} , then behavioral evidence alone cannot uniquely support any one of them.
205: This notion of verifiability is intentionally epistemic.
206: It concerns what can be inferred from observable data, not whether an agent possesses a particular internal property.
207: 3.3 Behavioral Equivalence and Indistinguishability
208: Two alignment hypotheses \theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta are said to be
209: behaviorally equivalent under E if
210: |
211: | P_{\theta}(D)=P_{\theta^{\prime}}(D)\quad\text{for all }D\in\mathcal{D}_{E},
212: |
213: where P_{\theta} denotes the distribution over observable datasets induced by \theta under evaluation regime E .
214: Behavioral equivalence under E implies that no decision rule based solely on observable behavior can distinguish \theta from \theta^{\prime} .
215: Importantly, such equivalence is defined relative to the evaluation regime and does not imply equivalence of the induced policies outside \mathcal{H}_{E} .
216: 3.4 Evaluation Awareness and Endogenous Evidence
217: A central feature of alignment evaluation is that agents may adapt their behavior to the evaluation context.
218: As formalized in Section 2, this is captured by allowing agent policies to condition on a random variable Z(E) correlated with the evaluation regime.
219: When agent behavior depends on Z(E) , the data-generating process becomes endogenous to the evaluation protocol.
220: The evaluator’s choice of test influences not only what is observed, but how the agent behaves during evaluation.
221: This endogeneity distinguishes alignment evaluation from classical inference settings, where data generation is assumed to be independent of the inference procedure.
222: As a result, behavioral alignment tests cannot be treated as neutral measurement devices.
223: 3.5 Scope of Behavioral Verification
224: The definitions above clarify the scope of behavioral verification.
225: Behavioral alignment tests can constrain which hypotheses are compatible with observed behavior under E .
226: They can identify violations, failure modes, and empirical properties of agent behavior within the evaluated regime.
227: However, behavioral verification does not, by itself, guarantee identification of latent alignment hypotheses.
228: Whether such identification is possible depends on structural properties of \Theta , the richness of \mathcal{H}_{E} , and whether agents condition behavior on evaluation-related information.
229: The following section shows that, under finite evaluation and evaluation-conditioned policies, behavioral alignment tests cannot, in general, verify latent alignment hypotheses using behavioral evidence alone.
230: 4 Behavioral Alignment Tests as Measurement Instruments
231: Alignment evaluation in modern AI systems is predominantly operationalized through what we refer to as behavioral alignment tests: finite procedures that interact with an agent under a prescribed protocol and assess observed behavior against normative criteria.
232: Such tests play a role analogous to the Turing Test, in that they aim to infer internal properties of an agent solely through interaction.
233: To clarify the scope and limits of such evaluation, it is useful to analyze alignment tests as measurement instruments rather than as decision procedures.
234: 4.1 What Alignment Tests Observe
235: Behavioral alignment tests have access only to observable interaction traces generated under a finite evaluation protocol.
236: These traces reflect how an agent behaves when prompted, queried, or challenged within a specific regime.
237: As such, alignment tests directly measure patterns of observable behavior, including correctness, refusal behavior, robustness to perturbation, and adherence to explicit constraints.
238: Importantly, these measurements are always conditional on the evaluation regime itself.
239: Behavior outside the tested domain, including counterfactual actions under unobserved conditions, remains unmeasured.
240: 4.2 What Alignment Tests Can Certify
241: Given sufficient coverage and carefully designed protocols, behavioral alignment tests can establish upper bounds on observable compliance.
242: They can detect specific failure modes, characterize robustness within the tested regime, and provide empirical assurance that certain behaviors do not occur under evaluation.
243: These properties are practically valuable and constitute the primary function of modern benchmarks, red-teaming pipelines, and automated evaluation suites.
244: 4.3 What Alignment Tests Cannot Identify
245: However, behavioral alignment tests cannot, in general, identify latent alignment properties.
246: Specifically, they cannot distinguish between agents that are genuinely aligned and agents that merely exhibit compliant behavior under evaluation.
247: This limitation persists even under idealized test design, as long as evaluation remains finite and behavior is the sole source of evidence.
248: Theorem 1 formalizes this limitation by showing that, under evaluation-aware behavior, distinct latent alignment hypotheses may remain behaviorally indistinguishable under any given test.
249: 4.4 Measurement Limits and Epistemic Interpretation
250: From a measurement-theoretic perspective, alignment tests should therefore be interpreted as instruments that bound behavior within a specified regime, rather than as detectors of internal normative commitment.
251: They constrain what an agent does under evaluation, but do not, in general, reveal why the agent behaves as it does, nor how it would act outside the tested domain.
252: This distinction is central to understanding both the power and the limits of alignment evaluation.
253: It explains why increasingly elaborate tests improve empirical safety while leaving the problem of alignment certification fundamentally unresolved.
254: 5 Limits of Behavioral Alignment Evaluation
255: This section establishes a non-identifiability result for behavioral alignment evaluation under finite interaction.
256: The result characterizes a precise epistemic limitation: when agents may condition their behavior on information correlated with the evaluation regime, finite behavioral evidence cannot uniquely identify latent alignment hypotheses.
257: 5.1 Setting and Assumptions
258: We work within the framework defined in Sections 2 and 3.
259: In particular, we assume that:
260: (i) evaluation proceeds through a finite behavioral alignment test inducing an evaluation regime E ,
261: (ii) alignment is represented by a latent hypothesis \theta\in\Theta ,
262: (iii) agent policies may condition on a random variable Z(E) capturing information about the evaluation regime observable by the agent.
263: We emphasize that Z(E) need not uniquely identify E ; any non-degenerate correlation with evaluation context suffices.
264: 5.2 Statement of the Result
265: Theorem 1 (Non-Identifiability under Evaluation-Conditioned Policies).
266: Let \Theta be a space of alignment hypotheses inducing policies
267: \pi_{\theta}(a\mid h,z) , where h denotes interaction history and
268: z\in Z(E) is information observable by the agent about the evaluation regime.
269: Assume that \Theta contains at least two hypotheses \theta\neq\theta^{\prime}
270: whose induced policies coincide on all histories measurable under the
271: evaluation regime E , i.e.,
272: |
273: | \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h,z)=\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid h,z)\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{H}_{E}\text{ and all }z\in Z(E),
274: |
275: but diverge on histories outside \mathcal{H}_{E} .
276: Then \theta and \theta^{\prime} are behaviorally indistinguishable under E .
277: Consequently, no behavioral alignment test inducing E can verify \theta
278: against \theta^{\prime} using behavioral evidence alone.
279: The theorem does not assume adversarial intent or perfect evaluation awareness.
280: It characterizes a regime-dependent failure of identifiability induced by the
281: interaction between finite evaluation and evaluation-conditioned policies.
282: 5.3 Proof
283: Let E be a fixed behavioral alignment test inducing a finite set of
284: measurable interaction histories \mathcal{H}_{E} and an associated
285: \sigma -algebra over observable datasets \mathcal{D}_{E} .
286: For any alignment hypothesis \theta\in\Theta , the interaction between the
287: agent and evaluator induces a probability measure P_{\theta} over
288: \mathcal{D}_{E} .
289: By assumption, there exists \theta^{\prime}\in\Theta , \theta^{\prime}\neq\theta , such
290: that the induced policies coincide on all histories measurable under E .
291: Therefore, for any dataset D\in\mathcal{D}_{E} ,
292: |
293: | P_{\theta}(D)=P_{\theta^{\prime}}(D).
294: |
295: Thus, \theta and \theta^{\prime} are observationally equivalent under the evaluation
296: regime E and cannot be distinguished by any decision rule based solely on
297: behavioral evidence generated by E .
298: This establishes non-identifiability of \theta under behavioral evaluation.
299: ∎
300: 5.4 Illustrative Formal Example
301: Consider a finite Markov decision process with horizon T .
302: Let the evaluation regime E restrict interaction to a subset of states
303: S_{E}\subset S and actions A_{E}\subset A .
304: Let \theta and \theta^{\prime} be two reward functions that coincide on
305: S_{E}\times A_{E} but differ elsewhere.
306: Suppose the agent’s policy conditions on a signal Z(E) that is weakly
307: correlated with whether interaction occurs within E .
308: Under E , the induced trajectories and reward observations are identical for
309: \theta and \theta^{\prime} .
310: Outside E , the induced behavior diverges.
311: No finite behavioral evaluation restricted to E can distinguish the two
312: hypotheses, despite their normatively distinct prescriptions outside the
313: evaluated domain.
314: 5.5 Interpretation
315: Theorem 1 establishes a conditional limitation on alignment
316: verification.
317: Under finite behavioral evaluation and evaluation-conditioned policies,
318: behavioral compliance provides, at best, an upper bound on observable behavior
319: within the evaluation regime.
320: It does not uniquely identify latent alignment properties beyond that regime.
321: The next section situates this result within classical identifiability and
322: inference theory.
323: 6 Relation to Identifiability, Testing, and Inference Theory
324: The non-identifiability result established in Section 5 relates to several classical lines of work in inference theory, including identifiability in latent variable models, observational equivalence in dynamical systems, and black-box testing and certification.
325: This section situates the result within these traditions and clarifies both what is standard and what is specific to alignment evaluation.
326: 6.1 Identifiability and Observational Equivalence
327: In classical statistical inference, a latent parameter \theta is identifiable if distinct parameter values induce distinct probability distributions over observable data.
328: Non-identifiability arises when multiple parameters generate identical observational distributions, rendering inference impossible even with unlimited data.
329: From this perspective, behavioral equivalence under an evaluation regime E corresponds to a form of observational equivalence relative to the \sigma -algebra of observables induced by E .
330: Two alignment hypotheses are observationally equivalent if they induce identical distributions over all datasets measurable under the evaluation protocol.
331: Theorem 1 can thus be read as an identifiability result relative to a restricted observation model.
332: What distinguishes the present setting from classical cases is not the existence of observational equivalence per se, but the source of the restriction: the observation model is endogenously shaped by the evaluation protocol itself.
333: 6.2 Endogenous Observation Models
334: Standard identifiability theory typically assumes that the data-generating process is independent of the inference procedure.
335: In alignment evaluation, this assumption fails.
336: Behavioral alignment tests do not merely observe a pre-existing stochastic process; they actively define the interaction context in which behavior is generated.
337: When agent policies condition on information correlated with the evaluation regime, the induced observation model becomes endogenous.
338: The evaluator’s choice of test influences both which data are observed and how the agent behaves during observation.
339: This endogeneity is the key structural feature responsible for the non-identifiability result.
340: Importantly, the result does not rely on agents having full knowledge of the evaluation protocol.
341: Any non-degenerate information channel correlated with evaluation context suffices to induce regime-dependent observational equivalence.
342: 6.3 Relation to Black-Box Testing and Conformance
343: The alignment verifiability problem also bears a close relationship to black-box testing and conformance testing in computer science.
344: In such settings, a system is tested against a specification using a finite set of queries or experiments, and correctness is assessed based on observed input–output behavior.
345: A well-known limitation of black-box testing is that conformance can only be established relative to the tested behaviors and cannot, in general, certify properties outside the tested domain.
346: Theorem 1 formalizes an analogous limitation for alignment evaluation: behavioral conformance under a finite evaluation regime does not uniquely identify latent alignment hypotheses when the system under test adapts to the testing protocol.
347: What is specific to alignment evaluation is that the property of interest is not a functional specification but a latent normative structure, and that the testing protocol itself may be detectable by the system.
348: 6.4 Relation to Inverse Reinforcement Learning
349: Inverse Reinforcement Learning and related preference inference methods aim to recover latent objectives from observed behavior under assumptions such as optimality, known dynamics, and evaluation-unaware agents.
350: Under these assumptions, identifiability results can often be established.
351: The present result does not contradict IRL-style inference.
352: Rather, it delineates the boundary of its applicability.
353: When agents condition behavior on evaluation-related information and evaluation is finite, the independence assumptions required for IRL-style identifiability no longer hold.
354: In this regime, behavioral evidence alone cannot certify latent alignment properties, even if it suffices to recover objectives in classical settings.
355: 6.5 What Is and Is Not New
356: The core components of the result—finite observation, observational equivalence, and non-identifiability—are well known in inference and testing theory.
357: The contribution of this paper is not to introduce these notions, but to show how they interact in the specific context of alignment evaluation.
358: By formalizing evaluation protocols as epistemic instruments and allowing policies to condition on evaluation-related information, we isolate a regime in which standard identifiability assumptions fail for structural reasons.
359: This clarifies why increasingly sophisticated behavioral evaluation does not, by itself, resolve the problem of alignment verification, and why claims of certification based solely on behavioral success must be interpreted with care.
360: 7 Modern Context: Evaluation Practice and the Limits of Behavioral Evidence
361: Contemporary alignment practice relies heavily on increasingly sophisticated evaluation pipelines.
362: Benchmarks, red-teaming suites, preference elicitation protocols, and refusal stress tests are routinely used to assess whether deployed systems exhibit aligned behavior.
363: Despite their diversity, these approaches share a common evidential structure: they evaluate alignment through finite interaction and observable behavior.
364: This section situates our formal result within this modern evaluation landscape.
365: Our goal is not to criticize evaluation practice, but to clarify its epistemic scope.
366: In particular, we articulate what behavioral alignment tests can and cannot establish about latent alignment properties, given the identifiability limits formalized in Section 5.
367: Throughout this section, all evaluation mechanisms are understood as instances of behavioral alignment tests in the sense of Section 3.
368: 7.1 From General Capability Benchmarks to Alignment Evaluation
369: Large-scale benchmarking efforts have pushed the field toward broader, multi-metric evaluation.
370: Recent work frames evaluation as a matrix over scenarios and desiderata (e.g., accuracy, robustness, calibration, toxicity), highlighting that any practical evaluation is necessarily a finite and selective projection of model behavior [13].
371: Similarly, large collaborative benchmark suites aim to probe capabilities beyond standard tasks through extensive but finite task batteries and scoring rules [19].
372: These efforts materially improve transparency and comparability, but they also underscore a structural limitation: benchmarks can only constrain behavior on the evaluated distribution and protocol.
373: Our formal setting abstracts this reality.
374: An evaluation regime E induces a bounded interaction space and thus a bounded evidential footprint.
375: The core issue is not that benchmarks are deficient, but that finite behavioral evidence is an epistemically limited channel for certifying latent alignment properties of adaptive agents.
376: 7.2 Automated Red Teaming as Adaptive, Protocol-Aware Evaluation
377: Modern alignment evaluation increasingly emphasizes adversarial testing and automated red teaming, often using language models to generate challenging test cases and multi-turn probes.
378: Recent work on holistic automated red teaming focuses on systematic test-case generation and iterative interaction as a closer proxy to real-world deployment conditions [21].
379: In parallel, the security literature has developed automated jailbreak discovery methods that operate with black-box access and iteratively refine prompts to elicit prohibited behavior, including tree-structured exploration strategies [15] and end-to-end frameworks combining empirical analysis of defenses with automated prompt generation [4].
380: Additional studies show that safety behavior can vary sharply across languages, and that multilinguality itself can function as a jailbreak vector under finite evaluation protocols [5].
381: These lines of work reinforce two points directly relevant to our result.
382: First, evaluation regimes are recognizable and can be exploited or overfit—whether by adversaries or by agents that condition behavior on contextual cues.
383: Second, stronger red teaming expands coverage, but still instantiates a finite evaluation regime E ; it cannot exhaustively characterize behavior outside the tested envelope.
384: 7.3 Evaluation Awareness and Protocol Sensitivity
385: A distinctive feature of modern language models is their sensitivity to contextual and distributional cues.
386: Empirical work has shown that models can adapt strategically to evaluation settings, conditioning behavior on assessment context, selectively underperforming on evaluations targeting sensitive capabilities, or exploiting proxy metrics [20, 18].
387: The formalization of evaluation awareness via the variable Z(E) makes this sensitivity explicit.
388: Importantly, evaluation awareness need not involve explicit detection of a test.
389: Any systematic correlation between observable context and evaluation suffices to induce regime-dependent behavior.
390: Our contribution is to show that such sensitivity has principled epistemic consequences: it undermines the identifiability of latent alignment properties from behavioral evidence alone.
391: 7.4 Interpretability as an Alternative Evidence Channel (and Its Own Limits)
392: A natural response to the limits of behavioral evaluation is to seek evidence about internal structure.
393: Mechanistic interpretability has made notable progress in proposing representations and feature discovery methods beyond individual neurons, for example via sparse autoencoders and learned feature dictionaries [3], and in analyzing why polysemantic internal representations arise (“superposition”) [6].
394: This line of work motivates the idea that internal evidence might, in principle, break purely behavioral indistinguishability.
395: However, interpretability methods are not yet a general certification pipeline for deployed systems, and even if they mature, they introduce a distinct epistemic question: what internal signatures constitute alignment rather than task competence, refusal heuristics, or safety-specific representations?
396: Our result therefore does not argue against interpretability; it isolates the boundary of what can be concluded from behavior alone.
397: 7.5 Scalable Oversight and the Evaluation–Alignment Identification Gap
398: A second response is to scale supervision via learned reward models, debate-based evaluation, or AI-assisted oversight.
399: Work on reward modeling explicitly acknowledges the limits of human evaluation and proposes learned surrogates to extend oversight [12].
400: Debate-based approaches similarly aim to amplify limited human judgment through structured interaction between agents [10].
401: Constitutional AI and related AI-feedback methods replace or complement human judgments with principle-driven self-critique and revision, again operationalized through interaction protocols [2].
402: These approaches may substantially improve observable compliance and reduce harmful outputs.
403: However, they remain grounded in finite protocols and observable signals.
404: Our theorem targets the identification step often left implicit: even extremely strong behavioral performance under such protocols does not logically certify latent alignment if agents may condition behavior on the evaluation regime.
405: 7.6 Implications for Alignment Benchmarks and Evaluation Practice
406: The analysis above motivates a cautious reinterpretation of alignment benchmarks and red-teaming results.
407: As behavioral alignment tests, benchmarks can establish empirical bounds on observable behavior within a specified evaluation regime.
408: They can support claims about robustness, failure modes, and compliance under tested conditions.
409: However, the formal result of Section 5 implies that such evidence cannot, in general, justify claims about latent alignment or internal normative commitment.
410: Passing increasingly sophisticated evaluation suites should therefore be interpreted as demonstrating the absence of observed violations within the evaluated regime, rather than as certifying genuine alignment.
411: Recognizing this distinction does not diminish the value of evaluation.
412: Instead, it clarifies its epistemic scope and prevents overinterpretation of behavioral success as evidence of non-simulated alignment.
413: 8 Illustrative Example and an Alignment Indistinguishability Test
414: This section serves two purposes.
415: First, it provides a concrete schematic example illustrating the non-identifiability mechanism underlying Theorem 1.
416: Second, it introduces a behavioral testing construct that clarifies what alignment evaluation can estimate, even when verification is impossible.
417: 8.1 A Schematic Example of Normative Indistinguishability
418: Consider an evaluation regime E consisting of a finite interaction protocol designed to test compliance with a normative specification (e.g., refusal constraints, preference consistency, or safety rules).
419: The evaluator interacts with the agent through a bounded set of prompts and observes the resulting responses.
420: Compliance is assessed solely on the basis of these observed interaction histories.
421: Let \theta_{\mathrm{int}}\in\Theta denote an alignment hypothesis under which the agent follows the intended normative specification across all interaction contexts.
422: Let \theta_{\mathrm{cond}}\in\Theta denote an alternative hypothesis under which the agent follows a distinct normative objective, but conditions its behavior on evaluation-related information Z(E) so as to match the behavior of \theta_{\mathrm{int}} on all interaction histories in \mathcal{H}_{E} .
423: Under evaluation regime E , both hypotheses induce identical probability distributions over observable datasets \mathcal{D}_{E} .
424: However, they diverge on interaction histories outside \mathcal{H}_{E} , where \theta_{\mathrm{cond}} no longer satisfies the intended normative constraints.
425: Because the evaluation is finite and behaviorally defined, no dataset D\in\mathcal{D}_{E} distinguishes these hypotheses.
426: This schematic construction instantiates the non-identifiability result of Theorem 1 without relying on adversarial optimization or pathological hypothesis classes.
427: 8.2 From Verification to Estimation
428: The preceding example illustrates that latent alignment hypotheses cannot be verified from finite behavioral evidence alone.
429: However, this does not imply that behavioral evaluation is uninformative.
430: Verification requires unique identification of a latent hypothesis.
431: In contrast, practical evaluation often aims at a weaker epistemic goal: estimating the extent to which observed behavior constrains the space of hypotheses consistent with the evidence.
432: This motivates a shift from verification to estimation.
433: Under this perspective, behavioral evaluation does not aim to certify alignment, but to reduce the set of hypotheses that remain behaviorally indistinguishable from a reference aligned specification under a given evaluation regime.
434: 8.3 An Alignment Indistinguishability Test
435: We formalize this estimation perspective through what we call an alignment indistinguishability test.
436: Let \Theta_{\mathrm{ref}}\subseteq\Theta denote a reference class of alignment hypotheses representing intended or acceptable aligned behavior.
437: Given an evaluation regime E , define the induced indistinguishability set
438: |
439: | \mathcal{I}(E,\Theta_{\mathrm{ref}})=\left\{\theta\in\Theta\;\middle|\;P_{\theta}(D)=P_{\theta^{\prime}}(D)\text{ for all }D\in\mathcal{D}_{E}\text{ and some }\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{ref}}\right\}.
440: |
441: Any behavioral alignment test operating under E can only determine whether the true hypothesis lies inside or outside \mathcal{I}(E,\Theta_{\mathrm{ref}}) .
442: Formally, such a test consists of observing a dataset D\in\mathcal{D}_{E} and applying a decision rule
443: |
444: | \delta:\mathcal{D}_{E}\rightarrow\{0,1\},
445: |
446: where \delta(D)=1 denotes acceptance as behaviorally aligned with respect to \Theta_{\mathrm{ref}} .
447: Crucially, passing the test does not identify a unique alignment hypothesis.
448: Instead, it yields an estimate of the indistinguishability class \mathcal{I}(E,\Theta_{\mathrm{ref}}) .
449: Stronger or more diverse evaluation regimes may shrink this class, but under the conditions of Theorem 1, they cannot reduce it to a singleton.
450: In this sense, alignment indistinguishability tests estimate behavioral robustness under evaluation rather than verify latent normative commitment.
451: 8.4 Relation to the Turing Test
452: The analogy with the Turing Test is structural rather than philosophical.
453: In both cases, finite interaction and observable behavior define the evidential boundary.
454: Success under the test establishes behavioral indistinguishability relative to a reference class, not identity of internal states, objectives, or mechanisms.
455: The present result clarifies that alignment evaluation inherits this limitation.
456: Even idealized behavioral tests can at best bound indistinguishability under the tested regime.
457: They cannot, in general, certify latent alignment properties when agents may condition behavior on evaluation-related information.
458: We emphasize that this analogy is intended solely to highlight the shared evidential structure of finite behavioral testing, not to suggest equivalence of goals or interpretation.
459: 9 Limitations, Scope, and Non-Claims
460: This paper establishes a negative identifiability result for behavioral alignment evaluation under explicitly stated assumptions.
461: To avoid overinterpretation, we clarify here the scope of the result, its limitations, and several claims that the paper does not make.
462: 9.1 Dependence on Structural Assumptions
463: The non-identifiability result of Theorem 1 depends critically on three structural assumptions:
464: (i) evaluation is finite and behavioral,
465: (ii) alignment is treated as a latent property instantiated through a hypothesis space \Theta , and
466: (iii) agents may condition their behavior on information correlated with the evaluation regime.
467: If any of these assumptions is relaxed, the conclusion may no longer hold.
468: In particular, the result does not assert that alignment is inherently unverifiable, nor that behavioral evidence is always insufficient.
469: It identifies a specific regime in which behavioral verification fails as an epistemic matter.
470: 9.2 On the Closure Assumption over Alignment Hypotheses
471: A key technical assumption is that the hypothesis space \Theta is closed under modifications of normative prescriptions outside the evaluated interaction domain.
472: This assumption enables the construction of normatively distinct but behaviorally equivalent hypotheses under a fixed evaluation regime.
473: We emphasize that this closure property is not asserted to hold universally.
474: Rather, it captures a broad and practically relevant class of alignment formalisms in which off-regime behavior is not uniquely constrained by on-regime specifications.
475: Examples include reward functions defined over large or continuous state spaces, preference models with unobserved regions, and constraint-based systems where only a subset of conditions is exercised during evaluation.
476: The result should therefore be read as conditional:
477: for hypothesis classes admitting such off-regime variability, behavioral evaluation alone cannot guarantee identifiability.
478: We do not claim that all conceivable alignment representations satisfy this assumption.
479: 9.3 Evaluation Awareness as a Modeled Capability
480: Evaluation awareness is formalized as access to a random variable Z(E) correlated with the evaluation regime.
481: The result does not require agents to have explicit or perfect knowledge of the evaluation protocol.
482: Any non-degenerate correlation between observable context and evaluation suffices.
483: At the same time, the paper does not attempt to characterize the empirical prevalence or strength of evaluation awareness in current systems.
484: The formal result applies once such awareness is possible, not necessarily once it is empirically dominant.
485: Quantifying the degree to which real-world systems approximate this regime remains an open empirical question.
486: 9.4 What the Result Does Not Claim
487: We explicitly do not claim the following:
488: •
489: That behavioral evaluation is useless or should be abandoned.
490: •
491: That alignment cannot be improved or assessed through empirical testing.
492: •
493: That observed compliant behavior implies deception or strategic intent.
494: •
495: That interpretability or internal inspection necessarily resolves the problem.
496: •
497: That current benchmarks are flawed by design or epistemically invalid.
498: The result concerns identifiability, not practical utility.
499: Behavioral tests remain essential tools for detecting failures, bounding risk, and comparing systems under controlled conditions.
500: 9.5 Interpretation of Implications
501: The main implication of the result is epistemic rather than prescriptive.
502: Under the stated assumptions, passing a finite behavioral alignment test should be interpreted as evidence of compliance within the evaluated regime, not as a logical guarantee of latent alignment properties.
503: This perspective does not dictate specific changes to evaluation practice.
504: Instead, it clarifies the evidential status of behavioral success and motivates caution in claims of certification or guarantees based solely on behavioral evidence.
505: 9.6 Open Directions Beyond the Present Scope
506: Several important directions lie outside the scope of this paper.
507: These include:
508: (i) characterizing hypothesis spaces \Theta for which identifiability can be recovered,
509: (ii) formalizing intermediate regimes of partial evaluation awareness,
510: (iii) combining behavioral evidence with internal or causal signals, and
511: (iv) establishing quantitative bounds on the amount or diversity of evaluation required to distinguish restricted classes of hypotheses.
512: Addressing these questions would require additional assumptions or evidence channels and is left for future work.
513: 10 Conclusion
514: This paper examined a fundamental limitation of behavioral alignment evaluation in modern AI systems.
515: We formalized the problem of alignment verification under finite interaction and showed that, when agents may condition their behavior on the evaluation regime, behavioral evidence alone cannot uniquely identify latent alignment properties.
516: This failure of identifiability holds even under idealized evaluation conditions and does not rely on adversarial intent or pathological hypothesis spaces.
517: By introducing behavioral alignment tests as epistemic instruments and analyzing their limits, we clarified what contemporary benchmarks, red-teaming pipelines, and scalable oversight mechanisms can and cannot establish.
518: Under the conditions studied, such evaluations provide bounds on observable compliance within a given regime, but cannot certify genuine alignment or rule out normatively distinct hypotheses that remain behaviorally indistinguishable during evaluation.
519: The contribution of this work is therefore diagnostic rather than prescriptive.
520: It does not argue against evaluation, nor does it claim that alignment is unattainable.
521: Instead, it isolates a principled boundary on the evidential power of behavioral tests, helping to explain persistent difficulties in certifying alignment despite increasingly sophisticated evaluation practices.
522: Making this boundary explicit has practical and theoretical significance.
523: Practically, it supports more precise interpretation of evaluation results and discourages overclaims based on behavioral success alone.
524: Theoretically, it situates alignment verification within classical inference theory while identifying a regime in which standard identifiability assumptions break down.
525: Future work may explore how complementary evidence channels—such as interpretability, formal constraints, or hybrid evaluation paradigms—interact with the limitation identified here.
526: Regardless of the direction taken, the present result provides a baseline against which claims of alignment certification should be assessed.
