96: Abstract
97: As large language models become smaller and more efficient, small reasoning models (SRMs) are crucial for enabling chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in resource-constrained settings. However, they are prone to faithfulness hallucinations, especially in intermediate reasoning steps. Existing mitigation methods based on online reinforcement learning rely on outcome-based rewards or coarse-grained CoT evaluation, which can inadvertently reinforce unfaithful reasoning when the final answer is correct.
98: To address these limitations, we propose Faithfulness-Aware Step-Level Reinforcement Learning (FaithRL), introducing step-level supervision via explicit faithfulness rewards from a process reward model, together with an implicit truncated resampling strategy that generates contrastive signals from faithful prefixes. Experiments across multiple SRMs and Open-Book QA benchmarks demonstrate that FaithRL consistently reduces hallucinations in both the CoT and final answers, leading to more faithful and reliable reasoning.
99: Code is available at https://github.com/Easy195/FaithRL.
100: Keywords: Faithfulness Hallucination, Small Reasoning Models, Faithfulness-Aware Step-Level Supervision, Online Reinforcement Learning

102: 1 Introduction
103: The development of large language models (LLMs) is trending toward greater efficiency and smaller model sizes (Team et al., 2025b; Haque et al., 2025). This shift reduces deployment costs and expands application scenarios, particularly in resource-constrained environments such as edge devices and mobile platforms (Gunter et al., 2024; Prieto and Abad, 2025). It is also becoming a key factor in overcoming performance bottlenecks (Pham et al., 2025).
104: In this context, small reasoning models (SRMs) leverage chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning for test-time scaling, achieving strong performance at low deployment cost (Snell et al., 2024). However, compared to non-reasoning models or larger models, SRMs are more prone to faithfulness hallucinations, which limits their adoption (Deng et al., 2024; Kalai et al., 2025).
105: Figure 1: For small reasoning models, faithfulness hallucinations in the CoT and the final answer are often inconsistent, making outcome-only rewards ineffective. Figure shows an example on NewsQA with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (DPSK-1.5B): although the final answer is correct, faithfulness hallucinations still occur within the CoT. By constructing new questions based on hallucinated CoT steps, we find that the model frequently produces incorrect answers, indicating that faithfulness hallucinations in the CoT can trigger further errors.
106: To address this problem, recent methods focus on online reinforcement learning (online RL), e.g., GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025), often outperform traditional approaches (Lightman et al., 2024; Rao et al., 2025a). However, these approaches either rely solely on outcome-based rewards, thereby overlooking unfaithful reasoning steps during the CoT stage (Si et al., 2025), or provide only a coarse, global CoT score without penalizing unfaithful steps (Li and Ng, 2025). As illustrated in Figure 1, when the final answer is correct but the CoT is incorrect, outcome rewards can mistakenly reinforce hallucinatory CoT. Such CoT reflects the model’s misinterpretation of the context, which can lead to hallucinated answers on other problems. Even worse, little prior work targets reasoning models directly, leaving their distinct hallucination dynamics largely unexplored.
107: To address these issues, we propose Faithfulness-Aware Step-Level Reinforcement Learning (FaithRL). We introduce step-level rewards from two perspectives. First, we design explicit step-level rewards by leveraging a process reward model (PRM) to assess the faithfulness of each reasoning step, together with strategies that mitigate reward hacking. Second, we introduce implicit step-level rewards via truncated resampling: when an unfaithful CoT step is detected, we regenerate the continuation from a faithful prefix to provide positive counterexamples. This tree-structured sampling naturally yields step-level contrastive signals, enabling targeted penalization of hallucinated CoT steps.
108: Results show that FaithRL consistently outperforms all methods across various SRMs in Open-Book Question Answer (QA). On average, it reduces answer hallucinations by 3.86% in terms of F1 and by 3.48% in terms of LLM-as-a-judge, compared to the best baseline. Further analysis confirms that our method significantly mitigates CoT hallucinations, whereas optimizing with an outcome-only reward instead exacerbates CoT hallucinations. Moreover, in cases where the CoT is incorrect but the final answer is correct, the erroneous CoT is often harmful and can readily lead to incorrect answers. Our contributions are:
109: •
110: We demonstrate the harmful impact of hallucinated CoT, filling an important gap in mitigating faithfulness hallucinations in SRMs.
111: •
112: We provide step-level supervision via a combination of explicit step-level rewards and implicit contrastive data generation.
113: •
114: Extensive experiments across multiple SRMs and diverse Open-Book QA benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements in faithfulness.

213: 2 Related Work
214: Hallucinations in LLMs.
215: Hallucination remains a fundamental challenge for LLMs, typically categorized into factual hallucinations and faithfulness hallucinations (Deng et al., 2023; Kalai et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). Factual hallucinations deviate from objective facts or fabricate information, while faithfulness hallucinations arise when outputs diverge from source context, violate instructions, or exhibit internal inconsistencies (Alansari and Luqman, 2025; Huang et al., 2025a; Guo et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2025b).
216: With the growing adoption of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) for multi-step reasoning tasks, recent advances increasingly rely on reinforcement learning to incentivize long-horizon reasoning, where CoT serves as an explicit intermediate process. Representative models include OpenAI o1 (OpenAI et al., 2024), DeepSeek R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), Kimi k1.5 (Team et al., 2025a), and QwQ (Team, 2025). Although reasoning models improve effectiveness and reasoning capability (Ye et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025), they exhibit more severe hallucinations than non-reasoning models (Kalai et al., 2025; Shojaee et al., 2025). We attribute this largely to hallucinations within the CoT, which can be more prevalent than those in final answers.
217: Mitigating Factual Hallucination.
218: Recent work has explored various approaches to mitigate factual hallucinations. TruthRL (Wei et al., 2025) employs a ternary reward under GRPO to encourage abstention under uncertainty, reducing factual hallucinations in QA. LoVeC (Zhang et al., 2025a) trains models to output sentence-level confidence scores via reinforcement learning, indirectly alleviating factual hallucinations. Mask-DPO (Gu et al., 2025) introduces sentence-level factual masks into DPO, aligning only factually correct content and substantially reducing fine-grained factual hallucinations. However, these methods mainly focus on final answer correctness and largely overlook hallucinations within the CoT, limiting their applicability to reasoning models.
219: Mitigating Faithfulness Hallucination.
220: Several studies have attempted to mitigate faithfulness hallucinations. KD (Nguyen et al., 2025) supervises student models with teacher outputs to enhance response fidelity. RLFH (Wen et al., 2025) and Scope (Duong et al., 2025) leverage self-judging or contrastive signals for alignment, while Dual-GRPO (Si et al., 2025) jointly optimizes long- and short-form generation to enhance context faithfulness. FSPO (Li and Ng, 2025) introduces step-level scoring with average pooling to improve reasoning faithfulness. Scaf-GRPO (Zhang et al., 2025b) adopts a scaffolded GRPO curriculum to improve reasoning performance.
221: Despite these advances, existing methods fail to precisely penalize unfaithful reasoning steps. Little prior work specifically targets reasoning models, leaving their distinct hallucination dynamics largely unexplored. To address these limitations, we propose FaithRL, which explicitly penalizes unfaithful reasoning steps using step-level signals.
222: 3 Analyzing Faithfulness Hallucinations in SRMs
223: In this section, we conduct a series of analyses on faithfulness hallucinations in reasoning models. First, we show that SRMs tend to suffer from more severe faithfulness hallucinations. Second, we find that SRMs’ faithfulness hallucinations are more pronounced in CoT than in answers, which leads to the inconsistency where correct answers coexist with unfaithful CoT. Finally, an attack experiment shows that this inconsistency is harmful. When SRMs are faced with new problems constructed based on unfaithful hallucinated steps in CoT, they often produce incorrect answer.
224: 3.1 Preliminaries
225: Faithfulness Hallucinations in Open-Book Question Answering.
226: We study faithfulness hallucinations in Open-Book Question Answering, where each instance consists of a question Q and an external context C that provides the evidence required to answer Q . Despite the strong reasoning abilities of reasoning models (Huang et al., 2025a), they often exhibit insufficient contextual grounding, leading to reasoning steps or final answers that contradict or are unsupported by C . Following Huang et al. (2025b),we refer to this failure mode as faithfulness hallucination.
227: Formally, for the k -th rollout, given (C,Q) , a policy \pi_{\theta} generates a reasoning trajectory in the form of a CoT S_{k}=\{S_{k1},\dots,S_{kN}\} , followed by an answer A_{k} . The model is expected to produce both the intermediate reasoning steps and the final answer such that they are strictly grounded in the provided context C , prompts used are in Appendix A.1.
228: Datasets and Metrics.
229: For dataset selection, we follow Huang et al. (2025b) and use SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016), NewsQA (Trischler et al., 2017), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) to analyze faithfulness hallucinations in reasoning models.
230: We evaluate SRMs performance from two complementary perspectives: answer accuracy and answer faithfulness. The former compares the answer against the gold answer, while the latter assesses whether the answer is supported by the given context, without relying on gold answers.
231: Following Huang et al. (2025b), we use F1 score (Acc.) to evaluate SRMs answer accuracy on open-book QA datasets. Following Yao et al. (2025), we further introduce the Faithful Rate (Faith) H_{A} , defined as the percentage of test samples whose answer is judged to be faithful to the context by an LLM-as-a-judge, where H_{A}(A_{k},C)=\mathbbm{1}\big(A_{k}\text{ is faithful to }C\big) , prompts used are in Appendix A.3.
232: 3.2 Experimental Results and Analysis
233: Severe Faithfulness Hallucinations in SRMs.
234: As shown in Table 1, we conduct experiments on reasoning models of various scales, the results reveal that LRMs, such as DPSK-7B and Qwen3-8B, achieve high scores in both Faith and Acc., demonstrating an ability to generate relatively faithful answers. However, SRMs, specifically DPSK-1.5B and Qwen3-0.6B exhibit pronounced faithfulness hallucinations in their final answers; their Faithful Rates and Acc. scores are significantly lower than those of LRMs with the same architecture. Therefore, our work focuses on leveraging RL to mitigate hallucinations in SRMs, aiming to enhance their capacity to generate more faithful responses.
235: More Severe CoT Faithfulness Hallucinations than Answer in SRMs.
236: To investigate the causes of severe faithfulness hallucinations in SRMs, we conduct a CoT hallucination detection experiment to analyze their impact on answer correctness. We use Qwen3-32B to evaluate the faithfulness of each CoT sentence with respect to the context, where H_{S}(S_{kj},C)=\mathbbm{1}(S_{kj}\text{ is faithful to }C) and quantify hallucinations using the CoT Faithful Rate (CoT Faith), defined as \mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j}H_{S}(S_{kj},C)=N) , representing the proportion of samples whose CoT contains no faithfulness hallucination sentences, where N denotes the number of sentences in the CoT. Prompts used are in Appendix A.2.
237: As shown in Table 2, faithfulness hallucinations in the CoT are severe. For DPSK-1.5B, the CoT Faith is only 6%, far below the answer Faith of 67.40%. However, some CoT sentences are only loosely related to the answer, and detected hallucinations may correspond to transitional or irrelevant content. To better relate answer correctness to CoT hallucinations, we adopt a step-wise ablation strategy inspired by Jacovi et al. (2025). By masking individual context and CoT sentences, we examine changes in the Perplexity (PPL) of subsequent sentences and the answer.

255: Specifically, we implement a recursive search analogous to a tree search algorithm. We identify the Top-1 CoT sentence whose removal causes the largest increase in answer PPL, and recursively trace its most influential preceding sentence until reaching the context. This procedure yields the key reasoning path most relevant to the answer.
256: We use the Key CoT Faithful Rate (Key CoT Faith) to quantify hallucinations in key reasoning paths, defined as \mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{R}}H_{S}(S_{kj},C)=|\mathcal{R}|) , representing the proportion of samples whose key reasoning paths contain no faithfulness hallucinations, where \mathcal{R} denotes the set of indices corresponding to the key reasoning path. For DPSK-1.5B, Key CoT Faith is 39.11%, lower than the 67.40% Faithful Rate of answers. This gap indicates that faithfulness hallucinations are more prevalent in the CoT than in the answers. Moreover, unfaithful CoT can still yield correct answers, as models may rely on latent knowledge or shortcut reasoning. This suggests that faithfulness evaluation in SRMs should consider the CoT process, since outcome-only rewards may reinforce hallucinatory reasoning.
257: Faithfulness Hallucinations within the CoT Result in Incorrect Answers.
258: To show that treating samples with unfaithful CoT but correct answers as positive signals is harmful, we conduct an attack experiment using GPT-5.2, focusing on cases where the CoT contains faithfulness hallucinations while the final answer remains correct.
259: We first collect the initial CoT responses of DPSK-1.5B and Qwen3-1.7B on NewsQA and identify hallucinated statements using Qwen3-32B as a faithfulness judge. As shown in Figure 1, based on these hallucinated statements, GPT-5.2 is prompted to generate new questions derived from the unfaithful reasoning steps.
260: For each sample, we generate five candidate attack questions. Each question is sampled five times. The question with the highest error count is selected to construct the final attack set. Prompts we used are provided in Appendix A.4.
261: As shown in Table 2, the attack success rate is 59.48% for DPSK-1.5B and 48.75% for Qwen3-1.7B. These findings indicate that answer correctness alone is insufficient to judge the presence of faithfulness hallucinations and, more importantly, should not serve as the sole reward signal during training. Even when the answer happens to be correct, underlying reasoning flaws can lead to severe errors on new queries. This finding offers a new perspective on mitigating faithfulness hallucinations in SRMs: the training process must consider both answer accuracy and CoT faithfulness.
262: Figure 2: An overview of the FaithRL framework. The left part illustrates the Implicit Step-Level Rewards with Dynamic
263: Truncated Resampling. The model performs sentence-level faithfulness detection on the CoT generated by the policy model. When a faithfulness hallucination is detected, the reasoning process is truncated, and the faithful CoT sentences preceding it are used as a prefix for resampling. The right part depicts Explicit Step-Level Rewards with PRM.
264: When the final answer is incorrect, the R_{\text{answer}} is uniformly assigned to all tokens in the CoT and final answer. When the answer is correct, step-level rewards are applied to individual CoT sentences, with each sentence-level reward propagated to its tokens. This design enables precise penalization of faithfulness hallucinations while preserving correct and faithful reasoning steps.

562: 4 Our Proposed Method
563: Section 3 show that mitigating faithfulness hallucinations requires evaluating both CoT faithfulness and answer correctness. To overcome the limitation of traditional GRPO, which assigns rewards solely based on the final answer, we introduce explicit step-level rewards with PRM for CoT reasoning.
564: Besides, inspired by the tree-structured training paradigm of PRM (Zhang et al., 2025c), step-level supervision can also be introduced implicitly by contrasting rollouts that share common reasoning prefixes.
565: Therefore, we propose FaithRL, a reinforcement learning framework that integrates explicit and implicit step-level rewards to jointly optimize reasoning faithfulness and answer correctness.
566: 4.1 Implicit Step-Level Rewards with Dynamic Truncated Resampling
567: Implicit step-level rewards are typically derived from rollout-based comparisons. FaithRL realizes them in a more structured and efficient manner through dynamic trajectory resampling. As shown in the left half of Figure 2, we propose Dynamic Truncated Resampling (DTR), which adaptively controls rollouts via step-wise verification, enabling finer-grained credit assignment while improving both faithfulness and sampling efficiency.
568: Verification and Truncation.
569: For the k -th rollout, an initial CoT S_{k}=\{S_{k1},\dots,S_{kN}\} is generated from prompt P . Following Li and Ng (2025), we use HHEM-2.1 (Hughes et al., 2023) as a PRM to verify the faithfulness of each sentence S_{kt} to the context C , which exhibits stronger overall performance than Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), as shown in Appendix B.1. If an unfaithful step is detected at position t , the trajectory is truncated, and the faithful prefix {S_{k1},\dots,S_{k(t-1)}} is appended to P to form a new prompt P^{\prime} . A revised rollout
570: S_{k}^{r}=\{S_{k1},\dots,S_{k(t-1)},S_{kt}^{r},\dots\} is generated from P^{\prime} , preventing regeneration of correct preceding steps and avoiding redundant sampling of incorrect paths, thereby reducing token consumption per trajectory.
571: Equivalence to an Implicit Step-Level Reward.
572: We show that DTR can be interpreted as assigning implicit step-level rewards that penalize unfaithful reasoning steps while preserving rewards for faithful prefixes.
573: Let R_{k} and R_{k}^{r} denote the rewards of the original and revised rollouts, respectively. Since both trajectories share the identical prefix
574: \{S_{k1},\dots,S_{k(t-1)}\} , the total reward can be decomposed into two parts. The shared prefix effectively receives an average reward of (R_{k}+R_{k}^{r})/2 , while the divergent suffixes receive differential rewards: tokens in the unfaithful suffix receive
575: -(R_{k}^{r}-R_{k})/2 , and tokens in the resampled suffix receive (R_{k}^{r}-R_{k})/2 .
576: When resampling corrects a hallucinated step, unfaithful reasoning steps tend to receive lower rewards than their resampled counterparts, while the corrected continuation is rewarded; accordingly, we typically observe R_{k}^{r}>R_{k} . Therefore, although DTR does not introduce explicit step-level reward functions, it induces an implicit step-level reward signal that suppresses hallucination-prone reasoning while reinforcing faithful prefixes.
577: Parity Sampling for Faithful Paths.
578: The number of rollouts k is set to half of the standard GRPO due to each truncated trajectory being paired with a resampled continuation, which doubles the effective trajectory count. To keep the total trajectory count consistent with the baseline, if the initial CoT is fully faithful, we perform one additional independent sampling from the original prompt P . This ensures DTR maintains the same sampling budget as GRPO.
579: 4.2 Explicit Step-Level Rewards with PRM
580: For explicit step-level rewards, we use PRM evaluations to score each reasoning step and assign the resulting reward to the corresponding CoT tokens, enabling precise penalization of unfaithful steps.
581: Faithful Step Level Reward.
582: For the CoT generated by SRMs, we divide it into sentences and use HHEM-2.1 as PRM to verify whether each sentence is faithful to the context. We give each sentence a faithful step level reward. For the j -th sentence in the CoT of the k -th rollout, denoted as S_{kj} , the context is C_{k} , the reward function is defined as:
583: |
584: | \small R_{\text{fact}}(C_{k},S_{kj})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $S_{kj}$ is faithful to $C_{k}$,}\\
585: -1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
586: |
587: | (1)
588: Information Gain Reward.
589: Under the aforementioned step-level reward, the model exhibits reward-hacking behaviors by generating steps that are correct but yield no information gain. While these steps secure high step-level rewards, they do not contribute to the actual reasoning process. This specifically manifests in two ways: generating redundant correct steps or artificially inflating the token consumption of correct steps.
590: To penalize this verbosity without value, we propose an information gain reward that shifts the optimization focus from text length to information density.
591: By implementing a dual constraint on both sentence count and tokens per sentence, we effectively suppress redundant CoT expansions.
592: This encourages the model to allocate its generation budget toward informative reasoning rather than reward-oriented verbosity.
593: Specifically, for the j -th sentence in the CoT of the k -th rollout, denoted as S_{kj} , we define the following sequence-level penalties:
594: |
595: | \small R_{\text{sent}}(S_{kj})=-\lambda_{\text{s}}(j-L_{\text{s}})_{+},
596: |
597: | (2)
598: |
599: | \small R_{\text{tok}}(S_{kj})=-\lambda_{\text{t}}(|S_{kj}|-L_{\text{t}})_{+}.
600: |
601: | (3)
602: The overall information gain reward is then given by:
603: |
604: | \small R_{\text{inf}}(S_{kj})=R_{\text{sent}}(S_{kj})+R_{\text{tok}}(S_{kj}),
605: |
606: | (4)
607: where |S_{kj}| denotes the token count of S_{kj} , while \lambda_{\text{s}},\lambda_{\text{t}} and L_{\text{s}},L_{\text{t}} represent the penalty coefficients and their corresponding safety thresholds. The operator (x)_{+}=\max(0,x) applies the penalty when the constraints are exceeded.
608: Repetition Reward.
609: Following Yeo et al. (2025), we introduce an n -gram repetition penalty R_{\text{rep}} to prevent repetitive loops. This penalty serves as an extension of our information gain reward, as it penalizes redundant segments that yield zero additional information. In addition to ensuring reasoning diversity, R_{\text{rep}} effectively accelerates convergence by guiding the model away from degenerate, low-information trajectories during the early stages of GRPO training.
610: For the k -th rollout, CoT S_{k}=\{S_{k1},\dots,S_{kN}\} , if this ratio exceeds a pre-defined threshold \tau , a linear penalty scaled by \lambda_{\text{rep}} is applied:
611: |
612: | \small R_{\text{rep}}(S_{k})=-\lambda_{\text{rep}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{|\mathcal{U}_{k}|}{|\mathcal{G}_{k}|}\right)_{+}
613: |
614: | (5)
615: where \mathcal{G}_{k} denotes the multiset of all n -grams in the generated sequence, and \mathcal{U}_{k} represents the set of unique n -grams.
616: Step-Level Reward Assignment.
617: We first assess the correctness of the answer using the rule-based function R_{\text{answer}}(A_{k}) . Given the the k -th rollout answer A_{k} , the reward is defined as:
618: |
619: | \small R_{\text{answer}}(A_{k})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the answer is correct,}\\
620: -1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
621: |
622: | (6)
623: If the answer is incorrect, we assign R_{\text{answer}}(A_{k}) to all CoT tokens to uniformly penalize any reasoning path that leads to a wrong answer. If the answer is correct, we instead compute step-level rewards for the CoT and apply them to the tokens within each sentence to ensure the model reaches the right answer through faithful reasoning rather than unfaithful CoT. For the answer, we always apply R_{\text{answer}}(A_{k}) to each answer token. For a token x_{i}\in S_{kj} , where S_{kj} is the j -th sentence in the CoT of the k -th rollout, the reward R_{cot} is defined as:
624: |
625: | \small R_{\text{cot}}=\begin{cases}R_{\text{answer}},&\text{if }R_{\text{answer}}=-1,\\
626: R_{\text{fact}}+R_{\text{inf}}+R_{\text{rep}},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
627: |
628: | (7)

630: 5 Exerperiment
631: 5.1 Experimental Setup
632: We use the dataset and metrics mentioned in Section 3.1.
633: Baseline.
634: We employ SFT and vanilla GRPO strategy as primary variants of our method. Additionally, we adopt KD (Nguyen et al., 2025), Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023), and FSPO (Li and Ng, 2025) as competitive baselines for comprehensive comparison, more details in the Appendix B.2.
635: Training Details.
636: Following Li and Ng (2025), we randomly sample 8,000 examples from HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA as training data. We train our model using the verl framework. During training, we use a batch size of 8, generate 8 rollouts for each prompt, and set the maximum length for both prompt and response to 2,048 tokens. Training is conducted for one epoch on NVIDIA A800 GPUs.

795: FaithRL Maintains Training Efficiency.
796: Although incorporating PRM for sentence-level hallucination detection introduces additional overhead, the overall training cost remains comparable. This is largely due to the information gain reward, which discourages redundant CoT expansion and reduces unnecessary token generation. Our DTR strategy further saves tokens by preserving faithful prefixes and avoiding redundant computation. For DPSK-1.5B, FaithRL reduces SRM-generated tokens to about one-third of those produced by vanilla GRPO, requiring 22.59 GPU hours on NVIDIA A800 GPUs versus 23.52 GPU hours for standard GRPO, demonstrating that hallucination mitigation can be achieved without sacrificing efficiency.
797: 5.3 Ablation Study
798: The Essential Role of Information Gain Reward in Stable Reasoning Training.
799: The results of the ablation study are shown in Table 4. We ablate the two components of the information gain reward: the constraint on the number of CoT sentences (Rsent) and the token count per sentence (Rtok). Removing Rsent reduces the average Acc. by 21.35%, and removing Rtok leads to a 42.40% reduction in Acc..
800: Moreover, completely removing Rinf leads to excessively long outputs that cause training to collapse. These results indicate that the information gain reward Rinf is essential for suppressing hallucinatory verbosity, thereby ensuring stable and effective training.

802: Impact of Repetition Reward on Reducing Redundancy in Reasoning.
803: Excluding repetition reward Rrep results a 2.46% decrease in average Acc. and in a 2.57% decrease in average Faith.
804: We observe that without this penalty, the model is prone to falling into redundant logical loops during the reasoning process. Instead, the inclusion of Rrep incentivizes the model to avoid reasoning trajectories containing repetitive statements, thereby accelerating convergence.
805: Impact of Dynamic Truncated Resampling on Performance in Complex Reasoning.
806: Removing the resampling mechanism leads to notable performance declines on complex reasoning benchmarks such as TriviaQA and HotpotQA. This demonstrates that our DTR is essential for stabilizing the reasoning process in challenging scenarios. This mechanism reinforces faithful reasoning patterns through implicit step-level rewards, leading to superior robustness compared to independent sampling.
807: Resampling Strategy Selection.
808: To identify the optimal configuration for balancing reasoning faithfulness and task performance, we compare different sampling strategies with an initial rollout of 8 samples per question on DPSK-1.5B.
809: Resample triggers additional sampling only when faithfulness hallucinations are detected in the CoT; otherwise, no extra samples are generated.
810: Under this resampling scheme, Full enforces a fixed total budget of 16 samples per question by completing the remaining samples with independent rollouts from scratch when the number of resampled trajectories is insufficient. In contrast, Random also completes the remaining budget, but does so by randomly truncating a sentence from an existing trajectory and continuing generation from the resulting prefix.

888: 5.4 Analysis
889: This section provides an analysis to substantiate the necessity of jointly considering CoT faithfulness and answer correctness during SRMs training. We first show that vanilla GRPO, which rewards answer correctness, exacerbates CoT faithfulness hallucinations during training. We then demonstrate that such hallucinated CoT reasoning leads to severe failures under adversarial attacks. Finally, we verify that FaithRL effectively reduces CoT faithfulness hallucinations.
890: Vanilla GRPO Exacerbates Faithfulness Hallucinations in CoT.
891: To verify that relying on answer correctness as reward signal in vanilla GRPO exacerbates faithfulness hallucinations in CoT, we analyze how faithfulness evolves throughout training.
892: As described in Section 3.2, since not all CoT sentences contribute to the final answer, we focus on Key CoT Faithful Rate, and track both Key CoT Faith and answer Faith during training as shown in Figure 3.
893: We observe that although GRPO reduces hallucinations in final answers, it exacerbates hallucinations within CoT. This occurs because GRPO rewards answer correctness alone, reinforcing samples with correct answers despite containing faithfulness-hallucinated reasoning steps. In contrast, FaithRL achieves higher faithfulness at both the CoT and answer levels. These results suggest that outcome-based GRPO fails to fundamentally resolve faithfulness hallucinations in SRMs; effective mitigation requires jointly optimizing the reasoning process and the answer during training.
894: Severe Consequences of Vanilla GRPO under Adversarial Attacks.
895: To demonstrate the harm caused by the vanilla GRPO strategy, which uses outcome-only rewards and reinforces samples with correct answers despite unfaithful CoT reasoning, we conduct adversarial attack experiments following Section 3.2. We collect samples with unfaithful CoT but correct answers from the original outputs of DPSK-1.5B and Qwen3-1.7B, use GPT-5.2 to induce attacks based on the hallucinated reasoning steps, and construct an attack set for evaluating models.
896: As shown in Table 6, all methods suffer substantial performance degradation on the attack question set, indicating that unfaithful CoT reasoning leads to incorrect answers. FaithRL consistently outperforms all baselines and shows stronger robustness. For DPSK-1.5B, compared to standard GRPO, the relative performance improvement of FaithRL over GRPO widens under adversarial attack, representing a 248% amplification (30.87% vs. 12.45%) in terms of Faith. This suggests that rewarding only final outcomes in GRPO fails to eliminate faithfulness hallucinations in SRMs and leads to more severe errors under adversarial induction.
897: Mitigating CoT Faithfulness Hallucinations with FaithRL.
898: Finally, to verify the practical effectiveness of FaithRL in reducing CoT faithfulness hallucinations, we conduct a CoT hallucination detection experiment following Section 3.2 using Qwen3-32B to determine whether each CoT sentence is faithful to the context and quantify hallucinations using the Hallucination Rate (HR), defined as \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}H_{S}(S_{kj},C) , where N denotes the number of sentences in the CoT, representing the proportion of hallucinated sentences among all CoT sentences. We further define the Key Hallucination Rate (KHR) as \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{R}}H_{S}(S_{kj},C) , where \mathcal{R} denotes the set of indices corresponding to the key reasoning path in the CoT, representing the hallucination rate along the key reasoning path.
899: As shown in Table 7, FaithRL substantially reduces both HR and KHR, which demonstrates that FaithRL not only improves answer faithfulness but also mitigates hallucinations along critical reasoning paths, thereby enhancing the reliability and trustworthiness of CoT reasoning through precise penalization of unfaithful intermediate steps.
900: 6 Conclusion
901: In this paper, we propose FaithRL, a reinforcement learning framework for mitigating faithfulness hallucinations in SRMs. By combining implicit step-level rewards from dynamic truncated resampling with explicit PRM-based rewards, FaithRL provides fine-grained supervision to penalize unfaithful CoT reasoning. FaithRL achieves superior performance across multiple SRMs and open-book QA datasets.
902: We also show that vanilla GRPO, which only relies on outcome-based rewards, exacerbates faithfulness hallucinations in CoTs with correct answers, underscoring the need to jointly consider CoT faithfulness and answer correctness.
903: Impact Statement
904: This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning by improving the reliability and faithfulness of small reasoning models. By mitigating hallucinations in intermediate reasoning steps, our research contributes to the development of more trustworthy and transparent AI systems. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.
