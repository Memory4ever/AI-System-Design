167: Abstract
168: The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed the development of autonomous agents capable of navigating complex environments.
169: However, existing evaluations primarily adopt a deductive paradigm,
170: where agents execute tasks based on explicitly provided rules and static goals, often within limited planning horizons.
171: Crucially,
172: this neglects the inductive necessity for agents to discover latent transition laws from experience autonomously,
173: which is the cornerstone for enabling agentic foresight and sustaining strategic coherence.
174: To bridge this gap, we introduce OdysseyArena,
175: which re-centers agent evaluation on long-horizon, active, and inductive interactions.
176: We formalize and instantiate four primitives,
177: translating abstract transition dynamics into concrete interactive environments.
178: Building upon this,
179: we establish OdysseyArena-Lite for standardized benchmarking,
180: providing a set of 120 tasks to measure an agent’s inductive efficiency and long-horizon discovery.
181: Pushing further, we introduce OdysseyArena-Challenge to stress-test agent stability across extreme interaction horizons (e.g., >> 200 steps).
182: Extensive experiments on 15+ leading LLMs reveal that even frontier models exhibit a deficiency in inductive scenarios, identifying a critical bottleneck in the pursuit of autonomous discovery in complex environments.
183: Our code and data are available at https://github.com/xufangzhi/Odyssey-Arena

187: 1 Introduction
188: The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs; Gemini Team, 2025; Anthropic AI, 2024)
189: has sparked unprecedented interest in autonomous agents that can perceive environments,
190: make decisions, and take actions to accomplish complex tasks.
191: These AI agents are increasingly deployed across diverse domains—from robotics (Wang et al., 2025a) and game playing (Akata et al., 2025) to scientific discovery (Sun et al., 2025b) and business automation (Xu et al., 2025c).
192: As the capabilities of LLMs expand, so does the demand for evaluation benchmarks that can reliably assess agent performance in realistic, dynamic settings.
193: Figure 1: Comparison between deductive and inductive settings in multi-turn agentic tasks.
194: While recent benchmarks have evolved from static question answering toward interactive decision-making (Xie et al., 2024; Vodrahalli et al., 2024; Chung et al., 2025),
195: they predominantly assess a deductive mode of intelligence where agents rely on extensive prior knowledge to complete tasks.
196: As illustrated in Figure 1,
197: such evaluation overlooks the essential inductive mode, in which agents are required to actively explore and induce the hidden rules underlying the environment.
198: This omission restricts the evaluation of an agent’s proficiency in complex environments where rules are not pre-specified.
199: We identify three critical capability dimensions that remain largely unaddressed in the current landscape.
200: First, extremely long-horizon interaction is often neglected by existing benchmarks that restrict episodes to fewer than 50 steps, which fails to capture the strategic coherence and error accumulation challenges inherent in sequences of thousands of steps.
201: Second, active exploration and trial-and-error are frequently bypassed by environments that provide fully specified success criteria,
202: whereas realistic deployment requires agents to actively probe, react to feedback, and iteratively adjust their actions through self-improvement (Yan et al., 2025).
203: Finally, inductive reasoning from interaction represents a critical evaluation gap in current benchmarks,
204: as most protocols assess deductive compliance to provided instructions rather than the capacity to infer latent rules and transition dynamics from experience.
205: In order to bridge this gap,
206: we introduce OdysseyArena,
207: a suite of interactive environments that re-centers evaluation on long-horizon, active, and inductive reasoning, which entails inferring latent transition laws from empirical interactions.
208: We formalize
209: environments as generative state transition functions:
210: (st+1,rt)=𝒯⁡(st,at),(s_{t+1},r_{t})=\mathcal{T}(s_{t},a_{t}),
211: where st∈𝒮s_{t}\in\mathcal{S} is the latent state and at∈𝒜a_{t}\in\mathcal{A} is the agent action.
212: The transition function 𝒯\mathcal{T} implicitly encodes the environment’s rules and regularities, which agents must actively induce from interaction in order to anticipate outcomes, plan effectively, and optimize behavior over long horizons. This makes 𝒯\mathcal{T} the central object of agent evaluation.
213: To systematically study an agent’s inductive capacity,
214: we decompose 𝒯\mathcal{T} into a taxonomy of four representative structural primitives: discrete latent rules, continuous stochastic dynamics, multi-objective periodic patterns,
215: and relational dependencies.
216: To facilitate empirical investigation into these abstract dynamics, we materialize these primitives into four diverse OdysseyArena environments. These environments are curated to be computationally efficient and lightweight while remaining functionally representative of real-world systems, providing a tractable and scalable testbed for the community.
217: Specifically,
218: Turn On Lights grounds discrete Boolean logic in interdependent bulb configurations,
219: while AI Trading presents continuous stochastic dynamics through multivariate stock-factor relationships.
220: Energy Dispatch requires agents to uncover periodic efficiency patterns under multi-objective constraints,
221: and Repo System engages agents in deducing the topological dependencies among software package versions in a virtual environment.
222: For practical evaluation,
223: we provide OdysseyArena-Lite as a standardized benchmark of 120 curated tasks.
224: This suite serves as a representative of a scalable task distribution,
225: optimized for high evaluation throughput while preserving the core challenges of active and inductive discovery.
226: Each task maintains interaction horizons that are computationally tractable yet sufficiently non-trivial to necessitate long-horizon planning and the active induction of latent rules.
227: In addition, we release OdysseyArena-Challenge, a stress-test suite with 1,000+ steps per task and 10 tasks per environment, intended to probe the limits of agent persistence, reasoning stability, and the ability to maintain coherent strategies over extremely long horizons. Together, these two settings balance accessibility, efficiency, and scalability, supporting both rapid iteration on current agents and rigorous evaluation of next-generation capabilities.
228: We evaluated over 15 trending LLMs,
229: spanning proprietary models and open-source models across different scales.
230: Overall,
231: commercial models consistently outperform open-source alternatives, with Gemini 3 Pro Preview achieving the highest success rate of 44.17.
232: Despite this, even the strongest commercial models broadly remain far below human-level performance across four environments in OdysseyArena-Lite,
233: highlighting substantial gaps in long-horizon reasoning, active exploration, and inductive generalization.
234: Beyond these aggregate results, we conducted a detailed, fine-grained analysis of agent behavior across different environments and tasks,
235: revealing patterns and failure modes that provide actionable insights for designing more capable and robust autonomous agents.
236: Our primary contributions are as follows:
237: (1) Novel Perspectives For Agent Evaluation:
238: We propose a novel evaluation paradigm centered on the capacity for autonomous discovery.
239: This shift refocuses agentic intelligence on the induction of latent world dynamics through long-horizon and active interaction.
240: (2) Reliable and Scalable Evaluation :
241: We instantiate the inductive paradigm evaluation into OdysseyArena environments,
242: from which we establish OdysseyArena-Lite as a standardized suite for efficient evaluation. Our environments further provide a foundation that scales to probe the limits of agentic intelligence.
243: (3) Extensive Evaluations and Insights:
244: Through an extensive evaluation of 15+ top-tier LLMs, we characterize the inductive bottleneck as a fundamental barrier to autonomous discovery while establishing rigorous benchmarking results for future research.
245: 2 Related Work
246: Interactive Benchmarks.
247: Interactive benchmarks for LLM agents have evolved from grounded language understanding in simplified grid-worlds (Shridhar et al., 2021; Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2019)
248: to sophisticated digital (Deng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024) and
249: real-world systems (Xu et al., 2025c; Yao et al., 2025).
250: Despite this progress,
251: a critical bottleneck remains in temporal depth: most environments favor short horizons or trajectories (typically dozens of steps; Rawles et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025a),
252: failing to capture the “butterfly effect” of error accumulation and the decay of long-term planning consistency (Liu et al., 2024).
253: Furthermore,
254: many existing protocols (Wang et al., 2025b; Patil et al., 2025) bypass exploratory requirements by providing gold instructions or detailed API docs.
255: OdysseyArena and derived benchmarks bridge these gaps by introducing long-horizon tasks that demand coherent internal states and robust recovery strategies over extended interaction sequences.

293: Inductive Reasoning.
294: Current agentic frameworks, such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023),
295: primarily rely on deductive reasoning or test-time interactions (Sun et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2025b) to apply internal knowledge or provided rules.
296: However,
297: the nature of intelligence necessitates inductive reasoning,
298: which refers to the capacity to infer latent rules and transition dynamics from raw observations (Lake et al., 2017).
299: While static benchmarks like ARC (Chollet, 2019) and Zebra-Logic (Lin et al., 2025) evaluate rule synthesis,
300: they remain passive and fail to capture the active discovery loop essential for autonomous agents (Lin et al., 2025).
301: While interactive environments like Mars (Tang et al., 2024) facilitate exploratory interaction, they often struggle to decouple pure induction from pre-trained knowledge priors.
302: In contrast with typical agent benchmarks shown in Table 1,
303: this work necessitates inducing latent world structures through extremely long-horizon interactions,
304: aligning with the concept of world models (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) while posing higher-order symbolic challenges for future agentic intelligence.
305: 3 OdysseyArena
306: 3.1 Preliminaries
307: Figure 2: Demonstrations of four OdysseyArena environments: Turn On Lights, AI Trading, Energy Dispatch, and Repo System. For clarity, we omit the task prompts here and present only the interaction trajectories. Full prompts are provided in Appendix C.
308: Interactive environments can be characterized as generative processes where the environment’s response to an action at∈𝒜a_{t}\in\mathcal{A} at a latent state st∈𝒮s_{t}\in\mathcal{S} is governed by a transition function 𝒯\mathcal{T}:
309: (st+1,rt)=𝒯⁡(st,at).(s_{t+1},r_{t})=\mathcal{T}(s_{t},a_{t}).
310: (1)
311: In this framework, 𝒯\mathcal{T} encapsulates the unobservable regularities and constraints that dictate the system’s evolution. Unlike standard reinforcement learning paradigms that often focus on policy optimization within known or fixed MDPs,
312: OdysseyArena emphasizes latent structure induction. To achieve long-horizon planning and optimal decision-making, agents must autonomously discover the functional form of 𝒯\mathcal{T} through strategic interaction.
313: To systematically characterize environment dynamics, we decompose the landscape of environment dynamics into a taxonomy of four orthogonal structural primitives. These primitives represent the fundamental mathematical motifs that lead to complex real-world systems (Clark, 1997; Li et al., 2024).
314: By isolating these irreducible structures,
315: we define a comprehensive set of world-modeling challenges that an autonomous agent must navigate:
316: •
317: Discrete Symbolic Rules: The transition is governed by Boolean logic over NN bits, where s∈{0,1}Ns\in\{0,1\}^{N}.
318: This requires the agent to perform symbolic hypothesis testing to uncover the latent causal dependencies and logical couplings.
319: •
320: Continuous Stochastic Dynamics:
321: The system evolves through a continuous state space s∈ℝds\in\mathbb{R}^{d} according to st+1=f⁡(st,at)+ϵs_{t+1}=f(s_{t},a_{t})+\epsilon, where 𝒯\mathcal{T} incorporates a latent functional signal ff and exogenous noise ϵ\epsilon. This necessitates statistical inference to disentangle underlying regularities from stochastic fluctuations.
322: •
323: Periodic Temporal Patterns:
324: The transition function exhibits cyclic regularities defined by a period PP, such that 𝒯⁡(s,a,t)≈𝒯⁡(s,a,t+P)\mathcal{T}(s,a,t)\approx\mathcal{T}(s,a,t+P). This necessitates identifying long-range temporal dependencies to optimize multi-objective trade-offs.
325: •
326: Relational Graph Structures:
327: The environment is defined by a graph G=(V,E)G=(V,E), where transitions involve non-local interactions between entities. Success requires relational reasoning over the topological constraints that govern global state changes.
328: This taxonomy ensures a comprehensive evaluation, as each structure induces a distinct cognitive requirement—ranging from logical deduction to relational abstraction—that is irreducible to the others.
329: OdysseyArena integrates these primitives into a diverse suite of environments designed to assess an agent’s fundamental capacity for world-structure induction, as illustrated in Figure 2.
330: We will introduce the respective environments in detail as follows.
331: 3.2 Env I: Turn On Lights
332: Overview.
333: This environment instantiates the discrete symbolic rules primitive by simulating a network of NN interdependent lights.
334: The agent aims to reach a target configuration s=𝟏s=\mathbf{1},
335: representing the state where all lights are illuminated,
336: through a sequence of toggling actions.
337: Dynamics are governed by latent Boolean couplings that remain fixed within one trial but vary across episodes,
338: where an intervention on a single light may trigger a deterministic cascade of state changes across the network.
339: Consequently, success requires active exploration to infer the underlying logic and the correct activation sequence.
340: Hidden Rules.
341: State transitions are governed by latent discrete rules that define how each action influences the configuration of lights.
342: For each episode, these rules are instantiated by randomly combining Boolean operators to create a unique logical network, while ensuring that the resulting dependencies are solvable.
343: As a result, an action targeting a particular light may deterministically affect multiple lights through indirect toggling or conditional activation.
344: The dependencies remain fixed within an episode but vary across episodes, and are not directly observable to the agent.
345: Consequently, the agent must infer the underlying logical structure through deliberate intervention and observation of state changes.
346: Action and Observation Spaces.
347: At each step, the agent takes a discrete action to activate/toggle a single light.
348: Although actions are defined over individual lights and remain fixed across episodes, their effects are not necessarily localized due to hidden logical couplings.
349: After each interaction, the agent observes the on/off status of all NN lights.
350: No information about the underlying logical dependencies or transition rules is revealed.
351: As a result, while the environment is fully observable with respect to the surface state, it is partially observable in terms of the latent transition dynamics induced by the hidden rule configuration.
352: Task Evaluation.
353: An episode is considered successful if all lights are turned on before the interaction budget is exhausted.
354: Performance is measured by the task success rate.
355: Solving the task efficiently requires the agent to identify and exploit the latent logical rules, rather than relying on myopic or brute-force exploration.
356: 3.3 Env II: AI Trading
357: Overview.
358: This environment instantiates the continuous stochastic dynamics primitive, where the agent manages a multi-asset portfolio to maximize cumulative returns ∑rt\sum r_{t}.
359: Market transitions are driven by latent factors obscured by stochastic fluctuations,
360: with daily news serving as indirect hints for future price movements.
361: Unlike reactive tasks, success here depends on the agent’s ability to induce the underlying market regularities, requiring it to disentangle the meaningful signal ff from noise to execute optimal multi-step, long-horizon trading strategies.
362: Hidden Rules.
363: Price transitions are governed by the function st+1=𝐖​zt+ϵs_{t+1}=\mathbf{W}z_{t}+\epsilon, where s∈ℝds\in\mathbb{R}^{d} denotes asset returns. In this formulation, 𝐖\mathbf{W} represents a latent factor loading matrix that maps a vector of unobserved market factors ztz_{t} to the price changes of dd assets.
364: While ztz_{t} and the noise ϵ\epsilon fluctuate at each timestep,
365: the structural relationships defined by 𝐖\mathbf{W} remain invariant within an episode but vary across tasks.
366: Consequently,
367: agents must treat the sequence of interactions as a noisy observation process to identify the functional form of 𝐖\mathbf{W} for effective long-horizon planning.
368: Action and Observation Spaces
369: At each step tt, the agent perceives an observation ot∈𝒪o_{t}\in\mathcal{O} comprising historical prices and news-derived indicators. Since oto_{t} serves as a noisy proxy for ztz_{t},
370: the agent must utilize the information within oto_{t} to estimate the underlying market state. Based on the perceived oto_{t}, the agent issues a combinatorial action at∈𝒜a_{t}\in\mathcal{A} specifying buy and sell quantities across all assets.
371: To enable portfolio reallocation within a single decision cycle, the environment enforces sequential execution, processing sell orders before buy orders, requiring the agent to jointly reason about asset selection, trade timing, and capital allocation under the transition logic 𝒯\mathcal{T}.
372: Task Evaluation.
373: Performance is measured by cumulative return over the trading horizon, adjusted for transaction costs and risk constraints.
374: Successful performance requires learning and exploiting the latent stochastic dynamics over time, as opposed to reactive or single-step trading strategies.

376: 3.4 Env III: Energy Dispatch
377: Overview.
378: This environment instantiates periodic temporal patterns by modeling a long-horizon energy dispatch problem in a dynamic power grid.
379: The agent acts as a dispatcher, allocating thermal, wind, solar, and battery resources to meet daily demand under budget constraints.
380: Unlike deductive optimization tasks, the environment enforces strict constraints: repeated budget or demand violations trigger immediate termination, simulating irreversible system failure.
381: Achieving a stable, low-carbon supply therefore requires anticipating latent efficiency cycles and planning over extended horizons to avoid systemic collapse.
382: Hidden Rules.
383: The core challenge lies in the discrepancy between the agent’s planned dispatch and the actual power generation.
384: The system evolves according to a transition logic where the realized output PrealP_{\text{real}} is determined by the agent’s rated action ata_{t} modulated by a latent efficiency vector EtE_{t},
385: formally Preal≈at⊙EtP_{\text{real}}\approx a_{t}\odot E_{t}. Here, EtE_{t} represents the time-varying efficiency for each power source.
386: Crucially, the efficiency factors for wind and solar are governed by distinct, unobserved periodic functions Et≈Et+TE_{t}\approx E_{t+T} with unique periods TT. Since these fluctuations are not directly observable, the agent must infer the underlying periodic structures from historical output gaps.
387: Action and Observation Space.
388: At each timestep,
389: the agent receives oto_{t} containing the current electricity demand DtD_{t} and operating budget BtB_{t}.
390: Based on these requirements, the agent issues a continuous action at∈ℝ4a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{4} specifying the rated output for each generation type and the net charge/discharge command for the battery.
391: The battery introduces inter-temporal dependencies,
392: allowing the agent to buffer energy across time steps. Crucially, the environment enforces a strict safety protocol: repeated violations of demand satisfaction or budget limits trigger an early termination, simulating an irreversible grid collapse.
393: Task Evaluation.
394: Success is defined by a rigorous multi-objective criterion. First, the agent must ensure survival by maintaining system stability without triggering early termination over the full horizon HH (e.g., 120 days). Second, upon completion, the aggregate performance must satisfy predefined thresholds for both Carbon Intensity (𝒞<τc\mathcal{C}<\tau_{c}) and Grid Stability (𝒮>τs\mathcal{S}>\tau_{s}).
395: 3.5 Env IV: Repo System
396: Overview.
397: This environment models a realistic software repository management scenario, where the agent must configure a Python project to execute successfully.
398: The agent interacts with a partially observable dependency ecosystem, in which resolving local failures may introduce new global inconsistencies.
399: Success requires systematic diagnosis, relational reasoning over dependencies, and careful planning under non-monotonic side effects.
400: Hidden Rules.
401: The transition logic 𝒯\mathcal{T} is defined by a latent dependency graph G=(V,E)G=(V,E). Here, nodes VV represent software packages with specific versions, and directed edges EE represent compatibility constraints (e.g., Pkg A v1.0 requires Pkg B ≥\geq v2.0). The system state st⊆Vs_{t}\subseteq V represents the currently installed environment configuration. Crucially, the graph GG is hidden from the agent. A transition st+1=𝒯⁡(st,at)s_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}(s_{t},a_{t}) triggered by an installation command involves a rigorous resolution process: the system automatically installs required ancestors and uninstalls conflicting nodes to maintain local consistency, potentially altering parts of sts_{t} not targeted by ata_{t} (side effects).
402: Action and Observation Space.
403: The agent perceives the environment through oto_{t}, consisting of terminal outputs, file structures, and execution logs. Since GG is latent, oto_{t} serves as a sparse signal revealing only the graph “broken edges” (e.g., ImportError).
404: Based on these error traces, the agent issues discrete symbolic actions at∈𝒜shella_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{shell}} (e.g., pip install).
405: Since actions act as high-level graph mutation operators, the agent must sequence them to navigate the combinatorial state space, deducing the topology of GG to resolve conflicts without explicitly observing the full picture.
406: Task Evaluation.
407: The task is evaluated by whether the agent achieves a globally consistent environment in which the full project executes successfully.
408: Partial or intermediate fixes (e.g., running individual sub-scripts) receive no reward if the global entry point fails, as local improvements may conflict with overall correctness.
409: This strictly binary protocol enforces global consistency, emphasizing long-horizon planning, relational reasoning over interdependent components, and robustness to delayed and indirect effects over myopic error patching.
410: 4 OdysseyArena-Lite and OdysseyArena-Challenge
411: Building upon the infinite task space provided by OdysseyArena,
412: we derive two distinct benchmarking protocols to serve as standardized instantiations designed to evaluate agent performance across different difficulties:
413: We primarily introduce OdysseyArena-Lite as a suite tailored for efficient and reproducible performance assessment.
414: Additionally, we provide OdysseyArena-Challenge for stress-testing agent stability and inductive resilience over extreme interaction horizons.
415: The benchmark construction process and sample details are described below.
416: 4.1 Task Configuration and Curation
417: To derive verifiable benchmarks from OdysseyArena,
418: we formalize each task as a deterministic instance sampled from a bounded parameter distribution (see Appendix A for details).
419: This transition from abstract primitives to concrete evaluation is achieved by decomposing each instance into its structural configuration and temporal trajectory.
420: Structural Configuration.
421: The configuration initializes latent rules 𝒯\mathcal{T} that remain invariant throughout an episode.
422: In Turn On Lights, this corresponds to stochastically generating a DAG with specified density and logical operator ratios; in AI Trading and Repo System, it instantiates linear coefficient matrices and package dependency graphs, respectively.
423: To ensure solvability, constraint satisfaction algorithms are applied during curation to guarantee a valid solution path for every rule set.
424: Temporal Trajectory.
425: To eliminate stochasticity,
426: we pre-determine all time-varying factors in OdysseyArena as fixed sequences within the task metadata. For environments with dynamic states such as AI Trading and Energy Dispatch,
427: components including daily factor fluctuations, efficiency curves, and resource budgets are pre-calculated. This ensures that the environment’s evolution is not subject to runtime randomness, allowing for an identical and fair comparison across different agents and experimental trials.
428: Task Sampling Strategy.
429: We sample tasks by defining valid ranges for key environment parameters, such as the number of entities or the depth of logical dependencies.
430: This sampling process is specifically calibrated to modulate task difficulty across two protocols. OdysseyArena-Lite occupies a tractable parameter set for efficient evaluation,
431: while OdysseyArena-Challenge targets the empirical limits of these ranges to stress-test agent stability over extreme horizons.
432: This approach enables a characterization of the inductive bottleneck across varying environmental challenges.
433: Detailed human anno processes are in Appendix D.
434: 4.2 Task Statistics
435: Under the OdysseyArena-Lite setting, we construct a fixed evaluation suite by sampling 30 tasks for each environment.
436: The maximum allowed number of interaction steps is environment-specific, reflecting the inherent complexity of the underlying dynamics.
437: Specifically, the step limits are set to 200 for TurnOnLights, and 120 for AI Trading, Energy Dispatch, and Repo Management, respectively.
438: This configuration yields a balanced benchmark that supports reliable comparison across environments while remaining computationally efficient for online evaluation.
439: While OdysseyArena-Lite serves as our primary evaluation setting and the basis for all main experiments in this paper,
440: we additionally introduce OdysseyArena-Challenge as a more demanding variant designed for stress-testing advanced agents, which substantially extends the required reasoning horizon, with tasks often exceeding 1,000 interaction steps.
441: This setting targets failure modes that do not manifest under standard budgets, such as long-term credit assignment breakdown and compounding planning errors.
442: Due to its significantly higher computational cost, OdysseyArena-Challenge is not used in our main evaluations, but is provided as an optional benchmark for future research.

607: 5.2 Main Results.
608: The General Performance Gap.
609: Table 2 illustrates a performance disparity between SOTA LLMs and human.
610: Unlike human participants who successfully resolve tasks by isolating causal variables and distilling latent rules through instinctive trial-and-error,
611: LLMs exhibit a marked deficiency in autonomous rule induction.
612: This gap highlights a fundamental failure in current agents to internalize latent world dynamics from experience,
613: leading to significant performance degradation as interaction horizons extend.
614: Proprietary Model and the Scaling Limit.
615: Frontier proprietary models, led by Gemini 3 Pro Preview,
616: consistently provides the current performance ceiling and substantially outperform other counterparts across most environments.
617: Despite this advantage, the ubiquitous failure in Energy Dispatch underscores a critical architectural limitation shared across the spectrum: an inability to synthesize periodic patterns over extended observation windows (∼\sim20 steps). This suggests that while increased scale enhances deductive compliance,
618: it remains insufficient to overcome the inductive bottleneck for robust world-structure modeling.

620: Deductive Proficiency vs. Inductive Deficiency.
621: To disentangle the root of failure,
622: we evaluate agents with explicit access to latent transition rules.
623: Figure 4 shows that frontier models achieve near-perfect success when the underlying
624: logic is provided, yet falter significantly without it.
625: This contrast identifies a fundamental asymmetry:
626: LLMs excel at deductive reasoning but lack the inductive capacity to autonomously synthesize environment mechanics from experience. These findings confirm that the primary bottleneck in OdysseyArena is the discovery of world dynamics rather than the complexity of the task logic itself.

629: To probe agentic limits,

632: Notably, 6 high-complexity tasks remain unsolved by any tested model, identifying a rigid inductive barrier.
633: This stagnation suggests that as environmental dimensionality scales,
634: the requirements for world-modeling exceed the capacity of current LLMs, highlighting a persistent gap between deductive compliance and autonomous discovery.

638: 6.2 Performance Saturation in Long-Horizon
639: Analysis of success rates across interaction sequences reveals a distinct performance saturation effect.
640: As illustrated in Figure 6, increasing the interaction budget beyond an initial exploratory phase yields negligible marginal gains for most models.
641: This plateau suggests a fundamental inductive bottleneck in long-horizon scenarios,
642: where extended interaction fails to rectify the absence of a coherent internal world model.
643: Furthermore, weaker models frequently underperform the random baseline, underscoring an inherent inability to extract latent regularities from environmental feedback.
644: These findings indicate that the primary barrier is not interaction volume but the underlying capacity for inductive discovery.
645: More details are in Appendix B.3.
646: 6.3 Action Loops and Inductive Stagnation
647: Analysis of agent trajectories reveals a prevalent failure mode characterized by persistent “action loops,”
648: where models repeat invalid operations despite receiving negative environmental feedback.
649: As shown in Figure 7, a higher loop ratio directly correlates with diminished success rates,
650: identifying a critical inability to capture hidden rules during interaction.
651: This repetitive behavior signifies inductive stagnation, as agents fail to synthesize latent world laws from unsuccessful trials to refine their long-term strategy.
652: Consequently, these cycles highlight the failure to transform trial-and-error into active discovery, underscoring the gap between deductive compliance and inductive world-modeling.
653: More loop ratio details can be found in Appendix B.5.
654: 7 Conclusion
655: This work introduces a paradigm shift in agentic evaluation by transitioning from deductive instruction-following to long-horizon,
656: inductive modeling. We formalize abstract environment dynamics into four structural primitives and instantiate them via OdysseyArena,
657: and then establish a standardized framework for the reproducible evaluation of an agent’s ability to discover latent transition laws.
658: The observed low-performance plateau across multiple flagships
659: reveals a fundamental inductive bottleneck that scaling alone cannot resolve, underscoring the necessity of moving beyond deductive compliance.
660: Future research toward more agentic intelligence should prioritize architectures capable of distilling latent transition laws from raw experience, thereby bridging the gap between passive rule-following and active discovery in complex, dynamic worlds.
661: Impact Statement
662: We introduces OdysseyArena, an environment for evaluating agents through long-horizon, active, and inductive interactions.
663: By prioritizing the autonomous discovery of latent world dynamics,
664: we facilitate the development of agents with enhanced strategic foresight in complex domains such as energy management and financial trading.
665: We advocate for a paradigm of responsible AI where agent reasoning remains interpretable and subject to robust human oversight.
666: Agents that learn through trial and error pose risks of triggering failures if they lack sufficient safety boundaries during the discovery process.
667: Furthermore, extreme interaction horizons present challenges in maintaining strategic coherence and preventing the accumulation of errors. We are committed to fostering reliable autonomous systems through rigorous benchmarking that emphasizes safety and ethical alignment.
