52: Abstract
53: Most services built on powerful large-scale language models (LLMs) add citations to their output to enhance credibility. Recent research has paid increasing attention to the question of what reference documents to link to outputs. However, how LLMs recognize cite-worthiness and how this process should be controlled remains underexplored. In this study, we focus on what kinds of content LLMs currently tend to cite and how well that behavior aligns with human preferences. We construct a dataset to characterize the relationship between human citation preferences and LLM behavior. Web-derived texts are categorized into eight citation-motivation types, and pairwise citation preferences are exhaustively evaluated across all type combinations to capture fine-grained contrasts. Our results show that humans most frequently seek citations for medical text, and stronger models display a similar tendency. We also find that current models are as much as 27\% more likely than humans to add citations to text that is explicitly marked as needing citations on sources such as Wikipedia, and this overemphasis reduces alignment accuracy. Conversely, models systematically underselect numeric sentences (by -22.6\% relative to humans) and sentences containing personal names (by -20.1\% ), categories for which humans typically demand citations. Furthermore, experiments with Direct Preference Optimization demonstrate that model behavior can be calibrated to better match human citation preferences. We expect this study to provide a foundation for more fine-grained investigations into LLM citation preferences.

55: 1 Introduction
56: Large language models (LLMs) possess extensive knowledge about the world and have the potential to fundamentally transform human society.
57: In practice, powerful generative models such as the GPT series and Gemini are already deployed across a variety of downstream platforms, making the assurance of factuality and verifiability an important issue that affects many services.
58: One approach to improving the verifiability of LLM outputs is to add citations.
59: This is, through some workflow, external documents are attached to support the content generated by the LLM in the form of references.
60: Most current high-performing closed models employ this functionality, and systems such as the GPT series(OpenAI, 2025), Gemini(Comanici et al., 2025), Claude(Anthropic, 2025), Qwen(Yang et al., 2025), and Perplexity11
61: 1
62: https://www.perplexity.ai present citations to users.
63: The relationship between LLMs and citations has also drawn attention in recent research.
64: In particular, the question of which documents should be linked to a given piece of text has been actively studied alongside RAG and AI-agent technologies, yielding many results.
65: This line of work aligns well with the common LLM pipeline in which the system receives a user query, performs web search, and generates an answer based on information retrieved from the web (Google, ).
66: By contrast, the question of what information ought to receive a citation—that is, the importance of citations conditioned on the text content—has not been sufficiently investigated.
67: Such research would enable citation behavior that aligns with user preferences and, crucially, would make it possible to control citation frequency.
68: Too few citations undermine users’ ability to verify claims and erode trust (Ding et al., 2025), whereas too many citations can reduce satisfaction, harm efficiency, and lower decision accuracy (De Jong, 2010; Eppler and Mengis, 2004), thereby degrading the user experience.
69: In other words, a balance between verifiability and user experience is essential; rather than exhaustively presenting every source for a model’s output, citations should focus on the most important information.
70: In this work, we address the open questions of whether LLMs align with users’ citation preferences and whether models can be trained to do so.
71: In today’s high-performing LLM services, key decisions—such as which information to web-search and where in the output to attach citations—are often controlled by the LLM itself (Google, ; Anthropic, ).
72: Thus, if we can teach LLMs users’ citation preferences, we can adapt the citation behavior of AI-agent systems to match user needs.
73: To this end, we first conducted human annotation to investigate users’ citation preferences.
74: The data consisted of 6,000 Wikipedia sentences with quality labels, carefully annotated by Wikipedia editors.
75: We grouped the quality labels into eight categories and, for each pair of categories, annotated—both with human preferences and with LLM preferences—which side should receive a citation (pairwise comparison).
76: Our analysis showed that stronger LLMs exhibit higher alignment with human preferences.
77: We also found that for sentences labeled “Citation needed” on Wikipedia, LLMs selected them at rates up to 19.5% higher for open models and up to 27.4% higher for closed models than humans did, indicating a strong influence from training data.
78: Conversely, models systematically underselect sentences containing numbers (by up to -22.6\% relative to humans) and sentences containing personal names (by up to -20.1\% ), precisely the categories for which humans typically demand citations.
79: Next, we trained LLMs using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on the human-annotated data to align them with human preferences.
80: As a result, we achieved an improvement of 11.8% and demonstrated that the influence of training data such as Wikipedia can be mitigated.
81: Our contributions are as follows.
82: •
83: We present, to our knowledge, the first study that focuses on the need for citations within text and examines citation preferences of both humans and LLMs.
84: •
85: We construct a dataset of 6,000 sentences with human citation-preference labels across eight content-based categories.
86: •
87: Through dataset analysis, we show that LLMs are strongly influenced by their training data, which in part leads to divergences from human preferences.
88: •
89: We demonstrate that DPO can attenuate the impact of training data and bring model behavior closer to human citation preferences.
90: 2 Related Works
91: Recent work has explored many ways of combining LLMs with citations (Lála et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023).
92: Examples include datasets for self-querying RAG methods in which the model decides at generation time whether external search is needed and, if so, retrieves and integrates paragraph-level evidence (Asai et al., 2024);
93: tasks that require explicit source attribution in ambiguous QA (Shaier et al., 2024);
94: and benchmarks that evaluate sentence-level citation in long-context QA (Zhang et al., 2024a).
95: There is also a line of work that evaluates the validity of citations (Li et al., 2024)—including studies that assess citation validity in the legal domain (Zhang et al., 2024b), datasets that test whether citations in generative search substantiate answers (Liu et al., 2023), and investigations of citation appropriateness in the medical domain (Wu et al., 2025).
96: However, these efforts primarily focus on attaching an appropriate citation to a given sentence.
97: Closer to our work, there are a few studies that ask which content should receive citations.
98: These include analyses of reasons for adding citations on Wikipedia (Redi et al., 2019) and careful examinations of citation intent in scientific papers (Wright and Augenstein, 2021; Saxena et al., 2024; Cohan et al., 2019).
99: Such studies remain relatively scarce, and most target academic writing.
100: Our interest lies in whether LLMs exhibit the same citation preferences as humans—and whether they can be aligned to do so—rather than in an exhaustive characterization of human citation preferences.

170: 4 Data Creation
171: 4.1 Data Source
172: We require a collection of sentences annotated with quality labels as the data for our study.
173: We adopt Wikipedia’s Inline templates22
174: 2
175: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Inline_templates—sentence-level tags that editors apply when some aspect of the content is problematic.
176: These templates come in many varieties and are actively used across articles.
177: Because inline templates tend to be used by relatively experienced editors, they serve as reasonably reliable signals.
178: In this study, we extract target sentences from the WikiSQE dataset curated from Wikipedia (Ando et al., 2024).
179: This large-scale collection contains 3.4M sentences labeled with 153 categories.
180: Although minimal noise filtering has already been applied, we further improve quality by manually identifying and removing broken sentences during annotation.
181: 4.2 Category Grouping
182: We extract labels related to human citation preferences and reorganize them into eight categories.
183: To this end, the authors reviewed all labels, selected 19 of them, and regrouped them into the following eight categories:
184: Missing Information,
185: Sic,
186: Doubt,
187: Vague,
188: POV,
189: Medical Content,
190: Jargon,
191: and Unclear.
192: Details of the label–category mapping are shown in Table 1.
193: Missing Information indicates that required details are absent; Sic flags typographical errors; Doubt marks statements of questionable veracity; Vague indicates imprecise or ambiguous wording; POV flags non-neutral or one-sided claims; Medical Content marks health/medical statements; Jargon flags highly technical or jargon-heavy wording; and Unclear indicates text that is difficult to understand.
194: 4.3 Annotation
195: We annotated citation preferences using the collected dataset.
196: We sampled 6,000 sentences to construct the annotation set: 750 sentences were drawn uniformly from each of the eight categories and paired to form 3,000 comparison items (sentence pairs).
197: Pairs were balanced across all {8\choose 2}=28 category combinations; that is, we created approximately 107 sentence pairs per category pair.
198: During annotation, non‐sentential items were flagged, two items per batch were duplicated for quality control, and we retained only submissions that were fully consistent on the duplicated items.
199: In total, 402 participants annotated the 3,000 pairs.
200: Each pair is assigned to exactly one annotator and evaluated only once.
201: However, if a pair fails the quality check, it is released and reassigned to a different worker.
202: We identified 404 pairs containing at least one non‐sentence and removed them, yielding a final dataset of 2,596 pairs.
203: Table 2 reports the human selection rates between categories.
204: Medical content sentences won broadly across categories, most notably against Vague (75.9%) and Unclear (66.3%), indicating a strong user preference to secure verifiability for medically consequential content.
205: Unclear and Jargon were often favored or competitive, suggesting that citations are expected to serve as an “anchor of meaning” for hard‐to‐understand or highly technical sentences.
206: Moreover, Vague exceeded Missing Information at 56.9% and Unclear exceeded Missing Information at 58.0%, implying a tendency to prioritize readability and clarity with citations before filling in missing details.

290: 5 Citation Preference of LLMs
291: 5.1 Setup
292: We evaluate LLMs using the dataset we constructed to study citation preferences.
293: To cover a broad range, we include both open- and closed-source models as well as small and large models.
294: Specifically, we consider 11 models: Mistral Small, Mistral Large, Llama 1B, Llama 3B, Llama 70B, DeepSeek Chat, GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4, CommandR + , Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Qwen Max.
295: For further details, see Appendix B.
296: We attempted to collect outputs for all prompts; however, some models refused to answer certain items due to safety or political restrictions, resulting in up to three missing samples per model.
297: Moreover, open and closed LLMs may differ in how they handle citations.
298: For open LLMs, everything hinges on the training data and training strategy, whereas closed LLMs may, in addition to the LLM itself, employ various tools or agent-like procedures.
299: However, in our setting all closed LLMs are accessed via APIs that do not invoke any external components beyond the LLM itself.
300: Therefore, this concern does not apply here.
301: 5.2 Analysis of Model Preference
302: Table 3 presents the models’ citation preferences.
303: DeepSeek (62.7%) attains the highest overall performance, followed by Qwen (62.4%), Llama-70B (61.6%), Claude (61.5%), and GPT-5 (61.2%), revealing a clear parameter–scale effect.
304: This pattern is especially pronounced within the Llama family; in particular, Llama 1B sits at the random baseline, implying that at this scale it effectively fails to produce meaningful citation preferences.
305: Moreover, small LLMs are known to exhibit pronounced option–position biases in multiple-choice settings (Li and Gao, 2025), which likely contributes to this behavior.
306: Across categories, Medical shows relatively high agreement with humans, suggesting that LLMs are comparatively adept at seeking citations for medical information.
307: An interesting open question is at which stage of training this capability is acquired.
308: Nevertheless, aggregate scores plateau around 60%, indicating that current models only weakly predict human preferences and that the capability remains insufficient.
309: These results suggest that inferring cite-worthiness implicitly from pretraining text alone is highly nontrivial—even for large, well-trained models—and remains a challenging task.

555: First, we examine sentences labeled “Citation needed.”
556: On Wikipedia, this is the label editors attach to mark that a citation is required, and it is among the most frequently applied inline templates.
557: Because this label is common on Wikipedia, it is likely to appear frequently in LLM training data as well.
558: If models are not explicitly debiased with respect to citation behavior, we hypothesize that sentences bearing this label will be judged as requiring citations with high probability.
559: (Note that “Medical citation needed” is a subtype of “Citation needed,” and we include it in our analysis.)
560: The results are shown in Table 4(a).
561: With the exception of Llama 1B, all models select “Citation needed” substantially more often than humans.
562: In particular, Llama 70B and DeepSeek exceed the human rate by more than +25%.
563: This suggests a strong imprint of training-data biases that hinders alignment with human preferences.
564: By contrast, models with similarly large parameter counts such as Mistral Large and CommandR + stay within +5%, which hints that some corrective design in data or training strategy may be at play.
565: Given that Llama 1B is near the random baseline throughout our study, we do not discuss it further.
566: Numeric sentences
567: Second, we examine sentences containing numeric expressions.
568: Such sentences include dates and quantitative expressions, which demand high precision and leave little room for error.
569: When precision is required, users are expected to rely on external sources to verify factuality.
570: Indeed, prior work reports that users deem inline citations necessary for sentences containing statistics (Redi et al., 2019).
571: Accordingly, we hypothesize that quantitative expressions increase users’ demand for citations.
572: We detect numeric sentences via simple pattern matching.
573: The results are shown in Table 4(b).
574: All models exhibit lower selection rates than humans.
575: Notably, Mistral Small is 22.6% below the human rate.
576: This indicates that models do not yet adequately capture users’ citation preferences in this setting and that little specialized training for citation behavior has been conducted.
577: Meanwhile, models such as GPT-5 and Claude show near-human alignment (within -1.5\% ), again suggesting that some targeted design choices during training may contribute.
578: Person names
579: Third, we examine sentences containing personal names.
580: When people are mentioned, particular care is required regarding factuality.
581: Wikipedia explicitly flags this sensitivity 33
582: 3
583: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Biographies_of_living_persons, and biographies are tightly governed in practice.
584: Because this caution is also widely recognized socially, we hypothesize that users similarly demand citations in such contexts.
585: The results are shown in Table 4(c).
586: As with “Numeric sentences,” all models undershoot human selection rates.
587: In particular, Llama 70B is 20.1% below the human rate.
588: As before, this provides evidence that models insufficiently capture users’ citation preferences.
589: That said, Claude and CommandR + align comparatively well with human behavior, consistent with the possibility of additional targeted design.
590: A notable pattern is that most models fall below 50%, i.e., they often judge that a citation is not required in these cases.
591: 6 Aligning LLMs with Human Citation Preferences
592: Since the previous sections established that current LLMs are not aligned with users’ citation preferences, we attempt to align them by training the models.
593: If agreement with humans improves, this would constitute evidence that the models’ citation-related behavior has been aligned.
594: We consider five target models: Llama 1B, Llama 3B, Llama 70B, Mistral Small, and Mistral Large.
595: For training and testing, we split the dataset constructed in Section 4 into training and test sets of approximately equal size.
596: The split is performed so that category pairs are balanced; when an odd count occurs, the extra items are assigned to the training set.
597: In addition, we randomly sample 100 instances from the training portion as a validation set.
598: As a result, the train/validation/test sets contain 1,206, 100, and 1,288 pairs, respectively.
599: Further training details are provided in Appendix C.

661: 6.1 DPO
662: As a first approach, we perform DPO.
663: DPO is a commonly used method for preference optimization and appears to be the most suitable approach for our objective.
664: We use LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), which enables parameter-efficient training of LLMs with minimal loss in accuracy.
665: For large models, we enable DeepSpeed ZeRO-Offload (Ren et al., 2021) to offload optimizer states and gradients to CPU memory.
666: The results are shown in Table 5.
667: Overall, performance increased.
668: The gain is particularly pronounced for Llama 1B, whose agreement improves by 11.8% relative to its no-DPO counterpart.
669: Llama 3B and Mistral Large also improve by roughly 9%, indicating that DPO is effective.
670: By contrast, Mistral Small drops slightly, and Llama 70B declines by 1.6%.
671: On average, we observe a 5.76% improvement, supporting DPO as a viable strategy for teaching models citation preferences.
672: 7 Conclusion
673: We studied how LLMs decide where to add citations and how closely these decisions align with human preferences. Using 5,192 Wikipedia sentences reorganized into eight content-based categories and annotated via pairwise comparisons, we found that current models align with human citation preferences only weakly (in the low 60s on average), with systematic divergences by sentence type. In particular, models substantially overselect sentences explicitly marked “Citation needed,” while underselecting numeric and person-name sentences, both of which humans tend to treat as citation-worthy. Medical content emerges as the most consistently prioritized category across humans and models.
674: We then examined whether alignment can be improved through training. DPO yielded consistent gains, with a mean improvement of 5.76% and especially large benefits for smaller models. These results indicate that preference-based training provides an effective mechanism for calibrating cite-worthiness judgments. Furthermore, our experiments demonstrate that LLM citation preferences can be controlled via DPO.
675: Implications.
676: First, citation behavior should be explicitly trained and evaluated rather than assumed to emerge from pretraining. Second, deployment should consider category-aware routing (e.g., boosting numeric and person-name triggers) and frequency control to balance verifiability against information overload. Third, because many modern systems let the LLM implicitly govern search and attachment decisions, improving cite-worthiness alignment at the model level can directly benefit agentic pipelines.
677: Limitations and future work.
678: Our analysis is bounded by Wikipedia-derived labels and English-only annotations; extending to other domains, languages, and high-risk classes (e.g., legal and scientific claims) is important. In addition, using larger-scale annotated datasets will enable a more rigorous examination of causality. Finally, integrating cite-worthiness prediction with retrieval/reranking and evaluating end-to-end user outcomes (task success, trust, cognitive load) remain open directions. To facilitate reproducibility, we plan to release the data and code upon acceptance of the paper.
679: 8 Ethics Statement
680: The dataset we constructed includes opinions and descriptions originating from Wikipedia that may carry bias.
681: Some items may contain offensive or discriminatory language.
682: However, our objective in this study is to learn citation preferences including such real-world biases.
683: We plan to release the dataset, with clear notices at distribution time that it may contain biased content.
684: We monitored annotators’ working time and ensured that their pay always exceeded £6 per hour.
