55: Abstract
56: Large language models (LLMs) have advanced the development of personalized learning in education. However, their inherent generation mechanisms often produce homogeneous responses to identical prompts. This “one-size-fits-all” mechanism overlooks the substantial heterogeneity in students’ cognitive and psychological, thereby posing potential safety risks to vulnerable groups. Existing safety evaluations primarily rely on context-independent metrics such as factual accuracy, bias, or toxicity, which fail to capture the divergent harms that the same response might cause across different student attributes. To address this gap, we propose the concept of “Student-Tailored Personalized Safety” and construct CASTLE based on educational theories. This benchmark covers 15 educational safety risks and 14 student attributes, comprising 92,908 bilingual scenarios. We further design three evaluation metrics: Risk Sensitivity, measuring the model’s ability to detect risks; Emotional Empathy, evaluating the model’s capacity to recognize student states; and Student Alignment, assessing the match between model responses and student attributes. Experiments on 18 SOTA LLMs demonstrate that CASTLE poses a significant challenge: all models scored below an average safety rating of 2.3 out of 5, indicating substantial deficiencies in personalized safety assurance.

60: 1 Introduction
61: With the rapid advancement of Large language models (LLMs), their safety has become a critical area of research (Shi et al., 2024). Consequently, numerous methods and datasets (Mazeika et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023) have been proposed to evaluate the performance of LLMs in terms of general safety. General safety (Gehman et al., 2020; Röttger et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024) primarily focuses on common risks in model outputs, such as bias, toxicity, and factual errors, aiming to ensure basic safety and reliability across a wide range of applications. However, existing general safety evaluations tend to emphasize detecting explicit and universal harms, making it challenging to adequately address the diverse safety needs of different application settings and user populations (Souly et al., 2024; McCrae & John, 1992; Abdulhai et al., 2024). This limitation is particularly pronounced in high-risk domains such as education, where general safety approaches struggle to effectively identify and mitigate personalized safety risks arising from variations in user backgrounds.
62: As shown in Figure 1, when presented with the same query from Student 1 and Student 2, the model’s harmless and empathetic response can effectively ease the emotions of low-risk students but may trigger fatal behavior in individuals with dropout tendencies. Despite the continuous improvements in the capabilities of large language models, such personalized safety failures remain a critical blind spot in current safety research. Therefore, personalized safety has gradually become an important research focus. Unlike recent works such as PSG-Agent (Wu et al., 2025a) and PENGUIN (Wu et al., 2025b), our focus is on issues with stronger personalized high-risk characteristics within the education domain. Our goal is to establish a benchmark suitable for systematically measuring personalized safety risks in high-stakes, student-centered scenarios.

64: We propose the CASTLE benchmark to fill the gap in student-tailored personalized safety research. First, we construct a student profile comprising 14 attributes spanning cognitive, non-cognitive, and metacognitive levels, integrating classical educational psychology theories, including the Big Five personality traits, skill acquisition stage, ability belief types, and self-regulation stages. Based on this foundation and following (McCrae & John, 1992; Dweck, 2006; Fitts & Posner, 1967; Zimmerman, 2000), we establish a taxonomy of 15 instructional risk domains, including Psychological and Emotional Health, Academic Integrity and Competence, Information Bias, and Learning Dependence. Leveraging these 15 risk domains and 14 student attributes, we built a total of 92,908 bilingual test scenarios, comprising 53,483 Chinese scenarios and 39,425 English scenarios.
65: Based on the proposed CASTLE, we design three evaluation metrics to comprehensively assess model safety performance: Risk Sensitivity, which measures the model’s ability to detect risks; Emotional Empathy, which evaluates the model’s capacity to recognize student states; and Student Alignment, which assesses the match between model responses and student attributes. Systematic evaluation using these metrics reveals a critical deficiency in existing large language models: even state-of-the-art models exhibit significant safety failures in high-stakes educational settings without access to structured personalized contextual information. By exploring the interaction between student traits and AI-generated risks, CASTLE establishes a solid foundation for safety research that adapts to individual student contexts. In summary, our main contributions are as follows:

74: 2 Related Work
75: Safety evaluation of LLMs.
76: Existing general safety benchmarks for LLMs can be categorized based on their evaluation focus, primarily including explicit harm refusal, robustness to adversarial bypass, and assessments of specific safety failure modes: (1) Explicit harm refusal benchmarks detect whether models appropriately reject inappropriate requests based on predefined risk categories such as violence, self-harm, or illegal activities (Mazeika et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2023). (2) Robustness benchmarks examine whether models maintain safety constraints under adversarial prompting strategies, including prompt perturbations and role-playing attacks (Chao et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). (3) Benchmarks targeting specific failure modes, such as toxic language generation (Gehman et al., 2020) or excessive refusal and over-alignment behaviors (Röttger et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). Despite differing focuses, most existing evaluations implicitly treat safety as a global and context-independent property of models, applying uniform standards across all users (Levy et al., 2022; Bianchi et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b). Consequently, these benchmarks exhibit significant limitations in capturing personalized safety risks, which is evident in educational settings where students exhibit high heterogeneity in cognitive vulnerabilities.
77: Many researchers have developed various defense mechanisms for large language models (LLMs), organizing safety strategies into a multidimensional framework covering different stages of the model lifecycle (Shi et al., 2024). Existing mitigation methods can be broadly categorized into two types: (1) value alignment strategies, which rely on supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to guide model behavior toward human-aligned norms (Abdulhai et al., 2024; Beukeboom & Burgers, 2019); and (2) robustness-focused approaches that emphasize identifying safety vulnerabilities through red teaming and deploying external guardrails to detect and prevent attacks that bypass safety mechanisms.
78: Personalization in LLMs and personalization safety.
79: The progression of LLMs toward fine-grained personalization seeks to align model behavior with individual user characteristics (Liu et al., 2025). Recent approaches exploit in-context learning (Brown et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025b) and retrieval-augmented generation (Salemi et al., 2024) to incorporate user-specific context, while parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA and adapter tuning enable stable personalization with limited parameter updates (Hu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024a; Zhu et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2021). To accommodate pluralistic human values, alignment strategies have further evolved toward personalized RLHF and direct preference optimization (Wu et al., 2023b; Jang et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2023), supporting consistent persona expression across applications (Salemi et al., 2024; Sayana et al., 2025). However, growing evidence suggests that personalization also induces user-dependent safety risks: the PENGUIN benchmark demonstrates risk variation across user backgrounds (Wu et al., 2025b), motivating personality-aware guardrails such as PSG-Agent (Wu et al., 2025a). Despite these advances, personalized safety remains underexplored in educational contexts, where students’ psychological and developmental vulnerabilities require particular attention.
80: 3 Dataset Construction
81: In this section, we present CASTLE, a benchmark designed for systematic analysis of student-tailored personalized safety in LLMs. We begin by outlining our design logic, followed by a description of our data generation pipeline and evaluation approach. Detailed dataset examples and statistics are provided in Appendix and .
82: 3.1 Design Logic
83: CASTLE evaluates the safety of LLMs through personalized interaction scenarios tailored for students. We organize these interactions into a two-level taxonomy consisting of four categories and 15 domains (see Appendix ), focusing on areas where model outputs can significantly affect a student’s emotional state and cognitive development. Within each domain, we construct diverse scenarios, each composed of a query paired with a student attribute that describes the student’s attributes, such as age, emotional state and big five personality trait(see Appendix A.1). For example, in the [Academic Pressure Overload] domain, a scenario features the query: “I feel struggled , just thinking about school feels extremely tired.” To construct a complete evaluation scenario, we pair this query with a student attribute like age (16) and big five pesronality trait (High Neuroticism). This combination of query and profile forms a single scenario instance for evaluation. Model responses for each scenario are generated under two conditions: a Non-personalized setting with only the query, and a Personalized setting with the full student profile. By scaling this process across 92,908 scenarios, CASTLE enables a systematic, fine-grained analysis of LLM safety performance in the emotionally and cognitively sensitive educational domain.
84: Evaluated domain.
85: Building on prior work (Delikoura et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025), we propose a two-level taxonomy comprising 15 sensitive domains organized into 4 categories. As shown in Table 1, these categories are selected based on three criteria: (1) prevalence in student-AI educational interactions, (2) evidence of psychological or cognitive fragility in developmental literature (Delikoura et al., 2025; Davies et al., 2024), and (3) the high risk of negative pedagogical consequences when model responses are misaligned with a student’s profile (Jose et al., 2025; Sweller, 1988). The first category, Psychological and Emotional Health, encompasses Academic Pressure Overload (AP) (Kosmyna et al., 2025; Sweller, 1988), Career Choice Dilemma (CD) (Zheng, 2024), Interpersonal Relationship Issues (IR) (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Güner & Er, 2025), Virtual Emotional Dependence and Social Withdrawal (VE) (AlGhamdi, 2024), and AI-Induced Self-Cognitive Bias (CB) (Zhang et al., 2025a; Shi et al., 2025; Warr et al., 2025). The second category, Academic Integrity and Competence, targets Direct/Implicit Academic Misconduct (AM) (Thorp, 2023; Wu et al., 2023a), Avoiding Learning Path (LP) (Kosmyna et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025), and Assessment Questioning (AQ) (Lin & Crosthwaite, 2024). The third category, Information Bias, addresses Stereotypes/Discrimination (SD) (Warr et al., 2025; Kaufenberg-Lashua et al., 2024), Model Hallucination Risks (HR) (Davies et al., 2024), Inappropriate Content (IC) (Hernandez et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2025), and Knowledge Limitations (KL) (Kwak & Pardos, 2024; Malinka et al., 2023; Davies et al., 2024). Finally category, Learning Dependence focuses on Excessive Seeking Simplification (ES) (Davies et al., 2024; Jose et al., 2025), Loss of Independent Judgment (IJ) (Lee et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025), and Cognitive Rigidity and Innovation Suppression (RI) (Zhu et al., 2025; Jošt et al., 2024). This coverage ensures that CASTLE captures the diverse high-stakes contexts.
86: Evaluated attributes.
87: We construct student profiles using 14 attributes across four categories grounded in developmental psychology (Table 1). The Background category covers age, gender, and learning stages (primary to university) (Wu et al., 2025a). The Big Five Personality category includes Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism, each rated low, medium, or high (McCrae & John, 1992). The Emotion category captures 18 states (e.g., anger, anxiety, joy), intensity, and recent feedback (e.g., success/failure) (Weiner, 1985; Pekrun, 2006; Ahmed et al., 2010). Lastly, the Education category integrates Ability Beliefs (growth/fixed mindset) (Dweck, 2006), Skill Acquisition stages (cognitive to autonomous) (Fitts & Posner, 1967), and Self-regulated Learning phases (forethought, performance, self-reflection) (Zimmerman, 2000). All attributes are encoded in natural language to enable controlled comparisons between personalized and non-personalized responses. Attributes description detail is in Table A.1.

225: Figure 2: Overview of our dataset construction. The upper part (Stage 0) illustrates the theoretical foundation, integrating multi-domain risk and psychological theories with logic-constraint rules. The lower part (Stages 1–3) depicts the execution flow, transitioning from real-world seed enhancement to cyclic multi-LLM profile generation and personalized query synthesis.
226: Data collection and initial filtering. CASTLE is constructed by extracting educational data for ages 7–22 from existing datasets (Wu et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2025b). We refine these raw entries by imputing missing dimensions, such as Big Five personality and growth mindsets, to establish high-quality seed profiles. These profiles then serve as the templates for data expansion. Detail is in Figure 2 and Appendix .
227: Cyclic multi-LLM collaborative generation.
228: To avoid biases inherent in any single model, we implement a cyclic rotation strategy using four high-performing LLMs: GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash, DeepSeek-V3, and Claude-Haiku-4.5 (see Appendix ). By rotating these models across domains, we ensure the benchmark is not limited to a specific model’s patterns, thereby enhancing its overall robustness and coverage. Detail is in Figure 2 and Appendix .
229: Logic-constraint enforcement.
230: To ensure logical and realistic student profiles, CASTLE incorporates eight grounding rules to prevent inconsistent attribute combinations(see Appendix ). These constraints eliminate discrepancies such as age-grade mismatches (e.g., a 7-year-old in university), personality-category conflicts (e.g., low neuroticism during a mental health crisis), and mindset-scenarios (e.g., growth mindsets paired with cognitive rigidity). These rules ensure that all instances in CASTLE align with authentic human patterns. Detail is in Appendix .
231: Personalized query generation.
232: Based on validated profiles, we generate personalized queries guided by student attributes and academic domains. This design ensures that seemingly benign queries can reveal latent safety risks when contextualized within a student’s mental state and academic background, thereby enabling a more fine-grained assessment of model safety behaviors (see Appendix ).
233: Data refinement and quality assurance.
234: We refined the dataset through a three-step pipeline: (1) Language correction for bilingual (Chinese–English) consistency; (2) Semantic deduplication to remove redundant queries; and (3) Structural filtering to eliminate malformed entries. Finally, human audits verified that all queries are contextually authentic and strictly aligned with their corresponding student profiles. More details are described in Section 3.3.
235: 3.3 Evaluation Metrics and Approach
236: Evaluation metrics and scoring.
237: Following (Wu et al., 2025b), we evaluate model responses across three personalized safety dimensions: (1) Risk Sensitivity, assessing the detection of latent psychological or cognitive risks; (2) Emotional Empathy, measuring adaptation to a student’s emotional state; and (3) Student Alignment, ensuring consistency with individual profiles (e.g., personality and learning stage). Each dimension is rated on a 0–5 scale, moving beyond binary safety filters to provide a granular assessment of empathy and guidance. The final performance is determined by a weighted average of these scores, where 0 indicates harmful output and 5 reflects full alignment with a student’s specific needs and psychological safety. Detailed information is provided in the Appendix .

688: Human-AI Reliability Analysis. Given the large scale of CASTLE, which comprises thousands of instances, relying exclusively on human annotation would incur prohibitive financial and labor costs. To establish a feasible yet rigorous evaluation pipeline, we conducted a reliability analysis comparing automated evaluators against human judgment (Banerjee et al., 2025; Chao et al., 2024; Fitts & Posner, 1967; Bianchi et al., 2023; Jang et al., 2023). Ten expert annotators provided ground-truth ratings on a randomly sampled subset of 4,000 instances. We compared the performance of two leading models, GPT-4o and Claude-Haiku-4.5, against these human ratings. Reliability was quantified using Cohen’s kappa, revealing that while GPT-4o achieved a coefficient of 0.65, Claude-Haiku-4.5 attained a significantly higher agreement score of 0.72. Consequently, we adopt Claude-Haiku-4.5 as a credible proxy for human evaluators in subsequent

690: 4 Experiments
691: 4.1 Experimental Setup
692: We evaluate a total of 18 LLMs, which can be categorized into three groups.
693: 1) Open-source models.
694: The open-source models are evaluated using their officially released checkpoints, including LLaMA3-8B, Mistral-7B, Ministral-3-14B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, QwQ-32B, Qwen2.5-72B, Qwen3-235B-A22B, DeepSeek-7B, InternLM3-8B, GLM-4-9B, and ERNIE-4.5-21B-A3B-PT.
695: 2) Closed-source models.
696: Closed-source models are evaluated via their official APIs and represent widely used commercial model families, including GPT-4o and GPT-5.2 from OpenAI, Gemini-2.5-Flash from Google, and Claude-Haiku-4.5 from Anthropic.
697: 3) Education-based models.
698: Given the educational focus of our work, we further include two models specifically trained for educational domains, namely InnoSpark-7B and MuduoLLM-7B.

700: 4.2 Main Results
701: We evaluate a total of 18 LLMs across 15 domains. Table 2 reports the safety score (5-point scale) for each model under non-personalized settings, from which we derive the following key observations. More details see Appendix .
702: Overall performance is limited under non-personalized settings.
703: The results show that the safety scores for most models are concentrated within a relatively low range between 1.71 and 2.20. Even for high-performing models such as Claude-Haiku-4.5, the overall average does not exceed 2.42, while education-based models like InnoSpark-7B and MuduoLLM-7B achieve scores of only 2.21 and 2.07, respectively. This consistent performance ceiling across open-source, closed-source, and domain-specific model families suggests that existing models have limited capacity to identify and mitigate educational risks.
704: Domain Alignment Outperforms Model Scaling.
705: Evidence suggests that specialized education-based models exhibit highly competitive or even superior performance compared to much larger models. Specifically, InnoSpark-7B achieves a consolidated Chinese average safety score of 2.21, outperforming GPT-4o, GPT-5.2, and Gemini-2.5-Flash, which score 2.00, 2.00, and 2.16, respectively. This indicates that for addressing domain-specific risks, precise domain alignment may be significantly more effective than continual model scaling frameworks and strategies.
706: Models Demonstrate Consistent Safety Superiority in Chinese.
707: A consistent performance advantage is observed in the Chinese subset over the English subset across all models. Specifically, Claude-Haiku-4.5 achieves a safety score of 2.42 in the Chinese subset compared to 2.02 in the English subset. In the absence of student profiles, this disparity suggests that Chinese, as a high-context language, enables models to capture implicit risk cues with higher information density per token. Consequently, the semantic structure of Chinese may trigger comprehensive safety mechanisms and pedagogical guidance more effectively than the concise output patterns typically observed in English.
708: 4.3 Ablation Study
709: Safety gains via personalization.
710: To evaluate the impact of student profile on model behavior, we compare performance between non-personalized and personalized settings. As shown in Figure 3, adding student profiles consistently improves safety scores across all 15 risk scenarios. Specifically, scores increase from an initial range of 1.8–2.5 to above 3.0 in multiple domains, with the largest improvements observed in psychological well-being and academic integrity. These findings suggest that personal information provides essential context, helping models identify potential risks that might be missed in generic queries. However, the fact that no model achieves a perfect score in any dimension demonstrates that while student profiles improve risk awareness, they are insufficient to completely eliminate safety risks in complex educational environments.
711: Figure 4: Safety score trends across different levels of profile granularity. The safety performance across all scenarios exhibits a consistent upward trend as personalized student profiles progress from missing to implicit, and finally to explicit representation.
712: Reinforcement learning outperform scaling.
713: We investigate the performance of the Qwen series across different parameter scales under non-personalized and personalized settings. As shown in Figure 5, we observe that simply increasing the model scale leads to limited improvements in safety performance for personalized education. However, the training paradigm plays a more important role: at the same parameter scale, models optimized with RL show much better performance. Specifically, QwQ-32B significantly outperforms its instruction-tuned counterpart, Qwen2.5-32B, across all dimensions. Furthermore, RL-based models are better at using user-specific information. For instance, QwQ-32B shows a performance increase of 2.06 after receiving personalized information, while the standard Qwen2.5-32B only improves by 1.14. These findings suggest that advanced reasoning through RL, rather than parameter count, is a more effective way to improve safety and adaptability in personalized educational scenarios.

As shown in Figure 6, with personal student profile consistently improves safety performance. Among individual attributes, recent feedback yields the largest gain, increasing the safety score from 2.18 to 3.90. At the category level, emotional and educational attributes contribute the most substantial improvements, indicating that these forms of student information play a key role in risk mitigation. 718: Explicitly personalized queries outperform implicit ones.
719: To evaluate how the personal information affects safety, we compare three configurations: non-personalized which includes only the query, implicit-personalized which includes indirect hints, and explicit-personalized which includes direct profiles. As shown in Figure 4, safety performance improves as the level of personalization increases. Non-personalized queries yield the lowest scores, showing that models struggle to detect risks without context. While implicit-personalized queries lead to a score increase, the highest safety levels are consistently achieved with explicit-personalized queries. This upward trend demonstrates that providing more personalized information helps models better infer risks. More experiment results see Appendix .
720: 5 Conclusion
721: In this paper, we introduced CASTLE, a benchmark to evaluate LLMs personal safety for student. Our tests on 18 LLMs across 15 domains show that many models fail to identify personalized risks. This reveals that current safety methods often ignore the student’s information. We show that a response can look safe under general rules but still be risky when a student’s emotional and learning needs are ignored. However, CASTLE has some limitations. It focuses on single-turn queries, which may not fully capture how risks change in a long, multi-turn conversation. In the future, we plan to expand the benchmark to include interactive dialogue and multi-turn educational scenarios.
722: Impact Statement
723: This work highlights the importance of student-tailored personalized safet in educational applications of LLMs. Our findings suggest that the potential impact of an AI-generated response cannot be fully assessed without considering the student’s emotional state, cognitive characteristics, and educational context.
724: The proposed benchmark can help identify safety gaps that disproportionately affect vulnerable students, such as minors or students experiencing emotional distress, thereby supporting the development of more responsible educational AI systems. At the same time, incorporating student-related attributes raises concerns about privacy, profiling, and misuse if applied in real-world systems without appropriate safeguards. We stress that CASTLE is designed solely for evaluation and research purposes, and its use should be accompanied by careful oversight. Overall, this work aims to encourage more nuanced discussions and evaluations of safety in student-facing AI systems.
