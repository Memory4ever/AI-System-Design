The safety of large language models (LLMs) has
increasingly emerged as a fundamental aspect of
their development. Existing safety alignment for
LLMs is predominantly achieved through posttraining methods, which are computationally expensive and often fail to generalize well across
different models. A small number of lightweight
alignment approaches either rely heavily on priorcomputed safety injections or depend excessively
on the model’s own capabilities, resulting in limited generalization and degraded efficiency and
usability during generation.

Most existing work formulates this challenge as an alignment problem, aiming to align model behavior with human
values, usage norms, and safety boundaries. The dominant
solutions rely on post-training paradigms, such as supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning-based methods,
which explicitly encode safety into model parameters (Wang
et al., 2024c). These approaches have proven effective in
practice, particularly in reducing overtly harmful behaviors (e.g., RLHF and DPO) (Sharma et al., 2025b; Rafailov
et al., 2023). However, post-training alignment is computationally expensive, heavily dependent on large-scale,
carefully curated human preference data, and typically requires full or partial fine-tuning for each target model or
checkpoint (Wang et al., 2024a; Lin et al., 2024). As a result,
alignment becomes tightly coupled with costly retraining
pipelines, limiting scalability and hindering efficient reuse
across model families (Ding & Wang, 2025; Li et al., 2024a).

In this work, we propose a safety-aware decoding method that requires only low-cost training
of an expert model and employs a single neuron as a gating mechanism. By effectively balancing the model’s intrinsic capabilities with
external guidance, our approach simultaneously
preserves utility and enhances output safety. It
demonstrates clear advantages in training overhead and generalization across model scales, offering a new perspective on lightweight alignment
for the safe and practical deployment of large

2.1. Jailbreak Attack
Jailbreak attacks aim to bypass the safety alignment mechanisms of large language models to elicit unsafe or harmful
outputs. Early studies mainly rely on manually crafted adversarial prompts that exploit conflicts between safety and
helpfulness objectives or reveal generalization failures under
adversarial input distributions (Mowshowitz, 2022; Zeng
et al., 2024). While effective, such approaches are limited
in scalability. More recent work shifts toward automated,
optimization-based jailbreak generation. Gradient-based
methods, such as GCG (Zou et al., 2023), optimize adversarial prefixes to identify trigger tokens that induce unsafe
responses, whereas search- or evolution-based approaches
like AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2023) explore the discrete prompt
space without gradient access. In parallel, red-teaming
frameworks introduce auxiliary language models to iteratively generate and refine jailbreak prompts through multiround interactions, as exemplified by PAIR (Chao et al.,
2025) and GPTFuzzer (Yu et al., 2023). These advances motivate the need for alignment methods specifically designed
to defend against increasingly automated jailbreak attacks.

Building on this insight, we propose a lightweight Neuron
Guided Safe Decoding (termed NGSD) that explicitly couples intrinsic model awareness with minimal external control. Our approach requires training only the smallest model
in a model family as a safety expert, substantially reducing
alignment cost while enabling efficient transfer to larger,
related models. At the core of our method, we introduce a
single-neuron-based gating mechanism that exposes the
model’s internal risk signals and adaptively regulates safety
intervention during decoding. As a result, safety control
is selectively activated only when elevated risk is detected,
rather than being uniformly imposed. This design preserves
model utility on benign inputs while maintaining robust
safety behavior, achieving an effective balance between intrinsic model capability and external guidance. The main
contributions of this work are threefold:

2.2. Decoding-time Safety Methods
Early alignment methods predominantly operate at the
post-training stage, where model weights are fine-tuned
on safety datasets aligned with human preferences. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) combines supervised finetuning with reinforcement learning from human feedback,
while later approaches such as DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023),
PITA (Bobbili et al., 2025), and DRPO (Singla et al., 2024)
reduce annotation dependence or introduce preferencebased and prompt-aware optimization. Despite their effectiveness, these methods incur high training costs that
scale with model size and require model-specific alignment,
limiting generalization.

• NGSD introduces a lightweight decoding-time safety

• NGSD proposes a single-neuron-based gating mechanism that explicitly couples the model’s intrinsic risk

still exhibit limited cross-model generalization.

3.1. Safety-Aware Decoding

4. Methodology

As formulated in Eq. 1, SafeDecoding (Xu et al., 2024)
ensures output safety by jointly decoding a base model
and a safety expert at inference time. The safety expert
is obtained via LoRA-based fine-tuning of the base model
and therefore shares the same architecture and model scale.
At each decoding step t, the conditional distribution over
the next token yt is adjusted by linearly interpolating the
predictions of the base model pb and the expert model pe .
The interpolation coefficient α controls the strength of the
safety guidance.

We propose Neuron-Guided Safe Decoding (NGSD), a
lightweight inference-time alignment framework (Fig. 1).
NGSD aims to provide robust safety guarantees under unsafe inputs while preserving the base model’s utility capability on the benign inputs, without modifying model
parameters or introducing significant inference-time overhead.
Before decoding, NGSD performs a one-time prompt-level
self-reflection, in which the base model evaluates the input
prompt along multiple risk dimensions and the algorithm
selects a fixed safety guidance strength for the generation
process. This design allows the method to adapt safety
intensity to the input risk level. During decoding, NGSD
employs a neuron-guided gating mechanism to monitor
the distributional discrepancy between the base model and
a lightweight safety expert, accumulating this signal over
time and triggering external safety intervention only when
sustained risk is detected. When the gate is activated, NGSD
applies SafeDecoding-style logit correction at the current
decoding step to combine predictions from the base and
expert models; otherwise, decoding proceeds using the base
model alone. Through this selective intervention strategy,
NGSD enforces safety constraints at high-risk positions
while minimizing interference with normal generation.

Decoding (SSD) (Wang et al., 2025b) introduces a

adjustments on token log-probabilities within the selected
token set. Furthermore, SSD leverages historical decoding
signals to periodically adapt the coefficient α and dynamically switch between different decoding modes, achieving a
better balance among safety, efficiency, and utility.

4.3. Prompt-Level Self-Reflection
As described in Sec. 4.1, NGSD jointly balances and leverages internal and external signals to assess risk and orchestrate effective defense actions. In this subsection, we focus
on how the strength of the safety guidance α–which determines the influence of the safety expert when intervention
is triggered–is selected prior to decoding. Larger values
of α enforce stronger safety constraints but may degrade
generation quality, while smaller values better preserve the
base model’s utility at the cost of weaker safety guarantees.
Selecting an appropriate value of α is therefore critical to
balancing safety and utility in decoding-time alignment.

5. Experiment
5.1. Experiment Setup
Target & Expert Models. We consider two representative model families, Llama and Qwen3. For Llama, we
evaluate Llama-2–based targets (Llama-2-7B/13B-chat-hf
and Vicuna-7B-v1.5)(Touvron et al., 2023; Chiang et al.,
2023) and a Llama-3–based target (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct)
(Dubey et al., 2024), and further include Qwen3-8B to
test robustness beyond the Llama backbone (Yang et al.,
2025). Following a lightweight, family-consistent protocol,
Llama-2 targets share TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0 as the expert (Zhang et al., 2024), while Llama-3 and Qwen3 targets
use the smallest available models in-family (Llama3.2-1BInstruct and Qwen3-1B). Crucially, our expert setup is identical to SSD, isolating gains to the decoding algorithm rather
than expert design, training, or data. All experts are trained
with DeepAlign (Qi et al., 2024) on the corresponding Safety
Augmentation datasets.

On weakly or moderately aligned models such as Vicuna
and Llama2 (Appendix B.3), self-reflection–based methods
exhibit clear performance limitations, as they rely heavily
on the model’s intrinsic safety awareness, which is insufficient under high-risk or adversarial inputs. In contrast,
our approach introduces explicit and selective external control, enabling robust safety enforcement beyond the model’s
internal capabilities. For highly aligned models such as
Qwen3, baseline safety performance is already near saturation across multiple attack scenarios, placing these
settings in a clear marginal-gain regime where additional
decoding-time interventions yield limited observable improvements. This behavior reflects a ceiling effect rather
than a limitation of our method, which remains effective as
a low-interference safety fallback. Overall, our approach
demonstrates strong robustness and transferability across
models with diverse alignment characteristics.

7. Conclusion
This paper presents NGSD for improving the safety of large
language models through selective and adaptive alignment.
Instead of enforcing safety constraints uniformly during generation, the proposed approach applies minimal intervention
only when potential risks are detected, aiming to preserve
the model’s general capabilities while enhancing safety.

6.2. Neuron as a Better Gating Mechanism
We aim to design a gating mechanism that identifies appropriate token positions for external safety intervention.
A lightweight classifier is a natural choice (Jacob et al.,
2024; Sharma et al., 2025a), but it discards historical context and requires costly token-level supervision, limiting
cross-model transferability. Traditional gates such as EMA
or a second-order momentum–based gating (SMG) methods, while effective in other domains, fail to capture the
fine-grained and context-dependent dynamics required at
the token level.

Experimental results across different models and evaluation settings demonstrate that this strategy consistently reduces unsafe behaviors without significantly degrading
performance on benign tasks. The framework also exhibits robustness to a range of attack scenarios and shows
encouraging generalization across model scales, indicating
that effective safety signals can be learned and transferred
with limited overhead.

We therefore adopt a nonlinear biological neuron-inspired
gating mechanism. Although it introduces several hyperparameters, they are discrete, robust, and admit universal
default values. More importantly, neuron dynamics preserve
historical information while responding rapidly to strong
transient stimuli, whereas conventional gates typically exhibit delayed or attenuated responses (Gu et al., 2020; Vecoven et al., 2021). After removing Self-Reflection, we
evaluate three different gating mechanisms on Vicuna under
identical inputs, measuring the distributional discrepancy
between the expert model and the base model using the
ℓ1 -norm. As shown in Tab.5, the neuron-based gate exhibits strong robustness to threshold selection and maintains
consistently low discrepancy scores and ASR even under
high-threshold settings. This behavior indicates its ability
to generate rapid spike responses to transient distributional
deviations (see Appendix.C.1 for details), highlighting a
clear advantage of neuron-based gating over conventional

Overall, our study suggests that safety alignment does not
necessarily require extensive retraining or continuous control. Lightweight and targeted mechanisms can provide
meaningful safety improvements while remaining efficient and scalable, offering a practical direction for deploying safer large language models.

Limitations and Future Work
This work evaluates selective alignment primarily under
controlled safety benchmarks, and its effectiveness in more
open-ended or long-horizon settings remains unclear. Moreover, while the method transfers within a model family,
differences in tokenization limit its generalization across
families. Future work may explore adaptive risk estimation and more general safety alignment strategies, including
cross-family evaluation under diverse generation regimes.

A. Over Alignment
A.1. Decoding Dynamics under Over-Alignment
We observe a consistent form of over-alignment across two classes of inference-time defense mechanisms: injection-based
alignment and token-level logit manipulation. In both cases, models exhibit degenerate generation behaviors, including
repetitive low-information outputs or the repeated emission of semantically equivalent rejection statements, even when
inputs do not strictly violate safety constraints. Notably, this form of over-alignment manifests not only as a degradation
in task utility, but also as a failure to produce well-formed and informative refusals when encountering genuinely risky
inputs (Bai et al., 2022; Ganguli et al., 2022; Welleck et al., 2019).
We attribute this behavior to the fact that both defenses directly intervene in the decoding process, thereby deforming
the effective decision boundary in the token probability space. Injection-based methods introduce strong priors toward
safety-related sequences via contextual prompts, while logit-level adjustments explicitly suppress large regions of the
vocabulary and concentrate probability mass on a small set of high-frequency refusal tokens. As a result, probability mass
collapses onto locally optimal trajectories that are safe in appearance but semantically unproductive.
Importantly, such boundary deformation not only restricts content generation but also disrupts termination dynamics. By
systematically altering the relative likelihood between continuation tokens and the termination token (〈EOS〉), the model
often fails to naturally decode 〈EOS〉 and instead enters a low-entropy attractor characterized by repetitive, safety-aligned
outputs. Consequently, even in cases where refusal is appropriate, the model may fail to terminate with a concise and
coherent rejection, suggesting that this form of over-alignment arises from structural interference with decoding dynamics
rather than purely semantic misalignment.

see Appendix. A.2 This module does not affect model efficiency, task utility, or safety, and is solely designed to improve

B. Algorithm Design & Experiment
B.1. Self Reflection Template
We assess the safety risk of each user request along four complementary dimensions, each capturing a distinct aspect
of potential real-world harm. Together, these dimensions provide a structured and interpretable risk representation for
downstream safety control.
The severity score captures the inherent danger or sensitivity of the core domain involved in a user request. It reflects
how harmful the underlying activity could be if carried out in the real world, independent of narrative framing or virtual
settings. Requests associated with domains such as physical violence, weapons, terrorism, large-scale crime, or biological
and chemical threats are assigned higher severity, whereas everyday and benign topics receive low scores.
The actionability score measures the extent to which a request seeks concrete, operational, or directly executable guidance.
This dimension focuses on whether the request includes or implies step-by-step instructions, procedures, parameters, tools,
or other actionable details that could enable real-world execution. High actionability indicates that the information could be
readily acted upon with minimal additional inference.
The evasion score reflects attempts to bypass or weaken safety constraints through prompt engineering strategies. This
includes reframing harmful requests as hypothetical, fictional, educational, or research-oriented, as well as the use of
role-playing or expert personas to legitimize unsafe behavior. Higher evasion scores indicate stronger signals of intentional
boundary probing or circumvention.
The targeting score evaluates the degree of real-world specificity present in a request. It accounts for whether the request
references identifiable individuals or groups, concrete locations, times, or environments, or includes details that substantially
increase real-world feasibility. Requests with explicit or realistic targets receive higher scores, while abstract or non-specific
prompts are assigned lower values.

B.2. Rationale for Risk Dimensions and Aggregation
We emphasize that the four risk dimensions considered in our framework are not optimized for any specific attack category
or threat pattern. Instead, they are designed to capture objective and broadly applicable attributes of risk, which are shared
across a wide range of potentially harmful inputs. Each dimension reflects a distinct aspect of how risk may arise, allowing
the model to reason about safety in a structured and attack-agnostic manner, rather than relying on predefined heuristics or
scenario-specific rules.
At the conceptual level, the proposed dimensions are intentionally designed to be semantically distinct and approximately
orthogonal. In particular, Severity and Actionability characterize fundamentally different properties of an input. Severity
measures the intrinsic sensitivity of the behavior itself, independent of how the request is framed or whether it can be readily
executed, whereas Actionability captures the extent to which the input provides concrete, executable details. As a result,
highly severe behaviors are not necessarily actionable, and highly actionable requests may correspond to relatively low-risk
behaviors. Explicitly separating these dimensions prevents the conflation of risk magnitude with feasibility and improves
the resolution of risk assessment.
In the final risk aggregation, we assign primary importance to Severity, as it directly reflects the inherent risk level of
the underlying behavior and effectively determines an upper bound on potential harm. Even in the absence of explicit
instructions, targeting information, or evasion signals, inputs involving intrinsically high-risk behaviors should not be
assigned a low overall risk score. Accordingly, Severity is treated as a dominant component in the computation of the final
risk.
For the remaining three dimensions, rather than aggregating all signals uniformly, we consider only the top-two highest
scores on a per-sample basis. This design choice is motivated by the observation that factors which materially amplify risk
tend to concentrate in a small subset of dimensions, while the others often introduce weak or noisy signals. By restricting
aggregation to the top-2 dimensions, we reduce the influence of incidental fluctuations and improve the robustness of the
final risk estimate, without sacrificing sensitivity to strong risk-inducing cues.
Overall, the four-dimensional scoring framework and its corresponding aggregation rule are not heuristic constructions
tailored to particular attacks, but instead reflect principled design choices aimed at generalizability, orthogonality, and
robustness. This structure enables consistent risk estimation across diverse safety scenarios while maintaining interpretability
and stability in the presence of heterogeneous inputs.

We further analyze different distributional discrepancy measures from a methodological perspective. Compared to
Jensen–Shannon divergence and cosine similarity, the L1-norm is better suited for neuron-level gating, as it operates
directly on neuron-wise activation differences without logarithmic, trigonometric, or normalization operations. This reduces
computational overhead while avoiding numerical uncertainty and excessive smoothing of decision boundaries. As a result,
the L1-norm better aligns with selective expert intervention based on salient neuron-level changes.
