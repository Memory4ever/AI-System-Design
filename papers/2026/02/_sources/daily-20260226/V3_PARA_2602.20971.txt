abstract1.1 | (Bubeck and Sellke, 2021) propose the connection between the law of robustness and robust generalization error as a fantastic open problem. The Law of Robustness states that overparameterization is necessary for models to interpolate robustly, i.e., the function is required to be Lipschitz. Robust generalization asks if small robust training loss implies small robust test loss. We solve this open problem, explicitly connecting the two for arbitrary data distributions. Specifically, we propose a nontrivial robust generalization error and convert it into a lower bound on the expected Rademacher complexity of the induced robust loss class. Our bounds recover the \Omega(n^{1/d}) regime of (Wu et al., 2023) and show that, up to constants, robust generalization does not change the order of the Lipschitz constant required for smooth interpolation. We conduct experiments to probe the predicted scaling with dataset size and model capacity – essentially testing whether real-world results are closer to (what is predicted by) (Bubeck and Sellke, 2021) or (Wu et al., 2023), and we find that for MNIST the lower bound Lipschitz Constant is of the order predicted by (Wu et al., 2023). In simple terms, for robust generalization error to be low, we find that the Lipschitz Constant has to be in a certain range, which we theoretically bound; and the amount of perturbation allowed is linked to the order of the Lipschitz Constant.
S1.p1.1 | Deep neural networks have demonstrated exceptional performance across different machine learning applications.(LeCun et al., 2015) One of the fundamental methodologies of deep learning involves overparameterized models. These networks are highly expressive and are often easy to optimize to (near) zero training error with SGD. (Mingard et al., 2025) In many settings, making the model even larger can improve test performance beyond the interpolation threshold, a phenomenon also known as double descent.(Nakkiran et al., 2019). (Bubeck et al., 2021) provide a theoretical explanation for why overparameterization is necessary. They introduce a sharp law of robustness for two layer neural networks showing that achieving robust interpolation characterized by a small Lipschitz constant requires more parameters than interpolation alone. (Bubeck and Sellke, 2021) then prove a universal version under isoperimetric covariate distributions, showing that smooth interpolation for O(1) Lipschitz requires p\gg nd parameters. Furthermore, (Wu et al., 2023) extend the theory beyond isoperimetry, providing robust-interpolation lower bounds for arbitrary bounded-support distribution.
S1.p2.1 | Despite their remarkable success, deep networks remain vulnerable to subtle perturbations, as revealed through the existence of adversarial samples. (Goodfellow et al., 2015) The most common mitigation strategy is adversarial training, where models are trained on adversarially perturbed examples to improve robustness. (Shafahi et al., 2019) Furthermore, methods such as margin-based measures, flatness-based measures, and gradient-based measures have also been utilized to improve adverserial robustness. (Scaman and Virmaux, 2019) Another line of work pursues certified robustness by constraining the Lipschitz constant of the model, which provides formal guarantees on the maximum possible perturbation impact. (Kim et al., 2023)

This is what Bubeck and Selke in their 2021 paper refer to as a "fantastic open problem." In this work, we solve it and make the following contributions:

S2.SS1.p1.1 | Modern neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial examples, a phenomenon first identified by (Szegedy et al., 2013; Goodfellow et al., 2014) The most widely used defense is adversarial training, which minimizes a worst-case objective over bounded perturbations (Madry et al., 2018), with principled variants such as TRADES making explicit the trade-off between standard and robust accuracy (Zhang et al., 2019). Furthermore, parameter-free evaluation suites such as AutoAttack are commonly used for reliable comparison due to robustness being highly sensitive to the threat model (Croce and Hein, 2020).
S2.SS1.p2.1 | Another line of work deals certified robustness by controlling (global or local) Lipschitz. Bounding a model’s Lipschitz constant yields worst-case guarantees on output variation under input perturbations, motivating Lipschitz regularization and Lipschitz-constrained architectures. Examples such as Parseval networks (Cissé et al., 2017), Lipschitz-margin training for scalable certification (Tsuzuku et al., 2018), and more recent work emphasizing expressive Lipschitz networks for improved certified robustness (Zhang et al., 2022).
S2.SS1.p3.1 | However, computing tight global Lipschitz constants for deep neural networks is difficult, so much work has focused on computable bounds and approximations. CLEVER relates robustness evaluation to local Lipschitz estimation (Weng et al., 2018), while optimization-based methods provide tighter upper bounds for neural networks (Fazlyab et al., 2019). Moreover,(Zühlke and Kudenko, 2025) provides a comprehensive survey unifying Lipschitz constants, adversarial attacks, robustness guarantees, and estimation methods.
S2.SS2.p1.1 | Recent theory connects model size and smooth interpolation through lower bounds on the Lipschitz constant of interpolating predictors. Bubeck, Li, and Nagaraj formulate and prove a sharp trade-off for two-layer networks, suggesting that overparameterization is necessary to achieve small Lipschitz constant while fitting the data (Bubeck et al., 2021). Bubeck and Sellke establish a universal version under isoperimetric-type covariate distributions, showing that smooth interpolation can require substantially more parameters than interpolation alone (Bubeck and Sellke, 2021). Wu, Huang, and Zhang extend this direction beyond isoperimetry and derive distribution-free robust-interpolation lower bounds, including an \Omega(n^{1/d}) regime for general bounded-support distributions (Wu et al., 2023). More recently, Das, Batra, and Srivastava generalize universal-law-type results beyond squared loss to a broad family of Bregman divergence losses (covering common classification losses) (Das et al., 2025), and related work also studies robustness laws under weight-bounded classes (Husain and Balle, 2021).
S2.SS3.p1.1 | A persistent challenge in adversarial robustness is robust generalization, where robust training error can be low while test error remains high. It is known that robust learning can require significantly more labeled data than standard learning, even in simple data models (Schmidt et al., 2018). (Carmon et al., 2019; Zhai et al., 2019). It has also been shown by (Yin et al., 2019) that robust generalization bounds exhibit unavoidable dimension dependence unless additional structure is imposed.
S2.SS3.p2.1 | A closely related phenomenon is robust overfitting: continued adversarial training can improve robust training loss while degrading robust test performance. Rice, Wong, and Kolter document robust overfitting across datasets and show that early stopping can match many improvements attributed to algorithmic tweaks (Rice et al., 2020). Subsequent work proposes mechanisms to mitigate robust overfitting via learned smoothing regularization (Chen et al., 2021) or by analyzing which subsets of adversarial examples drive overfitting and modifying training to counteract it (Yu et al., 2022).
S2.SS3.p3.1 | Morever, it has recently been shown why why robust generalization is intrinsically challenging from an expressive power perspective (Li et al., 2022).
S2.SS3.p4.1 | Finally, several recent papers develop stability-based and training-dynamics-based explanations for robust generalization behavior (Zhang et al., 2024a) (Cheng et al., 2024)(Zhang et al., 2024b). Additionally, recent work further formalizes the clean-generalization vs. robust-overfitting dichotomy through representation complexity and phase-transition analyses (Li and Li, 2025).

S9.p1.1 | We aim to empirically evaluate which of the proposed scaling laws better describes the behavior observed in real-world datasets. In particular, we compare the following candidate relationships between dataset size n , parameter count p , and effective dimension d : L(n,p)\propto\sqrt{\frac{nd}{p}} as proposed in (Bubeck and Sellke, 2021), and L(n,p)\propto n^{1/d} as suggested in (Wu et al., 2023). Directly computing global Lipschitz constants for deep neural networks is intractable in practice Scaman and Virmaux (2019). Consequently, our goal is to measure a data-dependent empirical lower bound and study how this quantity scales with n and p .
S9.SS1.p1.1 | We perform experiments on the MNIST (Deng, 2012) handwritten digit dataset. Our primary motivation for selecting MNIST is consistency with the original experimental setting used in (Bubeck and Sellke, 2021). Using the same dataset allows for more direct comparison between observed empirical scaling behavior and previously proposed theoretical bounds.
S9.SS1.p2.1 | Additionally, MNIST is one of the few standard vision datasets for which the effective intrinsic dimension is relatively well understood. While MNIST images lie in a 784-dimensional ambient pixel space, the data is known to concentrate on a significantly lower-dimensional manifold due to strong spatial correlations and structured class semantics (Li et al., 2018).
S9.SS1.p3.1 | Following prior empirical Lipschitz estimation work (Fazlyab et al., 2023), we therefore fix the effective dimension to d = 10. The dataset is loaded once and reused across experiments. For each experimental configuration, we construct deterministic subsets of size n using seeded random sampling without replacement. This ensures full reproducibility across runs while maintaining statistical independence across experimental configurations.
S9.SS2.p1.1 | To study the joint scaling of Lipschitz behavior with dataset size n and model capacity, we construct an experimental setup in which these two quantities can be varied independently while keeping all other factors fixed. In particular, we control model capacity through the number of trainable parameters p , while maintaining a fixed architectural structure to preserve inductive bias.
S9.SS2.p2.1 | We adopt a convolutional architecture in which the feature extraction layers are held constant across all experiments, and only the width of the fully connected layer is varied. This design ensures that low-level representations remain stable.

S9.SS3.p1.1 | Rather than training for a fixed number of epochs, models are trained until they enter a consistent overfitting regime. This design choice is motivated by our fundamental assumption of smooth interpolation.
S9.SS3.p2.1 | We therefore define overfitting using an early stopping rule. Let L_{\text{test}}^{(t)} denote test loss at epoch t . We track the best test loss observed so far and count consecutive epochs without improvement. Training terminates when test loss fails to improve for 10 consecutive epochs. This ensures models have entered an overfitting phase. Training uses Adam optimization, learning rate 10^{-3} , cross-entropy loss, and batch size 128.
S9.SS4.p1.1 | Following training, we compute a finite-sample empirical Lipschitz lower bound over the training dataset. This follows the methodology proposed in (Huang et al., 2024), which estimates realized sensitivity along observed data directions.
S9.SS4.SSS1.p1.1 | For all pairs of training samples x_{i},x_{j} , we compute class-wise local slopes: L_{i,j,c}=\frac{|\tilde{f}_{c}(x_{i})-\tilde{f}_{c}(x_{j})|}{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}}. For each class we compute the maximum observed slope: L_{c}=\max_{i,j}L_{i,j,c}. The empirical Lipschitz estimate is then L_{\mathrm{emp}}=\max_{c}L_{c}.
S9.SS4.SSS1.p2.1 | This quantity constitutes a lower bound on the global Lipschitz constant because it is restricted to observed sample directions rather than worst-case adversarial directions.
S9.SS4.SSS1.p3.1 | The computation is exact over the dataset and scales as O(n^{2}) , which motivates restricting experiments to n\leq 10^{4} .

S9.SS5.p3.1 | In particular, for these low-capacity models, the network outputs (logits) appear to be predominantly negative across certain subsets of the data. After applying the transformation \tilde{f}(x)=\tanh(f(x)) , this leads to outputs saturating near -1 . S9.SS5.p4.1 | To quantify scaling behavior, we assume a power law relationship of the form L=C\cdot n^{\alpha}\cdot p^{\beta} , where C is a constant, and \alpha,\beta capture the dependence on dataset size and model capacity respectively. Taking logarithms, this becomes \log L=\log C+\alpha\log n+\beta\log p , which allows linear estimation of the exponents.
S9.SS5.SSS1.p1.1 | We first estimate scaling exponents by isolating each variable independently. For fixed p , we fit L\propto n^{\alpha_{p}} , yielding one estimate \alpha_{p} per value of p . For fixed n , we fit L\propto p^{\beta_{n}} , yielding one estimate \beta_{n} per value of n . Since we have 10 values each of n and p , this produces 10 estimates of \alpha and 10 estimates of \beta . We then aggregate these via averaging: \alpha=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{p}\alpha_{p} and \beta=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{n}\beta_{n} . This approach allows us to verify whether scaling behavior is consistent across different slices of the experimental grid. Averaging across fixed- p and fixed- n fits yields \alpha\approx 0.163 and \beta\approx 0.028 .
S9.SS5.SSS2.p1.1 | As a complementary approach, we estimate the scaling exponents jointly using multiple linear regression in log-space. Specifically, we fit the model \log L=\log C+\alpha\log n+\beta\log p across all 100 data points simultaneously. This method leverages the full dataset and provides a global estimate of the scaling exponents \alpha and \beta . Fitting the joint model via ordinary least squares yields \alpha=0.1585\pm 0.027 and \beta=0.0301\pm 0.012 , with corresponding 95% confidence intervals \alpha\in[0.105,0.212] and \beta\in[0.006,0.055] . Both coefficients are statistically significant ( p<0.05 ). The regression achieves R^{2}=0.308 .
S9.SS5.SSS3.p1.1 | Combining both methods, our estimate for the growth of the Lipschitz constant is L_{\mathrm{emp}}\approx C\cdot n^{0.16}\cdot p^{0.03} .
S9.SS5.SSS3.p2.1 | We now compare this empirical scaling with Wu et al. (2023) and Bubeck et al. (2021): Bubeck-style: L\propto\sqrt{\frac{nd}{p}}\sim n^{0.5}p^{-0.5} ; Wu-style: L\propto n^{1/d}=n^{0.1} (for d=10 ). Our findings show that the dependence on n ( \alpha\approx 0.16 ) is much closer to the Wu-style prediction ( 0.1 ) than the Bubeck-style scaling ( 0.5 ), while the dependence on p is negligible and slightly positive ( \beta\approx 0.03 ), in stark contrast to the strong negative dependence ( -0.5 ) predicted by Bubeck-style bounds.

We also want to generalize our approach to Bregman Divergence losses. As seen in the experiments, there is a clear discrepancy: we perform classification with cross-entropy loss, while our theoretical results are derived for square losses. We also aim to improve the bounding of loss using local Lipschitz notions, thereby strengthening our bounds.
