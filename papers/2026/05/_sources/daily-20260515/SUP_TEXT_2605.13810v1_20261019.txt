 Abstract

 
 Vector quantization via random projection followed by scalar quantization is a fundamental primitive in machine learning, with applications ranging from similarity search to federated learning and KV cache compression. While dense random rotations yield clean theoretical guarantees, they require \Theta(d^{2}) time. The randomized Hadamard transform HD reduces this cost to O(d\log d) , but its discrete structure complicates analysis and leads to weaker or purely empirical compression guarantees.

 
 In this work, we study a variant of this approach: dithered quantization with a single randomized Hadamard transform. Specifically, the quantizer applies HD to the input vector and subtracts a random scalar offset before quantizing, injecting additional randomness at negligible cost. We prove that this approach is unbiased and provides mean squared error bounds that asymptotically match those achievable with
truly random rotation matrices. In particular, we prove that a dithered version of TurboQuant achieves mean squared error \bigl(\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)\bigr)\cdot 4^{-b} at b bits per coordinate, where the o(1) term vanishes uniformly over all unit vectors and all dimensions as the number of quantization levels grows.

 


 
 1 Introduction


 
 Vector quantization is a fundamental technique for compressing real-valued vectors in \mathbb{R}^{d} into compact binary representations. Given a vector x\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , a quantizer VectorQuant maps x to a sequence of b\cdot d bits \textsc{VectorQuant}(x) . The bits can then be input to another procedure VectorDequant , obtaining the decoded vector \widetilde{x}=\textsc{VectorDequant}(\textsc{VectorQuant}(x)) . The size b\cdot d of the compressed representation controls the distortion \|x-\widetilde{x}\|^{2} , or the expected distortion E[\|x-\widetilde{x}\|^{2}] if the procedures are randomized. Alternatively, instead of decoding \widetilde{x} , some function of it (e.g., the distance to another vector y ) is estimated.
This primitive plays an important role in many areas of machine learning, including similarity search  Charikar (2002) ; Jegou et al. (2010) ; Datar et al. (2004) ; Gao and Long (2024) ; Gao et al. (2025) , federated learning  Vargaftik et al. (2022) ; Vargaftik et al. (2021) ; Ben-Basat et al. (2024) , efficient transformers  Zandieh et al. (2025) , and model compression  Gholami et al. (2022) ; Ashkboos et al. (2024) ; Tseng et al. (2024) .



 
 A popular and well-studied approach to vector quantization is random projection followed by scalar quantization Charikar (2002) ; Datar et al. (2004) ; Dong et al. (2008) ; Andoni et al. (2015) ; Vargaftik et al. (2022) ; Vargaftik et al. (2021) ; Gao and Long (2024) ; Ashkboos et al. (2024) ; Tseng et al. (2024) ; Gao et al. (2025) ; Zandieh et al. (2025) . Here, one computes \textsc{VectorQuant}(x)=\mathsf{quant}(Rx) , where R is a random rotation matrix and \mathsf{quant} is a scalar quantizer (e.g., the sign function) applied coordinate-wise to the vector Rx . A key advantage of this approach is that it is data-oblivious : it requires no complex preprocessing tailored to the input distribution. Moreover, this method is amenable to theoretical analysis, since each coordinate of Rx is approximately normally distributed when R is a uniformly random rotation.



 
 However, applying a dense random rotation matrix R requires \Theta(d^{2}) time, which can be prohibitively expensive for high-dimensional vectors. To overcome this bottleneck, Ailon and Chazelle (2009) ; Sarlos (2006) ; Woolfe et al. (2008) ; Krahmer and Ward (2011) ; Halko et al. (2011) and others proposed to replace random projections with randomized Hadamard transforms . Specifically, one replaces R with the structured matrix HD , where H\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} is the normalized Hadamard matrix and D is a diagonal matrix with independent Rademacher ( \pm 1 ) entries on the diagonal. The resulting transform can be applied in O(d\log d) time using the fast Walsh-Hadamard algorithm, yielding a substantial speedup over dense rotations. Thanks to its low complexity and efficient parallel implementation, randomized Hadamard transform is often the method of choice in industrial deployments, see, e.g.,  Christiani (2025) ; Agarwal et al. (2024) .



 
 Unfortunately, HD is no longer a uniformly random rotation; in fact, it is not even a continuous random variable. As a consequence, the coordinates of HDx have a more complex distribution than in the dense case, and theoretical guarantees for quantization become harder to establish. Existing results are typically either empirical  Andoni et al. (2015) ; Suresh et al. (2017) ; Ordentlich and Polyanskiy (2025) or provable but with higher dimension or number of bits compared to those achievable with truly random rotations  Ailon and Liberty (2013) ; Krahmer and Ward (2011) ; Tropp (2011) ; Le et al. (2014) ; Cherapanamjeri and Nelson (2022) ; Ben-Basat et al. (2024) . A natural remedy is to use more general structured matrices obtained by composing multiple randomized Hadamard transforms, possibly interleaved with other (e.g., sparse diagonal) matrices  Dasgupta et al. (2011) ; Le et al. (2014) ; Zhang and Saab (2021) ; Kennedy and Ward (2016) ; Zilca and Mendelson (2026) . In particular, the last paper independently showed that the composition of just two randomized Hadamard transforms suffices to approximate 1 1

 In this paper, we study a simple alternative to composing multiple randomized Hadamard transforms, namely dithering . Specifically, we consider quantizers where each coordinate of HDx is shifted by a random amount (controlled by a parameter U ) before applying the scalar quantizer \mathsf{quant} .



 
 Such quantizers are quite simple, as they require only one application of the randomized Hadamard transform, followed by a subtraction of a random vector. At the same time, the random offset injects additional randomness into the quantization process, which facilitates the analysis.



 
 1.1 Our results


 
 We consider the following data-oblivious vector quantization problem. For an input vector
 x\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , a quantizer \textsc{VectorQuant}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{b\cdot d} outputs a binary string of bd bits, where b is the bit-width per coordinate. The dequantizer \textsc{VectorDequant}:\{0,1\}^{b\cdot d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d} maps this string back to a vector \widetilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} . We aim to design a pair of quantizer and dequantizer such that for any desired bit-width b minimize
the following expected distortion measure for any (worst-case) vectors:

 

 
 
 \sup_{x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}, 
 
 


 where the expectation is over the internal randomness of the quantizer. The restriction to unit vectors is only for normalization: for nonzero inputs one can store the norm separately, quantize x/\|x\|_{2} , and rescale the decoded vector. In the following, we assume that d is padded to a power of 2 , thus there exists a d\times d Hadamard matrix.



 
 Our main result shows that randomized Hadamard mappings HD , after dithering, provides compression guarantees that asymptotically match those achievable with truly random rotation matrices R . Moreover, our quantizer is unbiased, which is a desirable
property for numerous applications. In particular we show that our proposed algorithm has the following guarantees:



 
 Theorem 1.1 . 

 
 For every dimension d and every unit vector x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} , Algorithm   5 satisfies 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}[\widetilde{x}]=x 
 
 


 and 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{4^{b}},\qquad b\to\infty, 
 
 (1) 
 


 where the o(1) term vanishes as b\to\infty , uniformly over d and x . 





 
 The scaling constant in this theorem matches the bound obtained for TurboQuant in  Zandieh et al. (2025) that assumed fully random rotation matrices, up to the o(1) term.



 
 Furthermore, as noted in  Ben-Basat et al. (2026) , this also provides guarantees for a special case of the EDEN quantization method  Vargaftik et al. (2022) , also using fully random rotation matrices. Thus, Theorem   1.1 can be viewed as justifying the use a single randomized Hadamard matrix in those methods.



 
 We also provide a quantizer for inner products. Similarly to TurboQuant, we achieve this result by additionally quantizing the residual and showing that the quantization error is uncorrelated with the direction of the vector we dot product x with. Somewhat surprisingly, a classical mixed moment property of Rademacher random variables suffices to establish this fact with random rotations replaced by only a single HD matrix:



 
 Theorem 1.2 . 

 
 Let x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} be a unit vector and let
 y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} . Let \widehat{x} be the reconstructed vector produced by Algorithm   4 with a base bit-width b .
Then 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}|\left\langle y,\widehat{x}-x\right\rangle|^{2}\leq 13\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2}+1+o(1)\right)\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}{d\cdot 4^{b}}, 
 
 


 where the o(1) term vanishes as b\to\infty , uniformly over d and x . 



 
 Moreover, the output of Quantize (x) from Algorithm   4 can be stored using
 db+(3+\frac{1}{2\ln 2})d+O(\log(b+\log d)) 
total bits. 







 
 1.2 Related work


 
 In addition to the discussion in the introduction, in this section we elaborate on connections with some prior/concurrent works in more detail.



 
 Dithering, i.e., the use of a random offset in quantization, has a long history in quantization theory  Schuchman (1964) ; Zamir and Feder (1992) . In the context of random projections combined with quantization, dithering has been employed in  Datar et al. (2004) ; Dong et al. (2008) and more recently in  Gao and Long (2024) . However, their analysis assumed a truly random matrix R .



 
 Another approach to analyzing quantization after a single randomized Hadamard transform appears in  Ben-Basat et al. (2024) , where the authors use the Bentkus–Dzindzalieta tail bound to compare the tails of normalized Hadamard coordinates with Gaussian tails. They then partition the range of coordinates into intervals and bound the quantization error on each interval separately, leveraging the tail bound to control contributions from the outer intervals.
However, as the authors themselves note, this approach yields only a loose bound on the expected distortion.



 
 An alternative approach to bridging the gap between a single randomized Hadamard transform and a fully random rotation is taken by a very recent independent work  Zilca and Mendelson (2026) , which shows that composing two randomized Hadamard transforms HD_{1}HD_{2} suffices to approximate a uniform random rotation in Kolmogorov distance, with an error of O(d^{-1/5}) per coordinate. While this result provides a general-purpose surrogate for random rotations, the procedure is more complex, and applying it to quantization incurs an additive distortion overhead that does not vanish for fixed d .







 
 2 Construction of the quantizer


 
 For the ease of exposition, we separate the main mean squared error argument from the unbiasedness property. We first present a simpler baseline algorithm, Algorithm   1 , which achieves the claimed mean-squared error bound ( Eq.   1 ) but is slightly biased. In the later Section   A.3 , we present Algorithm   5 , which is an unbiased variant of Algorithm   1 .



 
 Our quantizer independently and randomly flips the signs of the input vector coordinates, mixes the coordinates using a normalized Hadamard transform, and then applies the same scalar quantizer to every transformed coordinate. The scalar quantizer is a dithered version of Lloyd quantization, which was also used in Turboquant Zandieh et al. (2025) and EDEN Vargaftik et al. (2022) . We define the scalar quantizer now, start by introducing some basic notations.
Let

 

 
 
 \varphi(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-t^{2}/2} 
 
 


 be the standard Gaussian density, and define

 

 
 
 F(t):=\frac{1}{A}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\varphi(s)^{1/3}\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\text{ where }A:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)^{1/3}\,\mathrm{d}t. 
 
 




 
 Such F is the CDF of a Gaussian random variable with mean 0 and variance 3 (see Claim   A.4 for the proof). In Algorithm   1 , the scalar quantizer applies F to each coordinate, imposes a shifted uniform grid on the resulting quantile levels, and reconstructs each grid bucket at the inverse image of its midpoint, i.e. reconstructs each bucket at the corresponding middle quantile.



 
 Specifically, we choose a uniformly random shift parameter U\in[0,1] and denote the parameter B=2^{b} (the target codebook size, i.e. number of possible quantized scalar values). Throughout this paper, we use [B] to denote \{0,1,...B-1\} . For j\in[B] , we define grid points

 

 
 
 h_{j}:=(j+U)/B. 
 
 


 Define h_{0}:=0 and h_{B}:=1 . Now for j\in[B] define buckets

 

 
 
 \mathcal{B}_{j}\leftarrow\{t\in\mathbb{R}:h_{j}\leq F(t)<h_{j+1}\}. 
 
 


 Finally, for j\in[B] define reconstruction values

 

 
 
 q_{j}\leftarrow F^{-1}\bigl((h_{j}+h_{j+1})/2\bigr).

 For the purposes of the analysis it is convenient to think of the quantizer as returning the corresponding middle quantile as opposed to its index. To this effect, it is convenient to introduce



 
 Definition 2.1 ( \mathsf{quant} function) . 

 
 For z\in\mathbb{R} define \mathsf{quant}(z) to return the middle quantile q_{j} of the bucket \mathcal{B}_{j} that contains z , i.e. \mathsf{quant}(z):=q_{j} for z\in\mathcal{B}_{j} . 





 
 With Definition  2.1 in place the task of bounding quantization error in all coordinates becomes, letting z=HDx , the task of bounding

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\sum_{i\in[d]}(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}))^{2}=d\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}(z_{1}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{1}))^{2}, 
 
 


 where the last equality uses the fact that the marginal distributions of coordinates of z are the same, i.e. they are all distributed as the Rademacher sum \sum_{j\in[d]}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} .

 The main guarantee of the Algorithm   1 is the following Theorem   2.2 .



 
 Theorem 2.2 . 

 
 For every dimension d and every unit vector x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} , Algorithm   1 satisfies 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{4^{b}},\qquad b\to\infty, 
 
 


 where the o(1) term vanishes as b\to\infty , uniformly over d and x . 





 
 To achieve the additional unbiasedness property, Algorithm   5 makes two changes
to the scalar quantizer. First, instead of pinning the grid endpoints h_{0},h_{B} at 0 
and 1 , it uses a shifted grid directly on the quantile levels F(t) , so
that every input t has a uniformly random position within a bucket. Second,
instead of reconstructing each bucket at the corresponding middle quantile by applying F^{-1} to the midpoint, it
uses a modified reconstruction map G , carefully chosen so that averaging G 
over
one bucket recovers the middle quantile value. This makes the scalar
reconstruction unbiased, and hence the vector estimator unbiased, while G 
remains close enough to F^{-1} on the central region to preserve the same
MSE bound. For details, see Algorithm   5 in Section   A.3 .





 
 3 Proof sketch of Theorem   2.2 


 
 In this section, we outline our proof ideas for Theorem   2.2 .
We can use orthogonality of HD to decompose the error coordinate-wise:

 

 
 
 \displaystyle\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\|x-DH^{\top}\widetilde{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\|HDx-\widetilde{y}\|_{2}^{2}= 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(y_{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathsf{quant}(\sqrt{d}y_{i})\bigr)^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i})\bigr)^{2}. 
 
 


 Therefore, to control the total MSE: \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2} , it is enough to bound the variance of the scalar quantizer \mathsf{quant}(\cdot) under the specific input distibution of z_{i} . For every coordinate i , this distribution is given by

 

 
 
 z_{i}=\sqrt{d}(HDx)_{i}=\sqrt{d}\sum_{j}H_{ij}D_{jj}x_{j}=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} 
 
 


 where \varepsilon_{j} are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables taking values \pm 1 with probability 1/2 . In contrast to using a truly random rotation or an i.i.d. Gaussian matrix, where z_{i} distributed as \mathcal{N}(0,\|x\|^{2}_{2}) , our case is more delicate, as distribution of \sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} does not depend only on \|x\|_{2} , but also on individual coordinates. It means that our oblivious scalar quantizer \mathsf{quant} should provide guarantees for any input distribution of the form z_{i}=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} , where \|x\|_{2}=1 . We therefore drop index i in what follows. Recall that B=2^{b} is the number of buckets. Our main guarantee for \mathsf{quant} is the following.



 
 Lemma 3.1 (Restated Lemma   A.1 ) . 

 
 For every dimension d , every unit vector x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} define z=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} where i.i.d. \varepsilon_{j}=\pm 1 with probability 1/2 . Then 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}|z-\mathsf{quant}(z)|^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}},\qquad B\to\infty, 
 
 


 where the o(1) term is uniform in d and x . 





 
 As we described above, Theorem   1.1 follows directly from Lemma   3.1 . The rest of this section is dedicated to Lemma   3.1 .



 
 Lemma   3.1 is a bit surprising - the codebook of \mathsf{quant} is built from the optimal quantization nodes for \mathcal{N}(0,1) , but we prove that the same error rate is achieved for a different distribution: the Rademacher sums z=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} . This distribution can be quite different from \mathcal{N}(0,\|x\|^{2}_{2}) for worst-case x . For example if x=(1,0,\dots 0) then z only takes values \pm 1 . However, it turns out that to prove Lemma   3.1 , we only need the fact that z is 1-subgaussian. This is captured by the following claim.



 
 Lemma 3.2 . 

 
 For every dimension d , every unit vector x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} define z=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}x_{j} where i.i.d. \varepsilon_{j}=\pm 1 with probability 1/2 . Then z is a 1 -subgaussian random variable, i.e. 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda z}\leq e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}. 
 
 






 
 Note that subgaussianity is a powerful property that in some cases allows us to simply substitute the distribution with the standard Gaussian. More formally, we will use the following (see Lemma   A.2 ):



 
 Claim 3.3 . 

 
 If \alpha>0 , z is 1 -subgaussian and \xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1) , then \mathbb{E}e^{\alpha z^{2}}\leq\mathbb{E}e^{\alpha\xi^{2}} . 





 
 Unfortunately, there is no straightforward way to apply this property to bound the expectation of the quantization error because the error function \min_{q_{j}}|t-q_{j}|^{2} is highly non-monotone – it could be that subgaussian distribution tends to concentrate around highs of the error function.



 
 We now move on to outline our proof for Lemma   3.1 . In fact, we will prove a bit more general version of Lemma   3.1 with any 1 -subgaussian input. 2 2 
 2 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 Note that \mathcal{N}(0,1) appearing from a truly random rotation is also 1 -subgaussian. From now on, we will write \mathbb{E}_{z} instead of \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon} .



 
 We separate the analysis of \mathbb{E}_{z,U}|z-\mathsf{quant}(z)|^{2} according to whether z lies in a “central interval” [-M,M] , i.e. we separately consider a tail event |z|>M and a central event |z|\leq M , for some carefully chosen threshold M=C\sqrt{\log B},C\in(4,6) . Such a narrow choice of values of C is due to the following tension: we need to guarantee that a O(\frac{1}{B}) shift in quantiles of F (see Algorithm   1 ) results in a o(1) shift in centroids in the central interval; this forces M not to be too large. But with the same choice of M we need the tail event to be unlikely enough that we can ignore the centroids construction outside of the central interval completely; this forces M to be not too small.



 
 3.1 Central event


 
 In the central event, we argue that buckets are narrow enough to treat standard Gaussian density on them as uniform. Then, to recover optimal error rate for our arbitrary 1 -subgaussian distribution, we induce uniformity by dithering , i.e. applying random shift on the quantization grid. More specifically, as we show in Lemma   A.3 , for our choice of M , any |t|<M and quantile shift \frac{U}{B}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0;1/B) we have the following

 
 • 
 
 T:=\Bigl[F(t)-F(\mathsf{quant}(t))\Bigr] is uniform on [-\frac{1}{2B};\frac{1}{2B}] .




 • 
 
 \frac{F^{\prime}(s)}{F^{\prime}(t)}=1+o_{B}(1) for all s\in[t;\mathsf{quant}(t)] where o_{B}(1) term is uniform in s and U .




 
 Then we can use first-order approximation and write

 

 
 
 \mathsf{quant}(t)-t=F^{-1}\left(F(t)+\frac{T}{B}\right)-t=(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{T/B}{F^{\prime}(t)}. 
 
 


 And, integrating over T , we bound

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}|t-\mathsf{quant}(t)|^{2}\leq(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{1}{12B^{2}}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}. 
 
 


 Note that the error \frac{T/B}{F^{\prime}(t)} , T\sim\mathrm{Unif}[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}] matches the typical error in a bucket of width \frac{1}{BF^{\prime}(t)} with a centroid placed in the middle. Since F^{\prime}(s) is almost constant over the bucket, this calculation differs from an actual error only by lower order terms.



 
 To get the total error bound we first take expectation over U 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{z,U}|z-\mathsf{quant}(z)|^{2}\leq(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{1}{12B^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{z}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(z)^{2}} 
 
 


 and then, by Claim   3.3 , since F^{\prime}(x) is a Gaussian density and z is 1 -subgaussian,

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{z}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(z)^{2}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}},\quad\text{where }\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). 
 
 


 Finally,

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{z,U}|z-\mathsf{quant}(z)|^{2}\leq(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{1}{12B^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}}=\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}} 
 
 






 
 3.2 Tail event


 
 In the tail event, we give a simple bound

 

 
 
 |z-\mathsf{quant}(z)|^{2}\leq 2|z|^{2}+2|\mathsf{quant}(z)|^{2} 
 
 


 and argue that both terms on the RHS are bounded in expectation by O(\mathrm{poly}\log B\cdot e^{-M^{2}/2})=o(\frac{1}{B^{2}}) using subgaussianity of z and our choice of M . A little more work is required to carefully bound |\mathsf{quant}(z)| since the centroid construction is randomized. For more details, see Lemma   A.6 .







 
 4 Inner product quantizer


 
 In this section we present our inner product quantizer and prove Theorem  1.2 . For a vector y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , in order to achieve approximation error for (\langle y,x\rangle-\langle y,\widetilde{x}\rangle)^{2} that scales as \frac{O(1)}{dB^{2}}\|y\|_{2}^{2} , we augment our quantizer for x with another basic quantizer that decorrelates the residual r=x-\widetilde{x} with y . This is achieved by a single application of a fresh HD matrix to the residual followed by a simple quantization procedure that uses a constant number of bits per coordinate in expectation. To obtain an explicit bound on the norm of the residual \|r\|_{2} , \widetilde{x} is projected onto the unit ball; such a projection \Pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d} can be implemented simply as \Pi(v)=v if \|v\|_{2}\leq 1 and \Pi(v)=v/\|v\|_{2} otherwise.



 
 Concretely, let r=x-\widetilde{x} be the residual vector, where \widetilde{x} is the result of quantization using our main VectorQuant procedure followed by projecting onto the unit ball.
Define the residual
scale by s=\frac{\left\|r\right\|}{\sqrt{d}}\in[0,\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}] .
We first quantize the scale using a basic scalar quantizer \textsc{ScalarQuant}(s) (Algorithm  2 below) and then quantize the residual’s coordinates based on the quantized scale (Algorithm  3 below).

 The intuition behind Algorithm   2 is that if the residual scale exceeds a minimum cutoff, i.e., s\geq\tau_{B} , then the quantized scale is
 \sigma=\tau_{B}2^{\mathsf{idx}_{\sigma}-1} satisfying s\leq\sigma<2s . On the other hand if s<\tau_{B} , then the quantized scale is set to
 \sigma=0 indicating that the residual is negligibly small (thus the residual quantization stage can be skipped). The quantized scales are indexed by \mathsf{idx}_{\sigma} , which can be encoded using

 Limitations


 
 This paper is primarily theoretical and studies a specific class of data-oblivious
quantizers based on a single randomized Hadamard transform and randomized dithering.
The results characterize worst-case mean-squared error and inner-product error, but do
not attempt to optimize all finite-bit constants or evaluate performance empirically
across downstream systems. Extending
the analysis to broader structured transforms, alternative scalar quantizers, and more
implementation-specific bit accounting would be interesting directions for future work.

 Appendix A Full proof of Theorem  1.1 


 
 A.1 Scalar Estimate


 
 In this section, we prove Lemma   A.1 , the scalar estimate that controls the coordinate-wise mean squared error. In the next section, we then use it to bound the error of vector quantization.



 
 For any unit vector a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , we define a random variable

 

 
 
 X_{a}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}, 
 
 


 where \varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d} are independent Rademacher random variables. We abbreviate \varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d}) . For the scalar quantizer appearing in Algorithm   1 , we write

 

 
 
 \mathsf{quant}(t):=q_{j}\quad\text{when }t\in\mathcal{B}_{j}, 
 
 


 i.e. \mathsf{quant}(\cdot) maps any scalar t\in\mathbb{R} to the corresponding reconstruction value, which is the preimage of the midpoint of the bucket in which F(t) lands. We recall that \mathsf{quant} depends on U,B in the following ways: U is a random offset that determines the shift of buckets, and B=2^{b} is the number of buckets. The rest of this section devotes to proving Lemma   A.1 , which bounds the mean squared quantization error for \mathsf{quant} applied to X_{a} .



 
 Lemma A.1 . 

 
 Uniformly over all d\geq 1 and all a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} satisfying \sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}=1 , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\bigl(X_{a}-\mathsf{quant}(X_{a})\bigr)^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}},\qquad B\to\infty. 
 
 






 
 As outlined in the proof sketch, the proof proceeds by separating the behavior of the scalar quantizer according to whether
the input lies in a “central” interval [-M_{B},M_{B}] , where M_{B}:=\sqrt{5\log B} . We first
show in Lemma   A.2 that every normalized Rademacher sum has
Gaussian-type tail and moment bounds. Inside the central interval, Lemma   A.3 
shows that the random shift U makes the displacement in the F -coordinate uniform at scale 1/B , and that F^{-1} can be replaced by its first-order approximation on this scale.
Outside the central interval, Lemma   A.6 gives a rough bound on the squared
quantization error; the tail bound from Lemma   A.2 then makes this
rough estimate negligible. Combining these two regimes gives Lemma   A.1 .



 
 The following lemma is standard (see e.g.,  [ 2 ] , proof of Lemma 1), but we reprove it here for completeness.



 
 Lemma A.2 . 

 
 For every unit vector a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , the random variable X_{a} satisfies 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X_{a}}\leq e^{\lambda^{2}/2}\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}. 
 
 


 Consequently, 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(|X_{a}|>s)\leq 2e^{-s^{2}/2}\qquad\text{for all }s>0. 
 
 


 Moreover, if G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1) , then 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{X_{a}^{2}/3}\leq\mathbb{E}e^{G^{2}/3}=\sqrt{3}. 
 
 






 
 Proof.

 
 By the definition of X_{a} and the independence between \varepsilon_{i} ’s, 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X_{a}}=\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}}. 
 
 


 For each Rademacher random variable \varepsilon_{i} , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{\lambda a_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\lambda a_{i}}=\cosh{(\lambda a_{i})}\leq e^{(\lambda a_{i})^{2}/2}. 
 
 


 Thus 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{\lambda X_{a}}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}e^{(\lambda a_{i})^{2}/2}=e^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}=e^{\lambda^{2}/2}. 
 
 (3) 
 


 The tail bound follows from Markov’s inequality applied to both e^{\lambda X_{a}} and e^{-\lambda X_{a}} and optimize over \lambda>0 . 



 
 For the last argument, since \mathbb{E}_{G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}e^{\tau G}=e^{\tau^{2}/2} for any \tau\in\mathbb{R} , conditioning on X_{a} and taking \tau=\sqrt{2/3}X_{a} gives 

 

 
 
 e^{X_{a}^{2}/3}=\mathbb{E}_{G}e^{\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}GX_{a}}. 
 
 


 Let G be independent of \varepsilon_{1},\ldots\varepsilon_{d} ,
we obtain 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}e^{X_{a}^{2}/3}=\mathbb{E}_{G}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}e^{\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}GX_{a}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{G}e^{G^{2}/3}=\sqrt{3}. 
 
 


 where the inequality follows from the earlier MGF bound Eq.   3 .
∎ 





 
 We next state our main lemma for the central event. It controls the quantization error for all real t (in particular, our random variable X_{a} ) that lies in the central interval [-M_{B},M_{B}] .



 
 Lemma A.3 . 

 
 Let M_{B}:=\sqrt{5\log B} . For all |t|\leq M_{B} , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}\bigl(t-\mathsf{quant}(t)\bigr)^{2}\leq(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{A^{2}}{12B^{2}}(2\pi)^{1/3}e^{t^{2}/3}. 
 
 


 where the o_{B}(1) term is uniform for |t|\leq M_{B} . 





 
 Before proving Lemma   A.3 , we state the following fact about F , which will be helpful for our proof. Let \Phi denote the CDF of the standard normal distribution.



 
 Claim A.4 . 

 
 For any t\in\mathbb{R} , F(t)=\Phi(t/\sqrt{3}) . 





 
 Proof.

 
 Recall that 

 

 
 
 F(t):=\frac{1}{A}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\varphi(s)^{1/3}\,\mathrm{d}s. 
 
 


 where A=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)^{1/3}\,\mathrm{d}t is the normalization factor. Since 

 

 
 
 \varphi(s)^{1/3}=(2\pi)^{-1/6}e^{-s^{2}/6} 
 
 


 is proportional to the density of \mathcal{N}(0,3) , F(t) is exactly the CDF of \mathcal{N}(0,3) . Thus for G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1) , 

 

 
 
 F(t)=\mathbb{P}[\sqrt{3}G\leq t]=\mathbb{P}[G\leq t/\sqrt{3}]=\Phi(t/\sqrt{3}). 
 
 


 ∎ 





 
 Corollary A.5 . 

 
 Let M_{B}=\sqrt{5\log B} . We have F(-M_{B}) , 1-F(M_{B}) , F(M_{B}+1)-F(M_{B}) , and F(-M_{B})-F(-M_{B}-1)\gg B^{-1} . 





 
 Proof.

 
 By Claim   A.4 and the symmetry of the Gaussian distribution, 

 

 
 
 F(-M_{B})=1-F(M_{B})=\Phi(-M_{B}/\sqrt{3}). 
 
 


 Since M_{B}^{2}=5\log B , the Gaussian tail estimate gives 

 

 
 
 \Phi(-M_{B}/\sqrt{3})=B^{-5/6+o(1)}. 
 
 


 which is in particular \omega(B^{-1}) . For the two increments, 

 

 
 
 \displaystyle F(M_{B}+1)-F(M_{B}) 
 \displaystyle=\int_{M_{B}}^{M_{B}+1}F^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\int_{M_{B}}^{M_{B}+1}\frac{(2\pi)^{-1/6}}{A}\exp(-t^{2}/6)\,\mathrm{d}t 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\geq\frac{(2\pi)^{-1/6}}{A}\exp(-(M_{B}+1)^{2}/6) 
 
 


 where the second equality follows from the definition of F .
Since M_{B}^{2}=5\log B and A is a constant, this is B^{-5/6-o(1)}=\omega(B^{-1}) as claimed. By the symmetry, the same bound holds for F(-M_{B})-F(-M_{B}-1) .
∎ 





 
 Proof of Lemma   A.3 .

 
 Recall that when defining the grid points in Algorithm   1 , we set h_{1}=(1+U)/B and h_{B-1}=(B-1+U)/B . As U varies in [0,1) , we always have h_{1}\leq 2/B and h_{B-1}\geq 1-(1/B) . On the other hand, since F is increasing, for |t|\leq M_{B} , we have F(-M_{B})\leq F(t)\leq F(M_{B}) . Applying Corollary   A.5 , we get 

 

 
 
 \omega(B^{-1})\leq F(t)\leq 1-\omega(B^{-1}). 
 
 


 So for all sufficiently large B , h_{1}<F(t)<h_{B-1} , i.e. F(t) never falls in one of the two endpoint buckets. 



 
 Therefore the effect of the random offset U is to uniformly choose the relative position of F(t) inside
a bucket. Equivalently, the midpoint of the bucket containing F(t) can be written as a random variable 

 

 
 
 F(t)+\frac{T}{B}, 
 
 


 where T is uniformly distributed on [-1/2,1/2] . And its preimage is 

 

 
 
 \mathsf{quant}(t)=F^{-1}\left(F(t)+\frac{T}{B}\right). 
 
 


 We next estimate \mathsf{quant}(t)-t using the first-order Taylor expansion of F^{-1} at the point F(t) . Formally, we claim that, uniformly over |t|\leq M_{B} and |T/B|\leq 1/(2B) , 

 

 
 
 \mathsf{quant}(t)-t=F^{-1}\left(F(t)+\frac{T}{B}\right)-t=(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{T/B}{F^{\prime}(t)}. 
 
 


 To show this, we observe that for s\in[0,T/B] , the point
 F^{-1}(F(t)+s) lies in the interval [-M_{B}-1,M_{B}+1] , for all sufficiently large B .
Indeed,
 F(M_{B}+1)-F(M_{B}) and F(-M_{B})-F(-M_{B}-1)\gg B^{-1} by Corollary   A.5 .
So for |s|\leq|T/B|\leq 1/(2B) , moving by s in the F -coordinate cannot move the preimage
past M_{B}+1 or -M_{B}-1 . 



 
 Now let y=F^{-1}(F(t)+s) . The mean value theorem gives 

 

 
 
 s=F^{\prime}(\xi)(y-t) 
 
 


 for some \xi between y and t . By the previous argument, both endpoints lie in [-M_{B}-1,M_{B}+1] , and hence so does \xi . Therefore, 

 

 
 
 |y-t|\leq|s|\sup_{|\xi|\leq M_{B}+1}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(\xi)}\leq\Theta\left(B^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{(M_{B}+1)^{2}}{6}\right)\right)=B^{-1/6+o(1)} 
 
 


 where the second step follows from F^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{(2\pi)^{-1/6}}{A}e^{-\xi^{2}/6} . This implies 

 

 
 
 |y^{2}-t^{2}|=|y-t|\,|y+t|\leq B^{-1/6+o(1)}\cdot O(M_{B})=B^{-1/6+o(1)}\cdot O(\sqrt{\log B})=o_{B}(1) 
 
 


 thus 

 

 
 
 \frac{F^{\prime}(t)}{F^{\prime}(y)}=\frac{e^{-t^{2}/6}}{e^{-y^{2}/6}}=\exp\left(\frac{y^{2}-t^{2}}{6}\right)=1+o_{B}(1) 
 
 


 uniformly over |t|\leq M_{B} and |s|\leq 1/(2B) .
Equivalently, 

 

 
 
 \frac{1}{F^{\prime}(F^{-1}(F(t)+s))}=(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(t)}. 
 
 


 Finally, integrating from 0 to T/B gives 

 

 
 
 F^{-1}\left(F(t)+\frac{T}{B}\right)-t=\int_{0}^{T/B}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(F^{-1}(F(t)+s))}\,\mathrm{d}s=(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{T/B}{F^{\prime}(t)}. 
 
 (4) 
 


 Using \mathbb{E}T^{2}=1/12 and F^{\prime}(t)=\frac{(2\pi)^{-1/6}}{A}e^{-t^{2}/6} , we obtain 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}(t-\mathsf{quant}(t))^{2}\leq(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{1}{12B^{2}}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}=(1+o_{B}(1))\frac{A^{2}}{12B^{2}}(2\pi)^{1/3}e^{t^{2}/3}. 
 
 


 ∎ 





 
 Next we consider the tail event when |t|=|X_{a}|>M_{B} . In this event, we use the following crude error bound, which suffices for the entire proof due to the small probability of the tail event.



 
 Lemma A.6 . 

 
 There is an absolute constant C such that, for every B\geq 2 and every t\in\mathbb{R} , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}(t-\mathsf{quant}(t))^{2}\leq C(t^{2}+\log B). 
 
 






 
 Proof.

 
 Fix the value of U . Since F^{-1} is increasing, the reconstruction values
 q_{0},\ldots,q_{B-1} are increasing. Hence every reconstruction value lies between
the two endpoint reconstruction values, and therefore 

 

 
 
 |\mathsf{quant}(t)|\leq R_{B}(U), 
 
 


 where 

 

 
 
 R_{B}(U):=\max\left\{\left|F^{-1}\left(\frac{1+U}{2B}\right)\right|,\left|F^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1-U}{2B}\right)\right|\right\}. 
 
 


 By Claim   A.4 , we have F^{-1}(p)=\sqrt{3}\,\Phi^{-1}(p) . Using the
standard Gaussian quantile bound 

 

 
 
 |\Phi^{-1}(r)|^{2}+|\Phi^{-1}(1-r)|^{2}\leq C\log(1/r),\qquad 0<r\leq 1/2, 
 
 


 we obtain 

 

 
 
 R_{B}(U)^{2}\leq C\log\left(\frac{2B}{1+U}\right)+C\log\left(\frac{2B}{1-U}\right). 
 
 


 Taking expectation over U , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}R_{B}(U)^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\log(2B)\,\,\mathrm{d}U+C\int_{0}^{1}\log\left(\frac{2B}{1-U}\right)\,\mathrm{d}U\leq C\log B. 
 
 


 Thus 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}(t-\mathsf{quant}(t))^{2}\leq 2t^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{U}q(t)^{2}\leq 2t^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{U}R_{B}(U)^{2}\leq C(t^{2}+\log B). 
 
 


 ∎ 





 
 With Lemma   A.2 , Lemma   A.3 , and Lemma   A.6 , we can now prove the scalar guarantee.



 
 Proof of Lemma   A.1 .

 
 Fix a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} with \sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}=1 , and write X=X_{a} . We split the
expectation according to the central event |X|\leq M_{B} . 



 
 We first consider the central contribution, i.e. when |X|\leq M_{B} . By Lemma   A.3 , and using the
uniformity of the o_{B}(1) term, there exists a deterministic sequence \alpha_{B}\to 0 such that 

 

 
 
 \displaystyle B^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\left[(X-\mathsf{quant}(X))^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M_{B}\}}\right] 
 \displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha_{B})\frac{A^{2}}{12}(2\pi)^{1/3}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}e^{X^{2}/3} 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha_{B})\frac{A^{2}}{12}(2\pi)^{1/3}\sqrt{3} 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=(1+\alpha_{B})\frac{A^{3}}{12} 
 
 


 where the second inequality follows from
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}e^{X^{2}/3}\leq\sqrt{3} as shown in Lemma   A.2 . Since A=(2\pi)^{1/3}\sqrt{3} , this is (1+\alpha_{B})\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2} .
It remains to show that the complement of the central event contributes o(B^{-2}) . We use Lemma   A.6 : 

 

 
 
 B^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\left[(X-\mathsf{quant}(X))^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M_{B}\}}\right]\leq CB^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[(X^{2}+\log B)\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M_{B}\}}\right]. 
 
 


 Then using the tail bound from Lemma   A.2 , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[X^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M_{B}\}}\right]=M_{B}^{2}\Pr[|X|>M_{B}]+\int_{M_{B}^{2}}^{\infty}\Pr[X^{2}>v]\,\,\mathrm{d}v\leq C(M_{B}^{2}+1)e^{-M_{B}^{2}/2}. 
 
 


 Also, 

 

 
 
 (\log B)\Pr[|X|>M_{B}]\leq C(\log B)e^{-M_{B}^{2}/2}. 
 
 


 Since M_{B}^{2}=5\log B , this gives 

 

 
 
 B^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\left[(X-\mathsf{quant}(X))^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M_{B}\}}\right]\leq CB^{2}(\log B)e^{-5\log B/2}=C(\log B)B^{-1/2}=o(1). 
 
 


 Combining the central and tail contributions, 

 

 
 
 B^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}(X_{a}-\mathsf{quant}(X_{a}))^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2}+o(1), 
 
 


 where the o(1) term is uniform over d and a .
∎ 







 
 A.2 Proof of Theorem   2.2 


 
 We now return to the vector quantizer in Algorithm   1 and prove Theorem   2.2 . The following lemma is the only
property of the randomized Hadamard transform that we need: after the normalization, each coordinate is exactly a Rademacher sum with a unit coefficient vector.



 
 Lemma A.7 . 

 
 Fix x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} , and let y=HDx and z_{i}=\sqrt{d}y_{i} 
for i\in[d] as in Algorithm   1 . For every i\in[d] , there exists a unit vector
 a^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} such that
 z_{i}=X_{a^{(i)}} . 





 
 Proof.

 
 By the definition of D , we have D=\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d}) .
Thus 

 

 
 
 z_{i}=\sqrt{d}\,(HDx)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{d}\,H_{i,j}x_{j}\varepsilon_{j}. 
 
 


 Define 

 

 
 
 a_{j}^{(i)}:=\sqrt{d}\,H_{i,j}x_{j}. 
 
 


 Since H is a normalized Hadamard matrix, H_{i,j}^{2}=1/d for every i,j . Hence 

 

 
 
 \sum_{j=1}^{d}(a_{j}^{(i)})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}dH_{i,j}^{2}x_{j}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}=1. 
 
 


 Therefore z_{i}=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{(i)}\varepsilon_{j}=X_{a^{(i)}} , as claimed.
∎ 





 
 With this, we can directly apply our scalar estimate for each coordinate and obtain the mean squared error bound for the vector quantizer.



 
 Proof of Theorem   1.1 .

 
 Fix x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} . Let y=HDx , and let z_{i}=\sqrt{d}\,y_{i} be the scalar input
quantized in coordinate i . If \widetilde{y} denotes the vector reconstructed in the
Hadamard domain, then, by the scaling in Algorithm   1 , 

 

 
 
 \widetilde{y}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}). 
 
 


 Since H and D are orthogonal, 

 

 
 
 \|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\|HDx-\widetilde{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}))^{2}. 
 
 


 Taking expectation over D and U , and using linearity of expectation, 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{D,U}(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}))^{2}. 
 
 


 By the Lemma   A.7 , each z_{i} is equal to X_{a^{(i)}} for a unit vector
 a^{(i)} . Applying Lemma   A.1 to each coordinate gives 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}))^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}}. 
 
 


 Then averaging over i , we obtain 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}}. 
 
 


 ∎ 







 
 A.3 An unbiased variant of Algorithm   1 


 
 In this section we modify the scalar quantizer, in particular the scalar reconstruction rule, so
that the resulting vector estimator is unbiased, while preserving the same
asymptotic distortion bound. Recall that

 

 
 
 F(t)=\frac{1}{A}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\phi(s)^{1/3}\,ds,\qquad A=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(s)^{1/3}\,ds. 
 
 


 Previously in Algorithm   1 , we define buckets with 1/B spacing in the F -coordinates, and reconstruct each bucket at the inverse image ( F^{-1} ) of its midpoint. In this section, we will use buckets with spacing 1/(B-1) , and we will replace F^{-1} with a reconstruction function
 G:[-\frac{1}{2(B-1)},1+\frac{1}{2(B-1)}]\to\mathbb{R} 
satisfying



 
 

 
 
 (B-1)\int_{r-\frac{1}{2(B-1)}}^{r+\frac{1}{2(B-1)}}G(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=F^{-1}(r)\qquad\text{for every }r\in(0,1). 
 
 (5) 
 


 Moreover, we will choose G such that in the central region [-\sqrt{5\log B},\sqrt{5\log B}] , G is close to F^{-1} . Formally:



 
 Lemma A.8 . 

 
 There exists an explicitly defined function G:[-\frac{1}{2(B-1)},1+\frac{1}{2(B-1)}]\to\mathbb{R} that satisfies Eq.   5 .
Moreover, it can be chosen so that for all |t|\leq\sqrt{5\log B} and all |v|\leq 1/2 , 

 

 
 
 G\left(F(t)+\frac{v}{B-1}\right)=t+\frac{v}{(B-1)F^{\prime}(t)}+o(1)\frac{1}{(B-1)F^{\prime}(t)}. 
 
 (6) 
 






 
 Proof.

 
 We denote the spacing of the grid buckets \delta:=\frac{1}{B-1} .
We start by defining G on the open interval I:=(-\frac{\delta}{2},1+\frac{\delta}{2}) as follows: for
 u\in(\frac{1-\delta}{2},\frac{1+\delta}{2}] and k\in\mathbb{Z} , whenever
 u+k\delta\in I , we define G as 



 
 

 
 
 G(u+k\cdot\delta):=F^{-1}(u)+\begin{cases}\delta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(F^{-1})^{\prime}\bigl(u+(j+1/2)\cdot\delta\bigr)&\text{ if }k>0\\
0&\text{ if }k=0\\
-\delta\sum_{j=k}^{-1}(F^{-1})^{\prime}\bigl(u+(j+1/2)\cdot\delta\bigr)&\text{ if }k<0\\
\end{cases}. 
 
 


 The key idea behind this definition is to enforce the recurrence 

 

 
 
 G(u+(k+1)\delta)-G(u+k\delta)=\delta(F^{-1})^{\prime}\bigl(u+(k+1/2)\delta\bigr). 
 
 


 We first argue that G satisfies Eq.   5 . For every r\in(0,1) , there is a unique pair of u and k such that we can write r-\delta/2=u+k\cdot\delta , it follows that
 G(r+\delta/2)-G(r-\delta/2)=\delta(F^{-1})^{\prime}(r). 
Therefore, 

 

 
 
 \frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}r}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{r-\delta/2}^{r+\delta/2}G(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)=\frac{1}{\delta}\left(G(r+\delta/2)-G(r-\delta/2)\right)=(F^{-1})^{\prime}(r), 
 
 


 i.e. 

 

 
 
 C:=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{r-\delta/2}^{r+\delta/2}G(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-F^{-1}(r) 
 
 


 is constant in r . Evaluating this at r=1/2 shows that C=0 . Indeed, on (\frac{1-\delta}{2},\frac{1+\delta}{2}] , G=F^{-1} , and F(t)=\Phi(t/\sqrt{3}) ( Claim   A.4 ) implies that F^{-1}(1/2)=0 and F^{-1}(1-r)=-F^{-1}(r) for every r . 



 
 This shows that G satisfies Eq.   5 on the open interval I . We extend it arbitrarily to the endpoints for notational convenience in the codebook. These endpoint values are irrelevant for Eq.   5 since they are measure-zero for the integral. 



 
 It remains to show Eq.   6 . We first compare G with F^{-1} .
Fix r\in I , and write r=u+k\delta again with the unique pair of u\in(\frac{1-\delta}{2},\frac{1+\delta}{2}] and k\in\mathbb{Z} . In the following, we focus on k\geq 0 , with the convention that \sum^{-1}_{j=0}(\cdot)=0 . The case k<0 holds symmetrically. By the definition
of G , 

 

 
 
 G(r)-F^{-1}(r)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left[\delta(F^{-1})^{\prime}\bigl(u+(j+1/2)\delta\bigr)-\int_{u+j\delta}^{u+(j+1)\delta}(F^{-1})^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right]. 
 
 


 Hence, by the midpoint rule error bound, 

 

 
 
 |G(r)-F^{-1}(r)|\leq O\left(\delta^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sup_{s\in[u+j\delta,u+(j+1)\delta]}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\right). 
 
 


 Equivalently, for J denoting the interval between u and r , 

 

 
 
 |G(r)-F^{-1}(r)|\leq O\left(\delta^{2}\int_{J}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\,\mathrm{d}s\right)+O\left(\delta^{3}\sup_{s\in J}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\right). 
 
 




 
 It remains to bound this RHS plus F^{-1}(r)-t , using our parameters from Eq.   6 : 

 

 
 
 r=F(t)+v\delta,\qquad|t|\leq M_{B},\qquad|v|\leq 1/2. 
 
 


 In Lemma   A.3 ( Eq.   4 ), we have shown that F^{-1}(r)-t=(1+o(1))\frac{v\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)} . Also, we have shown that
 F^{-1}(r)\in[-M_{B}-1,M_{B}+1] for all sufficiently large B . Combining this with the fact that F^{-1}(u)=O(\delta) for u\in(\frac{1-\delta}{2},\frac{1+\delta}{2}] , we have that for every s\in J , |F^{-1}(s)|\leq M_{B}+1 . And by the definition of F , one can observe that 

 

 
 
 (F^{-1})^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(F^{-1}(s))},\qquad(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{F^{-1}(s)}{3F^{\prime}(F^{-1}(s))^{2}}, 
 
 


 and 

 

 
 
 (F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)=\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2(F^{-1}(s))^{2}}{9}\right)\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(F^{-1}(s))^{3}}. 
 
 


 Combining all these, we get 

 

 
 
 \int_{J}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\,\mathrm{d}s\leq O\left(\frac{M_{B}}{F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}\right),\qquad\sup_{s\in J}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\leq O\left(\frac{M_{B}^{2}}{F^{\prime}(t)^{3}}\right). 
 
 


 Returning to Eq.   6 , we get that 

 

 
 
 \displaystyle G(r)-t 
 \displaystyle\leq F^{-1}(r)-t+O\left(\delta^{2}\int_{J}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\,\mathrm{d}s\right)+O\left(\delta^{3}\sup_{s\in J}\left|(F^{-1})^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)\right|\right) 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\frac{v\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}+\delta^{2}O\left(\frac{M_{B}}{F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}\right)+\delta^{3}O\left(\frac{M_{B}^{2}}{F^{\prime}(t)^{3}}\right) 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{v\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}+\mathrm{poly}\log B\cdot(B^{-1/6+o(1)}+B^{-1/3+o(1)})\cdot\frac{\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)} 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\frac{v\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}+o(1)\frac{\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)} 
 
 


 where in the third step we used M_{B}=\sqrt{5\log B} and \frac{\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}\leq\Theta(\delta\exp(M_{B}^{2}/6))=B^{-1/6+o(1)} .
∎ 





 
 We now describe the unbiased variant of Algorithm   1 in Algorithm   5 . The only change is the procedure of ConstructScalarCodebook , so we omit repeating the procedures VectorQuant and VectorDequant .



 
 
 
 1: 
 
 
 input: dimension d and bit-width b 
 


 
 2: 
 
 
 Let H\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} be a normalized Hadamard matrix and let B\leftarrow 2^{b} .
 


 
 3: 
 
 
 Sample independent Rademacher random variables \varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d} and set D\leftarrow\mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d}) .
 


 
 4: 
 
 
 Sample an offset U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1) .
 


 
 5: 
 
 
   


 
 6: 
 
 
 procedure ConstructScalarCodebook ( B,U )
 


 
 7: 
 
 
   Define buckets \mathcal{B}_{j}\leftarrow\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\lfloor(B-1)F(t)-U\rfloor+1=j\} for j=0,\ldots,B-1 .
 


 
 8: 
 
 
   Define reconstruction values q_{j}\leftarrow G(\frac{j+U-1/2}{B-1}) for j=0,\ldots,B-1 .
 




 Algorithm 5 Unbiased dithered scalar Gaussian quantization 
 
 
 In English, we use a shifted grid in the
 F -coordinate with spacing 1/(B-1) . For r\in(0,1) , define grid points

 

 
 
 h(r)=\left\lfloor(B-1)r-U\right\rfloor, 
 
 


 where U\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1) . We only need to focus on r\in(0,1) since later we will take r=F(t) , which is in (0,1) for any real t . Since 0<r<1 , the index satisfies
 h(r)\in\{-1,0,1,\ldots,B-2\} .
Thus h(r)+1\in\{0,1,\ldots,B-1\} , so the index is still encodable using
 \log_{2}B=b bits.
We denote the midpoint of the shifted cell containing r 

 

 
 
 m(r)=\frac{h(r)+U+1/2}{B-1}. 
 
 


 Then for every fixed r\in(0,1) ,

 

 
 
 m(r)=r+\frac{V}{B-1}, 
 
 


 where V is a random variable V\sim\operatorname{Unif}\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]. 
In the following, we again denote the scalar quantizer:

 

 
 
 \mathsf{quant}(t):=q_{j}\qquad\text{when $t\in\mathcal{B}_{j}$}. 
 
 


 Then by definition,

 

 
 
 \displaystyle\mathsf{quant}(t) 
 \displaystyle=G\left(\frac{\lfloor(B-1)F(t)-U\rfloor+U+1/2}{B-1}\right) 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=G\left(F(t)+\frac{V}{B-1}\right),\qquad V\sim\operatorname{Unif}\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right] 
 
 (7) 
 




 
 Combining this with the requirements on G ( Lemma   A.8 ) gives both the unbiasedness and the scalar estimate bound.



 
 Lemma A.9 . 

 
 For every t\in\mathbb{R} , \mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathsf{quant}(t)]=t .
Moreover, let
 X_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i} 
where \varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d} are independent Rademacher random
variables and a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} be a unit vector. Then,
uniformly over d\geq 1 and a , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\bigl(X_{a}-\mathsf{quant}(X_{a})\bigr)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2}+o(1)\right)B^{-2}. 
 
 






 
 Proof.

 
 We denote the spacing of grid buckets \delta:=\frac{1}{B-1} . Fix t\in\mathbb{R} . By Eq.   5 and 7 , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathsf{quant}(t)]=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{F(t)-\delta/2}^{F(t)+\delta/2}G(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=F^{-1}(F(t))=t. 
 
 


 This proves the unbiasedness. 



 
 It remains to prove the mean squared error bound, which parallels the proof to Lemma   A.1 . Let M_{B}:=\sqrt{5\log B} . We first consider the contribution from |X|\leq M_{B} . By Lemma   A.8 ,( Eq.   6 ), uniformly over |t|\leq M_{B} and |v|\leq 1/2 , 

 

 
 
 G(F(t)+v\delta)=t+\frac{v\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}+o(1)\frac{\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}. 
 
 


 In our case, v=V is uniform on [-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}] , therefore, \mathbb{E}V^{2}=\frac{1}{12} and thus 

 

 
 
 \displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}(t-\mathsf{quant}(t))^{2} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}(t-G(F(t)+V\delta))^{2} 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}\mathbb{E}(V+o(1))^{2} 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\frac{\delta^{2}}{12F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}, 
 
 




 
 uniformly for |t|\leq M_{B} . Now we take the expectation over X_{a} . Using F^{\prime}(t)=A^{-1}(2\pi)^{-1/6}e^{-t^{2}/6} , \delta=\frac{1}{B-1} , and \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{a}^{2}/3}\leq\sqrt{3} , this becomes 

 

 
 
 \displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\left[(X_{a}-\mathsf{quant}(X_{a}))^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{a}|\leq M_{B}\}}\right] 
 \displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\frac{\delta^{2}A^{2}}{12}(2\pi)^{1/3}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}e^{X_{a}^{2}/3} 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle=\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2}+o(1)\right)B^{-2} 
 
 


 which concludes the central contribution. We now bound the tail contribution. Similar to Lemma   A.6 , we use a crude bound following from the construction of G . For every t\in\mathbb{R} and every |v|\leq 1/2 , 

 

 
 
 |G(F(t)+v\delta)|\leq C\left(1+|t|+\frac{\delta}{F^{\prime}(t)}\right). 
 
 


 where C is a constant. Indeed, suppose first that t\geq 0 . In the definition of G in Lemma   A.8 , the values are obtained from the interval around 1/2 by summing midpoint values of (F^{-1})^{\prime} . Since (F^{-1})^{\prime} is increasing on [1/2,1) , the sum up to F(t)+v\delta is bounded by the corresponding integral up to F(t) , plus one endpoint term. This gives C(1+t+\delta/F^{\prime}(t)) . The case t<0 is symmetric, using that F^{-1}(1-s)=-F^{-1}(s) and that (F^{-1})^{\prime} is symmetric around 1/2 . 



 
 Consequently, 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}(t-\mathsf{quant}(t))^{2}\leq C\left(1+t^{2}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{F^{\prime}(t)^{2}}\right). 
 
 


 Applying this with t=X_{a} gives 

 

 
 
 
 \displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}\left[(X_{a}-\mathsf{quant}(X_{a}))^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{a}|>M_{B}\}}\right] 
 
 
 
 
 
 \displaystyle\leq O\left(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[(1+X_{a}^{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{a}|>M_{B}\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[e^{X_{a}^{2}/3}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X_{a}|>M_{B}\}}\right]B^{-2}\right). 
 
 


 Using Lemma   A.2 and the same argument in Lemma   A.1 , this tail contribution is at most 

 

 
 
 O(\mathrm{poly}\log B\cdot e^{-5\log B/2})=o(1)B^{-2}. 
 
 


 Combining the central and tail estimates, we obtain 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,U}(X_{a}-\mathsf{quant}(X_{a}))^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2}+o(1)\right)B^{-2}. 
 
 


 uniformly over all d\geq 1 and all unit vectors a\in\mathbb{R}^{d} .
∎ 





 
 Using Lemma   A.9 , we immediately obtain our main theorem ( Theorem   1.1 ), that \widetilde{x} is unbiased with the claimed mean squared error bounded.



 
 Proof of Theorem   1.1 .

 
 Regarding the mean squared error bound, as in the proof of 2.2 , fix x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} , we have that 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{D,U}(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}))^{2}. 
 
 


 where z_{i}=\sqrt{d}y_{i}=\sqrt{d}(HDx)_{i} follows the distribution of X_{a} for some unit vector a .
Applying Lemma   A.9 to each coordinate gives 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}(z_{i}-\mathsf{quant}(z_{i}))^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}}. 
 
 


 Then averaging over i , we obtain 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}\|x-\widetilde{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o(1)}{B^{2}}. 
 
 




 
 For the unbiasedness, we condition on D . Once D is fixed, the vector y=HDx 
and hence all z_{i}=\sqrt{d}\,y_{i} are deterministic. By the unbiasedness part of Lemma   A.9 , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathsf{quant}(z_{i})\mid D]=z_{i}. 
 
 


 Thus 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}[\widetilde{y}_{i}\mid D]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}_{U}[\mathsf{quant}(z_{i})\mid D]=\frac{z_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}=y_{i}. 
 
 


 Since this holds for every coordinate, 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}[\widetilde{y}\mid D]=y=HDx. 
 
 


 Therefore 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{U}[\widetilde{x}\mid D]=DH^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{U}[\widetilde{y}\mid D]=DH^{\top}HDx=x. 
 
 


 Finally, taking expectation over D gives
 \mathbb{E}_{D,U}[\widetilde{x}]=x .
∎ 









 
 Appendix B Analysis of the inner product quantizer


 
 We present the analysis of the inner product quantizer in this section, proving Theorem  1.2 . Similarly to how the analysis of our vector quantizer relied on a moment generating function bound for Rademacher random variables, the analysis of the inner product quantizer hinges on the following fourth moment bound satisfied by Rademacher random variables:



 
 Lemma B.1 (Mixed fourth moment) . 

 
 Let \eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d} be independent Rademacher signs. Let 

 

 
 
 X=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\eta_{j},\qquad Y=\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\eta_{j}, 
 
 


 where \left\|a\right\|=\left\|b\right\|=1 . Then 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}]=1+2\left\langle a,b\right\rangle^{2}-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}^{2}b_{j}^{2}\leq 3. 
 
 


 Consequently, 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}\bigl[X^{2}(A^{2}+3Y^{2})\bigr]\leq A^{2}+9. 
 
 






 
 Proof.

 
 Write 

 

 
 
 X^{2}=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}+2\sum_{j<k}a_{j}a_{k}\eta_{j}\eta_{k}=1+2\sum_{j<k}a_{j}a_{k}\eta_{j}\eta_{k}, 
 
 


 and similarly 

 

 
 
 Y^{2}=1+2\sum_{j<k}b_{j}b_{k}\eta_{j}\eta_{k}. 
 
 


 Multiplying and taking expectations, all terms vanish except those with the
same pair \{j,k\} . Therefore 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}]=1+4\sum_{j<k}a_{j}a_{k}b_{j}b_{k}. 
 
 


 Since 

 

 
 
 \left\langle a,b\right\rangle^{2}=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}b_{j}^{2}+2\sum_{j<k}a_{j}a_{k}b_{j}b_{k}, 
 
 


 we obtain 

 

 
 
 4\sum_{j<k}a_{j}a_{k}b_{j}b_{k}=2\left\langle a,b\right\rangle^{2}-2\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}b_{j}^{2}. 
 
 


 This proves the identity. The upper bound follows because
 \left\langle a,b\right\rangle^{2}\leq 1 and \sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}b_{j}^{2}\geq 0 . 



 
 Finally, since \mathbb{E}X^{2}=1 , for any \kappa>1 , we have 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}\bigl[X^{2}(A^{2}+(\kappa^{2}-1)Y^{2})\bigr]\leq A^{2}+3(\kappa^{2}-1) 
 
 


 Setting \kappa=2 gives the conclusion.
∎ 





 
 Roadmap.


 
 In what follows we prove an upper bound on the rate of the quantizer, i.e. show that it uses constant number of bits per coordinates, in Section  B.1 . We then analyze the error of the quantizer and prove Theorem  1.2 in Section  B.2 .





 
 B.1 Rate Bound


 
 In this section, we show that the output of the residual quantizer from Algorithm  3 can be stored in O(d) bits.



 
 Lemma B.2 . 

 
 Let B\geq 2 and let r\in\mathbb{R}^{d} such that \|r\|_{2}\leq 2 .
Run the ResidualQuant procedure of Algorithm   3 , using Algorithm   2 
for the residual scale. Then the output \mathsf{idx}_{\sigma},\ell,\lambda can be stored using
 \left(3+\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\right)d+O(\log\log(dB)) total bits. 





 
 Proof.

 
 We consider the non-trivial case \mathsf{idx}_{\sigma}>0 . Recall that v=HDr . Also recall that ScalarDequant outputs \sigma=\tau_{B}2^{\mathsf{idx}_{\sigma}-1}=\tau_{B}2^{\lceil\log_{2}(s/\tau_{B})\rceil} . By this definition, \sigma\geq s:=\frac{\|r\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}} .
Therefore, if we write Z_{i}:=\frac{|v_{i}|}{\sigma} , then 

 

 
 
 \sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}=\frac{\left\|HDr\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\left\|r\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\leq d 
 
 


 where the second step follows from the fact that HD is orthogonal.
Then by the definition of the level, 

 

 
 
 \ell_{i}\leq 1+\log_{2}^{+}(Z_{i}), 
 
 


 where \log_{2}^{+}(s)=\max\{0,\log_{2}s\} . Since
 \log s\leq s^{2}/2 for s\geq 1 , 

 

 
 
 \log_{2}^{+}(Z_{i})\leq\frac{Z_{i}^{2}}{2\ln 2}. 
 
 


 Thus 

 

 
 
 \sum_{i=1}^{d}(\ell_{i}+1)\leq\left(2+\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\right)d. 
 
 




 
 This bounds the bit usage of recording the levels \ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d} . Finally, when \|r\|_{2}\leq 2 , s=\frac{\|r\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\in[0,\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}] , thus as mentioned in Section   4 , \mathsf{idx}_{\sigma} can be encoded using 

 

 
 
 \lceil\log_{2}(\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}/\tau_{B})\rceil+2)\rceil=O(\log\log(dB)) 
 
 


 bits.
 \lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d} can be stored trivially in d total bits. Combining these give the claimed bound. 



 
 ∎ 





 
 Remark B.3 (Expected bits per level) . 

 
 Lemma   B.2 gives a deterministic O(d) bound on the total number of bits. Alternatively, we can also show that each level \ell_{i} can be stored in O(1) bits in expectation.
To that effect, define 

 

 
 
 Y_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{s}=\frac{(HDr)_{i}}{s}. 
 
 


 The coefficient vector of Y_{i} has norm one, since
 |H_{ij}|=d^{-1/2} and s=\left\|r\right\|/\sqrt{d} . Hence 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(|Y_{i}|>t)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/2}. 
 
 


 For k\geq 1 , the event \{\ell_{i}\geq k\} implies 

 

 
 
 |v_{i}|>\sigma 2^{k-1}\geq s2^{k-1}. 
 
 


 Therefore 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{P}(\ell_{i}\geq k)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2^{2(k-1)}}{2}\right), 
 
 


 so the expected unary length of each level is bounded by an absolute constant. 





 
 Remark B.4 (Entropy coding the levels) . 

 
 One could replace the unary encoding of the levels \ell_{i} by a Huffman (or other
entropy) code. This only gives a small improvement: since 

 

 
 
 \Pr(\ell_{i}\geq k)\leq 2\exp\left(-2^{2(k-1)}/2\right)\qquad(k\geq 2), 
 
 


 the level distribution has doubly-exponential tails, and its entropy is bounded
by a small absolute constant. In particular, 

 

 
 
 H(\ell_{i})\leq\frac{2}{e\ln 2}+\sum_{k\geq 2}a_{k}\log_{2}(1/a_{k})<1.579,\qquad a_{k}=2\exp\left(-2^{2(k-1)}/2\right). 
 
 




 
 Thus a prefix code would use, on average, fewer than (H(\ell_{i})+1)+1<3.579 bits per
coordinate including the sign bit, compared with the unary deterministic bound
 3+1/(2\log 2)\approx 3.72 . 







 
 B.2 Error Bounds for the Residual Quantizer


 
 In the next lemma, we bound the error of the residual quantizer (Algorithm  3 ). This guarantee will later be used in the analysis of our inner product quantizer (Algorithm  4 ).



 
 Lemma B.5 . 

 
 Let B\geq 2 and let r,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} .
Run the quantization procedure of Algorithm   3 , using Algorithm   2 
for the residual scale. Then 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}|\left\langle y,\widehat{r}-r\right\rangle|^{2}\leq 13\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}(\left\|r\right\|^{2}+B^{-2})}{d}. 
 
 






 
 Proof.

 
 If y=0 , the claim is immediate. So we consider the non-trivial case where y\neq 0 . Let s=\|r\|/\sqrt{d} . Also, let \mathsf{idx}_{\sigma} be the output of
 \textsc{ScalarQuant}(s) and let \sigma be the output of \textsc{ScalarDequant}(\mathsf{idx}_{\sigma}) . If \sigma=0 , then \widehat{r}=0 and
 s<1/(dB) , so \left\|r\right\|^{2}<1/(dB^{2}) . Hence 

 

 
 
 |\left\langle y,\widehat{r}-r\right\rangle|^{2}\leq\left\|y\right\|^{2}\left\|r\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}{dB^{2}}\leq 13\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}(\left\|r\right\|^{2}+B^{-2})}{d}. 
 
 




 
 Now assume \sigma>0 . Set A=\frac{\sigma}{s} .
As mentioned in Section   4 , by the definition of \sigma we have 1\leq A<2 . Let u=HDy and v=HDr . Conditional on D,r , the
variables q_{i}-v_{i} are independent and centered, and 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}\left[|\left\langle y,\widehat{r}-r\right\rangle|^{2}\mid D,r\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}^{2}(R_{i}^{2}-v_{i}^{2}). 
 
 


 Define 

 

 
 
 X_{i}=\frac{\sqrt{d}\,u_{i}}{\left\|y\right\|},\qquad Y_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{s}. 
 
 


 Since R_{i}=\sigma 2^{\ell_{i}}=As2^{\ell_{i}} and \ell_{i} is chosen to be the smallest level such that |v_{i}|\leq R , either \ell_{i}=0 in which case R_{i}=As , or \ell_{i}\geq 1 , in which case |v_{i}|>As2^{\ell_{i}-1} and multiplying by 2 gives R_{i}<2|v_{i}| . In both cases, we have 

 

 
 
 R_{i}^{2}-v_{i}^{2}\leq s^{2}\bigl(A^{2}+(2^{2}-1)Y_{i}^{2}\bigr). 
 
 


 Therefore 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}|\left\langle y,\widehat{r}-r\right\rangle|^{2}\leq\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}\left\|r\right\|^{2}}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{i}^{2}\bigl(A^{2}+3Y_{i}^{2}\bigr)\bigr]. 
 
 


 For each i , the coefficient vectors defining X_{i} and Y_{i} have norm one,
so the mixed fourth-moment lemma gives 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{i}^{2}\bigl(A^{2}+3Y_{i}^{2}\bigr)\bigr]\leq A^{2}+9. 
 
 


 Since A<2 , this yields 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}|\left\langle y,\widehat{r}-r\right\rangle|^{2}\leq 13\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}\left\|r\right\|^{2}}{d}\leq 13\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}(\left\|r\right\|^{2}+B^{-2})}{d}. 
 
 


 ∎ 







 
 B.3 Proof of Theorem   1.2 


 
 We now prove Theorem  1.2 , restated here for convenience of the reader:



 
 Theorem B.6 (Repeating Theorem   1.2 ) . 

 
 Let x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} be a unit vector and let
 y\in\mathbb{R}^{d} . Let \widehat{x} be the reconstructed vector produced by Algorithm   4 with a base bit-width b .
Then 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}|\left\langle y,\widehat{x}-x\right\rangle|^{2}\leq 13\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{2}+1+o(1)\right)\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}{d\cdot 4^{b}}, 
 
 


 where the o(1) term vanishes as b\to\infty , uniformly over d and x . 



 
 Moreover, the output of Quantize (x) from Algorithm   4 can be stored using 

 

 
 
 db+(3+\frac{1}{2\ln 2})d+O(\log(b+\log d)) 
 
 


 total bits. 





 
 Proof.

 
 We write B=2^{b} . Recall that the two-stage quantizer outputs
 \widehat{x}=\widetilde{x}+\widehat{r} , where
 r=x-\widetilde{x} . Condition on the randomness of the first stage, so
that r is fixed. By Lemma   B.5 , 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D_{\mathrm{res}}}\left[|\left\langle y,\widehat{r}-r\right\rangle|^{2}\mid D_{\mathrm{base}},U\right]\leq 13\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}(\left\|r\right\|^{2}+B^{-2})}{d}. 
 
 


 Taking expectation over the first stage gives 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}|\left\langle y,\widehat{x}-x\right\rangle|^{2}\leq 13\frac{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{D_{\mathrm{base}},U}\left\|x-\widetilde{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}+B^{-2}\right). 
 
 




 
 If we use \overline{x} to denote \widetilde{x} before projecting to the unit ball via \Pi , then by Theorem   2.2 , we have 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D_{\mathrm{base}},U}\left\|x-\overline{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o_{B}(1)}{B^{2}}. 
 
 


 Since the unit ball is a closed convex set and x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1} , point-wise it always hold that 

 

 
 
 \|x-\widetilde{x}\|^{2}_{2}=\|x-\Pi\overline{x}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}_{2} 
 
 


 thus 

 

 
 
 \mathbb{E}_{D_{\mathrm{base}},U}\left\|x-\widetilde{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\sqrt{3}/2+o_{B}(1)}{B^{2}}. 
 
 


 Substituting this bound proves the theorem. The total bit usage is db bits for \mathsf{idx} plus the bit usage from Lemma   B.2 . Note that we have \|r\|\leq 2 because after projecting onto the unit ball, both \|x\|,\|\widetilde{x}\|\leq 1 . 



 
 ∎
