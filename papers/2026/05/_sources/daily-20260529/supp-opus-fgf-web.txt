L49: We’re upgrading Claude Opus to a new version: Claude Opus 4.8. It builds on Opus 4.7 with improvements across benchmarks, and is a more effective collaborator. It’s available today for the same price.
L50: Opus 4.8 launches alongside several new features. Users on claude.ai now have control over the amount of effort Claude puts into a task. Claude Code has a new “dynamic workflows” feature that allows it to tackle very large-scale problems. And fast mode for Opus 4.8—where the model can work at 2.5× the speed—is now three times cheaper than it was for previous models.

L142: One of the most prominent improvements in Opus 4.8 is its honesty. We train all our models to be honest—for instance, to avoid making claims that they can’t support. But a general problem with AI models is that they sometimes jump to conclusions, confidently claiming to have made progress in their work despite the evidence being thin. Early testers report that Opus 4.8 is more likely to flag uncertainties about its work and less likely to make unsupported claims.
L143: This is borne out in cite17†our evaluations , which show that Opus 4.8 is around four times less likely than its predecessor to allow flaws in code it has written to pass unremarked.
L144: As always, we ran a detailed alignment assessment on the model before release. In terms of positive traits, our Alignment team concluded that Opus 4.8 “reaches new highs on our measures of prosocial traits like supporting user autonomy and acting in the user’s best interest.” The assessment also showed Opus 4.8 to have rates of misaligned behavior (such as deception or cooperation with misuse) that are substantially lower than Opus 4.7, and similar to our best-aligned model, Claude Mythos Preview.
L145: The full alignment assessment, accompanied by a suite of pre-deployment safety tests, is reported in the Claude Opus 4.8 System Card.
L146: cite30†Image L147: ## Also launching today
L148: 
L149: In addition to Claude Opus 4.8, we’re making the following updates:
L150:   * Dynamic workflows. This new feature, available in research preview, allows Claude to take on even bigger tasks in Claude Code. Claude can plan the work and then run hundreds of parallel subagents in a single session (and with Opus 4.8, the agents can run for even longer). It then verifies its outputs before reporting back to the user.
L151: For example, Claude Code with Opus 4.8 can now carry out codebase-scale migrations across hundreds of thousands of lines of code from kickoff to merge, with the existing test suite as its bar. You can read more about dynamic workflows—available in Claude Code for Enterprise, Team, and Max plans—in cite31†this post†claude.com .
L152:   * Effort control in cite32†claude.ai†claude.ai and Cowork. A new control alongside the model selector lets users choose how much effort Claude puts into a response. On higher effort settings, Claude will think more frequently and more deeply to give better responses. On lower effort settings, Claude will respond faster and use up a user’s rate limits more slowly. Users now have this choice—the effort control is available on all plans.
L153:   * The Messages API now accepts system entries inside the messages array. Developers can update Claude’s instructions mid-task without breaking the prompt cache or routing the update through a user turn. This can be used in a given harness to update permissions, token budgets, or environment context as an agent runs.
L154: cite33†Image: Video thumbnail L155: ## A note on effort
L156: Opus 4.8 defaults to high effort, which we judge to be the best overall balance of quality and user experience. On coding tasks, this effort level spends a similar number of tokens as Opus 4.7’s default, but with better performance. Users can choose “extra” (“`xhigh`” in Claude Code) or “max,” and the model will spend more tokens to get better results; we recommend using “extra” for difficult tasks and long-running asynchronous workflows.
L157: We have increased rate limits in Claude Code to accommodate the higher token usage of higher effort levels; users can select whichever makes sense for their particular project.
L158: ## What’s next?
L159: 
L160: Users will find Opus 4.8 to be a modest but tangible improvement on its predecessor. There’s still more to be done: we’re working on developing and releasing models that provide many of the same capabilities as Opus at a lower cost.
L161: Not only that, but we plan to release a new class of model with even higher intelligence than Opus. As part of cite34†Project Glasswing , a small number of organizations are currently using Claude Mythos Preview for cybersecurity work. Models of this capability level require stronger cyber safeguards before they can be generally released. We’re making swift progress on developing these safeguards and expect to be able to bring Mythos-class models to all our customers in the coming weeks.
L162: ## Availability
L163: 
L164: Claude Opus 4.8 is available everywhere today. Pricing for regular usage is unchanged from Opus 4.7: $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens. Pricing for fast mode is $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens. Developers can use `claude-opus-4-8` via the cite35†Claude API†platform.claude.com .

L41@P1-2: Frontier Governance Framework1 Introduction
L42@P2: OpenAI’s mission is to ensure that artificial general intelligence
L43@P2: (AGI) benefits all of humanity. To pursue that mission, we are
L44@P2: committed to safely developing and deploying highly capable AI
L45@P2: models, which create significant benefits and also bring new
L46@P2: risks. We build for safety at every step and share our learnings so
L47@P2: that society can make well-informed choices to manage new
L48@P2: risks from frontier AI.
L49@P2: 
L50@P2: We are committed to transparency about how our safety
L51@P2: practices align with emerging global legal and governance
L52@P2: frameworks. To that end, we have developed this Frontier
L53@P2: Governance Framework (FGF) to explain how we fulfill our
L54@P2: regulatory obligations and to document our current technical
L55@P2: and organizational processes for systemic risk assessment and
L56@P2: mitigation across key risk categories, including cybersecurity,
L57@P2: CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear), harmful
L58@P2: manipulation, and loss of control risks.
L59@P2: 
L60@P2: Our FGF is designed to meet the baseline legal requirements of
L61@P2: various frontier AI laws, including:
L62@P2: 
L63@P2: Under California’s Transparency in Frontier AI Act (TFAIA),
L64@P2: this FGF is our Frontier AI Framework, documenting OpenAI’s
L65@P2: technical and organizational protocols to manage, assess,
L66@P2: and mitigate catastrophic risks, as defined under the TFAIA.
L67@P2: 
L68@P2: Under the European Union’s General-Purpose AI Code of
L69@P2: Practice (the EU CoP), this FGF serves as our publicly
L70@P2: available summary of OpenAI’s Safety & Security Framework,
L71@P2: describing how we assess and mitigate systemic risks and
L72@P2: ensure adequate cybersecurity protection for models
L73@P2: covered under Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the EU AI Act).
L74@P2-3: 01 Frontier Governance FrameworkThe FGF overlaps in some areas with our existing Preparedness
L75@P3: Framework (PF). The PF and FGF together describe OpenAI’s
L76@P3: practices, and we will continue to use and evolve the PF to
L77@P3: define and operationalize OpenAI’s own approach to managing
L78@P3: the most serious risks from advanced AI systems, including in
L79@P3: situations where our internal practices go beyond current legal
L80@P3: requirements. For example, because the PF is intended both to
L81@P3: advance the science of managing severe risks of advanced AI
L82@P3: systems and to reflect OpenAI’s evolving practices, it may use
L83@P3: different definitions of catastrophic risk and does not depend on
L84@P3: specific legal compute thresholds like the FGF.
L85@P3: 
L86@P3: The FGF covers frontier models as defined under the TFAIA and
L87@P3: “general-purpose models with systemic risk” under the EU AI
L88@P3: Act. In this document, when we refer to “systemic” risks, we
L89@P3: mean both catastrophic risks under the TFAIA and systemic
L90@P3: risks under the EU AI Act. The systemic risk assessment and
L91@P3: mitigation processes described here apply to covered models
L92@P3: that OpenAI has deployed externally, and in some cases
L93@P3: internally with respect to risks resulting from circumventing
L94@P3: oversight mechanisms. We expect our approach will continue to
L95@P3: evolve, and we will update this FGF as our processes and
L96@P3: regulatory requirements change.
L97@P3: Our approach to mitigating frontier AI safety risks is informed
L98@P3: 
L99@P3: by emerging national and international AI risk management
L100@P3: standards, including ISO 42001, the NIST AI Risk Management
L101@P3: Framework, and frontier safety laws in relevant jurisdictions.
L102@P3: 
L103@P3: Our approach also draws on the proposal for Responsible
L104@P3: Scaling Policies first introduced by METR as well as industry
L105@P3: best practices.

L107@P4:

L109@P4:

L111@P4: 2.1 Systemic risk identification
L112@P4: OpenAI has implemented a variety of structured processes to
L113@P4: identify systemic risks stemming from our frontier models and to
L114@P4: develop risk scenarios and threat models through which these
L115@P4: systemic risks may develop or materialize.
L116@P4: 
L117@P4: This FGF’s definition of systemic risk includes foreseeable and
L118@P4: material risks of severe harm
L119@P4: 
L120@P4: from the development, storage, use, or deployment of our most
L121@P4: advanced frontier models, including risks that a model will
L122@P4: materially contribute to greater than 50 fatalities or $1 billion of
L123@P4: property damages or losses arising from a single incident.
L124@P4: 
L125@P4: We evaluate whether frontier capabilities create a risk of severe
L126@P4: harm through a holistic risk assessment process. This process
L127@P4: draws on our own internal research and signals, and, where
L128@P4: appropriate, incorporates feedback from academic researchers,
L129@P4: independent domain experts, industry bodies such as the
L130@P4: Frontier Model Forum, and the U.S. government, the European
L131@P4: Commission and other government agencies, as well as relevant
L132@P4: legal and policy guidance and requirements.
L133@P4-5: 03 Frontier Governance FrameworkBased on this analysis, this FGF definition currently
L134@P5: addresses the following systemic risk categories:
L135@P5: Cyber offense Risks of enabling large-scale sophisticated cyberattacks, including on critical systems, such as by
L136@P5: significantly lowering the barriers to entry for
L137@P5: malicious actors, or significantly increasing the
L138@P5: potential impact achieved in offensive cyber
L139@P5: operations, e.g. through automated vulnerability
L140@P5: discovery, exploit generation, operational use, and
L141@P5: attack scaling.
L142@P5: Chemical,
L143@P5: biological,
L144@P5: radiological &
L145@P5: nuclear (CBRN)
L146@P5: Risks of enabling chemical, biological, radiological,
L147@P5: and nuclear (CBRN) attacks or accidents, such as by
L148@P5: significantly lowering the barriers to entry for
L149@P5: malicious actors, or significantly increasing the
L150@P5: potential impact achieved, in the design,
L151@P5: development, acquisition, release, distribution, and
L152@P5: use of related weapons or materials.
L153@P5: Harmful
L154@P5: manipulation
L155@P5: Risks stemming from the strategic distortion of
L156@P5: human behavior, including the use of model
L157@P5: capabilities to conduct influence operations, election
L158@P5: interference, or other coordinated campaigns to
L159@P5: manipulate public opinion or undermine democratic
L160@P5: processes.
L161@P5: Loss of control Risks stemming from the inability to reliably
L162@P5: 
L163@P5: direct, modify, or shut down a model, including
L164@P5: evading the controls of a model developer or user, or
L165@P5: autonomous conduct that, if conducted by a human,
L166@P5: would constitute a crime of murder, assault,
L167@P5: extortion, or theft.
L168@P5-6: 04 Frontier Governance Framework2.2 Systemic risk analysis
L169@P6: Our risk assessment process may take place throughout a
L170@P6: model’s lifecycle, including during development and after
L171@P6: deployment. Risk assessments draw on various sources,
L172@P6: including a range of model evaluations, model-independent
L173@P6: information such as external research and data, literature
L174@P6: reviews, market analyses, consultation with internal and external
L175@P6: experts, insights from other deployed models, post-release
L176@P6: monitoring, and, if applicable, investigations of serious incidents
L177@P6: or critical safety incidents.
L178@P6: 
L179@P6: We conduct systemic risk modelling that informs how we
L180@P6: evaluate systemic risks before deployment. Currently, we
L181@P6: estimate the severity and probability of harm of risks related to
L182@P6: CBRN, cyber offense, and loss of control under this FGF. We are
L183@P6: still in the early stages of developing an approach for assessing
L184@P6: risks from harmful manipulation. Many of the risks stemming
L185@P6: from harmful manipulation, such as the use of model capabilities
L186@P6: to conduct influence operations, are best addressed through
L187@P6: system level mitigations, such as post-deployment monitoring,
L188@P6: rather than model evaluations before deployment.
L189@P6: We employ a risk assessment process that includes state-of-theart evaluations to test the specific threats and risk scenarios
L190@P6: identified through our threat modeling, assess a model’s
L191@P6: capabilities against systemic risk thresholds, and understand
L192@P6: and assess the effectiveness of our safeguards and mitigations.
L193@P6: We estimate systemic risks where appropriate and practicable
L194@P6: using risk tiers that concretely describe things an AI system
L195@P6: might be able to help someone do or might be able to do on its
L196@P6: own that could meaningfully increase risk of severe harm.
L197@P6-7: 05 Frontier Governance Framework2.3 Systemic risk acceptance determination
L198@P7: We employ science-backed evaluations that inform whether a
L199@P7: covered model presents systemic risks that remain within
L200@P7: acceptable levels and help us assess residual risk. Our
L201@P7: assessment recognizes that progress in model scaffolding and
L202@P7: elicitation techniques can add uncertainty, and we accordingly
L203@P7: treat one-time capability elicitations in a frontier model as a
L204@P7: lower-bound, rather than a ceiling, on capabilities that may
L205@P7: emerge in real world use and misuse. Our consideration of
L206@P7: residual risk takes account of the scale and probability of harm,
L207@P7: and is evaluated in light of the sufficiency of implemented
L208@P7: safeguards proportionate to the level of risk. In assessing
L209@P7: whether to accept residual risk, we review the risk tiers for each
L210@P7: systemic risk category and incorporate appropriate safety
L211@P7: margins. For example, out of an abundance of caution we have
L212@P7: treated models as crossing a capability threshold in
L213@P7: circumstances where we are unable to rule out that a new
L214@P7: threshold had been reached, even in the absence of direct
L215@P7: evidence that it has occurred.
L216@P7: 
L217@P7: Before deployment, models in scope of this Framework undergo
L218@P7: a suite of scalable evaluations designed to measure proxies that
L219@P7: approximate whether a model’s systemic risks reach a new risk
L220@P7: tier based on levels of performance that have been predetermined to indicate that a deployment may have reached a
L221@P7: capability threshold. Some evaluations can help rule out that a
