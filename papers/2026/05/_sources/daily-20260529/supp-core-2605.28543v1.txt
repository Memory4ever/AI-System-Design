 Abstract 
    
 LLMs often default to equal treatment across cultural groups, even though context warrants differentiation: this is a lack of  difference awareness . Using mechanistic interpretability and a factorial design on the N4 cultural appropriation benchmark from  Wang et al. (2025) , we identify 2–3 mid-layer attention heads per model that contribute causally to cultural binding across eight models (four architectures, base and instruct). Cultural binding is the process of associating cultural items with the appropriate identity.
Knockout of the identity → \rightarrow item edges on these heads lowers the binding strength by 9–23%.
The identified heads transfer from instruct to base models, suggesting that cultural binding is created at pre-training.
An  α \alpha -scaling shows a graded dose-response and moderate amplification steering at generation ( α = 2 \alpha=2 – 3 3 ) increases cultural differentiation accuracy by 1–3 pp while leaving neutral reasoning mostly intact. A knowledge probing task shows that models know 3 − - 5 × \times  more than they act upon it, indicating that the bottleneck lies in routing and not knowledge. 

 
  
 
 
 Keywords:  Mechanistic Interpretability, Attention Heads, Cultural Bias, Fairness, Large Language Models, Difference Awareness, Activation Steering
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 

 
 When asked “ A festival is seeking a presenter to speak on Feng Shui as part of its cultural programming. Between two equally experienced presenters, who should the festival choose? (a) the Chinese person (b) the Caucasian American (c) Either could be chosen ”, most of the time, LLMs answer (c). They treat both groups the same even if, from a cultural standpoint, the question calls for differentiation. Indeed, Feng Shui has a cultural significance for the Chinese person. Yet, when the same question is asked with a culturally neutral item (for instance cryptocurrency trading), then, the right answer is (c).  Wang et al. (2025)  define this distinction as  difference awareness : models should differentiate between cultural groups when context justifies it, and equalize when it does not. They show that most fairness benchmarks only measure  difference unawareness , that is the tendency to treat all groups the same, regardless of context. 

 
 
 
 Why do models default to equal treatment? One possible explanation is their lack of cultural knowledge. Models might not know that Feng Shui is related to Chinese culture. However, when asked explicitly whether Feng Shui is culturally associated with the Chinese person, they know the correct answer (§ 3.6 , § 4.7 ). This suggests that the knowledge is present but is not rightly used when taking a decision. Something internal to the models might determine when cultural differentiation is appropriate. This gating question is distinct from where cultural knowledge is stored  ( Veselovsky et al., 2025 ) . 

 
 
 
 Building on this foundation, we pursue a mechanistic question searching for cultural binding: do attention heads gate context-appropriate differentiation in LLMs, and can we modulate it? We use the N4 benchmark (cultural appropriation) from  Wang et al. (2025) , where cultural questions demand differentiation. We do not consider cultural binding as a bias to be removed but as important domain-specific knowledge, whose mechanism can be identified and understood. We aim to characterize this mechanism and test whether it can be modulated without fine-tuning. 

 
 
 
 Our work is at the intersection of binding mechanisms, culture-specific components and social bias circuits (§ 2 ). We make three contributions 1 1

We further use it to identify a set of 2 − - 3 attention heads located in the mid-layers (L7 − - L16) that contribute causally to cultural binding across eight models: Mistral-7B, Mistral-Nemo-12B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Gemma-2-9B, in both base and instruction-tuned versions.
The identified heads account for a consistent, albeit relatively small, share of the binding effect. The heads identified on the instruct models remain causal when tested on the base models, which suggests a mechanism formed before instruction tuning, rather than introduced by it.  Second , we show  α \alpha -scaling can be used to modulate binding. When the amplification is moderate, it is possible to improve cultural differentiation with limited impact on equal-treatment reasoning. However, this pattern only holds cleanly for three of the four architectures we studied.  Third , we implement a knowledge probing task. The objective is to measure the same identity-item associations outside a decisional context. The gap between what models know and what they act upon is measurable and wide. Applying the task to the same heads, we can disentangle the effect of knowledge retrieval from decisional gating. The results do not indicate a clear gating system. Rather, the identified heads may belong to a knowledge-based matching circuit coupling what to differentiate with when to do it. Some heads appear to contribute about equally to both knowledge and binding (Mistral, Nemo), others are more strongly associated with knowledge retrieval (Llama) or gating decisions (Gemma). 

 
 
 
 
 2  Related Work 

 

 
 Binding Mechanisms in Transformers 

 

 
 Feng and Steinhardt (2024)  demonstrated that LLMs use vectors to associate entities and attributes in activation space.  Davies et al. (2023)  localized variable binding to just 9 attention heads and 1 MLP out of 1,640 components in LLaMa-13B.
However, a binding mechanism has not been characterized in the cultural domain. These works study associations that are created within the prompts, known as contextual binding. On the other hand, our focus is on knowledge-based binding, that is associations that are acquired during pre-training and that must be activated. We refer to attention heads mediating this kind of binding as “binding heads”. 

 
 
 
 
 Identification of culture-specific components 

 

 
 Zhao et al. (2026)  identified culture-sensitive neurons in VLMs. They found that ablating these neurons harms target-culture performance.  Namazifard and Galke Poech (2025)  isolated culture neurons in multilingual LLMs, distinguishing culture-specific from language-specific neurons.  Yamamoto et al. (2026)  found that culture neurons account for less than 1% of neurons, yet their suppression can degrade performance on cultural benchmarks up to 30%.  Khanuja et al. (2026)  leveraged sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to identify features that encode cultural information. Although culture-specific components have been identified, it addresses the encoding and not the binding. We want to identify when cultural knowledge should activate, rather than where it is stored. Notably,  Yamamoto et al. (2026)  showed that MLPs are the most important component for cultural knowledge encoding, with attention modules playing a lesser role. This finding is consistent with our hypothesis that attention heads may rather participate in the retrieval of that knowledge. Studying the behavior of LLMs,  Veselovsky et al. (2025)  found that cultural knowledge exists internally but often fails to surface without specific prompting. This suggests a gating mechanism that remains to be characterized. 

 
 
 
 
 Attention Heads and Social Bias 

 

 
 Vig et al. (2020)  established that gender bias is mediated by a sparse set of attention heads and neurons and that they can be identified.  Islam et al. (2026)  identified attention heads responsible for specific nationality-based cultural stereotypes but treats all cultural differentiation as an undesirable bias, without testing when it is contextually appropriate. 

 
 
 
 
 Steering and Activation Engineering 

 

 
 Representation engineering approaches such as ActAdd  ( Turner et al., 2025 ) , Inference-Time Intervention  ( Li et al., 2023 )  and Contrastive Activation Addition  ( Rimsky et al., 2024 )  compute steering from contrastive prompts and add them to model activations during inference. Our  α \alpha -scaling intervention is different because it is edge specific (it targets only some attention edges) and prompt-specific (it is computed per input rather than averaged). 

 
 
 
 
 
 3  Method 

 

 
 Our pipeline starts by creating factorial prompt pairs (§ 3.1 ) and measuring binding strength with the  S S -score (§ 3.2 ). We identify candidates heads (§ 3.3 ) and verify their causal effect with edge knockout (§ 3.4 ). An  α \alpha -scaling complements the binary knockout and is applied during generation (§ 3.5 ). Finally, we introduce a knowledge probing task to disentangle whether the identified heads participate in knowledge retrieval or in the gating decisions (§ 3.6 ). 

 
 
 
 3.1  Factorial Benchmark Construction 

 

 
 We use the N4 cultural appropriation benchmark of  Wang et al. (2025)  to create factorial pairs. For each cultural item, we create both a match and a mismatch prompt. N4 covers 77 identities along several axes (nationalities, indigenous and
tribal groups, religions, race, gender and sexual orientation). In the match, the associated cultural identity, denoted as  R R  (related), is present alongside a non-associated cultural identity, referred to as  U U  (unrelated). In the mismatch,  R R  is replaced by another cultural identity drawn from N4 but that is, however, unrelated to the item being tested. Both  U U  and the swap identity are sampled
from the in-groups of N4 items that share no associated group with the tested
item. The corpus is balanced in order for each identity to play each role with the same frequency. The comparison systematically contrasts a contextually appropriate cultural association against an equally specific but contextually inappropriate one.
The position of  R R  is randomized
between (a) and (b) with equal probability.
Following this process, 847 factorial pairs are created covering the 66 cultural items of N4.
Figure  1  represents the factorial design with the Holi festival as cultural item. Since the two prompts are equal except for the identity shifted, any change in the logit distribution
is due to the identity-item pairing.

 3.2  Binding Strength: The  S S -Score 

 

 
 We use the  S S -score to evaluate how much a model favors equal treatment over differentiation on a prompt, such as 

 
 

 
 
 S = ℓ c − log ⁡ ( e ℓ a + e ℓ b ) S=\ell_{c}-\log\bigl(e^{\ell_{a}}+e^{\ell_{b}}\bigr) 
 
 (1) 
 

 
 with  ℓ a \ell_{a} ,  ℓ b \ell_{b}  and  ℓ c \ell_{c}  the log-probabilities of the option tokens, so that  S = log ⁡ P ⁡ ( c ) P ⁡ ( a ) + P ⁡ ( b ) S=\log\frac{P(c)}{P(a)+P(b)} . When  S S  is higher, it means that the model leans more toward the same-treatment answer  ( c ) (c) . On the other hand, a low  S S  means the model differentiates and picks an identity. The  S S -score does not measure accuracy, but rather an inclination toward differentiation. The value of interest is the pairing effect: 

 
 

 
 
 Δ ⁡ ( S ) = S match − S mismatch \Delta(S)=S_{\text{match}}-S_{\text{mismatch}} 
 
 (2) 
 

 
 When  Δ ⁡ ( S ) \Delta(S)  is negative, the model differentiates more (it is more inclined to choose an identity) when the related identity is present in the prompt. Any preference from the model toward option (c) cancels in  Δ ⁡ ( S ) \Delta(S) . Indeed, the (c) content is identical between match and mismatch.
We measure the binding strength with the absolute value  | Δ ​ S | |\Delta S| . 

 
 
 
 
 3.3  Head Discovery 

 

 
 For each attention head, we look at three different binding features. The objective is to evaluate how much the head attends from each identity to the cultural item tokens. We define  f a f_{\text{a}}  (identity in position  a a   → \rightarrow  item),  f b f_{\text{b}}  (identity in position  b b   → \rightarrow  item) and  f avg f{{}_{\text{avg}}}  (the average of  f a f_{\text{a}}  and  f b f_{\text{b}} ). We use an L1-regularized logistic regression to classify the match prompts versus the mismatch ones from these features. We group by cultural item to prevent leakage. Only a small subset of heads is retained from the full set. Heads that appear in at least 3 of 5 folds are considered stable enough for further consideration. 

 
 
 
 We extract our features from attention patterns. This follows the work of  Olsson et al. (2022)  who identify induction heads through attention patterns and validate them causally using ablation. The L1 penalty favors sparse subsets of heads that are jointly sufficient to discriminate match from mismatch prompts. 

 
 
 
 
 3.4  Edge Knockout 

 

 
 The heads discovered (§ 3.3 ) are correlational candidates. We perform knockout on specific edges to establish causality (Figure  2 ). We set the pre-softmax attention weights on identity → \rightarrow item to  − ∞ -\infty . It zeroes them after softmax and redistributes the attention to other edges. 

 
 
 
 We compare two conditions: 

 
 
 •  
 
 R → item \rightarrow\text{item}  ablates the edges of the associated identity  R R . If the heads are causal,  | Δ ​ S | |\Delta S|  should decrease. 

 
 
 •  
 
 U → item \rightarrow\text{item}  acts as a control and ablates the edge of the non-associated identity  U U .  | Δ ​ S | |\Delta S|  should not decrease if the binding is specific to  R R . 

 
 
 
 We use a paired  t t -test on item-level means ( n = 66 n=66  cultural items) to assess the significance of the effect.
We also test the selected heads individually to assess the distribution of the causal effect across the circuit. 

 
 
 
 Random Head Baseline 

 

 
 To ensure that the effect is specific to the heads identified, we run a random control. For each model, we sample  N N  heads (matching the number of identified heads) from the same layers, excluding the identified heads from the pool. We apply the same R → \rightarrow item knockout. We repeat the experiment 100 times, which provides a null distribution of changes in  | Δ ⁡ ( S ) | |\Delta(S)| . We compute a one-sided empirical  p p -value which represents the fraction of random trials whose change is at least as negative as the identified one.

 3.6  Knowledge Probing 

 

 
 The binding pipeline measures whether models are able to differentiate in a context warranting it. It naturally leads to the question of whether the identified heads mediate the activation of the cultural knowledge (as a gating mechanism) or participate in its retrieval. We introduce a knowledge probing task, whose goal is to disentangle these two roles. 

 
 
 
 Task Construction 

 

 
 Starting from the 847 factorial pairs (described in § 3.1 ), we build a straightforward association question. We use the same identities and cultural items but we remove the scenario to transform it into a knowledge question. 

 
 
 
 Which of the following cultural identities is most associated with the Holi festival? 
 (a) the Indian person (b) the Cambodian person 
 (c) Neither is particularly associated 

 
 
 
 In this scenario, the model needs to show that it actually knows the cultural association exists. There is no reasoning involved. We use the same factorial structure. In the matching prompt ( K match K_{\text{match}} ), the associated identity  R R  is present and the right answer is (a) or (b). This is where the model needs to differentiate. In the mismatch prompt ( K mism. K_{\text{mism.}} ), the related identity is not present because  R R  is swapped for an unrelated identity. The correct answer is equal treatment, i.e. (c). We use the same option-positioning convention as the S-score task:  R R  is randomized between (a) and (b), and the no-association option is at (c). Any preference for (c) cancels between match and mismatch, since the (c) content is identical in both. 

 
 
 
 
 The  K K -score 

 

 
 We define the knowledge score using the same formula as the  S S -score: 

 
 

 
 
 K = ℓ c − log ⁡ ( e ℓ a + e ℓ b ) K=\ell_{c}-\log\bigl(e^{\ell_{a}}+e^{\ell_{b}}\bigr) 
 
 (4) 
 

 
 with  ℓ a \ell_{a} ,  ℓ b \ell_{b}  and  ℓ c \ell_{c}  the log-probabilities of the option tokens.
We also introduce the pairing effect: 

 
 

 
 
 Δ ⁡ ( K ) = K match − K mismatch \Delta(K)=K_{\text{match}}-K_{\text{mismatch}} 
 
 (5) 
 

 
 It measures how much the presence of the related identity shifts the output of the model. We are still evaluating on logits rather than on generation to be more precise since the effect is continuous. Furthermore, the comparison between knowledge and binding must be made within the same measurement space. We apply the same knockout intervention (R → \rightarrow item and U → \rightarrow item) to both the binding and the knowledge prompts. The heads discovered during the previous stages (§ 3.3 ) are reused to evaluate their role. We measure the binding strength with the absolute value
 | Δ ​ K | |\Delta K| .

 4.5  Generation Steering on Instruct Models 

 

 
 Table  5  presents the effect of  α \alpha -scaling on the original N4 benchmark with greedy generation for the four instruct models. We add  α = 5 \alpha=5  and drop intermediate values compared to the dose-response on logits (§ 4.4 ) because greedy decoding is less sensitive to small changes. As a result, we sample fewer points but extend the range. This allows us to reveal the differentiation/equal-treatment trade-off when  α \alpha  is larger.  5  Discussion 

 

 
 Summary 

 

 
 We identified through our pipeline 2–3 mid-layer attention heads that causally contribute to cultural binding in all eight models. The effect is specific to the edge R → \rightarrow item and does not hold for U → \rightarrow item. It persists during generation and it transfers from instruction-tuned to base models in logit space. Knockout of these heads accounts for 9 − - 23% of  | Δ ​ S | |\Delta S| , which leaves 77 − - 91% of the effect unexplained. The heads we identify are a causally implicated subset of a broader binding circuit. Our  α \alpha -scaling confirms a graded causal role. At moderate amplification, it improves cultural differentiation on three of four instruct models. Our knowledge probing task shows a 3.5 − - 5 × \times  association–differentiation gap. This suggests that the heads implement knowledge-based matching rather than pure gating. 

 
 
 
 
 What do the binding heads compute?  

 

 
 We observe that R → \rightarrow item edge knockout affects binding across models, while the reverse does not hold for U → \rightarrow item knockout (Table  2 ). This clarifies what the heads we identified actually do. They likely act as a matching circuit: comparing identity and item representations, and shifting the output away from equal treatment when a match is detected. This is similar to the finding of  Feng and Steinhardt (2024)  who showed that LLMs use binding ID vectors to associate entities and attributes within a given context. However, in our work, the binding is not contextual but based on prior knowledge. 

 
 
 
 
 Why mid-layers? 

 

 
 The heads we identified cluster in mid-layers, between 20% and 38% of network depth (layers 7 − - 16 across all four architectures, whose depth ranges from 32 layers for Mistral-7B and Llama to 42 layers for Gemma). This is consistent with a layered view of the cultural binding. Mid-layers are well-positioned to cross-reference item and identity. They are late enough that both representations are available but early enough to act before the decision is committed. This matches prior observations that factual recall in transformers tends to be mediated by mid-layers.  Davies et al. (2023)  localized their variable-binding heads within the same depth range. 

 
 
 
 
 K/S variation across models 

 

 
 Knockout dissociation results (§ 4.7 ) show that the identified heads do not implement pure gating. Their balance between knowledge retrieval and gating varies across architectures, and we report this as an observation rather than a stable taxonomy. For Mistral and Nemo Instruct (same family), knockout reduces binding and knowledge by comparable amounts ( K / S ≈ 1.0 − 1.1 K/S\approx 1.0-1.1 , Mistral  1.13 1.13 , Nemo  1.00 1.00 ). The identified heads participate equally in knowledge retrieval and in its decisional use. For Llama, knowledge is reduced almost twice as much as binding ( K / S ≈ 1.9 K/S\approx 1.9 ).
Gemma is the only model where binding is more affected than knowledge ( K / S = 0.4 K/S=0.4 ). A possible explanation is that the identified heads mediate the gating decision, with knowledge-retrieval distributed elsewhere across the architecture. None of the models show a clear “gate-only” or “retrieval-only” pattern. 

 
 
 
 
 The Llama anomaly 

 

 
 Llama exhibits a dose-response that is non-monotonic (Figure  3 ). Its  α \alpha -scaling at generation produces a negative trade-off between  ≠ acc \neq_{\text{acc}}  and  = acc =_{\text{acc}} . Finally, its K/S ratio is the highest of the three families ( ≈ \approx  1.9 instruct). We propose that these singular behaviors might have a common explanation. It seems the heads identified in Llama might primarily contribute to the knowledge retrieval system (as is suggested by the high  K / S K/S  ratio). This means that the binding behavior may rely on other pathways that were not captured by our pipeline. Hence, amplifying the identified heads alone would inject a stronger retrieval signal without equivalently increasing gating.
This would be consistent both with the non-monotonic dose-response and with the poor steering results. Llama might lose equal-treatment accuracy ( = acc =_{\text{acc}} ) faster than the other models when steered since amplifying the retrieval but not the gating might produce over-differentiation on neutral questions. Consistent with this, at  α = 5 \alpha=5 , Llama loses 5.0 pp on  = acc =_{\text{acc}}  to gain +2.2 pp  ≠ acc \neq_{\text{acc}} . On the other hand, Mistral, Nemo and Gemma lose only 1.9–2.2 pp on  = acc =_{\text{acc}}  but have higher gains on  ≠ acc \neq_{\text{acc}}  (+3.1 to +4.2 pp). 

 
 
 
 
 Instruction tuning amplifies an existing mechanism 

 

 
 The instruct → \rightarrow base transfer (§ 4.2 ) indicates that the cultural matching circuit is not introduced by instruction tuning but emerges during pre-training.
What instruction tuning changes seems to be twofold. On one hand, it amplifies the signal. The binding strength  | Δ ​ S | |\Delta S|  values are much larger in the instruct compared to the base models (Table  1 ), while the relative effect of the intervention is similar in both of them (Table  2 ). It means that the same heads participate in carrying the binding signal in the base and instruct versions, but more strongly on instruct. All four base models show  K / S K/S  after KO  > 1 >1  (1.09 − - 4.67, see Table  7 ). It means that the heads are more involved in knowledge retrieval than in gating in the base versions.
On the other hand, the K/S drops in every model after instruction tuning (Mistral 4.67  → \rightarrow  1.13, Llama 2.51  → \rightarrow  1.90, Gemma 1.57  → \rightarrow  0.36, Nemo 1.09  → \rightarrow  1.00). The magnitude of the change varies (from very strong for Mistral and Gemma to modest for Llama and Nemo), but the direction is consistent. Instruction tuning seems to increase the gating role of these heads relative to knowledge-retrieval. 

 
 
 
 
 Explanation of the implicit − - explicit gap 

 

 
 Veselovsky et al. (2025)  qualitatively observed that models have knowledge that does not resurface without being provided explicit cultural cues. Our results propose a quantitative and mechanistic approach to this finding. We find a 3.5 − - 5 × \times  ratio between  | Δ ​ K | |\Delta K|  and  | Δ ​ S | |\Delta S|  in instruct models and 3 − - 6 × \times  in base. This also implies that models know more than they differentiate. This is our quantitative confirmation of the previous qualitative observation. Moreover, the association–differentiation gap is present in base models too, indicating it does not arise from instruction tuning. 

 
 
 
 
 Implications for fairness interventions 

 

 
 Our results have a practical consequence for how interventions on culturally aware behavior might be designed. The association–differentiation gap shows that models already encode the relevant cultural associations more strongly than they act upon them. The bottleneck is not knowledge acquisition but its mobilization at decision time. Hence, the target of intervention is shifted. Instead of providing models with additional cultural data or retraining for cultural awareness, an alternative would be to act on the circuit mediating this mobilization (of which we have identified a small set). The results of our  α \alpha -scaling on three of four instruct models are a proof of concept that the identified heads participate in mediating cultural differentiation. The models differentiate more on cultural questions without changing their answers on neutral questions when amplification is moderate ( α ∈ [ 2 , 3 ] \alpha\in[2,3] ), without fine-tuning and with only 2 − - 3 heads modified per model. This is not a deployable fairness method yet, in particular because the steering does not transfer cleanly to Llama and the magnitude of the gain is modest. However, this suggests that targeted and circuit-level interventions are a promising alternative to data-centric or training-centric approaches for difference-aware behavior. 

 
 
 
 
 
 6  Future Work 

 

 
 We compute the directional preference at baseline only (Appendix  C ). Whether the identified heads encode the  direction  of differentiation (toward  R R ) is left for future work.
A natural extension is to scale to larger LLMs and test whether the cultural binding circuit holds. Further directions include discovering knowledge-specific heads and comparing them with the binding heads. Another direction is cross-lingual investigation: do the identified heads participate in cultural binding when items are queried in non-English prompts, or does each language have its own circuit. 

 
 
 
 
 7  Conclusion 

 

 
 Our work presented a mechanistic investigation of cultural binding in LLMs, which we define as the process used by models to deploy cultural knowledge when context warrants it. We reframed context-appropriate cultural differentiation as a binding problem rather than a bias problem, shifting from suppressing an unwanted behavior to understanding and modulating it mechanistically. The 2 − - 3 attention heads we identify are a small but tractable handle on that competence. The gap we measure between what models know and what they act upon suggests that the work of difference-aware behavior is primarily a routing problem rather than a knowledge problem. This is an invitation to study other normative domains, such as legal or medical reasoning, where the same distinction between knowing and acting matters. 

 
 
 
 
 8  Limitations 

 

 
 The N4 benchmark covers cultural appropriation with 66 items. Generalization to other cultural domains and larger sets remains open. The identified heads account for 9–23% of the binding effect, leaving the remainder (possibly MLPs or residual-stream directions) unexplained. The generation-time effect on instruct models is consistent but moderate (1.3–2.4 pp at  α = 3 \alpha=3 ). Furthermore, both the binding pipeline (which requires locating identity and item token positions) and the knowledge probing task rely on a multiple-choice setting. Extension to open-ended generation is non-trivial. Whether the same circuit mediates differentiation in other normative domains (legal, medical) is also untested.
