 Abstract 
    
 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has become a widely adopted alternative to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) for aligning large language models with human preferences, eliminating the need for a separate reward model or reinforcement learning loop. However, recent theoretical analysis uncovers an asymmetric gradient behavior in DPO: the loss suppresses dispreferred responses substantially faster than it promotes preferred counterparts, causing the model to predominantly learn to avoid bad answers rather than to generate good ones. We propose  AdaDPO , a Self- Ada ptive variant of the  D irect  P reference  O ptimization algorithm that introduces per-preference-pair, stop-gradient-based coefficients derived directly from the policy model’s generation probabilities, with the reference model’s probabilities as an optional component. AdaDPO is constructed to enforce equality of gradient magnitudes between preferred and dispreferred probabilities; the practical implementation balances per-token gradients and applies a numerical clipping bound for stability, while retaining DPO’s original hyperparameter structure. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct trained on UltraFeedback under a setup nearly identical to that of SimPO, AdaDPO consistently outperforms DPO on AlpacaEval 2: it achieves higher length-controlled win rates (LC) in 81% of hyperparameter combinations, attains the global best LC (48.3%) and raw win rate (46.1%), and enlarges the LC-over-WR margin in 88% of combinations, indicating effective mitigation of length bias. Additional analyses on KL divergence, reward margin, and reward accuracy confirm that AdaDPO rectifies the gradient imbalance and yields more efficient optimization.  Because it operates purely at the loss level, AdaDPO can be dropped into existing preference-based alignment pipelines without changing data collection or model architectures.  The method requires only a few lines of code, and the same self-adaptive principle generalizes to a broad family of pairwise contrastive preference losses including SimPO, R-DPO, IPO, CPO, and ORPO. 

 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 

 
 Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences has become essential for deploying capable, controllable systems. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback  ( Christiano et al., 2017 ;  Ziegler et al., 2019 ) , typically implemented with Proximal Policy Optimization  ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) , established the dominant paradigm but introduces substantial complexity through its reliance on a separately trained reward model and an online RL loop. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 )  simplified this pipeline dramatically by reparameterizing the reward function and learning the policy directly from a fixed preference dataset via a binary cross-entropy loss, achieving strong empirical performance without reinforcement learning. 

 
 
 
 Despite DPO’s success, recent theoretical analysis  ( Feng et al., 2024 )  has revealed an asymmetric gradient pathology in its loss function. Using a field-theoretic analysis of DPO’s gradient vector field,  Feng et al. (2024)  show that the loss applies a substantially larger gradient to dispreferred responses than to preferred ones, with the asymmetry growing as the policy becomes more confident. As a consequence, DPO-trained models predominantly learn to suppress bad answers rather than to promote good ones—an effect that becomes especially pronounced when preferred and dispreferred responses are semantically close. This is not an artifact of hyperparameter choice but a structural property of the loss: the same coefficient  β \beta  multiplies both log-ratios, leaving no mechanism to balance the per-pair gradient magnitudes. Several DPO variants address related concerns: SimPO  ( Meng et al., 2024 ) , R-DPO  ( Park et al., 2024 ) , IPO  ( Azar et al., 2023 ) , CPO  ( Xu et al., 2024 ) , and ORPO  ( Hong et al., 2024 ) . However, none directly addresses the per-preference-pair gradient imbalance: the asymmetry persists in any pairwise contrastive loss with a shared coefficient on both terms. 

 
 
 
 We propose AdaDPO, a self-adaptive variant of DPO that resolves this imbalance by replacing the fixed  β \beta  in DPO’s implicit reward margin with per-preference-pair coefficients  β w \beta_{w}  and  β l \beta_{l} . Setting  β l = β \beta_{l}=\beta  (preserving DPO’s hyperparameter) and computing  β w \beta_{w}  via a stop-gradient-based ratio of generation probabilities, AdaDPO is constructed to enforce equality of gradient magnitudes on preferred and dispreferred probabilities at every optimization step (the length-normalized variant used in our experiments balances gradients per-token within a numerical clipping bound). The construction is theoretically principled, requires only a few lines of code to implement, and introduces no sensitive hyperparameters beyond those already in DPO. The same self-adaptive principle extends as a drop-in modification to any pairwise contrastive preference loss (SimPO, R-DPO, IPO, CPO, ORPO). 

 
 
 
 Contributions. 

 

 
 (i)  We rigorously derive the condition under which a DPO-style loss produces equal gradient magnitudes on preferred and dispreferred probabilities, and prove that AdaDPO satisfies it exactly through stop-gradient operators, providing a  principled  correction of the gradient imbalance (Section  3 ).
 (ii)  On Llama-3-8B-Instruct trained on UltraFeedback under a setup nearly identical to SimPO  ( Meng et al., 2024 ) , AdaDPO consistently outperforms DPO on AlpacaEval 2: higher length-controlled win rates in 81% of hyperparameter combinations, the global best LC (48.3%) and raw WR (46.1%), and an enlarged LC-over-WR margin in 88% of combinations, indicating effective mitigation of length bias (Section  5 ).
 (iii)  The same self-adaptive principle applies as a drop-in modification to any pairwise contrastive preference loss; we provide explicit constructions for SimPO, R-DPO, IPO, CPO, and ORPO that preserve each method’s existing hyperparameters (Section  6 ), highlighting a general recipe for balancing gradients in preference optimization. 

 
 
 
 
 
 2  Background 

 

 
 Direct Preference Optimization. 

 

 
 We are given a preference dataset  𝒟 = { ( x , y w , y l ) } \mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}  of triples consisting of a prompt  x x , a preferred response  y w y_{w} , and a dispreferred response  y l y_{l} , together with a reference policy  π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} . DPO  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 )  optimizes the policy  π θ \pi_{\theta}  by minimizing 

 
 

 
 
 ℒ DPO ​ ( π θ , π ref ) = − 𝔼 ( x , y w , y l ) ∼ 𝒟 ​ [ log ⁡ σ ⁡ ( β ​ log ⁡ π θ ​ ( y w ∣ x ) π ref ​ ( y w ∣ x ) − β ​ log ⁡ π θ ​ ( y l ∣ x ) π ref ​ ( y l ∣ x ) ) ] , \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\right)\right], 
 
 (1) 
 

 
 where  σ \sigma  is the sigmoid function and  β > 0 \beta>0  controls the deviation from  π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} . The same coefficient  β \beta  multiplies both log-ratios—a structural choice with consequences we examine next. 

 
 
 
 
 The gradient imbalance pathology. 

 

 
 Feng et al. (2024)  analyze the gradient vector field of  ℒ DPO \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}  and identify a structural asymmetry. Let  P w = π θ ​ ( y w ∣ x ) P_{w}\!=\!\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x) ,  P l = π θ ​ ( y l ∣ x ) P_{l}\!=\!\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x) ,  R w = π ref ​ ( y w ∣ x ) R_{w}\!=\!\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x) ,  R l = π ref ​ ( y l ∣ x ) R_{l}\!=\!\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x) , and let  x w = P w / R w x_{w}\!=\!P_{w}/R_{w} ,  x l = P l / R l x_{l}\!=\!P_{l}/R_{l} . The partial derivatives yield the gradient-magnitude ratios 

 
 
 
 

 
 
 Ratio x = | ∂ ℒ DPO / ∂ x w ∂ ℒ DPO / ∂ x l | = x l x w , Ratio P = | ∂ ℒ DPO / ∂ P w ∂ ℒ DPO / ∂ P l | = P l P w . \mathrm{Ratio}_{x}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}/\partial x_{w}}{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}/\partial x_{l}}\right|=\frac{x_{l}}{x_{w}},\qquad\mathrm{Ratio}_{P}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}/\partial P_{w}}{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}/\partial P_{l}}\right|=\frac{P_{l}}{P_{w}}. 
 
 (2) 
 

 
 
 
 As training progresses and the policy learns to prefer  y w y_{w} , both ratios fall below 1, meaning DPO suppresses  y l y_{l}  more aggressively than it promotes  y w y_{w}   ( Feng et al., 2024 ) . We note that DPO’s gradients with respect to  log-probabilities  are balanced by construction; the asymmetry analyzed by  Feng et al. (2024)  arises in probability and probability-ratio space, where it reflects the differential rate of change of  P w P_{w}  versus  P l P_{l}  during training. AdaDPO operates in this same space, deliberately introducing a compensating asymmetry in log-probability space to maintain promotion pressure on  y w y_{w}  as the policy becomes confident. This imbalance is structural rather than an artifact of hyperparameter choice: equation ( 1 ) applies the same coefficient  β \beta  to both log-ratio terms. 

 
 
 
 
 A concrete illustration. 

 

 
 Consider a single preference pair where, after some training, the policy assigns  P w = 0.10 P_{w}=0.10  and  P l = 0.02 P_{l}=0.02  (the model has learned to prefer  y w y_{w} ). With a uniform reference  R w = R l R_{w}=R_{l} , the gradient on  y w y_{w}  is roughly  0.02 / 0.10 = 1 / 5 0.02/0.10=1/5  of the gradient on  y l y_{l} , so the optimization signal on the dispreferred response is five times stronger than on the preferred one. As  P w P_{w}  increases further, the gradient on  y w y_{w}  shrinks toward zero and DPO continues to focus on suppressing  y l y_{l}  rather than reinforcing  y w y_{w} . AdaDPO eliminates this asymmetry by construction (Section  3 ). 

 
 
 
 
 Related work. 

 

 
 DPO variants such as SimPO  ( Meng et al., 2024 ) , R-DPO  ( Park et al., 2024 ) , IPO  ( Azar et al., 2023 ) , CPO  ( Xu et al., 2024 ) , and ORPO  ( Hong et al., 2024 )  improve DPO along specific axes yet leave the gradient imbalance unaddressed. While we adopt SimPO’s stable parameterization  ( Meng et al., 2024 ) , our work focuses on the orthogonal problem of gradient asymmetry, which persists even in length-normalized settings. A complementary line of work studies  likelihood displacement   ( Razin et al., 2025 ) , where the absolute likelihoods of both responses drift downward, with targeted fixes including DPOP  ( Pal et al., 2024 ) , Cal-DPO  ( Xiao et al., 2024 ) , and SPPO  ( Wu et al., 2024 ) . Displacement and gradient imbalance are distinct: the former concerns absolute magnitudes of  P w P_{w}  and  P l P_{l} , the latter their relative rate of change within each pair. Crucially, whereas standard DPO is balanced in log-probability space, AdaDPO intentionally introduces logit-space asymmetry to enforce balance in probability space. AdaDPO operates at the per-pair gradient level and is therefore composable with displacement-mitigation methods; its amplification of the gradient on  P w P_{w}  may also partially counteract displacement directly, which we leave for future work. 

 
 
 
 
 Concurrent work on gradient imbalance. 

 

 
 Two concurrent works also target DPO’s gradient asymmetry.  Ma et al. (2025)  (Balanced-DPO) introduce a global reweighting factor that balances average gradient contributions across the batch.  Zhu et al. (2025)  (SGDPO) add an auxiliary  pilot  term that resamples a sub-sequence from the policy logits. AdaDPO differs by enforcing  | ∂ ℒ / ∂ P w | = | ∂ ℒ / ∂ P l | |\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P_{w}|=|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P_{l}|   exactly and per preference pair  via the closed-form ratio of Proposition  1 , with no auxiliary terms, resampling, or extra forward passes, and no new tunable hyperparameters. 

 
 
 
 
 
 3  AdaDPO: Self-Adaptive Direct Preference Optimization 

 

 
 The method follows directly from the gradient analysis: we identify the mathematical condition that produces equal gradient magnitudes on  P w P_{w}  and  P l P_{l} , and construct per-pair coefficients that satisfy this condition exactly. The construction preserves DPO’s hyperparameter structure and is implementable in a few lines of code. Figure  1  summarizes the contrast between vanilla DPO and AdaDPO at the per-pair level. 

 
 
 
 Figure 1:  Schematic comparison of vanilla DPO (left) and AdaDPO (right). DPO’s shared coefficient  β \beta  produces systematically larger gradients on the dispreferred response; AdaDPO’s per-pair coefficients enforce equal-magnitude updates on  P w P_{w}  and  P l P_{l} . 
 
 
 3.1  Adaptive Coefficients for Balanced Gradients 

 

 
 DPO’s implicit reward margin uses the same coefficient  β \beta  for both log-ratio terms  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) . We replace it with per-preference-pair coefficients  β w \beta_{w}  and  β l \beta_{l} : 

 
 

 
 
 Δ AdaDPO = β w ​ log ⁡ P w R w − β l ​ log ⁡ P l R l = r ⁡ ( x , y w ) − r ⁡ ( x , y l ) . \Delta_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}=\beta_{w}\log\frac{P_{w}}{R_{w}}-\beta_{l}\log\frac{P_{l}}{R_{l}}=r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l}). 
 
 (3) 
 

 
 
 
 Proposition 1  (Gradient balance) 
 
 Let  ℒ \mathcal{L}  be a DPO-style loss with implicit margin  Δ AdaDPO \Delta_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}  of the form ( 3 ). Requiring  | ∂ ℒ / ∂ P w | = | ∂ ℒ / ∂ P l | \left|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P_{w}\right|=\left|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P_{l}\right|  yields the policy-space constraint 

 
 

 
 
 β w β l = P w P l , \frac{\beta_{w}}{\beta_{l}}=\frac{P_{w}}{P_{l}}, 
 
 (4) 
 

 
 while requiring balance in probability-ratio space,  | ∂ ℒ / ∂ x w | = | ∂ ℒ / ∂ x l | \left|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_{w}\right|=\left|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_{l}\right| , yields 

 
 

 
 
 β w β l = x w x l = P w ​ R l P l ​ R w . \frac{\beta_{w}}{\beta_{l}}=\frac{x_{w}}{x_{l}}=\frac{P_{w}R_{l}}{P_{l}R_{w}}. 
 
 (5) 
 

 
 The two constraints coincide when  R w = R l R_{w}=R_{l} . In practice, AdaDPO can adopt either balancing rule, yielding strictly equal per-pair gradient magnitudes in the chosen space. 

 
 
 
 
 
 3.2  Stop-Gradient Parameterization 

 

 
 The ratios in ( 4 ) and ( 5 ) depend on  P w P_{w}  and  P l P_{l} , themselves outputs of the policy model. Setting  β w \beta_{w}  directly to these values would propagate gradients through the coefficients and break the intended balance. We treat the coefficients as constants during backpropagation using the stop-gradient operator  sg ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{sg}(\cdot) . Setting  β l = β \beta_{l}=\beta  to preserve DPO’s hyperparameter: 

 
 

 
 
 β w = β ⋅ sg ⁡ ( P w P l ) or β w = β ⋅ sg ⁡ ( P w ​ R l P l ​ R w ) . \beta_{w}=\beta\cdot\mathrm{sg}\!\left(\frac{P_{w}}{P_{l}}\right)\quad\text{or}\quad\beta_{w}=\beta\cdot\mathrm{sg}\!\left(\frac{P_{w}R_{l}}{P_{l}R_{w}}\right). 
 
 (6) 
 

 
 Both formulations satisfy the balance condition exactly; AdaDPO uses the same  β \beta  values as DPO and adds only a numerical clipping constant  C C  that is robust over a wide range (Appendix  A.2 ), which we view as important for fair comparison and practical adoption. In our experiments we use the ratio-space variant  β w / β l = P w ​ R l / ( P l ​ R w ) \beta_{w}/\beta_{l}=P_{w}R_{l}/(P_{l}R_{w}) , which incorporates the reference policy and corresponds to the implementation in Listing  1 ; an ablation with the policy-space variant  β w / β l = P w / P l \beta_{w}/\beta_{l}=P_{w}/P_{l}  yields essentially identical AlpacaEval 2 performance across the  ( lr , β ) (\mathrm{lr},\beta)  grid, confirming that the choice of balancing space is not critical. 

 
 
 
 
 3.3  The AdaDPO Loss 

 

 
 The full AdaDPO objective replaces the fixed  β \beta  in DPO’s loss with the adaptive coefficients: 

 
 

 
 
 ℒ AdaDPO ​ ( π θ , π ref ) = − 𝔼 ( x , y w , y l ) ∼ 𝒟 ​ [ log ⁡ σ ⁡ ( β w ​ log ⁡ P w R w − β l ​ log ⁡ P l R l ) ] . \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta_{w}\log\frac{P_{w}}{R_{w}}-\beta_{l}\log\frac{P_{l}}{R_{l}}\right)\right]. 
 
 (7) 
 

 
 
 
 Applying Proposition  1  to the AdaDPO loss in ( 7 ) yields: 

 
 

 
 
 | ∂ ℒ AdaDPO / ∂ P w ∂ ℒ AdaDPO / ∂ P l | = β w β l ⋅ P l P w = 1 , | ∂ ℒ AdaDPO / ∂ x w ∂ ℒ AdaDPO / ∂ x l | = β w β l ⋅ x l x w = P w ​ R l P l ​ R w ⋅ P l ​ R w P w ​ R l = 1 . \left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}/\partial P_{w}}{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}/\partial P_{l}}\right|=\frac{\beta_{w}}{\beta_{l}}\cdot\frac{P_{l}}{P_{w}}=1,\qquad\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}/\partial x_{w}}{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AdaDPO}}/\partial x_{l}}\right|=\frac{\beta_{w}}{\beta_{l}}\cdot\frac{x_{l}}{x_{w}}=\frac{P_{w}R_{l}}{P_{l}R_{w}}\cdot\frac{P_{l}R_{w}}{P_{w}R_{l}}=1. 
 
 (8) 
 

 
 
 
 By construction, base AdaDPO enforces strict gradient equality between preferred and dispreferred probabilities at every step (Equation ( 8 )), directly correcting the structural asymmetry identified by  Feng et al. (2024)  and rectifying both  Ratio x \mathrm{Ratio}_{x}  and  Ratio P \mathrm{Ratio}_{P}  from Section  2 . The practical Stable AdaDPO variant used in our experiments approximates this balance per-token within the clipping bound  C C  (Section  6.1 ). 

 
 
 
 Intuition for the adaptive ratio. 

 

 
 The coefficients  β w \beta_{w}  and  β l \beta_{l}  are implemented using a stop-gradient operator (e.g.,  detach()  in PyTorch) to ensure they act as constant multipliers during the backward pass rather than differentiable parameters. This choice admits a clear mechanistic interpretation: the gradient on  P w P_{w}  is proportional to  β w / P w \beta_{w}/P_{w} , while the gradient on  P l P_{l}  is proportional to  β l / P l \beta_{l}/P_{l} . By setting  β w ∝ P w \beta_{w}\propto P_{w} , we exactly cancel the inverse-probability scaling that naturally shrinks the gradient as the model becomes confident. This ensures a persistent reinforcement signal that prevents the "vanishing promotion" problem inherent in vanilla DPO, where updates stagnate once the model identifies the preferred response.

 4  Experimental Setup 

 

 
 Base model and training data. 

 

 
 Following the Instruct setting of SimPO  ( Meng et al., 2024 ) , we use Llama-3-8B-Instruct  ( AI@Meta, 2024 )  as the base SFT model and train AdaDPO and DPO on the UltraFeedback preference dataset  ( Cui et al., 2024 ) , which contains 61,837 examples (59,876 train, 1,961 evaluation). We do not collect any new human data; all experiments use existing open-source preference datasets under their original licenses. 

 
 
 
 
 Hyperparameters and grid search. 

 

 
 We follow SimPO’s training settings: batch size 128, maximum sequence length 2048, cosine learning rate schedule with 10% warmup, training for 1 epoch. We grid-search learning rate  lr ∈ { 3 , 5 , 6 , 10 } × 10 − 7 \mathrm{lr}\in\{3,5,6,10\}\times 10^{-7}  and  β ∈ { 0.005 , 0.01 , 0.05 , 0.1 } \beta\in\{0.005,0.01,0.05,0.1\} , yielding 16  ( lr , β ) (\mathrm{lr},\beta)  combinations per method (32 training runs total). Following the standard reporting practice in this literature, we evaluate every grid cell on each benchmark and report both the best cell (Table  1 ) and aggregate statistics across the full hyperparameter grid (Section  5.3 ); the latter is robust to the choice of best-cell reporting convention. 

 
 
 
 
 Evaluation benchmarks. 

 

 
 We evaluate on three standard instruction-following benchmarks with GPT-4 Turbo as the judge. As is standard in LLM alignment work, we rely on GPT-4-Turbo as an automatic evaluator and therefore inherit its known biases (e.g., verbosity preference and occasional inconsistency), which we partially control for via length-controlled metrics but do not eliminate entirely.  AlpacaEval 2   ( Dubois et al., 2024 )  consists of 805 queries and reports length-controlled win rate (LC), raw win rate (WR), and average response length.  Arena-Hard v0.1   ( Li et al., 2024 )  contains 500 challenging technical queries and reports WR with 95% confidence intervals.  MT-Bench   ( Zheng et al., 2023 )  covers 8 categories with 80 multi-turn questions on a 1–10 scale. 

 
 
 
 
 
 5  Results 

 

 
 Throughout this section, “AdaDPO” refers to the length-normalized variant (Stable AdaDPO; Section  6.1 ), which is the implementation shown in Listing  1 . The base form of Equation ( 6 ) yields comparable best-case performance after tuning but exhibits less stable training dynamics on long responses (Appendix  A.1 ). 

 
 
 
 5.1  Optimization Dynamics 

 

 
 Figure  2  compares AdaDPO and DPO under two representative  β \beta  values (0.05 and 0.01) at fixed learning rate  5 × 10 − 7 5\times 10^{-7} . AdaDPO consistently achieves lower evaluation loss (Figure  2 a), higher reward accuracy (Figure  2 b), larger  β × KL \beta\times\mathrm{KL}  divergence margin (Figure  2 c), and larger reward margin  r ⁡ ( x , y w ) − r ⁡ ( x , y l ) r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})  (Figure  2 d). The performance ordering across all metrics consistently places AdaDPO above DPO at matched  β \beta . While the  β × KL \beta\times\mathrm{KL}  margin is dominated by the KL constraint strength, the other metrics—more directly tied to alignment quality—benefit clearly from balanced gradient updates, indicating that a large KL margin alone is insufficient: balanced updates are key to translating margin into improved performance.

 7  Discussion and Limitations 

 

 
 Why AdaDPO works. 

 

 
 AdaDPO counteracts the asymmetric gradient ratio of equation ( 2 ) by setting  β w / β l \beta_{w}/\beta_{l}  to scale with  P w / P l P_{w}/P_{l}  (or  P w ​ R l / P l ​ R w P_{w}R_{l}/P_{l}R_{w} ): when the policy becomes much more likely to generate  y w y_{w}  than  y l y_{l} , the gradient on  y w y_{w}  would otherwise vanish, and AdaDPO amplifies it to match the gradient on  y l y_{l} . Empirically, this balance translates into observable gains in reward accuracy, reward margin, and length-controlled win rate. 

 
 
 
 
 Stability of the Ceiling Constant. 

 

 
 In AdaDPO, the adaptive coefficient  β w \beta_{w}  is clipped by a ceiling constant  C C  to ensure numerical stability. While  C = 2 C=2  was used for all main experiments, our ablation study in Appendix  A.2  confirms that performance is robust across the range  C ∈ [ 1.5 , 2.5 ] C\in[1.5,2.5] . Training only becomes unstable when  C C  exceeds 5, at which point the adaptive coefficients can amplify gradients enough to cause instability. This relative insensitivity suggests that  C C  is a stable architectural choice rather than a hyperparameter requiring intensive per-task tuning. 

 
 
 
 
 Limitations. 

 

 
 (i) Single base model.  We use Llama-3-8B-Instruct; cross-model validation on Mistral, Qwen, and larger Llama checkpoints remains future work.
 (ii) Single preference dataset.  We train on UltraFeedback only; transfer to other preference-data sources (e.g., HH-RLHF, PKU-SafeRLHF) is untested.
 (iii) Arena-Hard underperformance.  On Arena-Hard, DPO marginally outperforms AdaDPO in raw win rate; the 95% confidence intervals overlap substantially, so the gap is not statistically significant. Arena-Hard does not provide a length-controlled metric, limiting our ability to disentangle length effects from quality on this benchmark.
 (iv) Scope of claims.  Our results apply to offline preference optimization in the LLM Instruct setting; extending AdaDPO to online RL, other modalities, and stronger safety objectives remains future work rather than a claim of this paper. 

 
 
 
 
 
 8  Conclusion 

 

 
 We identified the gradient imbalance in DPO as a structural property of its loss that causes models to suppress dispreferred responses far more aggressively than they promote preferred ones. AdaDPO resolves this by introducing per-preference-pair adaptive coefficients that enforce equality of gradient magnitudes on  P w P_{w}  and  P l P_{l}  (exactly in the base form, and per-token within a clipping bound in Stable AdaDPO implementation), with minimal code changes and no sensitive hyperparameters beyond DPO’s  β \beta . Two observations follow from our results. First, the consistency of AdaDPO’s gains across the 16-cell hyperparameter grid—LC win in 81% of combinations and an enlarged LC-over-WR margin in 88%—suggests that gradient balance, rather than careful  β \beta  tuning, is the constraint on length-controlled performance for DPO-style objectives. Second, the LC-over-WR margin improvement systematically tracks reduced response lengths, providing direct empirical evidence that DPO’s gradient asymmetry is mechanistically linked to length exploitation, and offering an alternative to the explicit length regularization used in prior work. Cross-model validation, transfer to alternative preference datasets, and empirical evaluation of the variants in Table  2  are natural next steps. More broadly, the per-pair gradient-balance condition appears to be a useful design lens for preference optimization beyond any specific loss function.
