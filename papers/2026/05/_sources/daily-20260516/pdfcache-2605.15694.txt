L7@P0: Abstract
L8@P0: Transformer models are rapidly becoming a cornerstone of modern Internet of Things (IoT) applications, yet their computational and memory demands far exceed the capabilities of a single typical
L9@P0: ultra-low-power IoT device. We present CATS, a
L10@P0: framework for distributed transformer inference on
L11@P0: ultra-low-power wireless devices, enabling multiple devices to collaboratively execute models far
L12@P0: larger than what a single device can sustain. At its
L13@P0: core, CATS is a communication-aware distributed
L14@P0: transformer inference scheme co-designed across
L15@P0: transformer partitioning, wireless communication
L16@P0: and training. It employs SomeGather, a new pruned
L17@P0: communication primitive that selectively broadcasts
L18@P0: activation columns to reduce communication bandwidth and RAM usage without sacrificing model
L19@P0: accuracy. Building on SomeGather, we design a
L20@P0: partitioning method that exploits this primitive for
L21@P0: efficient model parallelism. To cope with unreliable
L22@P0: wireless communication, CATS employs messagedropout during training, which mimics packet losses
L23@P0: and yields models that are robust to message loss
L24@P0: during inference. In real-world experiments, we
L25@P0: show that CATS brings distributed transformer inference to ultra-low-power wireless devices for the
L26@P0: first time, with deployments on up to 16 devices that
L27@P0: collaboratively execute transformer models up to 14
L28@P0: times larger than what a single device can run.
L29@P0: 1 Introduction
L30@P0: Transformer models have become the backbone of intelligent IoT systems, delivering state-of-the-art performance
L31@P0: in natural language processing, computer vision and timeseries analysis [Kaur and Jadhav, 2023; Zong et al., 2025;
L32@P0: Zhang et al., 2025b]. This remarkable capability, however,
L33@P0: comes at a substantial computational cost and typically requires powerful hardware, which conflicts with the tight resource budgets of IoT downstream devices like smart sensors.
L34@P0: To address these challenges and enable scalable deployment
L35@P0: of large transformer models on downstream devices, recent
L36@P0: work has identified distributed transformer inference (DTI) as
L37@P0: a promising paradigm [Liu et al., 2025b; Bochem et al., 2025;
L38@P0: Hu and Li, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025a]. In
L39@P0: many applications, such as sensor networks, devices naturally
L40@P0: operate in groups. By pooling resources from multiple devices,
L41@P0: DTI can execute transformers much larger than what a single
L42@P0: device can handle, thereby unlocking advanced transformer
L43@P0: models in highly resource-constrained environments.
L44@P0: Existing approaches, however, target powerful platforms
L45@P0: (e.g., Raspberry Pis or specialized microcontroller units
L46@P0: (MCUs) with tensor processing units) connected via highspeed wired links. In contrast, the vast majority of real-world
L47@P0: deployments, such as wireless sensor networks for environmental monitoring, infrastructure or agriculture, are far more
L48@P0: constrained [Sanislav et al., 2021; Rahaman and Azharuddin,
L49@P0: 2022; Sofi et al., 2022]. First, they typically rely on small batteries or energy harvesting as a power source and can thus only
L50@P0: support ultra-low-power devices, which consist of MCUs with
L51@P0: very limited memory and compute power [Borges et al., 2014;
L52@P0: Jamshed et al., 2022], several orders of magnitude lower than
L53@P0: that of e.g., a Raspberry Pi. Second, devices in such applications communicate via low-power wireless interfaces such
L54@P0: as Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), forming mesh networks to
L55@P0: cover large areas, enable flexible deployment and support mobile nodes [Zimmerling et al., 2020; Baumann et al., 2020;
L56@P0: Chai et al., 2024]. While creating unprecedented opportunities for smart IoT systems, this setting comes with several
L57@P0: fundamental challenges for DTI, which are still unexplored:
L58@P0: C1 Limited Computing Power DTI of transformers on
L59@P0: resource-constrained MCUs is bottlenecked by all key
L60@P0: hardware resources: scarce flash memory for storing
L61@P0: weights, limited RAM for intermediate activations and
L62@P0: constrained compute throughput for timely inference.
L63@P0: C2 Limited Communication Bandwidth Low-power wireless DTI is constrained by low-bandwidth, high-latency
L64@P0: inter-device links that turn communication into a dominant bottleneck. Mesh networks further exacerbate this
L65@P0: challenge as packets are forwarded over multiple hops.
L66@P0: C3 Unreliable Communication As wireless communication is inherently unreliable, communicated intermediate
L67@P0: activations may be lost, thereby significantly degrading
L68@P0: accuracy.
L69@P0: These constraints render existing DTI approaches [Liu
L70@P0: et al., 2025b; Bochem et al., 2025; Hu and Li, 2024;
L71@P0: Zhang et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025a] largely unsuitable in

L129@P1: usage but leave flash limits unaddressed [Liu et al., 2025b;

L138@P1: research and the demands of real-world IoT deployments.
L139@P1: Contributions. To close this gap, we introduce CATS (Collaborative Inference at the Sensor-level) (Figure 1). Our key
L140@P1: idea is to design the transformer partitioning, communication and training jointly around a pruned all-to-all primitive
L141@P1: called SomeGather. By pruning entire activation columns
L142@P1: in a communication-aware way, SomeGather turns a dense
L143@P1: transformer into one where feature subspaces are processed
L144@P1: purely locally, while a carefully chosen subset is shared. This
L145@P1: simultaneously cuts communication volume and reduces the
L146@P1: activations each device must store in RAM (addresses C1 and
L147@P1: C2) while maintaining accuracy. As a result, our partitioning
L148@P1: scheme based on SomeGather allows reducing the per-device
L149@P1: RAM, flash and computing load at the same time (C1), unlike existing approaches. To cope with stochastic message
L150@P1: losses (C3), we inject message-dropout (MD) during training, a structured dropout exposing the model to realistic loss
L151@P1: patterns making it robust to message loss during inference.
L152@P1: As a result, CATS enables efficient collaborative execution
L153@P1: of large transformer models across multiple ultra-low-power
L154@P1: wireless devices. Figure 1 shows CATS in action on 16 MCUs
L155@P1: communicating via a low-power wireless mesh. They jointly
L156@P1: execute a 14 million parameter Vision Transformer (ViT), over
L157@P1: 14× larger than fits on a single device. To the best of our
L158@P1: knowledge, this is the first demonstration of DTI on ultra-lowpower wireless hardware, showing that CATS substantially
L159@P1: cuts per-device resource requirements and makes large transformers practical on such platforms. In contrast, current stateof-the-art approaches [Hu and Li, 2024; Liu et al., 2025b;
L160@P1: Bochem et al., 2025] deployed on the devices would only
L161@P1: execute transformers no larger than what a single device can
L162@P1: run. Experiments further reveal that SomeGather reduces communication volume by up to 90 % and per-device activation
L163@P1: RAM by 67.5 % compared to unpruned DTI, while maintaining accuracy on four time-series prediction benchmarks and
L164@P1: MD reduces the relative prediction error increase from none to
L165@P1: 10 % message loss from up to 200 % to 23.6 % across datasets.
L166@P1: To summarize, our contributions are:
L167@P1: 1. A new communication primitive, SomeGather, and a partitioning scheme for transformer models that express all
L168@P1: cross-device communication as column-pruned all-to-all
L169@P1: exchanges, simultaneously reducing communication, perdevice computing load, RAM and flash footprint.
L170@P1: 2. MD, a structured dropout mechanism that simulates realistic message-loss patterns across all transformer layers,
L171@P1: improving robustness to lossy communication.
L172@P1: 3. The first end-to-end demonstration of DTI on ultra-lowpower wireless devices.

L206@P1: 3 Related Work
L207@P1-2: To our knowledge, no existing methods support DTI on ultralow-power wireless devices. Current approaches target substantially more powerful edge platforms such as RaspberryPis [Wei et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a; Wen et al., 2025],
L208@P2: Nvidia Jetsons [Xu et al., 2023], laptops [Liu et al., 2025b]
L209@P2: and virtual machines [Hu and Li, 2024], and assume wired
L210@P2: communication. The only study employing MCUs [Bochem
L211@P2: et al., 2025] uses Siracusa chips [Prasad et al., 2024] with multiple RISC-V cores, again connected via wired links. In contrast, CATS targets cost-effective, common wireless MCUs,
L212@P2: enabling DTI in an unaddressed regime. Given the lack of
L213@P2: complete end-to-end solutions, we therefore review prior work
L214@P2: that addresses individual aspects relevant to CATS.
L215@P2: 3.1 Partitioning Strategies for Transformers
L216@P2: DTI methods for edge devices differ in their partitioning strategies and which communication primitives they use for them.
L217@P2: Communication primitives. Most DTI methods build on
L218@P2: three communication primitives to distribute matrix multiplications: AllGather, AllReduce and ReduceScatter [Stahl
L219@P2: et al., 2021; Du et al., 2024]. Using AllGather, devices initially hold disjoint parts of a matrix and broadcast them so
L220@P2: that, afterwards, each device holds the entire matrix. Using AllReduce/ReduceScatter, devices hold partial summands
L221@P2: of a global sum and, through collaborative communication,
L222@P2: end up with the result of the sum, either the entire matrix
L223@P2: (AllReduce) or only parts (ReduceScatter). Efficient implementations of AllReduce/ReduceScatter, however, typically
L224@P2: assume specialized, structured communication topologies
L225@P2: (e.g., groups [Bochem et al., 2025]), and become RAM- and
L226@P2: communication-inefficient in unstructured mesh networks, as
L227@P2: we later quantify in Section 5.2.
L228@P2: Partitioning strategies. Recent DTI strategies for edge devices build on these primitives and can be classified into
L229@P2: three main categories. The first class [Liu et al., 2025b;
L230@P2: Wen et al., 2025; Hu and Li, 2024] splits the transformer
L231@P2: along the token dimension and communicates during selfattention. This approach improves computational throughput
L232@P2: and reduces RAM usage but still requires each device to store
L233@P2: the full set of weights, leaving the flash footprint unchanged.
L234@P2: The second class partitions the transformer along the feature dimension and distributes different attention heads across
L235@P2: devices [Bochem et al., 2025]. This lowers flash memory
L236@P2: requirements because each device only stores a subset of the
L237@P2: weights. However, this approach relies on the communicationand RAM-heavy AllReduce primitive.
L238@P2: The third class assigns smaller, independent models to
L239@P2: each device and then merges their outputs [Xu et al., 2023;
L240@P2: Liu et al., 2025a], or decouples transformer components [Wei
L241@P2: et al., 2024]. These methods are either restricted to imageclassification transformers or ultimately require merging
L242@P2: blocks and intermediate results using the previously mentioned
L243@P2: strategies, thereby inheriting similar limitations.
L244@P2: Positioning CATS. CATS builds on the strengths of the
L245@P2: second class by dividing the transformer along the feature
L246@P2: dimension and distributing attention heads across devices, reducing per-device flash memory usage. To avoid the communication and RAM overhead of AllReduce in mesh networks,
L247@P2: CATS relies on SomeGather. SomeGather is a pruned variant
L248@P2: of AllGather that broadcasts only a selected subset of activation columns, reducing both communication volume and
L249@P2: per-device RAM compared to pure AllGather. As Sections 4.2
L250@P2: and 4.3 detail, this tailored design simultaneously lowers flash
L251@P2: usage, RAM requirements and per-device computational load,
L252@P2: making it well suited for ultra-low-power wireless platforms.
L253@P2: 3.2 Communication-Aware Distributed Inference
L254@P2: Current DTI approaches do not consider constrained and unreliable mesh communication. However, methods for other types
L255@P2: of Neural Networks (NNs) address parts of these challenges.
L256@P2: Communication-aware pruning. Specialized pruning techniques [Mao et al., 2017; Abdi et al., 2020; Jian et al., 2023;
L257@P2: Qin et al., 2023] reduce communication latency by removing
L258@P2: transmitted NN connections. While prior work predominantly
L259@P2: targets convolutional NNs, CATS extends this idea to transformers. Moreover, we demonstrate that such pruning not
L260@P2: only lowers communication overhead but can also reduce
L261@P2: RAM requirements, an aspect that is overlooked in existing
L262@P2: communication-aware pruning strategies.
L263@P2: Distributed inference over lossy networks. Current work
L264@P2: on inference under message loss focuses on a two-device,
L265@P2: server-edge setting [Itahara et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2024;
L266@P2: Hou and Ohtsuki, 2025], where the edge executes the front
L267@P2: part of an NN and the server the back part. These approaches
L268@P2: show that simulating lossy communication during training
L269@P2: via tailored dropout between the front part and the back part
L270@P2: significantly improves robustness at inference time. CATS
L271@P2: generalizes this concept to distributed inference across multiple devices, where the execution of each layer is shared among
L272@P2: them. We introduce MD that applies dropout simulating lossy
L273@P2: communication to all layers, going beyond a single split point.
L274@P2: Distributed inference in mesh networks. Existing approaches explicitly consider the network graph either in a taskallocation problem [Samikwa et al., 2023; Jung and Lee, 2023;
L275@P2: Disabato et al., 2021] or during training [Jian et al., 2023]. In
L276@P2: realistic wireless settings, the network topology is dynamic,
L277@P2: influenced by environmental changes or device mobility (e.g.,
L278@P2: wearable devices) [Hao et al., 2025]. As a result, these approaches require repeated optimization and allocation steps
L279@P2: during changes, which incurs substantial overhead. CATS
L280@P2: follows a different strategy by implementing a communication
L281@P2: scheme that abstracts away the details of mesh connectivity
L282@P2: while providing properties that are particularly advantageous
L283@P2: for DTI (see Section 4.5).

L328@P3: 4.2 SomeGather
L329@P3: We illustrate SomeGather on a distributed matrix multiplication B = AW with column-split matrices. We consider
L330@P3: square matrices A and W with an equal partitioning across
L331@P3: devices, i.e., each device holds S columns of A, W and B.
L332@P3: Extensions to non-square matrices or uneven partitionings follow directly by adapting the respective dimensions. The next
L333@P3: section discusses application of SomeGather to transformers.
L334@P3: In a conventional AllGather-based implementation, devices
L335@P3: first exchange all columns of A. Each device then multiplies
L336@P3: its local copy of A with its local columns of W, yielding a
L337@P3: subset of the columns of B = AW on every device [Stahl et
L338@P3: al., 2021]. In this setting, RAM scales as O(CA + CW/D)
L339@P3: and the communication scales as O(CA), where CA and CW
L340@P3: denote the sizes of A and W. This is problematic on ultra-lowpower devices, because CA can be large.
L341@P3: SomeGather addresses this bottleneck by pruning communication of activations. To support arbitrary sequence lengths
L342@P3: N, pruning individual rows (i.e., timestamps) is ineffective.
