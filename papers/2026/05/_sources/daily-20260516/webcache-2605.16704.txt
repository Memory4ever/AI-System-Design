L179: More generally, instead of a single-step gradient, one may use trajectory-based task vectors (cite107†Ilharco et al., 2023 ), defined as the parameter difference $\tau_{\mathcal{S}}=\theta_{\mathcal{S}}-\theta_{0}$ resulting from fine-tuning $\theta_{0}$ on a dataset or dataset collection $\mathcal{S}$ for multiple steps.
L180: Task vectors capture longer-horizon training dynamics beyond the local linearization, and similarity between auxiliary and target tasks can be quantified via the dot product $\langle\tau_{\mathcal{S}},\tau_{\text{tar}}\rangle$.
L181: In the context of LLM post-training, dataset gradients are pooled at the sequence level before aggregation. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), the per-example loss typically corresponds to sequence-level cross-entropy, computed as the average negative log-likelihood over generated tokens. For reinforcement learning (RL), $\ell$ denotes a surrogate loss derived from trajectory-level rewards (e.g., GRPO-style objectives (cite108†Shao et al., 2024 )).
L182: ##### Limitations.
L183: While this additive utility model yields a simple and scalable valuation rule, it treats auxiliary datasets independently. This ignores two important aspects of the selection problem. First, auxiliary datasets may be redundant: multiple datasets can induce similar update directions, leading to diminishing returns when included together. Second, the usefulness of a dataset depends on the set of other datasets selected alongside it, rather than on its individual marginal contribution.
L184: These limitations motivate a joint valuation approach that accounts for interactions among datasets, which we develop next.
L185: ### 3.2 Kernel Mean Matching for Dataset Valuation
L186: We now relax the additive utility assumption in cite14†Sec. 3.1 and consider a joint valuation approach that accounts for interactions among auxiliary datasets. As discussed in cite9†Sec. 2.1 , the underlying post-training utility is unknown and naturally may be non-additive due to dataset interactions (cite106†Hu et al., 2024 ). In this section, we introduce a non-additive utility model and show that Kernel Mean Matching (KMM) yields the optimal valuation scores.
L187: ##### Non-additive utility model.
L188: 
L189: We model post-training utility as a function of the selected dataset subset $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{aux}}$. Let $g(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}g_{i}$ denote the joint update direction induced by post-training on $\mathcal{S}$. We define the utility as
L190: 
L191:  | $$u(\mathcal{S})\;\triangleq\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{tar}}(\theta_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{\text{tar}}(\theta_{0}-\eta g(\mathcal{S})),$$  |  | (7)
L192: which is generally non-additive in $\mathcal{S}$ due to interactions among datasets through the loss landscape. This utility captures the buyer’s objective of improving target-task performance considering all datasets in $\mathcal{S}$ globally.
L193: #### 3.2.1 KMM as Gradient Space Least Squares
L194: 
L195: To obtain a tractable optimization problem, we approximate the target loss locally around $\theta_{0}$. A second-order Taylor expansion yields
L196: 
L197:  | $$u(\mathcal{S})=\eta\langle g_{\text{tar}},g(\mathcal{S})\rangle-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}g(\mathcal{S})^{\top}H_{\text{tar}}\,g(\mathcal{S})+O\left(\eta^{3}\|g(\mathcal{S})\|_{2}^{3}\right),$$  |  | (8)
L198: where $g_{\text{tar}}=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{tar}}(\theta_{0})$ and $H_{\text{tar}}=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{tar}}(\theta_{0})$.
L199: To enable continuous optimization, we relax subset selection in cite109†Eq. 1 to weighted combinations. A subset $\mathcal{S}$ can be encoded as a binary vector $w\in\{0,1\}^{N}$ with $w_{i}=\mathbf{1}[i\in\mathcal{S}]$, so that $g(\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}g_{i}=Gw$, where $G=[g_{1},\dots,g_{N}]$ is the gradient matrix.
L200: Further relaxing to continuous weights $w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ and substituting into cite110†Eq. 8 defines the relaxed utility $u(w):=\eta\langle g_{\text{tar}},Gw\rangle-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}(Gw)^{\top}H_{\text{tar}}\,(Gw).$

L268: ##### Baselines.
L269: We compare our gradient method against several baselines, including DataModel (cite103†Ilyas et al., 2022 ) and a compressed sensing (CS) variant reframed into dataset level valuation methods, as well as SOTA method GradEx-Forward-Selection (FS) and GradEx-Random-Ensemble (RE) (cite123†Li et al., 2024 ) applied in a highly similar dataset selection setup. DataModel is a linear regression model which requires a large amount of evaluation metrics labels from training on randomly sampled auxiliary subsets.
L270: GradEx estimates dataset values through fast linear approximations to training dynamics and random projections for dimensionality reduction, yet it depends on specific properties of the cross-entropy loss, making them only applicable to SFT. We defer more baseline method details to cite65†App. G . We use cosine similarity for One-Step gradient and Task Vectors (TV) to remove the effect of gradient norms.
L271: ##### Evaluation Protocol.
L272: Given valuation scores over auxiliary datasets, according to cite124†Eq. 2 , we evaluate each method by constructing auxiliary subsets of varying sizes $k$ using a greedy selection of datasets with strictly positive scores, and measuring the resulting post-training performance on the target task. To summarize performance across budget levels, we report (i) the best performance achieved over all $k$, reflecting a method’s maximum attainable utility, and (ii) Borda count to aggregate rankings across $k$ values.
L273: To eliminate the confounding effect that selecting more data can trivially improve performance, we additionally introduce a fixed-compute evaluation protocol: the total number of optimizer updates is held constant across all values of $k$. By default, we allocate a fixed fraction of training steps to auxiliary data and distribute these steps uniformly across the selected auxiliary datasets (see cite79†App. H for sensitivity analysis for uniform design choice).
L274: This mixing strategy ensures that adding more auxiliary datasets redistributes, rather than increases, the auxiliary training budget. See full evaluation procedure in cite55†App. D .
L275: For multilingual evaluation, we use MMMLU (cite125†Lai et al., 2023 ) as the target metric for supervised fine-tuning to evaluate general multilingual capability, and MGSM (cite126†Shi et al., 2023 ), the multilingual extension of GSM8K (cite127†Cobbe et al., 2021 ), as the target metric for multilingual mathematical reasoning. For instruction following, we use strict instruction-level accuracy with IFEval (cite128†Zhou et al., 2023 ). Evaluation task statistics are in cite129†Tab. 11 .
L276: ### 4.2 Main Results
L277: ##### KMM delivers strong and consistent gains across post training algorithms.
L278: Across both GRPO (cite130†Tab. 2 ) and SFT (cite131†Tab. 3 ) evaluations, incorporating KMM consistently improves performance over the corresponding gradient-only baseline, for both the best-$k$ and average metrics. This improvement holds uniformly across all target languages, including Danish, Marathi, and Thai.
L279: Beyond these paired comparisons, KMM-enhanced methods achieve the strongest best-$k$ performance among all evaluated baselines, while their average performance is either comparable to or ranks second to the best-performing method.

L926: #### G.1.1 KMM
L927: Instead of enforcing the $\ell_{1}$ budget constraint $w\in\mathcal{W}_{k}$, we can incorporate sparsity directly into the objective via an $\ell_{1}$ penalty with regularization strength $\gamma>0$.
L928: Using the Gram matrix $K_{ij}=\langle g_{i},g_{j}\rangle$ and alignment vector $\beta_{i}=\langle g_{i},g_{\mathrm{tar}}\rangle$, we solve the unconstrained kernelized problem in Gram matrix form (leaving the quadratic term implicit can lead to a larger canonical form and significantly slower solve times for convex solvers):
L929:  | $$w^{*}\;=\;\arg\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\;\frac{1}{2}\,w^{\top}Kw\;-\;\beta^{\top}w\;+\;\gamma\|w\|_{1},$$  |  | (31)
L930: which is the Lagrangian relaxation of cite114†Eq. 10 with $\|w\|_{1}\leq k$ and $\lambda=1$. Equivalently, cite136†Eq. 31 can be viewed as a kernelized LASSO: the quadratic term $w^{\top}Kw$ penalizes selecting redundant datasets, while the linear term $-\beta^{\top}w$ rewards alignment with the target gradient, and the $\ell_{1}$ penalty promotes sparse valuation weights.
L931: As $\gamma$ increases, the solution becomes sparser; as $\gamma\to 0$, the solution approaches the minimum-norm matching solution in Gram space.
L932: We solve the $\ell_{1}$-regularized KMM objective as a convex quadratic program using CVXPY (cite253†Diamond and Boyd, 2016 ; cite254†Agrawal et al., 2018 ), with OSQP (cite255†Stellato et al., 2020 ) as the default solver.

Computing dataset-level gradients $\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and the target gradient $g_{\mathrm{tar}}$ requires $O(Nd)$ time and memory when gradients are materialized explicitly; in practice, compressed representations (e.g., random projections (cite98†Hu et al., 2025a ) or low-rank adapters) can substantially reduce the effective dimensionality $d$ without affecting relative alignment.
L251: Optimizing KMM in gradient space using first-order methods incurs $O(Nd)$ time per iteration, for a total cost of $O(TNd)$ over $T$ iterations, with $O(Nd)$ memory to store the gradient matrix. In contrast, the Gram-space formulation constructs a Gram matrix $K\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ at a cost of $O(N^{2}d)$ time and $O(N^{2})$ memory, followed by solving a convex quadratic program with worst-case cost $O(N^{3})$.
L252: In our setting, the number of datasets $N$ is typically small (tens to hundreds), while the gradient dimensionality $d$ can be extremely large (billions), making both formulations practical.

L261: 
L262: ### 4.1 Dataset Valuation Experimental Setup
L263: 
L264: We evaluate dataset valuation across post-training settings, using multilingual transfer as the primary controlled benchmark and later extending to other tasks to test generalization beyond multilingual settings.
L265: ##### Training.
L266: We evaluate two post-training paradigms with LoRA (cite117†Hu et al., 2022 ) rank $r=8$ by default. For multilingual SFT, we use the Aya Dataset (cite118†Singh et al., 2024 ) and select 26 languages that are supported by the underlying base model. For multilingual RL-GRPO, we use the GSM8K source split from Big-Math (cite119†Albalak et al., 2025 ) and translate problem prompts into 6 languages.
L267: We also include instruction-following experiments with math, code, and general instruction-tuning auxiliaries to test generalization beyond language-based tasks. We leave training dataset selection (cite61†Sec. F.1 ), hyperparameter details (cite120†Tab. 7 ,cite121†Tab. 8 ), and the effect of LoRA rank $r$ (cite122†Fig. 8 ) to the Appendix.
L268: ##### Baselines.
L269: We compare our gradient method against several baselines, including DataModel (cite103†Ilyas et al., 2022 ) and a compressed sensing (CS) variant reframed into dataset level valuation methods, as well as SOTA method GradEx-Forward-Selection (FS) and GradEx-Random-Ensemble (RE) (cite123†Li et al., 2024 ) applied in a highly similar dataset selection setup. DataModel is a linear regression model which requires a large amount of evaluation metrics labels from training on randomly sampled auxiliary subsets.
L270: GradEx estimates dataset values through fast linear approximations to training dynamics and random projections for dimensionality reduction, yet it depends on specific properties of the cross-entropy loss, making them only applicable to SFT. We defer more baseline method details to cite65†App. G . We use cosine similarity for One-Step gradient and Task Vectors (TV) to remove the effect of gradient norms.
L271: ##### Evaluation Protocol.
L272: Given valuation scores over auxiliary datasets, according to cite124†Eq. 2 , we evaluate each method by constructing auxiliary subsets of varying sizes $k$ using a greedy selection of datasets with strictly positive scores, and measuring the resulting post-training performance on the target task. To summarize performance across budget levels, we report (i) the best performance achieved over all $k$, reflecting a method’s maximum attainable utility, and (ii) Borda count to aggregate rankings across $k$ values.
L273: To eliminate the confounding effect that selecting more data can trivially improve performance, we additionally introduce a fixed-compute evaluation protocol: the total number of optimizer updates is held constant across all values of $k$. By default, we allocate a fixed fraction of training steps to auxiliary data and distribute these steps uniformly across the selected auxiliary datasets (see cite79†App. H for sensitivity analysis for uniform design choice).
