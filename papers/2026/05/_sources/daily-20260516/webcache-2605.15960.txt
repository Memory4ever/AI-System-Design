L47: # Imperfect World Models are Exploitable
L48: 
L49: Logan Mondal Bhamidipaty ^{†}^{†}thanks: l.m.bhamidipaty@ed.ac.uk Affiliation: University of Edinburgh    Esmeralda S. L50: ###### Abstract
L51: We propose a novel definition of model exploitation in reinforcement learning. Informally, a world model is exploitable if it implies that one policy should be strictly preferred over another while the environment’s true transition model implies the reverse. We analogize our definition with a prior characterization of reward hacking but show that the associated proof of inevitability does not transfer to exploitation.
L52: To overcome this obstruction, we develop a general theory of reward hacking and model exploitation that proves that exploitation is essentially unavoidable on large policy sets and yields the corresponding claim for hacking as a special case. Unfortunately, we also find that the conditions that guarantee unhackability in finite policy sets have no counterpart that precludes exploitation. Consequently, we introduce a relaxed notion of exploitation and derive a safe horizon within which it can be avoided.
L53: Taken together, our results establish a formal bridge between reward hacking and model exploitation and elucidate the limits of safe planning in world models.
L54: ## 1 Introduction
L55: Efficient real-world planning typically requires approximating complex dynamics (cite35†Simon, 1955 ; cite36†Javed and Sutton, 2024 ). In the best case, well-chosen approximations unravel otherwise intractable problems, as with admissible heuristics in search (cite37†Hart et al., 1968 ), model reduction in dynamical systems (cite38†Antoulas, 2005 ), and latent-space world models in sequential decision-making (cite39†Hafner et al., 2019 ).
L56: In the worst case, however, ill-chosen approximations can compromise safety, as with mimicry in evolutionary biology (cite40†Bates, 1862 ), arbitrage in financial markets (cite41†Ross, 1976 ), and speedrunning in video games (cite42†Scully-Blaker, 2014 ).
L57: In reinforcement learning (RL), this liability arises not only exogenously from adversaries but also endogenously from the optimizer itself: an agent trained to maximize total expected reward under an imperfect model^{1}^{1} 1 We use “model” to mean the transition model of a Markov decision process (cite7†Section 2 ) or an agent’s approximation to it, sometimes called a world model (cite43†Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018 ).
L58: We do not use it to mean the policy (as a generative model of actions) as is sometimes meant in other RL literature. may discover behavior that performs well in simulation but poorly in reality (cite44†Jakobi et al., 1995 ; cite45†Tobin et al., 2017 ). This failure mode is called model exploitation (cite43†Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018 ; cite46†Janner et al., 2019 ), and a natural question is whether it can be avoided.
L59: This question pertains to notable results in RL theory including the simulation lemma (cite47†Kearns and Singh, 2002 ) and the value equivalence principle (cite48†Grimm et al., 2020 ). Both implicitly use predictive performance to measure the quality of a model’s approximation. The simulation lemma does so cardinally by bounding the error from optimizing under an imperfect model, while the value equivalence principle does so locally by characterizing when two models induce identical Bellman updates.
L60: Modern world models also emphasize predictive performance, typically learning latent-space models of transitions (cite39†Hafner et al., 2019 ; cite49†Balestriero and LeCun, 2025 ).
L61: We argue that while this emphasis on performance is necessary for efficient planning, it is not sufficient for safe planning. Recent work on reward specification (cite50†Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017 ; cite51†Abel et al., 2021 ) views reward functions as observations of the designer’s intent to communicate a goal, which may be an imperfect reflection of a true objective. Empirical transition models are similarly imperfect.

Both modify the MDP to cope with model uncertainty; our work instead characterizes when that uncertainty inverts the policy ordering.
L254: ##### World models.
L255: A growing family of methods trains policies entirely inside learned dynamics models, from the Dreamer line (cite90†Hafner et al., 2020 ; cite91†Hafner et al., 2021 ; cite92†Hafner et al., 2025a ; cite93†Hafner et al., 2025b ) to MuZero (cite94†Schrittwieser et al., 2020 ) to recent JEPA-style predictive representations (cite49†Balestriero and LeCun, 2025 ). These methods measure model quality through predictive accuracy or downstream task performance, both of which are cardinal.
L256: Our results show that low prediction error does not preclude ordinal failures: a model can be accurate yet still invert the policy ordering induced by the true dynamics (cite64†Theorem 2 ).
L257: ##### Outside machine learning.
L258: Optimizing against an imperfect model is a recurring failure mode across disciplines, so there is an extensive literature that exists outside machine learning. For example, in macroeconomics, the Lucas critique (cite95†Lucas, 1976 ) warns that policy rules derived from an estimated model can fail when agents adapt to the policy itself, and robust control (cite96†Hansen and Sargent, 2008 ) responds by optimizing against worst-case model perturbations.
L259: In sim-to-real robotics, controllers optimized in simulation routinely exploit discrepancies with real-world physics (cite44†Jakobi et al., 1995 ; cite45†Tobin et al., 2017 ). Our formalization of exploitation as a value inversion applies in principle to any setting where a proxy model guides optimization over a structured decision space.
L260: ## 5 Conclusion
L261: There is increasing interest in approaches to decision-making that combine a forward predictive model with planning. Although there is substantial literature on the design and analysis of such model-based algorithms, the emergence of world models that make predictions directly from rich low-level sensor streams pushes the boundaries of these methods. This calls for a fresh look at the questions of model robustness and safety of the computed policies.
L262: In this paper, we formalize the concept of model exploitation and study the conditions under which policies trained on empirical models may be safe. We find that in sufficiently rich policy classes, exploitability is inevitable. However, under a relaxed definition, we can establish conditions for unexploitability.
L263: We suggest that this could guide the development of new approaches to safe policy synthesis, as well as parallel methods for quantifying the quality of synthesized policies with respect to such safety objectives.
L264: ##### Limitations and future work.
L265: We identify three main limitations of our work. First, our results, like those in cite53†Skalse et al. (2022) , concern MDPs with finite state and action spaces. We defer extensions to continuous and partially observable MDPs (POMDPs) to future work, noting that solving the former may yield the latter (since every POMDP can be written as a continuous belief-state MDP (cite97†Åström, 1965 )). Second, also like in cite53†Skalse et al. (2022) , our notion of exploitation is binary and restrictive.
L266: In practice, it is unlikely that small, rare value inversions will pose meaningful safety concerns. Future work could refine our notion of exploitation by introducing a more granular measure of exploitability (e.g., deriving bounds using correlation metrics (cite98†Laidlaw et al., 2025 ) or expanding upon $\varepsilon$-exploitation). Third, there are, as of yet, no known closed-form necessary and sufficient conditions that guarantee unexploitability in finite policy sets.
L267: Although we question whether such simple conditions exist (cite99†Question 1 ), there is still area to explore. Researchers interested in pursuing this direction may, for instance, find it productive to begin work on special finite policy sets and specific MDPs (e.g., characterize exploitation on deterministic policies in linear MDPs).

L316: We first provide a table summarizing all relevant notation.
L317: Notation  | Meaning  | Definition
L318: --- | --- | ---
L319: $\mathcal{S}$  | State space  |
L320: $\mathcal{A}$  | Action space  |
L321: $\mathcal{T}$  | Transition model  | $\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})$
L322: $d_{0}$  | Initial state distribution  | $d_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})$
L323: $\mathcal{R}$  | Reward function  | $\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$
L324: $\gamma$  | Discount factor  | $\gamma\in[0,1)$
L325: $\mathcal{M}$  | Markov decision process  | $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},d_{0},\mathcal{R},\gamma)$
L326: $\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{R}$  | Environment (MDP without reward)  | $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},d_{0},\_,\gamma)$
L327: $\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{T}$  | Task (MDP without transitions)  | $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\_,d_{0},\mathcal{R},\gamma)$
L328: $\pi$  | Stationary policy  | $\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})$
L329: $\Pi$  | Policy set  |
L330: $\Pi^{+}$  | Interior of all stationary policies  | $\{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})^{|\mathcal{S}|}:\pi(a|s)>0\ \forall_{s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}\}$
L331: $\hat{\Pi}$  | Finite policy set  |
L332: $\mathcal{F}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}(s,a)$  | Discounted visit counts  | $\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbbm{1}(s_{t}{=}s,a_{t}{=}a)$
L333: $J(\pi)$  | Policy value  | $\langle\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}\rangle$
L334: $J_{\mathcal{T}}(\pi)$  | Value under transition model $\mathcal{T}$  | $\langle\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{T}}\rangle$
L335: $J_{\mathcal{R}}(\pi)$  | Value under reward function $\mathcal{R}$  | $\langle\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}^{\pi}\rangle$
L336: $J_{i}(\pi)$  | Value under $\mathcal{R}_{i}$ or $\mathcal{T}_{i}$  | (context determines index)
L337: $V^{\pi}(s)$  | State-value function  | $\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}[G(\tau)\mid s_{0}=s]$
L338: $r^{\pi}(s)$  | Per-state expected reward  | $\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\,\mathcal{R}(s,a)$
L339: $(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\prime})$ hackable  | Reward hacking  | $\exists_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ J_{\mathcal{R}}(\pi)>J_{\mathcal{R}}(\pi^{\prime})$ and $J_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}(\pi^{\prime})>J_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}(\pi)$
L340: $(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime})$ exploitable  | Model exploitation  | $\exists_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ J_{\mathcal{T}}(\pi)>J_{\mathcal{T}}(\pi^{\prime})$ and $J_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(\pi^{\prime})>J_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(\pi)$
L341: Equivalent  | Same policy ordering  | $\forall_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ J_{1}(\pi)\geq J_{1}(\pi^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow J_{2}(\pi)\geq J_{2}(\pi^{\prime})$
L342: Trivial  | Constant value  | $\forall_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ J(\pi)=J(\pi^{\prime})$
