L68: # Rethinking the Security of DP-SGD: A Corrected Analysis of Differentially Private Machine Learning
L69: 
L70: Wenhao Wang Affiliation: Monash University, Australia email: wenhao.wang@monash.edu , Shujie Cui Affiliation: Monash University, Australia email: shujie.cui@monash.edu , Hui Cui Affiliation: Monash University, Australia email: hui.cui@monash.edu and Xingliang Yuan Affiliation: University of Melbourne, Australia email: xingliang.yuan@unimelb.edu.au
L71: ###### Abstract.
L72: Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) has been widely adopted to protect training data in machine learning. The privacy guarantee of DP-SGD and the DP-mechanisms built upon it is usually analyzed through a formal security game, in which an adversary infers whether a particular individual data record is included in the training dataset based on the mechanism’s output.
L73: Privacy leakage is characterized by the adversary’s privacy curve, which reports the false negative rate (FNR) as a function of the false positive rate (FPR). The privacy guarantee is defined as the lower bound of this curve.
L74: We observe that the privacy guarantees claimed for these mechanisms in many existing papers are derived from a mismatched game setting.
L75: Specifically, they formalize the mechanisms as the Subsampled Gaussian Mechanism (SGM), where Gaussian noise is added to the sum of gradients computed from a Poisson-sampled batch of data. Indeed, the training procedure in these mechanisms introduces an additional normalization step: the noisy sum is further normalized either by the expected batch size or by the sampled batch size. Thus, these mechanisms should be formalized as either the Expected-Averaged SGM (EASGM) or the Batch-Averaged SGM (ASGM).
L76: The privacy auditing of DP-SGD suffers from the same issue, as it assesses the privacy guarantee of DP-SGD by treating it as an SGM.
L77: We therefore re-analyze the privacy guarantee of these mechanisms under the corresponding EASGM and ASGM formalizations. Our analysis shows that, in theory, these DP-SGD mechanisms can yield weaker privacy guarantees than the SGM-based guarantee, suggesting that, in some settings, the true privacy leakage can exceed the reported SGM-based guarantee.
L78: We also empirically audit the leakage of implementations of four state-of-the-art DP-SGD algorithms, including the implementation used in Meta’s Opacus library, and show that empirical leakage exceeds the SGM-based guarantees. Finally, we conduct a thorough code audit of Opacus versions v0.9.0–v1.5.4 and derive a privacy guarantee for the latest Opacus implementation.

L80: 
L81: Differential privacy, DP-SGD, Correctness
L82: ## 1. Introduction
L83: 
L84: Figure 1. The left figure illustrates the procedure of the DP game, while the right figure presents the correct mechanism formalization proposed in this paper and the incorrect mechanism formalizations used in prior works (cite56†Abadi et al., 2016 ; cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ; cite58†Fu et al., 2024 ).
L85: Deep learning models leak sensitive information of the training data (cite59†Du et al., 2025 ; cite60†Xiang et al., 2025 ). Differential Privacy (DP) is the de facto standard and has been widely adopted for mitigating such risks (cite61†Aerni et al., 2024 ). For example, Google recently introduced VaultGemma (cite62†Google Research, 2025 ), a large language model (LLM) trained with DP to provide strong privacy guarantees, which they describe as “the world’s most capable differentially private LLM.”
L86: DP-SGD brief and privacy analysis. The state-of-the-art differentially private training method is Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) (cite56†Abadi et al., 2016 ), and it has been used by companies including Google (cite63†Google Privacy Sandbox, 2025 ), Meta (cite64†Yousefpour et al., 2021 ) for privacy protection in machine learning services. Meta also provides an open-source DP-SGD implementation through its Opacus library (cite64†Yousefpour et al., 2021 ).
L87: DP-SGD updates the model round by round, and each round consists of three basic steps: (i) Form a batch $B$ via Poisson sampling, including each data point with probability $q$; (ii) Compute the gradient $g_{i}$ of each sample in the batch; and (iii) Clip each $g_{i}$ at the threshold $C$ into $\overline{g}_{i}$, get the aggregated value $\sum_{i\in B}\overline{g}_{i}$, and add calibrated Gaussian noise $G$ with standard deviation $\sigma$ to obtain the noisy sum $\mathcal{S}=\sum_{i\in B}\overline{g}_{i}+G$.
L88: These three steps are called Subsampled Gaussian Mechanism (SGM). In different DP-SGD mechanisms, $\mathcal{S}$ could be normalized and then used to update the model. For instance, in the initial DP-SGD proposed by Google (cite56†Abadi et al., 2016 ), the noisy sum of clipped gradients $\mathcal{S}$ is normalized by the expected batch size $N\cdot q$, which we denote as the Expected-Averaged SGM (EASGM) (cite56†Abadi et al., 2016 ; cite65†Li et al., 2025 ), where $N$ is the size of the training dataset.
L89: In a recent DP-SGD variant (cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ) (Algorithm 1 of (cite66†Bu et al., 2020a )), $\mathcal{S}$ is normalized by the batch size $|B|$, and is referred to as Batch-Averaged SGM (ASGM).
L90: The privacy guarantee of the DP-SGD training process can be decomposed into per-round guarantees, which are then composed using standard composition techniques (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 ). DP-SGD’s per-round privacy guarantee is typically analyzed through a formalized game between a challenger and an adversary (cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ; cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ).
L91: In this game, the challenger applies one round of DP-SGD to generate a model update on one of two neighboring datasets, $D$ and $D^{\prime}$, which differ by a single individual $d$, and then releases the update. The adversary has the knowledge of both neighboring datasets and the parameters of DP-SGD, including the sampling ratio $q$ and the noise scale $\sigma$, and aims to determine which dataset has been used to generate the model update.
L92: Privacy leakage is characterized by the adversary’s privacy curve, which reports the false negative rate (FNR) as a function of the false positive rate (FPR). The privacy guarantee is defined as the lower bound of this function (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 ). Depending on the tool or framework (e.g., RDP (cite69†Mironov et al., 2019 ) and f-DP (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 )) used for the analysis, the obtained privacy guarantee could be close or far away from the optimal lower bound.
L93: For instance, Edgeworth Expansion (cite70†Zheng et al., 2020 ) can yield a tighter privacy guarantee than the Central Limit Theorem (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 ). The closer to the optimal lower bound, the tighter the privacy guarantee. Tighter guarantees are desirable because they certify the same privacy level with less noise and improved output quality. However, exact tightness is often difficult to obtain; thus, prior work often derives nearly tight guarantees instead (cite71†Wang et al., 2023 ).
L94: Mismatched formalization of normalized DP-SGD. However, for the privacy analysis, as shown in Fig. cite72†1 , prior studies (cite56†Abadi et al., 2016 ; cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ) formalize one-round EASGM-based and ASGM-based DP-SGD as SGM in the game, thereby omitting the normalization step in the training procedure, and accordingly use the SGM guarantee to bound their per-round privacy leakage.
L95: Indeed, the SGM-based guarantee may be unsound for the actual mechanism, because privacy guarantees depend directly on the output distribution of the mechanism, whereas this mismatch changes the distribution. Previous work (cite73†Lebeda et al., 2025 ) identifies this issue for EASGM-based mechanisms, but does not formally analyze the real privacy guarantees.
L96: Privacy auditing of DP-SGD. Theoretical bounds assume idealized, bug-free implementations, which may not hold in practice due to implementation errors (cite74†Cebere et al., 2026 ). Thus, the privacy leakage of implementations could deviate from theoretical expectations (cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ). Numerous studies (cite75†Nasr et al., 2021 ; cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ; cite60†Xiang et al., 2025 ) have attempted to audit whether DP-SGD implementations violate theoretical privacy guarantees.
L97: Following the DP security game, they instantiate the adversary as a distinguisher $\phi$, repeatedly execute the DP-SGD implementations with two neighboring datasets, and use $\phi$ to infer which dataset was used based on obtained model updates. Finally, they compare the empirical privacy leakage, measured by FNR at a fixed FPR, with the corresponding theoretical value.
L98: If the empirical FNR is above the FNR at the same fixed FPR implied by the privacy guarantee, no privacy violation is considered to have been found in the implementation. Many studies have found no violation in DP-SGD implementations in practice (cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ; cite76†Koskela and Mohammadi, 2025b ; cite77†Nasr et al., 2025 ; cite78†Steinke et al., 2023a ; cite60†Xiang et al., 2025 ).
L99: However, prior auditing works, including (cite76†Koskela and Mohammadi, 2025b ; cite78†Steinke et al., 2023a ; cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ; cite77†Nasr et al., 2025 ; cite60†Xiang et al., 2025 ), have primarily focused on auditing per-round SGM-based DP-SGD, without considering the normalization variants used in practice.
L100: For instance, Steinke et al. (cite78†Steinke et al., 2023a ) audit the DP-SGD implementation in Algorithm 2 of (cite79†Steinke et al., 2023b ), and Xiang et al. (cite60†Xiang et al., 2025 ) follow the same setting. Nasr et al. (cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ) audit the implementation in Algorithm 2 of (cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ), and Koskela et al. (cite76†Koskela and Mohammadi, 2025b ) follow the setting in Algorithm 4 of (cite80†Koskela and Mohammadi, 2024 ).
L101: Consequently, these works do not audit the privacy leakage of implementations of EASGM- and ASGM-based DP-SGD algorithms, leaving it unclear whether such implementations truly satisfy the theoretical SGM privacy guarantee.
L102: Mismatched formalization of other DP mechanisms. Many other state-of-the-art differentially private mechanisms are built on EASGM-based or ASGM-based DP-SGD, including DP–FETA (cite65†Li et al., 2025 ), PrivImage (cite81†Li et al., 2024a ), dp–promise (cite82†Wang et al., 2024 ), DP–SUR (cite58†Fu et al., 2024 ), DISK (cite83†Zhang et al., 2025 ), and TrainDPGAN (cite84†Bie et al., 2023 ). These mechanisms also rely on SGM-based privacy analysis to derive their privacy guarantees.
L103: Consequently, the privacy analysis of these mechanisms suffers from the same mismatch as that of normalized DP-SGD.
L104: The theoretical privacy analysis guides the configuration of model training in reality to achieve the required privacy guarantee. However, these mismatches may underestimate the actual privacy leakage, resulting in a training procedure failing to satisfy the claimed privacy guarantee. Thus, it is necessary to re-examine the privacy guarantees used for EASGM-based and ASGM-based DP-SGD and their derivatives in the correct game setting.
L105: Our contributions. In this paper, we revisit the security analysis of per-round EASGM- and ASGM-based DP-SGD by explicitly incorporating their normalization steps into the privacy game, and derive corresponding privacy guarantees.
L106: Our analysis is conducted in the $f$-DP framework, a de facto standard framework for differential privacy analysis (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 ; cite68†Nasr et al., 2023 ; cite60†Xiang et al., 2025 ), which characterizes privacy through a trade-off function between the false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR). We compare the derived guarantees with the one-round SGM guarantee established in (cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ).
L107: To the best of our knowledge, only Bu et al. (cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ) provide a tight one-round privacy guarantee in the $f$-DP framework and an explicit trade-off function, making their work a natural baseline for comparison.
L108: Our analysis reveals that the privacy guarantees of EASGM and ASGM become weaker as the output dimension increases, and they are generally weaker than that of SGM when the output dimension exceeds certain values. For instance, our analysis shows that, in some cases, under the same FNR of $0.5$, the FPRs of EASGM and ASGM fall below $0.2$, whereas the previously claimed value is above $0.4$ (Fig. cite85†5 ).
L109: Furthermore, when the sampling ratio is 1, our analysis is tight: the FNR–FPR trade-off is achievable by an adversary without any amplification or relaxation introduced by analytical approximation. This finding suggests that the privacy leakage of EASGM- and ASGM-based DP-SGD implementations can exceed the guarantee claimed under SGM.
L110: We also audit the privacy of EASGM- and ASGM-based implementations to assess whether their privacy leakage can exceed the SGM guarantee with both synthetic and real-world datasets. We perform tight auditing to detect maximum privacy leakage. However, because tight auditing is computationally prohibitive on large datasets when the output dimension matches real-world scenarios, our auditing focuses on small datasets.
L111: We observe that the audited privacy leakage increases with the output dimension and can exceed the SGM guarantee.
L112: We also conduct a code audit of privacy_engine.py across Opacus versions v0.9.0 to v1.5.4 and identify an additional DP-SGD variant in recent v1.$\ast$ releases that normalizes the noisy gradient by $\lfloor Nq\rfloor$. We denote this variant by FEASGM (Definition cite86†A.1 ).
L113: Although a previous Opacus bug report suggests that, for large datasets, the privacy guarantee of FEASGM is tightly bounded by that of SGM (Issue #571 (cite87†tudorcebere, 2023 )), our analysis shows that FEASGM can still provide a significantly weaker privacy guarantee than SGM even in the large-dataset regime (Appendix cite35†A.4 ).
L114: To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to provide a systematic per-round privacy analysis of EASGM- and ASGM-based DP-SGD. We summarize our contributions as follows:
L115: Re-analyzing privacy guarantee of DP-SGD. We conduct a comprehensive analysis of the per-round privacy guarantees of EASGM- and ASGM-based DP-SGD using tight analysis tools and derive a bound that can be used to estimate the actual strength of these guarantees. Moreover, we visualize this bound using a Central Limit Theorem (CLT)-based approximation, providing an intuitive understanding of their privacy behavior.
L116: Our analysis shows that the privacy guarantees of EASGM and ASGM can become substantially weaker than the corresponding SGM-based guarantees, especially in high-dimensional settings.
L117: Auditing the privacy leakage of DP-SGD implementations. We audit the per-round DP-SGD procedures used in four state-of-the-art differentially private machine learning methods—DP-SUR (cite58†Fu et al., 2024 ), DPSGD-HF (cite88†Tramer and Boneh, 2020 ), DP-FETA (cite65†Li et al., 2025 ), and PrivImage (cite81†Li et al., 2024a ). DPSGD-HF, DP-FETA, and PrivImage rely on Meta’s Opacus library for their DP-SGD implementations.
L118: For these three methods, we audit the implementations using Opacus versions consistent with their algorithmic descriptions. Empirical evaluations on widely used public datasets—CIFAR-10, MNIST, and FMNIST—show that, under the audited settings, all four implementations exhibit privacy leakage exceeding the SGM guarantee established in (cite57†Bu et al., 2020b ).
L119: Thorough code auditing of the Opacus library. We examine the evolution of the privacy_engine.py file in the Opacus library from v0.9.0 to v1.5.4 and find that version v0.13.0 use ASGM, versions v0.14.0–v1.4.$\ast$ use EASGM, and versions v1.5.$\ast$ use FEASGM (Section cite27†5.5 ). We further analyze the privacy guarantee of multi-round FEASGM-based DP-SGD and show that FEASGM can yield a weaker privacy guarantee than SGM (Appendix cite35†A.4 ).

L140: ###### Definition 2.1.
L141: Differential Privacy (cite58†Fu et al., 2024 ). A randomized algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\epsilon,\delta)$-differential privacy if, for any two adjacent datasets $D$ and $D^{\prime}$, and for all possible output sets $O$, $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in O]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\in O]+\delta$, where $\epsilon>0$ and $\delta\in[0,1]$ are privacy parameters.
L142: Here, $\epsilon$ and $\delta$ (collectively referred to as the privacy budget) quantify the level of privacy loss: smaller values of either parameter imply stronger privacy guarantees. Datasets $D$ and $D^{\prime}$ are said to be neighboring if they differ by at most one individual record, i.e., $D=D^{\prime}\cup\{d\}$ or $D^{\prime}=D\cup\{d\}$.
L143: A functional view of DP: $f$-DP. To interpret the privacy guarantee of a mechanism $\mathcal{M}$, prior work uses the security game illustrated in Fig cite72†1 (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 ; cite92†Annamalai et al., 2024a ).
L144: The adversary’s guess in the DP game can be formalized as the following hypothesis test, $\mathbf{H_{0}}:O\sim\mathcal{M}(D)$, and $\quad\mathbf{H_{1}}:O\sim\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})$. Consider a rejection rule $\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\{0,1\}$ in above hypothesis testing setup.
L145: Two key types of errors are: Type I error (false positive rate): $\alpha=\Pr[\mathcal{R}(y)=1\mid\mathbf{H_{0}}]$, the chance of incorrectly rejecting $\mathbf{H_{0}}$ when it is actually true; Type II error (false negative rate): $\beta=\Pr[\mathcal{R}(y)=0\mid\mathbf{H_{1}}]$, the chance of incorrectly accepting $\mathbf{H_{0}}$ when $\mathbf{H_{1}}$ is true.
L146: The adversary aims to reduce one type of error while fixing the other, by determining the smallest achievable $\beta$ for a given $\alpha$, leading to the following definition.
L147: ###### Definition 2.2.
L148: Trade-off function (cite67†Dong et al., 2019 ). Given two distributions $P$ and $Q$, the trade-off function is defined as: $T(P,Q)(\alpha)=\inf_{\mathcal{R}}\left\{\beta_{\mathcal{R}}:\alpha_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\alpha\right\}$. Let $\mathcal{R}$ be a rejection rule for the hypothesis testing problem $(P,Q)$, which aims to determine whether a given sample $\mathcal{O}$ is drawn from $P$ or $Q$. That is, $H_{0}:\mathcal{O}\sim P,\quad H_{1}:\mathcal{O}\sim Q$.
L149: The infimum is taken over all such rejection rules $\mathcal{R}$.
L150: In the DP game, the trade–off function $f=T(P,Q)$ for neighboring outputs $P=\mathcal{M}(D)$ and $Q=\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})$ provides the tightest characterization of the mechanism’s privacy leakage for the pair $(D,D^{\prime})$. It determines the adversary’s minimum Type II error under a fixed Type I error $\alpha$ (cite93†Kaissis et al., 2024 ): $f(\alpha)$.
L151: Hence, the trade–off function directly characterizes the pairwise privacy leakage of $\mathcal{M}$; In particular, if $T(P,Q)\geq T(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})$, then the pairwise privacy leakage corresponding to $(P,Q)$ is smaller than that corresponding to $(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})$.
