L46: Finite-Horizon Safety at Runtime via Parallel Rollouts
L47: Taekyung Kim Affiliation: Department of Robotics    Hideki Okamoto Affiliation: Toyota Motor North America, Research & Development, Ann Arbor, MI, 48105, USA <first_name.last_name>@toyota.com    Bardh Hoxha Affiliation: Toyota Motor North America, Research & Development, Ann Arbor, MI, 48105, USA <first_name.last_name>@toyota.com    Georgios Fainekos Affiliation: Toyota Motor North America, Research & Development, Ann Arbor, MI, 48105, USA <first_name.last_name>@toyota.com    Dimitra Panagou Affiliation: Department of Robotics Affiliation: Department of Aerospace Engineering, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, 48109, USA taekyung@umich.edu, dpanagou@umich.edu
L48: ###### Abstract
L49: Safety-critical autonomy in unstructured environments poses significant challenges for online safety certification under evolving constraints. We propose Policy Library Control Barrier Function (PL-CBF), a runtime safety filter that evaluates a library of fallback policies via parallel finite-horizon rollouts, selects the least invasive safe mode, and enforces safety by solving a quadratic program that minimally modifies a nominal policy.
L50: We provide a theoretical analysis based on a finite-horizon language metric over closed-loop behaviors, characterizing policy-library coverage requirements for certifying finite-horizon safety.
L51: Simulations on a planar double-integrator (4 states), highway driving with abrupt friction changes using a realistic nonlinear vehicle model (8 states), and 3D quadrotor navigation in crowded dynamic environments (12 states) demonstrate improved safety coverage over single-policy safety filters while retaining millisecond-level runtime. cite29†[Code]†github.com cite30†[Project Page]†www.taekyung.me ^{1}^{1} 1 Project page: cite30†https://www.taekyung.me/plcbf†www.taekyung.me cite31†[Video]†youtu.be L52: ## I INTRODUCTION
L53: The deployment of autonomous systems in unstructured and real-world environments represents one of the most significant engineering challenges of the modern era.
L54: In contrast to classical settings where constraints and model parameters are fixed and fully modeled, a deployed robot operates under perception-limited information: the safe area is revealed online [cite32†1 , cite33†2 , cite34†3 ], dynamic obstacles appear and disappear from view [cite35†4 , cite36†5 ], and operating conditions may change over time [cite37†6 , cite38†7 , cite39†8 ].
L55: Consequently, the relevant safety objective is not merely stability with respect to a known invariant set, but the ability to ensure constraint satisfaction in real time under the latest perceived model and constraints.
L56: Formal verification tools such as Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis provide strong safety guarantees by computing the viability kernel [cite40†9 ]. However, these approaches typically face the curse of dimensionality and become computationally intractable for the high-dimensional systems and complex constraints that arise in robotics. Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) provide a more scalable alternative by enforcing pointwise constraints on the control input [cite41†10 ].
L57: Yet, standard CBF synthesis often presumes access to an explicit barrier function for the full nonlinear system under a fixed constraint description, which is difficult to obtain in general and may not remain valid as perception updates change the effective constraints online [cite42†11 ].
L58: A complementary line of work addresses the feasibility aspect by coupling safety filtering with a certified recovery maneuver. In particular, Backup CBFs [cite43†12 ] enforce recursive feasibility by leveraging a designated backup policy and a terminal controlled invariant set, so that the closed loop can be driven to a region where safety can be maintained indefinitely.
L59: While conceptually appealing, constructing such a backup policy together with a corresponding terminal invariant set for complex nonlinear dynamics is often difficult in practice, especially when the operational envelope and constraints depend on online perception.
L60: More recently, rollout-based safety filters have been proposed to mitigate the need for offline barrier synthesis. Policy CBFs (PCBFs) construct finite-horizon safety certificates directly from trajectory rollouts [cite44†13 , cite45†14 ], enabling runtime certificate construction without offline value-function computation. However, existing runtime rollout-based filters still hinge on the validity of a single designated fallback policy.
L61: In unstructured settings, no single maneuver is reliably safe across all perception updates: for example, a stop maneuver can fail under sudden friction loss, and a fixed evasive maneuver can become infeasible when newly detected obstacles or narrow passages invalidate that behavior. This single-fallback dependence induces conservatism and can lead to failures even when an alternative safe maneuver exists.

We represent this set as the $0$-superlevel set of a continuously differentiable function $h_{k}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$:
L117: 
L118:  | $$\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mid h_{k}({\bm{x}})\geq 0\}.$$  |  | (6)
L119: We do not impose a monotonicity assumption on the sequence $\{\mathcal{C}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$: newly observed free space may enlarge the certified region, whereas newly detected obstacles or occlusions may invalidate regions previously regarded as safe.
L120: 
L121: Using the same notation $\varphi^{\pi}_{(\cdot)}(\cdot)$ introduced in cite8†Sec. II-A , the look-ahead state at time $t+\tau$ from the current state ${\bm{x}}(t)$ under policy $\pi$ is $\varphi^{\pi}_{\tau}({\bm{x}}(t))$, for all $\tau\geq 0$.
L122: ### III-B Problem Statement
L123: Our primary objective is to design a safety filter that tracks a desired nominal policy $\pi_{\textup{nom}}$ while ensuring that the system remains within the perceived safe set. Because safety must be certified online using only the current perceived safe set $\mathcal{C}_{k}$ available over the sensing interval indexed by $k=\kappa(t)$, infinite-horizon safety certification is generally not theoretically possible without strong assumptions in this setting.
L124: We therefore consider a finite-horizon safety problem over a planning horizon $T$.
L125: ###### Problem 1 (Finite-Horizon Safety).
L126: 
L127: At time $t$, let $k=\kappa(t)$. Given the current perceived safe set $\mathcal{C}_{k}$, synthesize a control policy $\pi$ close to $\pi_{\textup{nom}}$ such that
L128: 
L129:  | $$\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi}\big({\bm{x}}(t)\big)\in\mathcal{C}_{k},\quad\forall\tau\in[0,T].$$  |  | (7)
L130: Solving cite47†Problem 1 recursively across sensing updates allows the robot to navigate safely through the currently observed environment while continuously incorporating new perceived safety constraints $\{h_{k}\}$.
L131: ###### Remark 1 (Connections to Model Predictive Control (MPC)).
L132: The finite-horizon safety formulation in cite47†Problem 1 is closely related to MPC practices. Theoretically, recursive feasibility in finite-horizon MPC often requires a terminal constraint set. However, in environments where such a terminal set is difficult to synthesize online, practitioners often omit terminal constraints as the resulting MPC remains highly effective in practice [cite48†15 , cite49†16 ].
L133: Our formulation which drops the requirement for certifying infinite-time invariance is analogous to dropping the terminal constraint in MPC. However, a key distinction lies in the computational objective. While MPC typically requires solving a computationally expensive optimization problem online, our goal is to design a safety filter that is computationally efficient to construct a safety certificate at runtime.
L134: ## IV POLICY LIBRARY CBF
L135: ### IV-A The Policy Library CBF Formulation
L136: 
L137: To overcome the limitations of relying on a single fallback maneuver, we propose the Policy Library Control Barrier Function (PL-CBF). Instead of relying on a single backup or fallback policy, we define a library of $K$ candidate fallback policies
L138: 
L139:  | $$\Pi=\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{K}\}.$$  |  | (8)
L140: We then define the finite-horizon policy library value function^{2}^{2} 2 For the remainder of this paper, we omit the subscript $k$ and denote the current perception snapshot $h_{k}$ simply by $h$ when the meaning is clear.
L141: 
L142:  | $\displaystyle H_{T}^{\Pi}({\bm{x}})$  | $\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{\pi_{i}\in\Pi}\left\{H_{T}^{\pi_{i}}({\bm{x}})\right\}$  |  | (9)
L143:  |  | $\displaystyle=\max_{\pi_{i}\in\Pi}\left\{\inf_{\tau\in[0,T]}h\left(\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi_{i}}({\bm{x}})\right)\right\}.$  |
L144: Conceptually, if any policy in the library $\Pi$ can maintain nonnegative constraint values over $[0,T]$, then ${\bm{x}}$ is considered safe for horizon $T$. This induces the PL-CBF safe set
L145: 
L146:  | $$\mathcal{S}_{T}^{\Pi}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\pi_{i}\in\Pi}\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mid H^{\pi_{i}}_{T}({\bm{x}})\geq 0\}.$$  |  | (10)
L147: We emphasize that the explicit construction of $\mathcal{S}_{T}^{\Pi}$ is used solely for theoretical analysis to characterize how the certified safe set evolves as the policy library $\Pi$ changes. In the runtime algorithm, we do not compute the boundary of $\mathcal{S}_{T}^{\Pi}$ or perform operations over the entire state space $\mathcal{X}$. At runtime, we only evaluate $H^{\Pi}_{T}({\bm{x}})$ at the current system state ${\bm{x}}$.
L148: We defer a discussion of the (idealized) infinite-horizon limit and its relation to CBFs and forward invariance to cite20†Sec. V-C .

L171: ### IV-B Safety Filter Synthesis
L172: We synthesize the safety filter using a QP that minimally deviates from the nominal control ${\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}=\pi_{\textup{nom}}({\bm{x}})$. To minimize the invasiveness of the safety filter, we propose selecting the active policy based on the volume of the admissible control space. While standard approaches might select the policy yielding the highest safety value, a higher safety margin does not necessarily imply a less restrictive constraint on the control input.
L173: For each policy $\pi_{i}\in\Pi$, the set of safe control inputs is defined by the corresponding CBF constraint:
L174:  | $$\begin{split}K_{\textup{cbf}}({\bm{x}};\pi_{i})=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}\mid\nabla H^{\pi_{i}}_{T}&({\bm{x}})^{\top}(f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}})\\
L175: &\geq-\alpha(H^{\pi_{i}}_{T}({\bm{x}}))\}.\end{split}$$  |  | (11)
L176: 
L177: We select the least invasive policy index $i^{\star}$ that maximizes the volume of this admissible set:
L178:  | $$\begin{split}i^{\star}&=\argmax_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textup{safe}}({\bm{x}})}\operatorname{Vol}\left(K_{\textup{cbf}}({\bm{x}};\pi_{i})\right),\\
L179: \mathcal{I}_{\textup{safe}}({\bm{x}})&\coloneqq\{\,i\in\{1,\dots,K\}\mid H^{\pi_{i}}_{T}({\bm{x}})>0\,\}.\end{split}$$  |  | (12)
L180: 
L181: The final control input is obtained by solving the following QP (see cite51†Alg. 1 ):
L182:  | $$\begin{split}{\bm{u}}^{\star}&=\argmin_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}\quad\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}\|_{2}^{2}\\
L183: \textrm{s.t.}\quad\nabla&{H_{T}^{\pi_{i^{\star}}}({\bm{x}})}^{\top}(f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}}){\bm{u}})\geq-\alpha(H_{T}^{\pi_{i^{\star}}}({\bm{x}})).\end{split}$$  |  | (13)
L184: To construct the constraint in (cite50†13 ), we require the gradient of the value function, i.e., $\nabla H_{T}^{\pi_{i^{\star}}}({\bm{x}})$. Since the value function is defined by the rollout of nonlinear dynamics, analytical gradients are generally unavailable. Therefore, we utilize automatic differentiation through the dynamics to compute the gradient efficiently at runtime [cite52†17 ].
L185: To mitigate gradient errors arising from discrete-time integration, cubic spline interpolation can be applied to the rollout trajectory as discussed in [cite45†14 ].
L186: ### IV-C Parallel Computation and Runtime Efficiency
L187: A significant advantage of the proposed PL-CBF framework is its computational structure which is naturally amenable to parallelization. Unlike HJ reachability analysis which suffers from the curse of dimensionality due to spatial gridding, our method relies on independent trajectory rollouts.
L188: The computation of the closed-loop trajectory $\varphi_{(\cdot)}^{\pi_{i}}({\bm{x}})$ and policy value function $H^{\pi_{i}}_{T}({\bm{x}})$ for each policy $\pi_{i}\in\Pi$ is decoupled and can be executed simultaneously on parallel computing hardware such as GPUs.
L189: This parallel architecture allows the safety filter to scale efficiently with the size of the policy library $K$. By constructing the safety certificate online at runtime, PL-CBF avoids offline synthesis tied to a single fixed model or constraint realization. Its effectiveness therefore depends on the suitability and diversity of the policy library $\Pi$ for the runtime scenarios encountered.
L190: ## V THEORETICAL ANALYSIS
L191: ### V-A Language Metric and Approximate Bisimulation
L192: 
L193: We formalize similarity between finite-horizon closed-loop trajectories induced by different fallback policies using language and approximate (bi)simulation metrics from the verification literature on metric transition systems [cite53†18 , cite54†19 ].
L194: The finite-horizon trajectory is $\tau\mapsto\varphi_{\tau}^{\pi}({\bm{x}})$ for $\tau\in[0,T]$. In the general framework, the language of a system is the set of external trajectories it can generate, and language metrics are Hausdorff distances between such trajectory sets induced by a trajectory metric [cite54†19 , Def. 2].

L244: ### V-C Relation to CBFs and Forward Invariance
L245: 
L246: Although PL-CBF uses a finite-horizon rollout value, the discussion in this subsection is purely conceptual and considers the idealized infinite-horizon limit.
L247: For a fixed policy $\pi$, define the infinite-horizon policy value function as $H_{\infty}^{\pi}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\inf_{t\geq 0}h(\varphi_{t}^{\pi}({\bm{x}}))$. By definition, $H_{\infty}^{\pi}({\bm{x}})\leq h({\bm{x}})$ for all ${\bm{x}}$. Moreover, $H_{\infty}^{\pi}$ is non-decreasing along the closed-loop trajectory induced by $\pi$, satisfying the monotonicity condition:
L248: 
L249:  | $$\nabla H_{\infty}^{\pi}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\left(f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}})\pi({\bm{x}})\right)\geq 0.$$  |  | (19)
L250: These properties are equivalent to $H_{\infty}^{\pi}$ satisfying a Hamilton-Jacobi variational inequality in the viscosity sense [cite40†9 ]:
L251: 
L252:  | $\displaystyle\min\Big\{$  | $\displaystyle h({\bm{x}})-H_{\infty}^{\pi}({\bm{x}}),$  |  | (20)
L253:  |  | $\displaystyle\nabla H_{\infty}^{\pi}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\left(f({\bm{x}})+g({\bm{x}})\pi({\bm{x}})\right)\Big\}=0.$  |
L254: Consequently, $H_{\infty}^{\pi}$ is a CBF on the set $\mathcal{S}^{\pi}_{\infty}$, where $\mathcal{S}^{\pi}_{\infty}\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\mid H_{\infty}^{\pi}({\bm{x}})\geq 0\}$ [cite44†13 , Thm 1].
L255: 
L256: For a policy library $\Pi$, define $H_{\infty}^{\Pi}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\max_{\pi_{i}\in\Pi}H_{\infty}^{\pi_{i}}({\bm{x}})$. Since the pointwise maximum of multiple CBFs is a CBF by utilizing nonsmooth analysis and generalized gradients in [cite60†22 ], $H_{\infty}^{\Pi}$ is also a CBF.
L257: Finally, if the library contains a backup policy $\pi_{\textup{b}}$ (cite61†Def. 2 ), i.e., $\pi_{\textup{b}}\in\Pi$, then the PL-CBF safety filter recovers the infinite-time safety (forward invariance) and recursive feasibility properties associated with backup-based safety filters.
L258: ### V-D Inclusion of Nominal Policy
L259: Many backup-based safety filters often exhibit unnecessary conservatism by modifying the nominal control even when the system is operating safely [cite43†12 , cite44†13 , cite45†14 , cite57†21 ]. This phenomenon, which we refer to as “safety evaluation on backup” [cite62†23 ], occurs because safety is typically evaluated based on the feasibility of a specific backup maneuver rather than the nominal plan itself.
L260: If the backup policy is inherently conservative or currently ill-suited to the environment, the filter may restrict the nominal controller unnecessarily to ensure the backup remains feasible.
L261: The PL-CBF addresses this by explicitly including the nominal policy $\pi_{\textup{nom}}$ in the library $\Pi$. Consider a scenario where pre-defined fallback policies are only marginally feasible or unsafe, yet the nominal plan remains safe, and $\pi_{\textup{nom}}\in\Pi$. In this case, the PL-CBF selects $\pi_{\textup{nom}}$ as the least invasive policy.
L262: The resulting safety constraint $K_{\textup{cbf}}({\bm{x}};\pi_{\textup{nom}})$ naturally contains the nominal control input ${\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}=\pi_{\textup{nom}}({\bm{x}})$. Consequently, the solution to the QP in (cite50†13 ) yields ${\bm{u}}^{\star}={\bm{u}}_{\textup{nom}}$, resulting in zero intervention.

L265: ## VI RESULTS
L266: We evaluate PL-CBF as a runtime safety filter in three simulation case studies of increasing difficulty. We compare against four representative safety filters using a single fallback (or backup) policy: Model Predictive Shielding (MPS) [cite57†21 ], gatekeeper [cite33†2 ], Backup CBF [cite43†12 ], and the Policy CBF (PCBF; cite9†Sec. II-B ) [cite45†14 ]. Across all experiments, methods share the same nominal controller and differ only in (i) the safety-filter’s algorithm and (ii) the fallback policy.
L267: All experiments were conducted on a MacBook Air with the Apple M4 CPU.
L268: ### VI-A Safe Set Under Multiple Fallback Policies
L269: 
L270: We first illustrate set-wise behavior in a planar double-integrator example where a reference viability kernel can be computed. Let ${\bm{x}}=[{\bm{p}}^{\top}\ {\bm{v}}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ with ${\bm{p}}=[x\ y]^{\top}$ and ${\bm{v}}=[v_{x}\ v_{y}]^{\top}$, and dynamics
L271: 
L272:  | $$\dot{{\bm{p}}}={\bm{v}},\quad\dot{{\bm{v}}}={\bm{u}},\quad{\bm{u}}\in[-a_{\max},a_{\max}]^{2},$$  |  | (21)
L273: with $a_{\max}=0.5~\textup{m/s}^{2}$. The unsafe set is a disk $\mathcal{O}=\{{\bm{x}}\mid\|{\bm{p}}\|_{2}\leq r_{\mathrm{obs}}\}$, and we use $h({\bm{x}})=\|{\bm{p}}\|_{2}-r_{\mathrm{obs}}$, yielding $\mathcal{C}=\{{\bm{x}}\mid h({\bm{x}})\geq 0\}$. Since the state dimension is $4$, we can compute the viability kernel for (cite64†21 ) using HJ reachability [cite65†24 ] and visualize it on the velocity slice $(v_{x},v_{y})=(2,0)$ in m/s.
L274: To highlight the dependence of single-policy certificates on the chosen maneuver, we consider two simple fallbacks: a stopping policy $\pi_{\textup{stop}}$ implemented as component-wise saturated full deceleration until rest, i.e., $-a_{\max}\,\mathrm{sign}({\bm{v}})$, and a “turn-up” evasive policy $\pi_{\uparrow}$ that applies saturated acceleration in $+y$. Fig. cite66†1 compares the certified safe sets obtained under these two fallback choices.
L275: PL-CBF uses the same library in both rows, $\Pi=\{\pi_{\textup{nom}},\pi_{\textup{stop}},\pi_{\uparrow},\pi_{\downarrow}\}$, where $\pi_{\downarrow}$ applies saturated acceleration in $-y$.
L276: Importantly, a single-policy method may recover a different safe set if a different fallback is chosen, but each fixed fallback certifies only the states compatible with that particular maneuver. As a result, replacing the stopping fallback with a different maneuver may recover states that require that specific behavior while losing those that are safe only under stopping.
L277: PL-CBF avoids this tradeoff by evaluating a finite library online and certifying safety whenever at least one library policy remains feasible over the horizon, yielding the largest safe set.
