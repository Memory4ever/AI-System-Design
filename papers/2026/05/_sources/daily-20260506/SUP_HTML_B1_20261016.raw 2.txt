L100: # Learn-to-learn on Arbitrary Textual Conditioning:
L101: A Hypernetwork-Driven Meta-Gated LLM
L102: Luo Ji Affiliation: Geely AI Lab, Geely Auto Group, Zhejiang, China    Qi Qin Affiliation: Peking University, Beijing, China    Ningyuan Xi Affiliation: Geely AI Lab, Geely Auto Group, Zhejiang, China    Teng Chen Affiliation: Geely AI Lab, Geely Auto Group, Zhejiang, China    Qingqing Gu Affiliation: Geely AI Lab, Geely Auto Group, Zhejiang, China    Hongyan Li Affiliation: Geely AI Lab, Geely Auto Group, Zhejiang, China Correspondence to: lihongyan_csu@163.com
L103: ###### Abstract
L104: Conventional LLMs may suffer from corpus heterogeneity and subtle condition changes. While finetuning can create the catastrophe forgetting issue, application of meta-learning on LLMs is also limited due to its complexity and scalability. In this paper, we activate the meta-signal of $\beta$ within the SwiGLU blocks, resulting in a meta-gating mechanism that adaptively adjusts the nonlinearity of FFN.
L105: A hypernetwork is employed which dynamically produces $\beta$ on textual conditions, providing meta-controllability on LLMs. By testing on different condition types such as task, domain, persona, and style, our method outperforms finetuning and meta-learning baselines, and can generalize reasonably on unseen tasks, condition types, or instructions. Our code can be found in cite88†https://github.com/AaronJi/MeGan†github.com .

L111: ## 1 Introduction
L112: The vast diversity of tasks, styles, domains, and other conditions present in the environment for both human and AI, which raises the problem of meta-learning, or in other words, the capability of ‘learn-to-learn’ given different contexts.
L113: While Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated astonishing capabilities across diverse natural language processing (NLP) tasks, they remain relatively deficient in the seamless, dynamic adaptation to those conditions (cite89†Reinhart et al., 2025 ; cite90†Zamaraeva et al., 2025 ). When finetuning on a specific downstream task, their general abilities are often degraded, sometimes termed ‘catastrophic forgetting’ or ‘alignment tax’, which hurts their adaptation capability to new conditions.
L114: This performance erosion presents a fundamental challenge to developing robust and versatile AI systems, preventing the application of personalized systems or expert-level emotional supporters (cite91†Srinivas et al., 2025 ).
L115: Varied meta-training methods are proposed to alleviate these issues, including in-context learning (ICL) (cite92†Min et al., 2022 ; cite93†Sinha et al., 2024 ; cite94†Coda-Forno et al., 2023 ), gradient-based meta-learning (cite95†Finn et al., 2017 ; cite96†Song et al., 2020 ), parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) (cite97†Hu et al., 2022 ; cite98†Tian et al., 2024 ; cite99†Li et al., 2025 ), and adapter methods (cite100†Houlsby et al., 2019 ; cite101†He et al., 2022 ; cite102†Hu et al., 2023 ).
L116: However, ICL usually lags behind finetuning, produces high variance results, resulting in the issue of ‘lost in needle’ by creating a long context window (cite103†Ponce & Etchegoyhen, 2025 ). Gradient or adapter-based methods have high computational overhead in NLP tasks or are difficult to scale up. PEFT methods like LoRA are computationally efficient and have good adaptivity. However, they generally require downstream task-specific training which limits ,their applications.
L117: cite104†Image: Refer to caption Figure 1: Paradigm of MeGan analogous to neuro-system. Nueromodulator meta-controls classical neurotransmitters, shaping the synaptic plasticity to meta-plasticity. Similarly, we implement a hypernetwork that converts textual condition inputs into meta signals. These signals are combined with layer index embedding, then meta-control the gating of LLM FFNs.
L118: In contrast, human nervous system overcomes the aforementioned issues, where the basic stimulus is responded by synaptic plasticity based on classical neurotransmitters; and neuromodulator produces the adaptive gain control, through several mechanisms including gating, guidance, and stabilization (cite105†Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005 ).
L119: The system then achieves meta-plasticity, which seamlessly adapts to changing environmental contexts without the metabolic cost of physical rewiring (cite106†Servan-Schreiber et al., 1990 ). Analogously, pretrained LLM can be considered as a generalized query-response processor. Instead of directly prompting or finetuning on it, we implement a hypernetwork with textual condition as input, which produces a meta-signal on the original LLM.
L120: With original LLM parameters frozen, the hypernetwork is trained by post-hoc adaptation on meta-training samples, which then adapt the LLM conditioned on target tasks (Figure cite107†1 ).
L121: Figure 2: Comparison of meta-learning paradigms on LLMs.
L122: Similar to Text-to-LoRA (cite108†Charakorn et al., 2025 ), a LoRA-based architecture driven by a text-input hypernetwork (Figure cite109†2 (C)), we also introduce the architecture customization based on textual conditions, to provide higher adaptability. The architecture is similar to the parallel adapter (Figure cite109†2 (E)), by implementing the hypernetwork as a parallel module with FFN, with the cross-attention between LLM latent and textual conditions.
L123: Different from adapters which directly control the LLM latent (cite110†Qiu et al., 2025 ), our hypernetwork produces the meta-signal to adaptively adjust the activations of feedforward network (FFN), resulting in different levels of nonlinearity. This design mirrors the mechanism of the neuron’s f-I (frequency-current) curve (cite111†Silver, 2010 ), and is consistent with previous observations that FFNs encode persona or style semantics earlier and better than self-attention (cite112†Poonia & Jain, 2025 ).
L124: To achieve this, we replace the Swish_{1} activation with $\text{Swish}_{\beta}$, resulting a new type of FFN block called $\beta-$SwiGLU. The hypernetwork produces $\beta$ by a bottleneck structure, extracting key conditional patterns by information compression (cite113†Schmidhuber, 2009 ; cite114†Sutskever, 2023 ; cite115†Delétang et al., 2024 ), replacing the original self-gating by meta-gating. Correspondingly, the entire framework is named by Me ta-Ga ting-on-Conditio n (MeGan).
L125: MeGan is trained by supervised fine-tuning (SFT), with an auxiliary L-2 regularization on MLP weights. We experiment it on different settings of meta-learning, with different meta-conditions such as task, domain, persona, style, and sentiment. Our framework outperforms prompting finetuning, or meta-learning baselines, showing good zero-shot performance on unseen tasks or condition types. Further analysis shows that MeGan is scalable, robust and parameter-efficient.
L126: Distribution of $\beta$ also reveals that MeGan adaptively captures the semantics of conditions by dynamic $\beta$ calculation. To summarize, our main contributions include:
L127:   * •
L128: 
L129: We propose MeGan, a meta-gating framework on meta-learning, which mirrors nueroscience mechanism.
L130: 
L131:   * •
L132: 
L133: We implement a novel hypernetwork, which converts arbitrary conditions into a self-adaptive meta-signal, steering the nonlinearity of $\beta-$SwiGLU blocks.
L134: 
L135:   * •
L136: 
L137: Thorough theoretical studies and empirical experiments are conducted to verify our methodology’s performance, stability, and scalability.

L155: ## 2 Related Work
L156: 
L157: While transformer has proven to be a robust general-purpose architecture and also a few-shot learner, it still struggles in complicated, unseen tasks. Meta-learning, which deals with such types of problems, is therefore investigated on LLMs. Below, we discuss several categories of paradigms in comparison to our method (Figure cite109†2 ).
L158: #### Adapter methods.
L159: To avoid the overfit issue of finetuning, studies are conducted by implementing an extra adapter alongside the main LLM backbone, with the main LLM parameters unchanged. Depending on the locations, they can be classified into series adapter (cite100†Houlsby et al., 2019 ; cite110†Qiu et al., 2025 ) and parallel adapter (cite101†He et al., 2022 )(Figure cite109†2 (D) and (E)).
L160: LLM-Adapters (cite102†Hu et al., 2023 ) systematically experiment with different types of adapters, and conclude that the parallel adapter can adapt well to versatile and changing tasks.
L161: Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods can also be considered as a special type of architecture-agnostic adapters, such as LoRA (cite97†Hu et al., 2022 ) (Figure cite109†2 (B)). However, LoRA requires a specific downstream training set, which may not be viable in the meta-learning case. Studies also show that the performance LoRA may suffer from heterogeneity corpus (cite98†Tian et al., 2024 ).
L162: #### Meta-in-context methods.
L163: Direct application of traditional meta-learning such as MAML (cite95†Finn et al., 2017 ; cite96†Song et al., 2020 ) on NLP tasks is limited, due to its complicated bi-level optimization (Figure cite109†2 (A)). Instead, many meta-learning studies on LLMs incorporate the in-context information, including MetaICL (cite92†Min et al., 2022 ), MetaICT (cite117†Chen et al., 2022 ), meta-in-context learning (cite94†Coda-Forno et al., 2023 ), MAML-en-LLM (cite93†Sinha et al., 2024 ) and MICRE (cite118†Li et al., 2024 ).
L164: In this work, we show that ICL can fail for some specific adaptation conditions.
L165: #### Meta-on-LoRA methods.
L166: 
L167: Another category leverages the adaptation of low-rank parameters of LoRA. Such methods include MLtD (cite116†Hou et al., 2022b ), which applies MAML on LLM with either task-adaptive reparameterization (TARP) or task-adaptive model structures (TAMS), hydraLoRA (cite98†Tian et al., 2024 ), which uses asymmetric LoRA to route MoE, and also Meta-LoRA (cite99†Li et al., 2025 ), which conducts sample reweighting by gradient similarity.
L168: #### Meta with customized structures.
L169: There is also a special work called Text-to-LoRA (cite108†Charakorn et al., 2025 ) (Figure cite109†2 (C)), which implements a hypernetwork, that receives textual task descriptions and outputs the LoRA parameters. Its hypernetwork can be trained by the FT loss or a reconstruction loss with pretrained LoRA.
L170: In this paper, we propose another meta-control paradigm for LLMs, which leverages a novel gating mechanism, the meta-gating (cite119†Lin et al., 2021 ; cite120†Hou et al., 2022a ; cite121†Hou et al., 2023 ) (Figure cite109†2 (F)). Replacing the original self-gating in the FFN, we let our gating to be adaptive with a hypernetwork on auxiliary conditions, similar to Text-to-LoRA. We employ the FT loss since (cite108†Charakorn et al., 2025 ) observes that the reconstruction loss fails to generalize on unseen tasks.
L171: We also have training-inference-consistent adaptation on text instructions.

L185: ## 3 Preliminary
L186: 
L187: ### 3.1 LLM Modules
L188: #### Activation.
L189: 
L190: Starting from a generalized $\beta$-Sigmoid,
L191: 
L192:  | $$\sigma_{\beta}(x):=1/(1+\exp{(-\beta x)})$$  |  | (1)
L193: 
L194: the famous Sigmoid $\sigma(x)$ is its special case ($\beta=1$). Based on Sigmoid, the $\text{Swish}_{\beta}$ ($\beta>0$) and its special form SiLU ($\beta=1$) has the following formulation:
L195: 
L196:  | $\displaystyle\text{Swish}_{\beta}(x)=x*\sigma_{\beta}(x)$  |  | (2)
L197:  | $\displaystyle\text{SiLU}(x):=\text{Swish}_{1}(x)=x*\sigma(x)$  |  | (3)
L198: As $\beta$ increases, the nonlinearity of $\text{Swish}_{\beta}$ is amplified. At two extreme situations, $\text{Swish}_{\beta}$ becomes Linear when $\beta\rightarrow 0$, and ReLU when $\beta\rightarrow+\infty$. Modern LLMs (LlaMA, Mistral, Qwen, etc) usually adopt SiLU as a standardized implementation to reduce pretraining complexity.
L199: #### Self-Gating.
L200: 
L201: Within the feedforward network (FFN) blocks of LLM, GLU (Gated Linear Units) structure is adopted which uses a gating network to reweight the MLP output. By using $\text{Swish}_{1}$ (SiLU) as the activation function of gating, the FFN block has the SwiGLU architecture:
L202:  | $\displaystyle y=W_{\text{down}}\left(\text{Swish}_{1}\left(W_{\text{gate}}x\right)\otimes(W_{\text{up}}x)\right)$  |  | (4)
L203:  | $\displaystyle W_{\text{up}},W_{\text{gate}}\in\mathcal{R}^{D\times C},W_{\text{down}}\in\mathcal{R}^{C\times D},C>D$  |
L204: where $\otimes$ denotes the element-wise multiplication, $D$ is the hidden size, and $C$ is the FFN intermediate size. In practice, $C$ is usually a multiple of $D$. SwiGLU can also be considered as self-gating which means the same latent is both inputs of the MLP and the gating network.
L205: ### 3.2 LLM architecture
L206: 
L207: LLM is a decoder-only transformer with interleaved self-attention and FFN layers:
L208: 
L209:  | $\displaystyle e_{0}$  | $\displaystyle=\text{emb}(x)\in\mathcal{R}^{D},logit=\text{proj}(e_{L})$  |
L210:  | $\displaystyle e_{l}$  | $\displaystyle=\text{FFN}(\text{self-attn}(e_{l-1})),l=1,\cdots,L$  |  | (5)
L211: where $x$ is the text input, $L$ is the total number of layers, $e_{l}$ is the latent on the $l$-th layer, and $logit$ is the final output logit. $\text{emb}(x)$ represents the tokenization and embedding layer, while proj is the final projection layer.
L212: ### 3.3 Hypernetworks
L213: 
L214: Given a base model $y=G_{w}(x)$, a hypernetwork (cite123†Ha et al., 2017 ) can be formed with $w$ generated by another auto-adaptive network: $w=\mathcal{H}_{\theta}(z)$ where $z$ is the adaptive input, and $\theta$ is the hypernetwork trainable parameter.

L218: ## 4 Problem Formulation
L219: ### 4.1 Data format
L220: 
L221: The problem of meta-training can be formulated on a collection of tasks $\mathcal{T}_{1:C}$, where $C$ is the total number of meta-tasks. For each task, the data has a format $(x,y)$, where $x$ denotes the input, $y$ denotes the output. During the training, the model learns to condition on the samples of a subset of tasks

L223:  | $$\mathcal{T}_{i}=\{(x^{i}_{j},y^{i}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{i}},~\forall i\in[1,C]$$  |  | (6)
L224: where $N_{i}$ is the sample number of the $i$-th task. During the test stage, the model needs to adapt to an arbitrary task $\mathcal{T}^{\star}$
L225: 
L226:  | $$\arg\max{P(y^{\star}|x^{\star})},(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\mathcal{T}^{\star}$$  |  | (7)
L227: 
L228: with two typical settings of target tasks in meta-learning:
L229: i) Low resource (LR): $\mathcal{T}^{\star}$ belongs to the $C$ meta-training tasks, but with relatively limited training samples.
L230: ii) Unseen (US): $\mathcal{T}^{\star}\notin\mathcal{T}_{1:C}$, outside the scope of LLM.

L232: In Section cite33†6 , we investigate both settings to provide a comprehensive understanding of model meta-performances.
L233: ### 4.2 Meta-on-conditions
L234: Compared to baselines in Table cite125†2 , in this paper, we propose that meta-learning can also be conducted on generalized textual conditions. Such conditions can be readily obtained, especially in the meta-learning setting, where the model adaptation is frequently required on the switch of different types of meta-conditions, such as task, domain, persona, style, sentiment, and emotion.
L235: These conditions, however, are insufficiently studied by previous meta-learning methods except Text-to-LoRA^{1}^{1} 1 However, their experiment solely explores the adaptation on task-wise description..
L236: To learn-to-learn on such conditions, we reformulate such datasets with the format of $(x,y,z)$, where $z$ is the auxiliary annotation of those conditions. During the meta-training, $z$ is first inserted into different instructional prompts (depending on the condition type), then is used to adapt the model. During inference, the model produces the best output conditioned on the test $z$: $\arg\max{P(y^{\star}|x^{\star},z^{\star})}$.
L237: In this formulation, $z$ can semantically bridge the meta information between training and target tasks. Table cite125†2 summarizes different condition types, possible attributes, example prompts, and typical relative datasets.

L243: #### Beta-SwiGLU.
L244: 
L245: To provide an extra DoF (degree of freedom) for meta-control, we reactivate the $\beta$ of SiLU ($\text{Swish}_{\beta=1}$ function (Eq (cite126†3 )); accordingly, the SwiGLU (Eq (cite127†4 )) becomes $\beta\text{-SwiGLU}$, with $\text{Swish}_{1}$ replaced by $\text{Swish}_{\beta}$:
L246: 
L247:  |  | $\displaystyle y=W_{\text{down}}\left(\text{Swish}_{\beta}\left(W_{\text{gate}}x\right)\otimes(W_{\text{up}}x)\right),\beta>0$  |  | (8)
L248: 
L249: where $\beta$ dynamically adjusts the slope of the Swish function.
L250: Apparently, when $\beta=1$, $\beta\text{-SwiGLU}$ decays to the original SwiGLU. Also, the gradient of $\text{Swish}_{\beta}$ is bounded by $1/4|x|^{2}$ with detailed derivation in Appendix cite51†A.2 . This property ensures that training $\beta\text{-SwiGLU}$ is friendly.
L251: #### The hypernetwork.
L252: We implement a lightweight hypernetwork which projects the textual condition $z$ into $\beta$ on each layer, denoting $\mathcal{H}_{\theta}(z,l)$. We first replace the placeholder of prompt $p$ (examples in Table cite125†2 ) with $z$, then process it with the tokenizer and embedding layer of the LLM itself. $\mathcal{H}_{\theta}(z)$ then implement a cross attention with a reduced dimension $R$, added with layer embedding, and finally feed into a one-layer, bias-free, MLP.

L256:  | $\displaystyle\beta^{l}=$  | $\displaystyle W(e^{z}+\text{layer-emb}(l)),l=1,2,\cdots,L$  |
