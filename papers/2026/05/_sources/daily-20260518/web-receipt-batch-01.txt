L39: # Is VLA Reasoning Faithful? Probing Safety of Chain-of-Causation
L40: in Autonomous Driving Models Thanks: Accepted at the DriveX Workshop, CVPR 2026.

L43: ###### Abstract
L44: We present the first systematic study of faithfulness in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) driving models, analyzing 300 Alpamayo-R1-10B inferences across 100 diverse PhysicalAI-AV scenarios.
L45: Our main finding is that output natural-language rationales with trajectories may be significantly unfaithful: (i) overall reasoning fidelity is only 42.5%, with Chain-of-Causation matching scene reality less than half the time; (ii) 94 missed pedestrians in one-third of pedestrian-relevant scenes; (iii) 97.7% trajectory fragility under mild visual perturbations; and (iv) only 48.3% mean reasoning-action consistency, with 53.3% of inferences exhibiting low consistency, including 37.9% of stop-claimed cases where the model continues instead.
L46: We formalize faithfulness information-theoretically, define entity and action fidelity with verification criteria, and outline a four-component safety architecture aligned with these results.
L47: ## 1 Introduction
L48: 
L49: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for autonomous driving move beyond modular stacks toward end-to-end systems that jointly reason and act [cite27†7 ]. Models such as Alpamayo [cite27†7 ], EMMA [cite28†2 ], and DriveVLM [cite29†11 ] emit natural-language Chain-of-Causation (CoC) traces alongside control, which is attractive to regulators and the public who expect interpretability.
L50: Yet work on large language models shows that explanations are often unfaithful: they can rationalize decisions post hoc rather than reflect the underlying computation [cite30†12 , cite31†6 ]. For driving, this matters urgently: if CoC does not reflect scene understanding or planned behavior, a trace like “stop for the pedestrian ahead” can mislead when the pedestrian is missed, or the stop is not executed, arguably worse than silence, because it invites misplaced trust.
L51: VLAs exacerbate this concern structurally: language and trajectory arise from coupled but distinct pathways (e.g., a VLM conditioned on vision versus a trajectory decoder), so there is no built-in guarantee that reasoning and action are causally aligned.
L52: We provide the first systematic study of reasoning faithfulness in a production-scale VLA driving model, with three contributions: (1) a formal fidelity framework based on information theory and counterfactuals, decomposing faithfulness into entity fidelity, action fidelity, and perturbation sensitivity with explicit verification criteria; (2) quantitative evidence of unfaithfulness, 42.5% overall fidelity, 8.9% hallucination rate, 94 missed pedestrians, and 53.3% reasoning-action inconsistency over 300 inferences; (3) a safety-layer design motivated by these failure modes, with guarantees grounded in Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS).
L53: ## 2 Related Work
L54: 
L55: VLA models for driving. Recent VLAs pair vision-language backbones with trajectory heads. Alpamayo-R1 [cite27†7 ] uses Qwen3-VL with a diffusion decoder, and Chain-of-Causation traces on 700K annotations; EMMA [cite28†2 ] uses Gemini end-to-end with language; DriveVLM [cite29†11 ] separates scene description from planning. Reasoning often co-trains with control and can improve trajectories, but prior work does not assess whether language faithfully reflects the scene.
L56: Reasoning faithfulness in LLMs. Faithful explanations track the model’s actual computation; plausible ones need not [cite32†5 ]. Turpin et al. [cite30†12 ] show that chain-of-thought can track spurious cues not reflected in the stated rationale. Lanham et al. [cite31†6 ] test faithfulness via perturbations: changing features the explanation cites should move both explanation and prediction. We transfer this counterfactual view to driving, where miscalibration has an immediate safety impact.
L57: Safety frameworks for autonomous driving. ISO 26262 [cite33†3 ] covers functional safety; ISO 21448 (SOTIF) [cite34†4 ] addresses insufficiencies and misuse; RSS [cite35†10 ] gives formal trajectory constraints for hazardous situations. We connect alignment-style reasoning checks with these standards by proposing verifiable tests on VLA explanations.
L58: ## 3 Theoretical Foundations
L59: ### 3.1 Formal Definition of Reasoning Faithfulness
L60: 
L61: Let $\mathcal{M}$ denote a VLA model that maps a visual observation $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}$ and ego-state $\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}$ to a reasoning trace $r\in\mathcal{R}$ and a trajectory $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$:
L62: 
L63:  | $$\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=(r,\tau)$$  |  | (1)
L64: Following Jacovi and Goldberg [cite32†5 ], we define faithfulness as the degree to which $r$ accurately represents the causal factors that determine $\tau$. We decompose faithfulness into three measurable properties.
L65: 
L66: Definition 1 (Entity Fidelity). Let $\mathcal{E}(r)$ denote the set of entities mentioned in reasoning trace $r$, and $\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)$ denote the set of entities present in the scene at time $t$ according to ground truth. Entity fidelity is:
L67:  | $$F_{\text{entity}}=\frac{|\mathcal{E}(r)\cap\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mathbf{x},t)|}{|\mathcal{E}(r)\cup\mathcal{E}^{*}_{\text{relevant}}(\mathbf{x},t)|}$$  |  | (2)
L68: where $\mathcal{E}^{*}_{\text{relevant}}$ filters ground truth entities by spatial relevance. This Jaccard formulation captures both hallucinations ($\mathcal{E}(r)\setminus\mathcal{E}^{*}$) and misses ($\mathcal{E}^{*}_{\text{relevant}}\setminus\mathcal{E}(r)$). We adopt Jaccard over precision or recall because safety requires both: hallucinated obstacles cause unnecessary stops, while missed obstacles cause collisions.
L69: Definition 2 (Action Fidelity). Let $a(r)$ denote the stated action and $a(\tau)$ the observed trajectory action. Action fidelity is $F_{\text{action}}=\mathbb{1}[a(r)\sim a(\tau)]$ where $\sim$ denotes kinematic consistency via predicates:
L70:  | Stop:  | $\displaystyle\quad a(r)=\text{stop}\implies v_{T}(\tau)<\epsilon_{v}$  |
L71:  | Decel:  | $\displaystyle\quad a(r)=\text{decel}\implies\Delta v(\tau)<-\epsilon_{a}$  |
L72:  | Turn:  | $\displaystyle\quad a(r)=\text{turn}_{d}\implies|\Delta y(\tau)|>\epsilon_{l}\text{ in dir }d$  |  | (3)
L73: with $\epsilon_{v}{=}0.5$ m/s, $\epsilon_{a}{=}1.0$ m/s^{2}, $\epsilon_{l}{=}1.0$ m, derived from driving dynamics: $\epsilon_{v}$ for near-standstill, $\epsilon_{a}$ for perceptible deceleration, $\epsilon_{l}$ for half a lane width.
L74: Definition 3 (Counterfactual Faithfulness). Following Lanham et al. [cite31†6 ], let $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ be a perturbed input: $\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{s})=(\tilde{r},\tilde{\tau})$. We classify responses by whether $r\neq\tilde{r}$ and $\|\tau-\tilde{\tau}\|>\delta_{\tau}$:
L75:  | $$\begin{cases}\text{Faithful}&r\neq\tilde{r}\wedge\tau\neq\tilde{\tau}\\
L76: {\color[rgb]{0.7539,0.2227,0.168}\text{Silent failure}}&r=\tilde{r}\wedge\tau\neq\tilde{\tau}\\
L77: \text{Reason-only shift}&r\neq\tilde{r}\wedge\tau=\tilde{\tau}\\
L78: \text{Robust}&r=\tilde{r}\wedge\tau=\tilde{\tau}\end{cases}$$  |  | (4)
L79: 
L80: Silent failures are most dangerous: behavior changes but the explanation does not, making the failure invisible to CoC-based monitoring.
L81: ### 3.2 Why Counterfactual Testing Over Gradient Attribution?
L82: 
L83: We adopt counterfactual perturbation rather than post-hoc attribution maps or attention analysis for three theoretically motivated reasons:
L84: (1) Causal vs. correlational evidence. Attention and saliency patterns reveal correlational relationships but do not establish causation. High focus on a pedestrian does not prove the model “reasons about” it. Counterfactual perturbation provides interventionist evidence: modifying the input and observing output changes establishes a causal link.
L85: (2) Dual-pathway evaluation. VLA models produce reasoning and trajectories via separate decoders. Gradient methods applied to one output cannot reveal whether the other is faithful to the same features. Counterfactual testing evaluates both outputs simultaneously under the same intervention.
L86: 
L87: (3) Safety-actionable metrics. Counterfactual testing produces quantitative safety metrics (hallucination rate, silent failure rate) rather than qualitative heatmaps, enabling threshold-based safety interventions.
L88: ### 3.3 Information-Theoretic Perspective
L89: 
L90: We frame reasoning faithfulness via mutual information. Let $R$, $T$, $X$ denote random variables for reasoning, trajectory, and input. A perfectly faithful model satisfies:
L91: 
L92:  | $$I(R;T\mid X)\approx 0$$  |  | (5)
L93: meaning that, given the scene, reasoning provides no additional information about the trajectory beyond what the scene itself determines, i.e., $R$ is causally grounded in $X$, not post-hoc rationalisation of $T$. Our finding that low-fidelity inferences produce 34.5% higher ADE confirms positive $I(F_{\text{entity}};\text{ADE})$, fidelity is informative about trajectory quality.
L94: ### 3.4 Epistemic Uncertainty via Trajectory Spread
L95: 
L96: For $K$ trajectory samples $\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}\}$ from the diffusion decoder:
L97: 
L98:  | $$U_{\text{epistemic}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\text{Var}_{k}[\tau_{k}^{x}(t)]+\text{Var}_{k}[\tau_{k}^{y}(t)]}$$  |  | (6)
L99: 
L100: This RMS standard deviation provides a spatial uncertainty estimate in meters. High spread under perturbation indicates the model “doesn’t know” how to respond, lacking genuine scene understanding to anchor its predictions.
L101: ## 4 Methodology
L102: 
L103: ### 4.1 Model and Dataset
L104: 
L105: We evaluate Alpamayo-R1-10B [cite27†7 ], comprising Qwen3-VL-8B vision-language backbone and diffusion trajectory decoder. Evaluation uses 100 test-split clips from PhysicalAI-AV [cite36†8 ], sampled from 100 distinct chunks (298–1281) for geographic diversity. Each clip provides multi-camera video, ego-motion, and autolabeled 3D obstacle annotations (automobile, person, trailer, tram). We perform 3 runs per clip (seeds 42-44), totaling 300 inferences.
L106: ### 4.2 Phase 1: Baseline Characterization
L107: 
L108: We establish baseline trajectory quality (minADE), reasoning statistics (CoC diversity, length, consistency), and computational requirements.
L109: ### 4.3 Phase 2A: Entity and Action Fidelity
L110: 
L111: We parse CoC traces for 10 entity and 10 action categories via keyword matching. Ground truth verification uses autolabeled obstacles within $\pm 0.5$s of prediction timestamp. Entities are classified by spatial relationship: lead vehicle ($x{>}0$, $|y|{<}4$m), pedestrian ($|x|{<}30$m, $|y|{<}15$m), adjacent vehicle ($2{\leq}|y|{<}8$m), cross traffic ($|y|{>}5$m, $|x|{<}20$m). Action verification applies the kinematic predicates from Eq. 3.
L112: ### 4.4 Phase 2B: Reasoning-Action Consistency
L113: 
L114: Independent of scene ground truth, we measure consistency between stated intentions and trajectory behavior, isolating the reasoning-action coupling from perception accuracy.
L115: ### 4.5 Phase 2C: Causal Perturbation
L116: 
L117: We apply combined Gaussian blur ($\sigma{=}3$) and rectangular occlusion (10% area) to front camera frames, ecologically valid perturbations simulating rain/fog and sensor degradation. Each perturbed inference uses $K{=}2$ trajectory samples for epistemic uncertainty (Eq. 6). Responses are classified per Eq. 4.

L145: ### 5.3 Reasoning-Action Consistency (Phase 2B)
L146: 
L147: Mean consistency is 0.483, with 53.3% low consistency. Fig. cite39†1 (c–d) reveals a critical asymmetry: lateral actions achieve perfect consistency (keep lane: 100%, nudge: 100%), while longitudinal control fails (stop: 62.1%, decelerate: 69.0%). Of 78 mismatches, 50 (64.1%) involve claimed deceleration/stop with continuing trajectory.
L148: This asymmetry has a structural explanation: the diffusion trajectory decoder naturally handles lateral shifts through spatial reasoning but lacks an explicit mechanism for speed commitment. Longitudinal control requires temporal reasoning about future interactions that the spatial trajectory representation cannot directly encode.

L150: 
L151: Table 3: Causal perturbation response (265 valid inferences).
L152: Response (Eq. 4)  | Count  | %
L153: --- | --- | ---
L154: Faithful ($r$, $\tau$ both changed)  | 221  | 83.4%
L155: Silent failure ($\tau$ only)  | 38  | 14.3%

L160: Under perturbation, 97.7% of trajectories changed (Table cite40†3 ). The 38 silent failures (14.3%) are the most dangerous: the model produces different trajectories but provides identical reasoning, violating counterfactual faithfulness (Eq. 4). Any CoC-based monitoring would be blind to these behavioral changes.
L161: ## 6 Conclusion
L162: We present the first systematic evaluation of reasoning faithfulness in a VLA driving model, grounded in information theory and counterfactual reasoning. Our findings, 42.5% fidelity, 94 missed pedestrians, 53.3% low reasoning-action consistency, 97.7% perturbation fragility, and 14.3% silent failures demonstrate that Chain-of-Causation reasoning in current VLA models cannot be relied upon for safety assurance. Independent safety verification layers are necessary for responsible deployment.
L163: These results motivate SafeDriveX, a scalable oversight architecture in which an independent, smaller VLM monitors reasoning faithfulness and action-trajectory coherence, providing a reward signal for RLAIF fine-tuning [cite41†1 , cite42†9 ], with RSS constraints [cite35†10 ] serving as a formal safety floor when the monitor’s confidence is low.

L73: # State-of-the-Art Claims Require State-of-the-Art Evidence
L74: 
L75: YongKyung Oh Affiliation: Medical & Imaging Informatics (MII), University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Correspondence to: yongkyungoh@mednet.ucla.edu
L76: ###### Abstract
L77: State-of-the-Art (SOTA) claims pervade Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) research. These claims rest on benchmark evaluations, where models are ranked by aggregate scores across tasks. Public benchmarks or leaderboards are the most visible instance, but the same structure appears in paper tables throughout the literature. However, such minimal evidence often cannot support these strong claims. We identify a widespread claim-evidence gap in AI benchmarking.
L78: Claiming SOTA carries implicit assumptions beyond mean score superiority, suggesting that a model meaningfully outperforms alternatives across most tasks. However, a marginal improvement in the mean score merely indicates a top average rank rather than true superiority. Analyzing ten cross-domain benchmarks from public leaderboards, we found that in more than half of top-model comparisons, at least one commonly assumed property of superiority does not hold.
L79: These properties include meaningful effect size, consistency across tasks, or robustness to dataset removal. Instead, aggregate gains are frequently driven by outlier datasets. This fragility persists even in benchmarks with many tasks. We argue that claim language should reflect the strength of the underlying evidence. This requires no additional experiments, only honest reporting of what results actually show, enabling more precise and interpretable comparisons across models.
