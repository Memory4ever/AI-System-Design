L14@P0: Abstract
L15@P0: Quantization can preserve predictive accuracy under low-bit deployment while
L16@P0: silently breaking algorithmic recourse: an actionable change that flips a decision
L17@P0: before quantization may fail after quantization, or become substantially more costly.
L18@P0: We formalize counterfactual sensitivity under quantization through validity, cost,
L19@P0: and direction stability, and introduce two metrics: Validity Drop (VD) and Counterfactual Recourse Gap (CRG) that reveal recourse failures invisible to accuracy.
L20@P0: We propose Counterfactual-Faithful Quantization (CFQ), which trains quantizer
L21@P0: parameters and mixed-precision bit allocation to preserve counterfactual behavior
L22@P0: by enforcing the target outcome at teacher recourse points under a global bit budget.
L23@P0: A margin-based analysis gives a sufficient condition for recourse transfer under
L24@P0: bounded quantization perturbations. Experiments on ADULT, GERMAN CREDIT,
L25@P0: and COMPAS show that accuracy-matched baselines can significantly degrade
L26@P0: recourse stability, while CFQ maintains accuracy and substantially improves VD
L27@P0: and CRG across bit budgets.
L28@P0: 1 Introduction
L29@P0: Modern ML systems are increasingly deployed under strict efficiency constraints, where quantization
L30@P0: is the default tool for reducing memory footprint and inference latency [11, 20]. Quantization-aware
L31@P0: training (QAT) and post-training quantization (PTQ) are typically evaluated through a single lens:
L32@P0: does the quantized model preserve predictive accuracy? [28, 2]. Yet for decision-making systems
L33@P0: that provide algorithmic recourse, accuracy is not the only requirement. Recourse and counterfactual
L34@P0: explanations seek a minimal actionable change to an input that achieves a desired outcome, i.e.,
L35@P0: a smallest-change explanation or recommendation [27, 32, 38, 37]. In high-stakes settings, this
L36@P0: recommendation is not merely interpretability; it is a promise of what actions would work. If the
L37@P0: deployed model changes, that promise can break.
L38@P0: This paper highlights a deployment mismatch: quantization can preserve average-case accuracy while
L39@P0: altering the local decision boundary geometry that determines counterfactuals. The reason is simple:
L40@P0: accuracy constrains predictions on the data distribution, whereas recourse depends on the geometry of
L41@P0: the classifier in neighborhoods around individual points and along actionable directions. Quantization
L42@P0: perturbs weights (and sometimes activations) in a structured but non-uniform way, which can shift
L43@P0: margins and rotate decision boundaries in precisely those neighborhoods that matter for recourse.
L44@P0: As a consequence, two models with nearly identical test accuracy can yield substantially different

L55@P1: the algorithmic recourse framing [37], the counterfactual “smallest change” perspective [38], and the
L56@P1: causal view that recourse corresponds to feasible interventions rather than arbitrary perturbations.

L86@P1: 2 Related Work
L87@P1: Recourse and counterfactual explanations. Counterfactual explanations aim to answer “what
L88@P1: needs to change” in an input to achieve a desired model outcome, typically by proposing a small,
L89@P1: human-interpretable modification that flips the prediction [38]. Work on algorithmic recourse
L90@P1: emphasizes that such modifications must be actionable: recommended changes should respect
L91@P1: immutability constraints (e.g., age), feasibility constraints (e.g., realistic ranges), and domain-specific
L92@P1: notions of effort or preference [37, 18]. This line of research has made clear that counterfactual
L93@P1: recommendations are not only about proximity, but also about the plausibility and implementability of
L94@P1: the suggested actions, which often requires careful constraint design and cost modeling. Importantly
L95@P1: for our setting, recent discussions highlight that recourse may become unreliable when the underlying
L96@P1: predictive model changes or is uncertain, motivating notions of recourse reliability and stability
L97@P1: beyond a single fixed predictor [34].
L98@P1: Robustness of recourse to model change. A closely related literature studies how recourse
L99@P1: degrades under model updates such as retraining, distribution shift, or the existence of multiple
L100@P1: equally accurate models that behave differently for individuals. This phenomenon is often framed
L101@P1: through predictive/model multiplicity, where a prediction task admits many comparably accurate
L102@P1: predictors with conflicting decisions, implying that the associated recourse recommendations can vary
L103@P1: widely across equally plausible deployed models [26, 4, 31]. Complementing this view, empirical and
L104@P1-2: 2theoretical evidence shows that recourse computed for a fixed model can become invalid after realistic
L105@P2: data- and model-shift events, motivating stability as a first-class requirement. To address these
L106@P2: issues, robust recourse methods typically modify the recourse generation procedure itself, designing
L107@P2: recommendations that remain valid across sets or distributions of plausible future models (e.g., via
L108@P2: adversarial or distributionally robust formulations) [36, 29]. Related work also studies robustness
L109@P2: of counterfactual explanations under model multiplicity through formal guarantees across multiple
L110@P2: models [22]. While these approaches explicitly acknowledge that models evolve, they generally treat
L111@P2: the deployment pipeline as given and focus on hedging in the optimization that produces recourse.
L112@P2: Our work is complementary but distinct: we study a ubiquitous deployment transformation model
L113@P2: quantization and ask how to design quantization so that recourse remains stable after compression
L114@P2: (see Fig 2).

L126@P2: Quantization and mixed precision. Quantization
L127@P2: reduces inference cost by representing weights and
L128@P2: activations with low-bit numerical formats, and
L129@P2: quantization-aware training (QAT) incorporates lowprecision effects during learning to improve accuracy at deployment bitwidths [16]. Representative
L130@P2: QAT approaches include LSQ, which learns layerwise step sizes to adapt uniform quantizers to task
L131@P2: and activation statistics, and clipping-based methods
L132@P2: that learn activation ranges to reduce saturation and
L133@P2: improve low-bit fidelity [9, 5]. Mixed-precision quantization goes further by assigning different bitwidths
L134@P2: across layers under a resource budget, using either
L135@P2: hardware-aware policy search or sensitivity/curvature
L136@P2: proxies to identify layers whose precision most affects performance [39, 6]. Recent post-training quantization (PTQ) methods for large models also highlight that quantization error is highly non-uniform across channels and layers, motivating data-driven
L137@P2: layer/channel selection and scaling strategies [10, 23]. Despite strong progress, these methods primarily optimize predictive performance under resource constraints, and do not explicitly target the
L138@P2: stability of counterfactual recourse as an objective. To our knowledge, no prior work optimizes
L139@P2: quantization including both bit allocation and quantizer parameters for recourse stability as a
L140@P2: first-class objective.

L292@P3: 4 Method
L293@P3: 4.1 Core idea
L294@P3: CFQ treats post-training quantization as a structured model shift that can preserve predictive accuracy
L295@P3: while altering the geometry that determines actionable counterfactuals. Let f denote the full-precision
L296@P3: model and let fq denote the quantized model obtained by inserting quantizers into weights and/or
L297@P3-4: 4activations and choosing a possibly mixed-precision bit allocation. CFQ enforces that a recourse
L298@P4: action computed with the full-precision model remains effective after quantization. To this end, CFQ
L299@P4: introduces a teacher recourse action δfp(x; ytgt) computed on the full-precision model and uses it as
L300@P4: a supervision signal for quantization.
L301@P4: In principle, δfp(x; ytgt) could be the exact solution of the constrained recourse problem. In practice, repeatedly solving an inner optimization to high accuracy is computationally expensive. CFQ
L302@P4: therefore computes δfp using a small number K of projected gradient steps on a differentiable surrogate objective and then stops gradient through this inner procedure when optimizing quantization.
L303@P4: More precisely, let Lcf(x, δ; ytgt, f) denote a differentiable counterfactual loss whose minimization
L304@P4: encourages f(x + δ) = ytgt. For classification, a standard choice is the cross-entropy with the target
L305@P4: class evaluated at the perturbed input. Starting from δ
L306@P4: (0) = 0, the teacher recourse action is approximated through the projected-gradient recursion δ

L324@P4: 4.2 CFQ objective
L325@P4: CFQ learns quantization to preserve recourse validity while maintaining task accuracy under a
L326@P4: bit budget. Let Ltask(fq(x), y) denote the standard supervised objective, such as cross-entropy
L327@P4: for classification, evaluated on the quantized model. CFQ augments this objective with a validity
L328@P4: preservation term that forces the quantized model to assign the target outcome at the teacherrecourse point x + δfp(x; ytgt). The simplest strong CFQ objective is defined as LCFQ =
L329@P4: E(x,y)[Ltask(fq(x), y) + η Lvalid(fq(x + δfp(x; ytgt)), ytgt)] + λ BitCost, where η ≥ 0 controls
L330@P4: the strength of counterfactual validity preservation and λ ≥ 0 trades off predictive performance
L331@P4: against resource usage. The term Lvalid(·, ·) can be instantiated as cross-entropy with the target label,
L332@P4: so that the quantized model is explicitly trained to satisfy the counterfactual constraint at x + δfp.
L333@P4: The expectation E(x,y)[·] is taken over the training distribution, and ytgt is either specified by the
L334@P4: application, for example the favorable outcome, or chosen as a function of (x, y), for example the
L335@P4: opposite class in binary classification.
L336@P4: We set η, the validity preservation weight, and λ, the bit-cost trade-off, by a small validation sweep.
L337@P4: Specifically, we grid search η ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1, 2} and λ ∈ {10−4, 10−3, 10−2, 10−1}, selecting the
L338@P4: pair that maximizes validation accuracy subject to the bit-budget target. Equivalently, we minimize
L339@P4: Ltask + η Lvalid among models whose expected BitCost is within a small tolerance of Btot. Unless
L340@P4: stated otherwise, we use η = 1 and choose λ to match the desired budget, where a higher λ yields
L341@P4: lower BitCost.
L342@P4: CFQ can incorporate additional terms that improve cost and direction stability without significantly
L343@P4: increasing computational complexity. A first optional add-on matches the teacher action to a student action δq(x; ytgt) computed with the quantized model using the same K projected steps but
L344@P4: replacing f by fq, namely δq(x; ytgt) = ΠK
L345@P4: A(x)
L346@P4: (0; x, ytgt, fq). In contrast to the teacher action δfp,
L347@P4: which is detached, we do not stop gradients through δq when Lmatch is enabled. Consequently,
L348@P4: Lmatch backpropagates into the quantized model parameters, including quantizer parameters and the
L349@P4: mixed-precision policy, through the unrolled K projected steps; we therefore use small values of K,
L350@P4: typically 1–3, to keep this overhead modest. CFQ then adds a direction/cost matching regularizer
L351@P4: defined as Lmatch = Ex[α1∥δq(x; ytgt) − δfp(x; ytgt)∥1 + α2 |c(δq(x; ytgt)) − c(δfp(x; ytgt))|],

L362@P5: buffer at the counterfactual point, which reduces the chance that quantization-induced perturbations
L363@P5: flip the outcome. When used, the full objective becomes LCFQ + Lmatch + β Lhinge for some β ≥ 0.

L368@P5: B = {2, 3, 4, 8}. CFQ learns the layer-wise bitwidths {bℓ}
