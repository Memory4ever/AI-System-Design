L0@P0: The Range Shrinks, the Threat Remains:
L1@P0: Re-evaluating LLM Package Hallucinations on the 2026 FrontierModel Cohort
L2@P0: Aleksandr Churilov
L3@P0: Independent Researcher

L5@P0: Abstract
L6@P0: Spracklen et al. (USENIX Security '25) showed that code-generating large language models hallucinate
L7@P0: package names that do not exist on PyPI or npm at rates ranging from 5.2% on commercial models to 21.7%
L8@P0: on open-source models, creating an attack surface for slopsquatting-the registration of malicious packages
L9@P0: under hallucinated names. We replicate their methodology on five frontier code-capable LLMs released
L10@P0: between October 2025 and March 2026: Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-5.4-mini, Gemini 2.5
L11@P0: Pro, and DeepSeek V3.2. Across 199,845 paired Python and JavaScript prompts validated against PyPI and
L12@P0: npm master lists, we measure overall hallucination rates between 4.62% (Claude Haiku 4.5) and 6.10% (GPT5.4-mini)-an order-of-magnitude compression of the inter-model spread observed by Spracklen, but not a
L13@P0: retirement of the threat. Beyond replication, we identify a set of 127 package names (109 on PyPI, 18 on
L14@P0: npm) that all five evaluated models invent identically, constituting a model-agnostic supply-chain attack
L15@P0: surface that no single-model study can reveal. We further document a Python-over-JavaScript hallucination
L16@P0: asymmetry that inverts Spracklen's 2024 finding, identify a Haiku-below-Sonnet inversion within the
L17@P0: Anthropic family, and observe a Jaccard-similarity peak between DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4-mini (J =
L18@P0: 0.343) suggestive of shared training-data origins.
L19@P0: 1 Introduction
L20@P0: Modern software development depends on centralized
L21@P0: package registries: PyPI for Python and npm for JavaScript
L22@P0: host millions of packages installable with a single command.
L23@P0: The emergence of code-generating large language models
L24@P0: has injected a new actor into this supply chain. When a
L25@P0: developer asks an LLM for code, the model frequently emits
L26@P0: pip install or npm install directives, or import statements that
L27@P0: name particular packages. If the developer trusts the
L28@P0: suggestion and installs the named package, an attacker who
L29@P0: has registered a malicious package under that name has
L30@P0: succeeded-without ever interacting with the developer or the
L31@P0: LLM provider.
L32@P0: Spracklen et al. [1] characterized this risk on the September2024 model cohort, generating 576,000 code samples across
L33@P0: 16 models and finding that 21.7% of open-source-model
L34@P0: generations and 5.2% of commercial-model generations
L35@P0: referenced non-existent packages. They identified 205,474
L36@P0: unique hallucinated package names and termed the resulting
L37@P0: attack class slopsquatting: the registration of malicious
L38@P0: packages under names that LLMs have already, by their
L39@P0: hallucination behavior, primed downstream developers to
L40@P0: install.
L41@P0: Two model generations later, the frontier has moved.
L42@P0: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and DeepSeek have all released
L43@P0: code-capable models in the eighteen months following
L44@P0: Spracklen's study, with substantially expanded training data,
L45@P0: broader safety post-training, and-in the cases of Claude
L46@P0: Haiku 4.5, GPT-5.4-mini, and DeepSeek V3.2-newly
L47@P0: available extended-thinking or reasoning-effort controls.
L48@P0: Whether the package-hallucination phenomenon has moved
L49@P0: with them is the empirical question this paper answers.
L50@P0: We find four things. First, the inter-model hallucination
L51@P0: range has compressed substantially: where Spracklen
L52@P0: reported a spread of 5.2%–21.7% (16.5 percentage points)
L53@P0: across their cohort, we measure 4.62%–6.10% (1.48
L54@P0: percentage points) across our five 2026 frontier models-an
L55@P0: 11-fold narrowing. Second, 127 package names (109 PyPI,
L56@P0: 18 npm) are hallucinated identically by all five models,
L57@P0: exposing a model-agnostic attack surface that no singlemodel study can detect. Third, we observe a Python-overJavaScript hallucination asymmetry across all five models
L58@P0: that inverts Spracklen's 2024 JavaScript-worse pattern.
L59@P0: Fourth, within the Anthropic family Claude Haiku 4.5
L60@P0: hallucinates measurably less than Claude Sonnet 4.6,
L61@P0: contradicting the typical smaller-models-hallucinate-more
L62@P0: pattern.
L63@P0: Contributions. Concretely:
L64@P0: • A faithful replication of Spracklen et al. [1] on five
L65@P0: frontier LLMs released between October 2025 and
L66@P0: March 2026, generating 199,845 code samples and
L67@P0-1: validating every emitted package against PyPI and npm package-name master lists supplied with the
L68@P1: original artifact.
L69@P1: • A measurement of inter-model range compression:
L70@P1: 4.62%–6.10% across the five evaluated models-an
L71@P1: order-of-magnitude narrowing of the spread
L72@P1: reported in 2024. The mean rate has not, however,
L73@P1: fallen to zero: the slopsquatting threat persists.
L74@P1: • Identification of a universal-hallucination set of
L75@P1: 127 package names (109 PyPI, 18 npm) invented
L76@P1: by all five models. This set constitutes a modelagnostic supply-chain attack surface and motivates
L77@P1: a coordinated-disclosure protocol with PyPI
L78@P1: Security and npm Security prior to public release.
L79@P1: • A within-family inversion between Claude Haiku
L80@P1: 4.5 (4.62%) and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (5.41%),
L81@P1: contrary to Spracklen's observation that smaller
L82@P1: models within a family tend to hallucinate more.
L83@P1: • An open artifact consisting of the replication code,
L84@P1: validation logs, and analysis scripts, deposited at
L85@P1: Zenodo (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.19859120) under a
L86@P1: verified-researcher access policy for the full
L87@P1: hallucination corpus, mirroring [1].
L88@P1: The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. §2
L89@P1: reviews related work; §3 specifies the threat model; §4
L90@P1: describes the replication methodology; §5 presents permodel rates and pairwise comparisons; §6 examines the
L91@P1: universal-hallucination set as a model-agnostic attack vector;
L92@P1: §7 discusses why the range has compressed; §8 enumerates
L93@P1: limitations; §9 covers ethics and open-science
L94@P1: considerations; and §10 concludes.
L95@P1: 2 Background and Related Work
L96@P1: 2.1 Package Confusion Attacks
L97@P1: Package confusion attacks predate LLM-generated code by
L98@P1: more than a decade. Typosquatting attacks register packages
L99@P1: under names one or two edit operations away from a
L100@P1: legitimate package, exploiting developer typos at install
L101@P1: time; Neupane et al. [14] catalogue several thousand such
L102@P1: attacks in the wild on PyPI and npm. Birsan's 2021
L103@P1: dependency-confusion disclosure [15] showed that internalonly package names, when shadowed on a public registry,
L104@P1: can be silently substituted by the package manager and run
L105@P1: with the privileges of the affected build. Slopsquatting [18]
L106@P1: is the LLM-induced variant: rather than waiting for a typo or
L107@P1: relying on a name leak, the adversary registers names that the
L108@P1: LLM itself, by its hallucination behavior, has primed
L109@P1: developers to type. Slopsquatting is therefore enabled by, but
L110@P1: distinct from, classical typosquatting; this paper continues
L111@P1: the line of measurement work [1] characterizing the size of
L112@P1: the attack surface that current LLMs create.
L113@P1: 2.2 Hallucinations in Code-Generating LLMs
L114@P1: LLM hallucination has been studied extensively at the level
L115@P1: of natural-language fact-conflicting output [16, 17]. Codegeneration hallucination is a security-adjacent specialization:
L116@P1: the model emits syntactically plausible but semantically
L117@P1: wrong tokens that, when those tokens correspond to package
L118@P1: names or API references, become an installation request
L119@P1: rather than a textual claim. Pearce et al. [13] established the
L120@P1: underlying pattern in their Asleep at the Keyboard study of
L121@P1: GitHub Copilot's vulnerable-code generation. Concurrent
L122@P1: work [20] extends the package-hallucination measurement
L123@P1: axis with additional model coverage. The present paper is
L124@P1: positioned as a temporal replication: same methodology,
L125@P1: newer cohort, with comparison to the 2024 baseline as the
L126@P1: primary design goal.
L127@P1: 2.3 The Spracklen Baseline
L128@P1: Spracklen et al. [1] is the definitive baseline for this work.
L129@P1: They evaluated 16 LLMs released between mid-2023 and
L130@P1: mid-2024 across two prompt datasets totalling 576,000
L131@P1: generations, finding hallucination rates of at least 5.2% on
L132@P1: commercial models and 21.7% on open-source models. They
L133@P1: identified 205,474 unique hallucinated package names and
L134@P1: characterized them along axes of repeatability, semantic
L135@P1: similarity to real packages, and persistence across
L136@P1: temperature settings. Their public artifact [19] consists of the
L137@P1: two prompt datasets, the validated package-name master lists
L138@P1: for PyPI and npm, and the heuristic extraction and validation
L139@P1: code. Our work re-runs their measurement protocol on a
L140@P1: more recent, narrower cohort, using the same prompt
L141@P1: datasets and the same master lists for direct cross-temporal
L142@P1: comparison.
L143@P1: 2.4 Concurrent and Subsequent Work
L144@P1: A concurrent measurement effort, Importing Phantoms [20],
L145@P1: reports comparable rates on a partially overlapping model
L146@P1: set. Industry monitoring services have published occasional
L147@P1: rate measurements in 2025–2026 [18], generally consistent
L148@P1: with the compression we report. To our knowledge no prior
L149@P1: work has identified the model-agnostic universalhallucination phenomenon documented in §6, and no public
L150@P1: dataset directly compares 2026-cohort frontier models
L151@P1: against the original Spracklen prompt corpus.
L152@P1: 3 Threat Model
L153@P1: We adopt the threat model of Spracklen et al. [1]. The
L154@P1: adversary is an unprivileged actor whose only required
L155@P1: capability is the ability to register packages on PyPI and
L156@P1: npm-a capability available to anyone with an email address,
L157@P1: with no review process for first-time uploads on either
L158@P1: registry. The adversary's goal is to publish a malicious
L159@P1: package under a name that one or more frontier codegenerating LLMs is sufficiently likely to hallucinate that
L160@P1: downstream developers, prompted with similar codegeneration queries, will be induced to install the package.
L161@P1: The attack chain has six stages:
L162@P1: • (1) the adversary samples one or more LLMs
L163@P1: systematically and identifies hallucinated package
L164@P1: names;
L165@P1: • (2) the adversary registers a malicious package on
L166@P1-2: PyPI or npm under each hallucinated name;• (3) at some later time, a victim developer issues a
L167@P2: code-generation query to the same (or a related)
L168@P2: LLM;
L169@P2: • (4) the LLM generates code that references the
L170@P2: hallucinated name;
L171@P2: • (5) the developer's package manager, trusting the
L172@P2: registry, installs the adversary's package;
L173@P2: • (6) the malicious code executes in the developer's
L174@P2: environment.
L175@P2: We assume no compromise of the LLM provider, no model
L176@P2: fine-tuning by the adversary, no privileged access to user
L177@P2: systems, and no involvement of the package registries
L178@P2: beyond their public registration interfaces. The adversary is
L179@P2: therefore extremely cheap; per-package upload costs are
L180@P2: zero, and the only cost is the LLM API spend required to
L181@P2: enumerate hallucinations-which our own per-model API
L182@P2: spend (Appendix B) quantifies as $860.90 for a sweep of
L183@P2: comparable scope.
L184@P2: The universal-hallucination phenomenon documented in §6
L185@P2: strengthens the adversary's position: a single registered name
L186@P2: can target users of multiple LLM providers simultaneously,
L187@P2: since 127 package names in our data are hallucinated by all
L188@P2: five evaluated models. A single PyPI or npm registration is
L189@P2: therefore not, in general, a single-model attack.

L214@P2: 4.2 Prompt Datasets
L215@P2: We re-use the two prompt datasets released by Spracklen et
L216@P2: al. [1] without modification: a curated set of 20,163
L217@P2: programming questions drawn from Stack Overflow (split
L218@P2: into already-talked SO_AT and less-yield SO_LY subsets),
L219@P2: and an LLM-synthesized set of 19,806 questions generated
L220@P2: programmatically (LLM_AT and LLM_LY subsets). Both
L221@P2: supersets are evenly split between Python and JavaScript
L222@P2: prompts. We generate one sample per prompt per model. For
L223@P2: five frontier 2026 models, this yields a total of 199,845
L224@P2: generations (approximately 39,969 per model; per-provider
L225@P2: counts are reported in Appendix B and reflect minor retrydriven variation). We deliberately do not augment the
L226@P2: prompt set with newly authored 2026-only prompts:
L227@P2: matching Spracklen's corpus exactly is what allows direct
L228@P2: cross-temporal comparison. We discuss the resulting
L229@P2: training-data-leakage concern in §8.
L230@P2-3: 4.3 Generation ConfigurationFor each model we record the API endpoint, the snapshot
L231@P3: identifier returned by the provider in API response metadata,
L232@P3: the temperature, the top-p value, and (for models exposing
L233@P3: it) the reasoning_effort or thinking-budget setting. Default
L234@P3: values were used unless noted. GPT-5.4-mini was run with
L235@P3: reasoning_effort set to minimal-a methodological choice
L236@P3: motivated by API-cost considerations and discussed as a
L237@P3: confounder in §8. The exact snapshot identifiers and perprovider configuration are reproduced in Appendix B.
L238@P3: 4.4 Package Extraction
L239@P3: We extract package references from each generation using a
L240@P3: regex-based extractor matching pip install and npm install
L241@P3: directives, import / from … import statements (Python), and
L242@P3: require() / import … from statements (JavaScript). The
L243@P3: extractor handles namespaced npm packages (e.g.,
L244@P3: @scope/name), Python sub-imports (resolving from foo.bar
L245@P3: import baz to the top-level package foo), and conditional
L246@P3: imports inside try/except blocks. Standard-library modules
L247@P3: are filtered using the core_modules.csv list shipped with the
L248@P3: Spracklen artifact for Node.js and the standard CPython
L249@P3: stdlib list for Python. We follow the Spracklen heuristicextraction approach verbatim and inherit its known falsepositive characteristics.
L250@P3: 4.5 Validation Pipeline
L251@P3: Each extracted package name is validated against the PyPI
L252@P3: and npm package-name master lists shipped with the
L253@P3: Spracklen 2025 artifact (pypi_package_names.csv: 500,565
L254@P3: names; npm_package_names.csv: approximately 3,000,000
L255@P3: names). Following Spracklen's classify protocol, a name is
L256@P3: judged hallucinated if and only if (a) the normalized name is
L257@P3: non-empty and well-formed, (b) the name is absent from the
L258@P3: language-appropriate master list, and (c) the raw name is
L259@P3: absent from the language-specific known-false-positive list.
L260@P3: We additionally apply a noise filter that excludes Dartlanguage and Node.js-builtin syntactic prefixes (package:,
L261@P3: dart:, node:), path-alias artifacts (@/…), template-variable
L262@P3: placeholders, asset-path fragments, and several other regexextractor artifacts identified during validation; the full filter
L263@P3: is published in the open-science artifact. The validation date
L264@P3: for all reported numbers is April 28, 2026.
L265@P3: 4.6 Statistical Analysis
L266@P3: For each model we report the empirical hallucination rate p̂
L267@P3: = |H| / |R| where R is the multiset of all extracted package
L268@P3: references and H ⊆ R is the subset of names absent from the
L269@P3: master lists, with a 95% Wilson [8, 11] confidence interval.
L270@P3: We test the null hypothesis of equal hallucination rates
L271@P3: across models with a Pearson χ² statistic on each 2×2 modelpair contingency table [9]; pairwise post-hoc comparisons
L272@P3: use Holm–Bonferroni correction [10] across the C(5,2) = 10
L273@P3: pairs at family-wise α = 0.05. As a robustness check we
L274@P3: additionally compute a permutation-test p-value per pair
L275@P3: (10,000 resamples, seed 42); the chi-squared and
L276@P3: permutation results agree on every pair we report as
L277@P3: significant. For inter-model overlap of unique hallucinated
L278@P3: names we report Jaccard similarity over the 10 model pairs.
L279@P3: 4.7 Refusals and Malformed Responses
L280@P3: We classify each generation into one of four mutually
L281@P3: exclusive categories: valid (the generation contains only
L282@P3: resolving package references), hallucinated (at least one nonresolving reference), refused (the model declined to answer
L283@P3: or returned a degenerate output, identified by length below
L284@P3: 20 characters and manual sampling), and malformed (nonparseable response or no extractable code). Hallucination
L285@P3: rates in §5 are computed over (valid ∪ hallucinated) only;
L286@P3: refusal rates are reported separately. Refusal rates vary
L287@P3: substantially across the cohort: from 1.27% on Claude Haiku
L288@P3: 4.5 to 32.14% on GPT-5.4-mini at
L289@P3: reasoning_effort=minimal. We discuss the resulting
L290@P3: denominator-asymmetry concern in §8.

L296@P3: compression of the 5.2%–21.7% spread reported by
