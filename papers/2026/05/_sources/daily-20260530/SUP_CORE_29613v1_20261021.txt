Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) offer a parallel alternative, yet their decoding strategies remain under-explored in ASR contexts. This paper analyzes three decoding schemes for DLM-based ASR: fixed-number, static confidence threshold, and dynamic confidence threshold. We propose measuring round-wise accuracy using Negative Log-Likelihood-based uncertainty as a proxy for decoding progress. Our results show that both threshold-based strategies significantly outperform fixed-number schemes in accuracy and speed. We attribute this to a property unique to ASR: most tokens reach high confidence early, allowing reliable ones to be harvested aggressively while leaving only difficult tokens for later rounds. Notably, the static-threshold strategy matches the accuracy of autoregressive decoding while offering superior efficiency. † † footnotetext: ∗ Work done as an advisory role only. † Corresponding Author. 1 Introduction Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) has advanced through self-supervised pretraining Baevski et al. (2020) ; Schneider et al. (2019) ; Hsu et al. (2021) ; Chen et al. (2022) , large-scale weakly supervised training Radford et al. (2023) ; Pratap et al. (2024) ; Peng et al. (2024) , and LLM-based decoder modeling Grattafiori et al. (2024) ; Bai et al. (2023) ; Chen et al. (2024) ; Wu et al. (2023) ; Chu et al. (2023) . However, LLM-based ASR remains slow due to autoregressive (AR) decoding and large parameter counts. Recent work Wang et al. (2025) has therefore explored Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) Nie et al. (2025) ; Ye et al. (2025) for ASR, which enable bidirectional-attention-based parallel decoding by iteratively predicting multiple masked positions and committing high-confidence tokens. Since token commitment in DLMs relies on heuristic confidence criteria, prior work has explored several strategies: fixed-number decoding, which commits a fixed number of high-confidence tokens; static thresholding, which commits tokens above a fixed confidence threshold; and dynamic thresholding, which adapts the commitment criterion by the current confidence distribution Fu et al. (2025) ; Ben-Hamu et al. () ; Nie et al. (2025) ; Chang et al. (2022) ; Yu et al. (2025) ; Wu et al. (2025) . However, DLM-based ASR Wang et al. (2025) has remained centered on fixed-number decoding, leaving alternative strategies without systematic evaluation. We therefore compare these strategies in terms of final accuracy–speed trade-offs and round-wise decoding progress, showing that thresholding strategies are more effective and revealing where decoding time and accuracy gaps arise. Concretely, we analyze decoding progress through prediction reliability and token throughput. We quantify reliability using token-level Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL), using an AR configuration of the same DLM as a reference because it predicts each token with maximal contextual information and consistently outperforms parallel strategies. We measure throughput by counting newly unmasked tokens in each round, and further examine per-round confidence distributions to understand how each strategy converts confidence estimates into token-commitment decisions. Our contributions are threefold. First, we systematically compare fixed-number, static-threshold, and dynamic-threshold decoding for DLM-based ASR, showing that thresholding strategies provide a better accuracy–speed trade-off. Second, we analyze round-wise decoding behavior using NLL and token throughput, revealing early high-confidence commitments followed by slower refinement. Across all strategies, stricter commitment criteria ( i . e ., smaller k k , higher C C , or lower f f ) consistently reduce WER at the cost of higher RTF, revealing the accuracy–speed trade-off in diffusion-based decoding. Compared with fixed-number decoding, threshold-based strategies form a better Pareto frontier, achieving comparable WER with lower RTF. Static and dynamic thresholding show similar recognition accuracy, but static thresholding often provides lower RTF, especially in Fig. 1 (b,c), making it the most balanced choice. Static thresholding also approaches the LLM-based AR baseline while offering substantial speedups. With block size 4 and C = 0.95 C=0.95 , it achieves 2.81% WER, nearly matching the AR reference at 2.78%, while running 1.7 × 1.7\times faster in RTF. As block size increases, decoding becomes faster but less accurate; in the fully parallel setting, static thresholding reaches 4.13% WER while achieving a 3.5 × 3.5\times speedup. 4.2 Round-wise Progress Assessment To further analyze reliability, latency concentration, and strategy-specific token commitments, we conduct a round-wise assessment. We compute progress on utterances with at least 32 text tokens, setting P r = 1 P_{r}=1 when the first 32 tokens are fully unmasked. 4.2.1 Prediction Reliability using Uncertainty Trajectory Directly measuring round-wise WER is impractical because insertion and deletion errors break alignment across decoding rounds. We therefore use cumulative uncertainty as a proxy for prediction reliability. As shown in Fig. 2 (a-c), a larger final gap from the AR reference generally corresponds to higher WER, indicating that uncertainty trajectories reflect recognition reliability. Most strategies show a sharp uncertainty increase near the end, suggesting that later rounds commit more low-confidence tokens; early deviation above the AR trajectory may indicate premature incorrect commitments. Under matched RTF (Fig. 2 (d)), static-threshold decoding tracks the AR trajectory most closely and achieves the lowest WER: 3.07% versus 4.47% for fixed-number and 3.52% for dynamic thresholding. Dashed lines indicate mean stopping rounds. 4.2.2 Inference Speed using Number of Unmasked Tokens To identify where speed differences arise, we analyze per-round token throughput, measured by the number of newly unmasked tokens per round. For a fair speed-focused comparison, we choose settings with comparable WERs within 3.05%–3.10%: static thresholding ( C = 0.95 C=0.95 ), dynamic thresholding ( f = 0.2 f=0.2 ), and fixed-number decoding ( k = 1 k=1 ). As shown in Fig. 3 , static thresholding finishes in the fewest rounds on average (6.1), followed by dynamic thresholding (9.5) and fixed-number decoding (32.0), consistent with their RTFs of 0.046, 0.081, and 0.298, respectively. The difference mainly comes from early-round token commitments. Static thresholding shows a heavy-start pattern, committing many tokens early before tapering to final refinement, whereas dynamic thresholding proceeds more steadily and fixed-number decoding is limited by its per-round budget. These results suggest that static thresholding accelerates decoding by exploiting high-confidence tokens early, leaving late-stage refinement as the main speed bottleneck. The plot shows the fraction of tokens with a confidence score S S greater than or equal to a threshold s s . 4.3 Confidence Distribution and Decoding Efficiency To explain the efficiency differences among decoding strategies, we examine the underlying confidence distribution and compare ASR with math reasoning on GSM8K Cobbe et al. (2021) . Specifically, we measure the fraction of tokens whose confidence exceeds a threshold s s under parallel decoding. As shown in Fig. 4 , ASR confidence scores are highly concentrated near the upper end: 93.7% of tokens satisfy s ≥ 0.90 s\geq 0.90 , and 91.1% remain above s = 0.95 s=0.95 . In contrast, GSM8K shows a much more dispersed distribution, with only 59.1% and 45.7% of tokens exceeding the same thresholds. This likely reflects ASR’s near-monotonic speech-text alignment and unique transcription target, unlike the more variable reasoning paths in GSM8K. This high-confidence skew explains the strategy-specific efficiency trends. Static thresholding exploits this profile by unmasking most high-confidence tokens early and leaving only the low-confidence tail for refinement. Dynamic thresholding is more conservative because its criterion can be rewritten as ( k + 1 ) ​ ( 1 − c ( k ) ) < f (k+1)(1-c^{(k)})<f , or c ( k ) > 1 − f / ( k + 1 ) c^{(k)}>1-f/(k+1) , so committing more tokens requires a threshold approaching 1. Fixed-number decoding is limited by its constant k k budget and cannot exploit abundant high-confidence tokens. 5 Conclusion We systematically compared fixed-number, static-threshold, and dynamic-threshold decoding for DLM-based ASR. Our analysis shows that ASR confidence scores are highly concentrated, enabling thresholding strategies, especially static thresholding, to commit reliable tokens early while reserving difficult positions for later refinement. These findings suggest that future DLM decoding strategies for ASR should focus on accelerating late-stage refinement without sacrificing recognition accuracy. Limitations Our study is limited to clean read English speech from LibriSpeech test-clean. Since our finding relies on highly skewed ASR confidence scores, it may not fully generalize to noisy, spontaneous, or multilingual speech, where acoustic ambiguity, phonology, scripts, or tokenization may alter the confidence distribution and accuracy–speed trade-off. Extending the analysis to noisier benchmarks and multilingual ASR is left for future work. Ethical Considerations This work uses publicly available datasets, pretrained models, and benchmarks, including LibriSpeech, Whisper-medium.en, LLaDA-8B-Instruct, and GSM8K, for non-commercial research purposes in accordance with their licenses, access conditions, and intended use. We do not collect new human-subject data or conduct user studies. ASR systems may produce errors and should be deployed with appropriate validation and human oversight in high-stakes settings.
