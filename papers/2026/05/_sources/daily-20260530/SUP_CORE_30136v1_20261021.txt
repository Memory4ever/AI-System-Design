However, these systems tend to rapidly accumulate extremely long conversation histories during interaction. As conversations lengthen, relevant information is increasingly diluted by irrelevant context, leading to degraded performance. In this work, we present Agent-Radar , a training-free context management method that dynamically steers each agent’s attention toward relevant context with a novel temporal and spatial decay mechanism. Our experiments demonstrate that Agent-Radar outperforms state-of-the-art methods across five different benchmarks, yielding gains of up to 7.64 absolute points. Furthermore, our analysis shows that Agent-Radar remains effective and robust as the number of agents and interaction rounds increases. Finally, the ablation study shows that core components in Agent-Radar are crucial to performance and generalizable in different settings. 1 Introduction LLM-based multi-agent systems have demonstrated remarkable performance on complex tasks ( Liang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024b ; Du et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2024 ) . Despite these successes, agent communication often accumulates extremely long context windows rapidly ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) and suffers from communication redundancy ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Zhang et al., 2025a ; Du et al., 2024 ; Liang et al., 2024 ) . As a result, the original task description, key constraints, and other critical evidence are often buried within the context, making it difficult for LLMs to reliably identify and attend to them. This phenomenon, also known as the “lost-in-the-middle” effect, frequently induces hallucinations and logical errors during generation ( Becker et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2024a ; Cemri et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ) . This issue is further exacerbated as systems scale. Increasing numbers of agents and interaction rounds cause communication histories to grow rapidly, amplifying context dilution and undermining the reliability of large-scale agent collaborations ( Cemri et al., 2025 ; Becker et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ) . Prior context management methods have sought to mitigate this challenge in two ways. One line of work applies periodic summarization to compress accumulated interaction history ( Wang et al., 2025a ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . Another adopts pruning strategies that limit context growth by removing redundant agents or communication edges ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2025b ) . While effective at improving efficiency, these methods introduce information loss. Specifically, compressing or pruning messages can discard subtle but critical signals, potentially misleading downstream reasoning and propagating errors across subsequent interactions ( Shen et al., 2025b ; Acharjee et al., 2025 ) . We propose a shift from context compression or pruning to selectively steering agents’ attention over relevant context. In the original Transformer architecture, the attention mechanism retrieves information by matching queries to keys, allowing the model to assign importance weights across preceding tokens in the context ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) . Accordingly, in multi-agent systems, an agent’s current generation step naturally acts as a query seeking to retrieve relevant information from the communication history. Unlike prior methods that act as hard, binary filters over context, we instead steer attention toward a subset of relevant content while preserving the full transcript, thereby avoiding information loss. To achieve this, we develop Agent-Radar , a training-free method that dynamically guides agents’ attention toward relevant context without modifying the underlying communication history. To identify which parts of the history should guide each generation step, Agent-Radar selects context by jointly modeling semantic relevance, spatial distance, and temporal recency. First, inspired by organizational network theory ( Borgatti et al., 2009 ; Allen, 1984 ) , where influence decays with structural distance, Agent-Radar formulates the agent interaction topology as a graph and applies a spatial decay factor based on inter-agent distance, prioritizing messages from closely interacting agents. Second, Agent-Radar incorporates temporal decay to down-weight stale information, reflecting evidence from both long-context modeling and human memory that relevance diminishes over time ( Finkenbinder, 1913 ; Sun et al., 2023 ; Ma et al., 2023 ) . Third, Agent-Radar performs fine-grained semantic matching by segmenting messages into sentence-level units to extract relevant evidence at the current step. Finally, Agent-Radar integrates these signals through a lightweight attention-steering mechanism, enabling agents to emphasize key instructions, critical constraints, and useful intermediate evidence, without compressing or pruning history tokens. We evaluate Agent-Radar on five benchmarks using three base LLMs. Compared with SOTA context management methods, including compression-based approach ( Wang et al., 2025a ) and pruning methods ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2025b ) , Agent-Radar achieves gains of up to 7.64 absolute points. As a plug-in method, Agent-Radar can be seamlessly integrated into diverse multi-agent systems, including debate-based systems ( Du et al., 2024 ) , GPTSwarm ( Zhuge et al., 2024 ) , and AutoGen ( Wu et al., 2024 ) . Across these frameworks, Agent-Radar consistently improves their performance by up to 12.87 absolute points and generalizes across different interaction topologies. Furthermore, Agent-Radar remains robust as systems scale to larger numbers of agents and longer interaction rounds. Finally, ablation studies confirm that each component contributes meaningfully to the overall performance gains. 2 Problem Formulation We model the multi-agent system architecture as a graph 𝒢 = ( 𝒱 , ℰ , ℱ ) \mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}) . Unlike a flat document collection, multi-agent communication is generated by different agents at different rounds, and these source and temporal signals affect whether a previous response is likely to be useful for the current active agent. In particular, a sentence from a previously generated output can remain semantically relevant to the inference objective of the current agent while being outdated, redundant, or weakly grounded. Pure semantic retrieval ignores these signals and may therefore over-select stale or indirectly propagated evidence. To this end, we formulate Agent-Radar as retrieval over the spatio-temporal communication history. For each generated output o j ( τ ) o_{j}^{(\tau)} in the communication history, we assign a scalar relevance score to each generated output that combines a spatial decay factor and a temporal decay factor: r i ​ ( o j ( τ ) , t ) = ϕ s ​ ( v j , v i ) ⏟ spatial decay ⋅ ϕ t ​ ( τ , t ) ⏟ temporal decay , r_{i}(o_{j}^{(\tau)};\,t)\;=\;\underbrace{\phi_{s}\!\bigl(v_{j},\,v_{i}\bigr)}_{\text{spatial decay }}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\phi_{t}\!\bigl(\tau;\,t\bigr)}_{\text{temporal decay }}, (3) where ϕ s \phi_{s} and ϕ t \phi_{t} are the spatial and temporal decay factors. Given these scores, we retrieve fine-grained, sentence-level attention-aligned context by combining this spatio-temporal score with semantic similarity to the query received by the current agent. Spatial Decay. The organizational network theory ( Borgatti et al., 2009 ; Allen, 1984 ) finds that information relevance in collaborative networks typically decays with structural distance. In a multi-agent workflow, responses from nearby collaborators are more likely to contain direct, actionable evidence, whereas information propagating from distant agents often introduces indirect noise or redundant context. Based on this insight, we use an exponential form because each additional relay can be viewed as retaining only a fixed fraction of the useful signal. If one extra hop retains a fraction λ s \lambda_{s} of the relevance, then k k extra hops retain λ s k \lambda_{s}^{k} . Therefore, the first term in Equation 3 models spatial relevance as a decay over graph distance: ϕ s ​ ( v j , v i ) = λ s d 𝒢 ​ ( v j , v i ) − 1 , 0 < λ s < 1 , \phi_{s}(v_{j},v_{i})=\lambda_{s}^{\,d_{\mathcal{G}}(v_{j},\,v_{i})-1},\quad 0<\lambda_{s}<1, (4) where d 𝒢 ​ ( v j , v i ) d_{\mathcal{G}}(v_{j},v_{i}) is the distance from Section 2 and λ s \lambda_{s} controls the decay rate. For direct neighbors or self-generated critiques, we set ϕ s = 1 \phi_{s}=1 . Generated outputs from agents further away receive exponentially lower weights. Temporal Decay. As the discussion evolves, earlier responses should not be treated as equally informative as recent exchanges, because later rounds often refine, correct, or supersede earlier reasoning. This intuition is consistent with the recency effect observed in human memory ( Finkenbinder, 1913 ) and with temporal decay mechanisms in long-horizon sequence modeling ( Sun et al., 2023 ; Ma et al., 2023 ) . We use the same idea for temporal decay that discounts responses by their age: ϕ t ​ ( τ , t ) = λ t ( t − τ − 1 ) , 0 < λ t < 1 , \phi_{t}(\tau;\,t)=\lambda_{t}^{\!\bigl(t-\tau-1\bigr)},\quad 0<\lambda_{t}<1, (5) where τ \tau is the time when the response was produced, t − τ − 1 ≥ 0 t-\tau-1\geq 0 is the age of the generated output, and λ t \lambda_{t} governs the decay rate. The most recent response receives a weight of 1 1 , and older responses are discounted exponentially. This keeps recent evidence significant while still allowing older but useful responses to contribute when they are semantically relevant. Retrieval. Unlike previous methods that retrieve whole message chunks, steering attention to an entire generated response can still introduce irrelevant details, since only part of the message may be useful for the current query. We evaluate Agent-Radar on three categories of benchmarks. For open-ended question answering, we use HotpotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) , 2WikiMultihopQA ( Ho et al., 2020 ) , and MuSiQue ( Trivedi et al., 2022 ) to evaluate the system’s ability to retrieve and synthesize information across multiple documents. We report F1 scores for these open-ended QA benchmarks. For mathematical reasoning, we use MATH-500 ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) , which represents competition-level math problems ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) . We report accuracy for MATH-500. For general reasoning, we adopt MMLU-Pro ( Wang et al., 2024a ) , an enhanced benchmark designed to assess robust reasoning capabilities across diverse domains. We report accuracy for MMLU-Pro. Base Models. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 ( Team, 2025 ) as the base model for all main experiments. To verify the generalizability across different LLMs, we experiment with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) and Qwen3-32B ( Team, 2025 ) . These results are reported in Appendix A.1 . Baselines. We compare Agent-Radar against single-agent, context management, and multi-agent system baselines. The single-agent baselines include Vanilla , which directly solves the task, and Self-Consistency (CoT-SC) ( Wei et al., 2022 ; Kojima et al., 2022 ) , which samples multiple reasoning paths and applies majority voting. The context management baselines include Compaction method ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025a ) , AgentPrune ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) , and AgentDropout ( Wang et al., 2025b ) , which are pruning-based methods that limit context growth by removing redundant agents or communication links. Finally, we evaluate plug-in compatibility with representative multi-agent systems, including GPTSwarm ( Zhuge et al., 2024 ) , AutoGen ( Wu et al., 2024 ) , and Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) ( Du et al., 2024 ) . Prior work has shown that multi-agent debate system performance often improves initially but then declines as the number of rounds or agents increases ( Becker et al., 2026 ; Estornell and Liu, 2024 ; Lin and Hooi, 2025 ; Liu et al., 2024b ) . This degradation typically occurs because long conversations often become redundant, circular, and less task-focused, yielding low-quality feedback. As stale messages occupy more of the context window, the original task constraints receive less attention, causing agents to drift from the primary objective, and may make harmful incremental revisions. Since Agent-Radar dynamically aligns each agent’s attention toward relevant context throughout the interaction, we evaluate whether it improves robustness in MAD by varying the number of agents and communication rounds in Figure 2 . As the number of agents increases, both vanilla MAD and Agent-Radar exhibit the expected trend of initial improvement followed by decline. This trend shows that more agents can bring useful collaboration at first, while too many agents also introduce redundant or distracting messages. Agent-Radar shows a more robust scaling pattern. Vanilla MAD reaches its best F1 score with 4 agents and then drops as more agents are added. In contrast, Agent-Radar peaks at 5 agents and remains strong at 6 agents, and maintains a clear advantage across all agent configurations. When scaling the number of communication rounds, both methods improve at first and then become stable. Noteably, across all round configurations, Agent-Radar maintains a clear advantage over vanilla MAD. These results indicate that Agent-Radar improves the robustness of MAS under larger agent populations and longer multi-round communication. 4.3 Ablation Study and Analysis In this section, we ablate the key design choices in Agent-Radar on three representative benchmarks : MuSiQue, MATH-500, and MMLU-Pro. Specifically, we examine Agent-Radar by replacing the semantic matching method and the attention steering method. Then, we isolate the contributions of spatial and temporal decay by removing them individually or jointly. We use the same experimental settings as the main evaluation. Table 4 reports the results. Different Semantic Matching Methods. To validate the dense semantic matcher in Agent-Radar , we compare it with a sparse lexical retrieval variant based on BM25. In this variant, we keep the Agent-Radar framework unchanged, including spatial decay, temporal decay, and attention steering, but replace the dense semantic matcher with BM25. As shown in Table 4 , the BM25 variant improves over the AgentDropout baseline, increasing MATH-500 from 82.60 to 85.20, and MMLU-Pro from 67.40 to 68.40. However, it shows slightly lower performance on MuSiQue, decreasing from 37.06 to 36.76. Furthermore, the default Agent-Radar configuration consistently achieves performance gains of 2.96 points on MuSiQue, 3.60 points on MATH-500, and 1.00 points on MMLU-Pro over BM25. These results suggest that dense semantic matching is better suited for MAS communication, where agents may express related information through semantically related but lexically different descriptions. Importantly, the BM25 variant still outperforms the baseline, indicating that the gains of Agent-Radar do not solely depend on a particular retriever. Table 4 : Ablation results. The score drops from 39.72 to 28.41 on MuSiQue, from 88.80 to 84.40 on MATH-500, and from 69.40 to 66.20 on MMLU-Pro. Without spatial decay, messages from distant agents may receive similar weight as those from closely collaborating neighbors, potentially steering attention toward indirect or less relevant context. This suggests that modeling topology structure is important for maintaining effective context selection in MAS. Ablating the temporal decay shows a similar pattern. The score drops from 39.72 to 32.83 on MuSiQue, from 88.80 to 84.60 on MATH-500, and from 69.40 to 67.80 on MMLU-Pro. Without temporal decay, earlier messages may receive similar importance to more recent and relevant evidence, making the model more vulnerable to distraction from outdated context. This suggests that recency helps filter stale context in multi-round reasoning. We further ablate both decay terms and score context only by semantic similarity. This variant underperforms the full Agent-Radar model. This shows that semantic similarity alone is not sufficient for robust context selection in MAS. By incorporating spatial and temporal signals, Agent-Radar better distinguishes relevant evidence from merely similar but less useful historical messages. Interestingly, we observe that removing both decay variants can outperform the single-decay ablation variants on MuSiQue and MATH-500. This behavior is reasonable because pure semantic retrieval produces a monolithic score across all candidate messages, whereas removing one decay component may over-weight or under-weight certain messages. For example, spatial-only weighting may favor nearby but outdated messages, while temporal-only weighting may favor recent but structurally distant messages. This can make the selected context less reliable than using semantic similarity alone. Nevertheless, the full Agent-Radar model consistently achieves the strongest performance, indicating that spatial and temporal signals are most effective when combined. Efficiency Analysis. We further evaluate whether the added retrieval and attention-steering steps introduce substantial runtime cost. The detailed efficiency comparison is provided in Appendix A.4 . Agent-Radar increases end-to-end latency from 968.8 to 1196.5 seconds per task over the original multi-agent system baseline. In contrast, Compaction and AgentDropout incur larger latency increases due to extra generation, pruning decisions, or topology updates. Overall, Agent-Radar incurs a modest computational overhead from context retrieval and attention steering, while delivering substantial improvements in reasoning performance. 5 Related Work Context Management in Multi-agent Systems. LLM-based multi-agent systems have demonstrated significant progress in decomposing and resolving complex tasks through collaborative planning, role specialization, and iterative discussion ( Guo et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2023 ; Tian and Zhang, 2026 ; Tran et al., 2025 ) . Despite their advantages, agent communication rapidly accumulates into long contexts, burying the original task instruction and other salient evidence in the middle of the interaction history, which can degrade reasoning quality and induce errors ( Liu et al., 2024a ; Cemri et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ) . Recent work addresses this issue mainly from two directions. First, a line of research improves communication efficiency by sparsifying the communication topology ( Li et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2025b ; Shen et al., 2025a ; Liu et al., 2024c ; Zhang et al., 2024a ) . Specifically, AgentPrune ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) and AgentDropout ( Wang et al., 2025b ) explicitly reduce context redundancy by pruning messages or eliminating redundant agents during multi-round collaboration, thereby lowering token cost while preserving or even improving task performance. Second, recent context learning approaches manage context by dynamically updating each agent’s instruction during interaction. In particular, Wu et al. (2025) and Chen et al. (2025a) compress or filter the accumulated context history to reduce context length, while M2CL ( Hua et al., 2026 ) learns a context generator to produce round-specific context prompts. In contrast, Agent-Radar preserves the original multi-agent workflow and improves how each agent attends to salient information within the accumulated context, enabling more effective use of long interaction histories. Attention Steering. Compared to the aforementioned context management methods, attention steering methods directly guide LLMs during inference toward the user-specified part of the context, without shortening or rewriting the input. Selective Prompt Anchoring (SPA) ( Tian and Zhang, 2025 ) adjusts the logit distribution to emphasize the specified context. SEKA ( Li et al., 2026 ) learns a universal “relevance subspace" for a given task and constructs a projection matrix that amplifies the relevant features. PASTA ( Zhang et al., 2024b ) identifies and reweights a subset of attention heads to redirect the model’s attention to user-specified parts. Selective Self-Attention (SSA) ( Zhang et al., 2024c ) augments the softmax nonlinearity with a principled temperature scaling strategy. TOAST ( Shi et al., 2023 ) learns feature selection modules that guide attention toward task-relevant information. However, these methods require manual specification of anchor content, limiting their adaptability to diverse reasoning contexts, especially in multi-agent systems where context evolves autonomously. Agent-Radar addresses this limitation by automatically retrieving relevant context from the communication history and steering each agent’s attention toward that context during inference. 6 Limitation Despite the consistent improvements observed in our experiments, Agent-Radar has some limitations. First, Agent-Radar requires a selection of threshold and decay factors. Our parameter analysis shows that Agent-Radar is relatively robust to these choices. Future work could explore adaptive thresholds and decay factors that are automatically adjusted according to the communication topology, context length, or model uncertainty. Second, the quality of the retrieval signal can impact the performance of Agent-Radar . Stronger retrieval models, uncertainty-aware scoring, or explicit contradiction detection may further improve robustness. Lastly, Agent-Radar requires attention-steering backends such as SPA or PASTA. Our experiments show that the method is not tied to a single backend. Developing more efficient and broadly compatible steering backends would further improve the practicality of Agent-Radar . 7 Conclusion We presented Agent-Radar , a plug-and-play attention-based context management method for multi-agent systems. Across five benchmarks, Agent-Radar significantly improves performance and outperforms existing context management methods. Our analysis further shows that Agent-Radar remains effective across various communication topologies and when the system scales to accommodate more agents and interaction rounds. C.1 Different Communication Topologies In the main experiments, we initialize the communication graph as a random graph. In the analysis experiments, we further evaluate two additional topologies, namely layered graphs and fully connected graphs. Figure 4 demonstrates all MAS communication topologies. Figure 4 : MAS communication topologies in fully connected, random, layered, and debate topologies. Random Graph A random graph is constructed by sparsely sampling edges from a complete graph. We follow AgentPrune [ Zhang et al., 2025a ] for the random-topology setup to ensure a fair comparison with pruning-based MAS baselines. Prior work has shown that irregular sparse communication structures can outperform fully connected ones [ Qian et al., 2025 ] , partly because random shortcut connections reduce the average path length between agents and induce small-world properties. Intuitively, such connections allow information to propagate more efficiently across otherwise distant agents. Complete Graph In the multi-agent setting, a complete graph is implemented as a directed graph that would become fully connected if converted into its undirected counterpart. After execution according to the topological order, the final agent aggregates the preceding discussion and produces the final answer or reflection. Layered Graph A layered graph organizes agents into multiple stages, analogous to the layered structure of a multilayer perceptron. Agents in the first layer first receive the query and generate their responses. These responses are then passed to agents in the next layer together with the original query, and the process continues sequentially across layers. Following Qian et al. [2025] , the final layer contains a single agent that integrates the information from the previous layer and outputs the final solution. LLM-Debate LLM-Debate [ Du et al., 2024 ] is designed as a multi-round debate process in which, at each round, every agent observes the outputs of all agents from the previous round before generating its own response. As a result, this framework can be viewed as forming a temporally fully connected communication graph. C.2 Compression Implementation We adopt the compression prompt from Wang et al. [2025a] .
