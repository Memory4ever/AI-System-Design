Existing approaches require either ground-truth verifiable rewards, restricting training to domains with automatic correctness checks (e.g., mathematics, code execution), or human preference labels, which are expensive to collect and prone to reward hacking. Recent label-free methods replace ground-truth verifiers with self-referential signals like majority voting or token entropy over a model’s own outputs, but risk reinforcing a model’s own errors. In this work we propose Cross-Model Entropy (CME) , the mean log-likelihood of a generator’s response under a separate verifier model, as a label-free reward signal for RL post-training. CME is continuous, training-free, and grounded in the principle that responses a verifier finds unsurprising are likely correct or high quality. Because the verifier is independent of the generator, the signal cannot be gamed through self-consistency. We integrate CME into GRPO with no other changes to the training loop, extending label-free RL to open-ended instruction following—a regime where self-referential signals are inapplicable or poorly suited. On open-ended instruction following (UltraFeedback prompts, evaluated on AlpacaEval 2.0), CME rewards beat the untrained base in head-to-head LLM-as-Judge comparisons across four model families (Qwen, Llama, Gemma, OLMo) and three training regimes (pretrained, SFT, and instruction-tuned), with tie-adjusted win rates ranging from 52.5 % 52.5\% to 71.4 % 71.4\% . Per-token surprise concentrates gradient signal where responses differ in quality, without ground-truth labels. 1 Introduction Reinforcement learning has emerged as a central technique for improving the reasoning and alignment of large language models. Methods like PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) and GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) have driven gains on mathematical reasoning, code generation, and instruction following Guo et al. (2025) . These approaches rely on ground-truth verifiers, which restrict training to domains where correctness is automatically checkable. The alternative is reward models trained from human preference data Ouyang et al. (2022) , with variants like DPO Rafailov et al. (2023) eliminating the reward model and RLAIF Bai et al. (2022) ; Lee et al. (2023) substituting an LLM judge for human annotators. However, each of these methods requires preference data, which is expensive to collect and prone to reward hacking Skalse et al. (2022) . Recent work has explored label-free alternatives. TTRL Zuo et al. (2025) and Evol-RL Zhou et al. (2025) use majority voting over sampled responses as a pseudo-label for GRPO, with Evol-RL adding a novelty term to mitigate the “cognitive collapse” that majority-voting alone can induce. RL-Without-GT Xin et al. (2025) substitutes surface signals (e.g. format, length) for ground-truth correctness on mathematical reasoning. RENT Prabhudesai et al. (2025) , EM-RL Agarwal et al. (2025) , and INTUITOR Zhao et al. (2026) take a different route, using the generator’s own token entropy or self-certainty as an intrinsic reward that pushes the policy toward sharper output distributions. While each of these methods is effective in its target setting — typically math or code with verifiable structure — they share a common property: the reward derives entirely from the generator’s own outputs. We refer to such rewards as self-referential . The risk is structural: when the model is systematically wrong in a way multiple rollouts agree on, or assigns high confidence to incorrect outputs, the reward reinforces the error rather than correcting it. Majority-voting variants additionally require a canonical answer per prompt and do not extend to open-ended generation — ruling out the quality and alignment objectives that motivate modern post-training Rafailov et al. (2023) ; Bai et al. (2022) . We propose a different approach. Instead of asking the generator to vote on its own outputs or measure its own confidence, we ask whether a separate capable model finds the generator’s response surprising. Cross-Model Entropy (CME) , the negative log-likelihood of a generator’s response under a separate verifier model, is a continuous, token-level, training-free signal that requires no ground-truth labels. Low CME indicates the verifier finds the response unsurprising. Maximizing expected CME minimizes the reverse KL D KL ( π θ ∥ π ϕ ) D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\phi}) up to a generator-entropy term, a useful target when the verifier is more competent in-domain than the generator. The approach is viable because open-weight instruction-tuned models like Gemma-3 Gemma Team (2025) are capable enough to serve as meaningful evaluators of response quality, even when they are not frontier models. We integrate CME into GRPO with no other changes to the training loop, extending label-free RL to open-ended instruction following—the regime that motivates modern post-training and where self-referential signals are inapplicable or poorly suited. We train on a subset of UltraFeedback and evaluate AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate ( Dubois et al., 2024 ) using LLM-as-Judge, finding that CME rewards beat the untrained base across four model families (Qwen, Llama, Gemma, OLMo) and three training regimes (pretrained, SFT, instruction-tuned). On SFT backbones, CME -GRPO matches DPO trained on the same base using only UltraFeedback prompts and none of its preference annotations. We additionally show that performance scales with verifier capability, and that a randomly initialized verifier control underperforms every real-weighted verifier—confirming that the signal comes from cross-model evaluation rather than generic regularization. 2 Method Preliminaries: GRPO Group Relative Policy Optimization ( Shao et al., 2024 ) fine-tunes a policy π θ \pi_{\theta} without a learned critic. Given a prompt x x , GRPO samples G G responses { y i } i = 1 G \{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G} from π θ \pi_{\theta} , scores each with a reward r i r_{i} , and computes group-normalized advantages A ^ i = ( r i − μ r ) / σ r \hat{A}_{i}=(r_{i}-\mu_{r})/\sigma_{r} . The policy is updated via a clipped surrogate objective with a KL penalty to a frozen reference π ref \pi_{\text{ref}} ; the full objective is stated in Appendix A . Standard GRPO assigns a single scalar reward per response. In prior label-free work, r i r_{i} is derived from majority voting ( Zuo et al., 2025 ) or format proxies ( Xin et al., 2025 ) . We instead derive r i r_{i} from cross-model entropy. Cross-Model Entropy Reward We replace GRPO’s reward with a label-free signal derived from a verifier model π ϕ \pi_{\phi} drawn from a different family than the generator π θ \pi_{\theta} . Let y i = ( y i , 1 , … , y i , T i ) y_{i}=(y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,T_{i}}) be a response tokenized by the generator. The per-token cross-model entropy at position t t is CME i , t = − log ⁡ π ϕ ​ ( y i , t aligned | x , y i , < t aligned ) , \mathrm{CME}_{i,t}=-\log\pi_{\phi}\!\left(y_{i,t}^{\mathrm{aligned}}\,\big|\,x,\,y_{i,<t}^{\mathrm{aligned}}\right), (1) where y i , t aligned y_{i,t}^{\mathrm{aligned}} denotes the verifier token(s) spanning the character range of the t t -th generator token, computed by the alignment procedure of Appendix B . We negate the cross-entropy so that lower verifier surprise yields higher reward: r i , t = − CME i , t r_{i,t}=-\mathrm{CME}_{i,t} . Per-position advantages A ^ i , t = ( r i , t − μ r , t ) / σ r , t \hat{A}_{i,t}=(r_{i,t}-\mu_{r,t})/\sigma_{r,t} are normalized across the G G responses at each position, and positions with no verifier counterpart are masked from the normalization and contribute no gradient. See Appendix B for edge cases and a worked example. 3 Experiments We fine-tune generators spanning four families (Qwen, Llama, Gemma, OLMo) and three training regimes (pretrained, SFT, and instruction-tuned), in each case using gemma-3n-E4B-it ( Gemma Team, 2025 ) as a frozen verifier. Training uses a subset of UltraFeedback ( Cui et al., 2024 ) with all preference annotations and chosen/rejected completions discarded; only the prompts are retained. The reward is the token-level CME from Eq. ( 1 ) computed over the full response, with character-level alignment to handle the cross-family tokenizer mismatch. This setup stresses the cross-family setting: the verifier shares no family with most generators, and for pretrained backbones it must provide useful gradient signal to models that have never been instruction-tuned. Full training details are in Appendix E . Evaluation. We evaluate on the 805-prompt AlpacaEval 2.0 set ( Dubois et al., 2024 ) , replacing the default GPT-4 reference with gemma-3-1b-it — a 1B instruction-tuned model comparable in size to the models under test. For each experiment we draw greedy responses from up to four models: the base checkpoint, our CME-GRPO fine-tune, the same-family instruct checkpoint, and the gemma-3-1b-it reference. We run pairwise GPT-5.2 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 LLM-as-Judge ( Zheng et al., 2023 ) evaluations per prompt with randomized presentation order, and report tie-adjusted win rate ( wins + 1 2 ​ ties ) / N (\text{wins}+\tfrac{1}{2}\text{ties})/N over N = 805 N=805 prompts. Direct head-to-head comparisons (Base vs. CME-GRPO, CME-GRPO vs. Instruct) isolate CME-GRPO’s gain over same-family checkpoints; vs.-reference comparisons place all three policies on a common quality axis. Results. Table 1 reports tie-adjusted pairwise win rates across all generators and both judges. CME-GRPO beats its untrained base in every head-to-head comparison, with margins from 52.5 % 52.5\% to 71.4 % 71.4\% , and on instruction-tuned backbones it surpasses the original Instruct release on the same reference axis. Most notably, CME-GRPO matches DPO trained on the same SFT base, winning 44.0 % 44.0\% against OLMo-2-0425-1B-DPO using only UltraFeedback prompts and none of its preference annotations. CME-GRPO treats the verifier as a stand-in for a quality oracle, so a natural question is whether the verifier’s own capability matters. We test this directly by holding the generator fixed ( Qwen2.5-0.5B ) and varying the verifier across eight conditions: a randomly initialized control, same-family verifiers ( Qwen2.5-0.5B , Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct , Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct ), and cross-family verifiers spanning 270M to 4B parameters. What the objective predicts. Maximizing expected CME minimizes the reverse KL D KL ( π θ ∥ π ϕ ) D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\phi}) up to a generator-entropy term (Appendix C ), so the natural reading is that stronger verifiers — being closer to the high-quality response distribution — should provide a more useful target. The token-level variant of Eq. ( 1 ), with character-aligned weights and group-normalized per-position advantages, is a heuristic extension that targets the same direction in distribution space without admitting the same closed form. We test this prediction empirically. Figure 3 reports CME-GRPO win rates against the base and instruct comparators. Three effects emerge. (i) The random-weighted control underperforms every real-weighted verifier ( 55.8 % 55.8\% vs base, 22.8 % 22.8\% vs instruct; real-weighted verifiers exceed 59.2 % 59.2\% and 25.2 % 25.2\% respectively), confirming that CME’s signal comes from cross-model evaluation rather than generic regularization. (ii) Among real verifiers, win rate increases with verifier capability: the largest verifier ( gemma-4-E4B-it ) ties for best vs base ( 70.0 % 70.0\% ) and is best vs instruct ( 34.0 % 34.0\% ), while the smallest ( gemma-3-270m-it ) sits at the bottom. The trend is not strictly monotonic in parameter count, but win rate generally increases with verifier size. (iii) Same-family verifiers underperform the best cross-family ones, and scaling within the Qwen family ( Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct ) yields no additional gain — capability matters, but family alignment does not substitute for it. (2025) shows that RL fine-tuning of Qwen-Math models can improve from random reward signals, suggesting some label-free gains reflect surface properties rather than learned signal. Our random-verifier control quantifies this effect in the open-ended setting: a random verifier does provide some lift over the base ( 55.8 % 55.8\% vs 50 % 50\% ), but every real-weighted verifier exceeds it, and gains scale with verifier capability. 5 Conclusion We introduced CME, a label-free reward for RL post-training defined as the negative log-likelihood of a generator’s response under a separate verifier model. CME is a drop-in replacement for the reward in GRPO and extends label-free RL to open-ended instruction following. Across four model families and three training regimes, CME-GRPO beats the untrained base in every head-to-head comparison and matches DPO on SFT backbones using only prompts. Performance scales with verifier capability, and our random-verifier control confirms the signal comes from cross-model evaluation rather than generic regularization. Limitations. CME requires a verifier held in memory alongside the generator, adding compute and memory overhead. Because the reward measures how unsurprising a response is to the verifier rather than whether it is correct, CME is in principle vulnerable to drift toward verifier-style outputs or mode collapse toward the verifier’s prior; we did not observe either failure in our experiments, but we did not test long-horizon training where these effects might emerge. Our experiments cover only small generators (0.5B–1.5B parameters) and English-language UltraFeedback prompts; behavior at larger scale or in other languages remains to be evaluated. We do not run self-entropy GRPO baselines (RENT, EM-RL) directly; our same-family verifier comparison in Section 3 is the closest mechanically applicable analog. Finally, we rely on LLM-as-Judge for evaluation rather than human ratings, which introduces judge-specific biases despite our use of two independent judges with high agreement. Appendix B Character-Level Token Alignment When the generator π θ \pi_{\theta} and verifier π ϕ \pi_{\phi} use different tokenizers (e.g., Qwen vs. Gemma), token boundaries do not align directly, so token-level CME requires mapping verifier log-probabilities onto generator positions. We decode both token sequences to a shared character string, and for each generator token y i , t y_{i,t} covering character span [ a t , b t ) [a_{t},b_{t}) , identify the set 𝒱 t \mathcal{V}_{t} of verifier tokens whose spans overlap [ a t , b t ) [a_{t},b_{t}) . The aligned verifier log-probability at generator position t t is log ⁡ π ϕ ​ ( y i , t aligned ∣ x , y i , < t aligned ) = ∑ s ∈ 𝒱 t w t , s ⋅ log ⁡ π ϕ ​ ( v s ∣ x , v < s ) , \log\pi_{\phi}(y^{\text{aligned}}_{i,t}\mid x,y^{\text{aligned}}_{i,<t})=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}w_{t,s}\cdot\log\pi_{\phi}(v_{s}\mid x,v_{<s}), where w t , s = | span ⁡ ( v s ) ∩ [ a t , b t ) | / | span ⁡ ( v s ) | w_{t,s}=|\mathrm{span}(v_{s})\cap[a_{t},b_{t})|/|\mathrm{span}(v_{s})| distributes a verifier token’s log-probability across generator positions in proportion to character overlap. The weights satisfy ∑ t w t , s = 1 \sum_{t}w_{t,s}=1 for every verifier token, so per-position rewards aggregate to the correct total verifier log-likelihood of the response regardless of tokenizer mismatch. Edge cases. When generator and verifier tokens cover identical spans, 𝒱 t \mathcal{V}_{t} contains a single verifier token with weight one. When the generator tokenizer is coarser, verifier tokens fall fully inside generator tokens and contribute their full log-probability (the unweighted sum equals the joint character-span log-probability by the chain rule). When the verifier tokenizer is coarser, fractional weighting prevents the verifier token’s log-probability from being counted multiple times. Generator tokens that decode to whitespace or have no overlapping verifier token are assigned zero aligned log-probability and masked from advantage normalization. Worked example. For the response "unhappiness" with generator tokenization ["un", "happiness"] (spans [ 0 , 2 ) [0,2) , [ 2 , 11 ) [2,11) ) and verifier tokenization ["unhappy", "ness"] (spans [ 0 , 7 ) [0,7) , [ 7 , 11 ) [7,11) ), the verifier token "unhappy" straddles both generator tokens: 2 / 7 2/7 of its log-probability falls in "un" and 5 / 7 5/7 in "happiness" . The verifier token "ness" sits entirely within "happiness" and contributes its full log-probability. The two aligned per-position log-probabilities sum to the total verifier log-likelihood of "unhappiness" . Why not average? Averaging verifier log-probabilities over 𝒱 t \mathcal{V}_{t} would make the aggregate reward depend on tokenization granularity rather than response content: a generator token covering k k verifier tokens would receive 1 / k 1/k of their summed log-probability rather than the full sum. This would also underweight spans the verifier tokenizes finely — exactly the spans where verifier surprise is most informative. Appendix C Reverse-KL Connection The CME reward connects to reverse-KL distillation through the cross-entropy decomposition. For the sequence-level reward r ⁡ ( y ) = log ⁡ π ϕ ​ ( y ∣ x ) r(y)=\log\pi_{\phi}(y\mid x) , 𝔼 y ∼ π θ ​ [ r ​ ( y ) ] = − H ⁡ ( π θ , π ϕ ) = − H ( π θ ) − D KL ( π θ ∥ π ϕ ) , \begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[r(y)]&=-H(\pi_{\theta},\pi_{\phi})\\ &=-H(\pi_{\theta})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\phi}),\end{split} so maximizing expected CME pulls π θ \pi_{\theta} toward π ϕ \pi_{\phi} . This identity is exact for the sequence-level reward under the verifier’s tokenization; the token-level variant of Eq. 1 , with character-aligned weights and group-normalized per-position advantages, is a heuristic extension that targets the same direction in distribution space without admitting the same closed form. CME -GRPO is thus closely related to MiniLLM ( Gu et al., 2024 ) , which optimizes reverse KL between student and teacher via policy gradient on student samples scored by the teacher. The differences are operational: GRPO’s group-normalized advantages depend only on the relative ranking of CME values within a sampled group, and a KL anchor to the reference policy bounds drift toward the verifier. We therefore view CME -GRPO as an RL formulation of on-policy reverse-KL distillation, with the verifier acting as a critic rather than a target distribution. Appendix D Related Work D.1 RLHF and Preference Optimization The modern RLHF pipeline was formalized by Ouyang et al. (2022) in InstructGPT, which trained GPT-3 to follow instructions using human preference labels and PPO Schulman et al. (2017) . This established the three-stage recipe—SFT, reward modeling, and RL—that underpinned the first generation of aligned language models. A key limitation of RLHF is the cost of human preference data. Bai et al. (2022) introduced Constitutional AI and RLAIF, using an LLM to generate preference labels from written principles. Lee et al. (2023) compared RLAIF to RLHF and found comparable alignment quality, validating AI feedback as a substitute. CME goes further: rather than prompting a model to judge quality, we derive a reward directly from token-level probabilities, requiring no prompting or structured judgment. DPO Rafailov et al. (2023) eliminated the reward model by re-parameterizing the RLHF objective as a classification loss on preference pairs. DPO is simpler than PPO-based RLHF but requires offline preference pairs. Online variants such as Online DPO and IPO Azar et al. (2024) address distribution shift by generating fresh completions during training. CME-GRPO is fully online and label-free. D.2 Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards The insight enabling GRPO Shao et al. (2024) was that mathematical correctness provides a free, scalable reward: reward 1 if the final answer is correct, else 0. DeepSeek-R1 Guo et al. (2025) scaled this approach and showed emergent reasoning behaviors including self-reflection without supervised reasoning traces. Subsequent improvements include DAPO Yu et al. (2025) , which introduced decoupled clipping and dynamic sampling for large-scale GRPO stability, and PRIME Cui et al. (2025) , which augments GRPO with process-level reward shaping. All operate in verifiable domains; we extend GRPO to non-verifiable domains by substituting cross-model entropy for the ground-truth verifier. D.3 Self-Improvement Without Labels STaR Zelikman et al. (2022) bootstrapped reasoning by iteratively fine-tuning on correct self-generated rationales, requiring only binary answer verification. Self-consistency Wang et al. (2022) showed that majority voting over reasoning paths improves accuracy at inference time. TTRL Zuo et al. (2025) converted self-consistency into a training signal, using majority vote as a pseudo-label for GRPO. RL-Without-GT Xin et al. (2025) showed that format and response length serve as effective surrogate signals for math training. Evol-RL Zhou et al. (2025) identified “cognitive collapse” in majority-voting methods—models losing solution diversity—and added a semantic novelty reward. MM-UPT Wei and others (2025) extended unsupervised GRPO to multimodal LLMs. All of these methods are fundamentally self-referential: the reward derives entirely from the model’s own outputs. If the model is systematically wrong in a way multiple rollouts agree on, the reward reinforces the error. CME-GRPO addresses this by drawing the reward from an external model in a different family. D.4 Knowledge Distillation. Maximizing expected CME minimizes D KL ( π θ ∥ π ϕ ) D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\phi}) on-policy, the reverse-KL objective optimized by MiniLLM ( Gu et al., 2024 ; Hinton et al., 2015 ) . CME -GRPO differs operationally: group-normalized advantages depend only on the relative ranking of CME values within a sampled group, and a KL anchor to the reference policy bounds drift toward the verifier — yielding an RL formulation in which the verifier acts as a critic rather than a target distribution. D.5 Cross-Model Likelihood as a Correctness Signal Prior work ( Gorbett and Jana, 2026 ) establishes that verifier likelihood ranks correct above incorrect responses across diverse tasks and model families, framed as a passive inference-time correctness signal for evaluation and selection. Our work translates this passive signal into an active training objective: rather than scoring fixed responses, we use verifier likelihood as the reward in policy optimization, and introduce a token-level variant with dense per-position advantages. D.6 Process Reward Models Outcome reward models (ORMs) assign a single scalar to a complete response, creating sparse credit assignment. Lightman et al. (2023) addressed this with process reward models (PRMs) trained on human-labeled step-level correctness (PRM800K), which consistently outperform ORMs on reasoning tasks. The bottleneck is annotation cost. Math-Shepherd Wang et al. (2024) automated PRM supervision via Monte Carlo estimation. Luo et al. (2024) scaled this further, and Zheng and others (2025b) showed LLM-as-a-judge outperforms MC estimation for PRM data quality. Zheng and others (2025a) surveys PRMs across math, code, multimodal, and agentic domains. The token-level CME variant provides per-token process rewards that are training-free, requiring only verifier forward passes. D.7 Reward Signal Reliability and Goodhart’s Law Shao et al. (2025) showed that Qwen2.5-Math models improve substantially even with random, format-only, or incorrect reward signals, suggesting RLVR on these models may surface latent pretraining knowledge rather than new reasoning. The effect is model-family-specific: Llama and OLMo models do not improve with spurious rewards. This motivates evaluating CME on both Qwen and non-Qwen generators to ensure results reflect genuine signal quality. Reward hacking more broadly Skalse et al. (2022) ; Pan et al. (2022) is mitigated in CME by the cross-family verifier design: the generator cannot exploit biases of a model from a different training lineage as easily as it can exploit its own self-consistency. D.8 LLM Evaluation Benchmarks Mathematical reasoning benchmarks used in our evaluation include the MATH dataset Hendrycks et al. (2021) , MATH-500 Lightman et al. (2023) , AIME 2024 Mathematical Association of America (2024) , and AMC 2023. Instruction-following quality is measured via AlpacaEval 2.0 Dubois et al.
