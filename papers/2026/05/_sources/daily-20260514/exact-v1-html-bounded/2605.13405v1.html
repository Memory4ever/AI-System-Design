Abstract

Model growth from a given checkpoint aims to accelerate training of a larger model, offering potential resource savings. Despite recent interest, warmstarting has seen limited practical adoption in large-scale training. We attribute this to two underexplored factors: (1) an overemphasis on preserving the smaller model’s performance at initialization, which constrains operator design for new architectures, and (2) insufficient analysis of how growth interacts with hyperparameters and scaling behavior, compounded by inconsistent growth factors across the literature.

We show that preserving the base model’s initial post-growth performance is not necessary for strong final performance, and that simple, architecture-agnostic growth strategies can outperform more complex warmstarting operators. Crucially, we empirically identify an upper bound on the growth factor gg beyond which training from scratch is more efficient. We observe this across multiple ablation setups. Notably, this limit is also present, but unreported, in prior published results. Across our experiments on dense MLPs and dense language models, we find that a 2×2\times growth factor is the most reliable in yielding convergence speedups, with gains most pronounced under 20 tokens/parameter budgets and diminishing as budget increases. We fit scaling laws over these observations to provide predictive guidance for practitioners deciding when and how much to grow. Together, our analysis provides practical guidelines and empirical limits for model growth.

1 Introduction

Training state-of-the-art deep learning models requires significant computational resources. As models scale from millions to billions of parameters, practitioners face an important question: can we reuse the compute invested in training smaller models when scaling up? Model growth or warmstarting (WS), i.e., initializing larger models from trained smaller checkpoints, offers potential efficiency gains, yet remains largely absent from production pipelines despite substantial research (Gong et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2021; Rae et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023a; Wang et al. 2023b; Deng et al. 2023; Samragh et al. 2024; Du et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2026).

Deep Learning (DL) success has been built on pushing the loss frontier across tasks, applications, and data modalities (Krizhevsky et al. 2012; He et al. 2016; Vaswani et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2020; Radford et al. 2021; Rombach et al. 2022; OpenAI 2024). As training efficacy improves, processing larger datasets drives corresponding increases in model capacity (Alabdulmohsin et al. 2023). Current research on frontier models builds on seminal scaling law studies demonstrating the necessity of simultaneously growing both model size and data processed (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022). This co-scaling question continues to evolve across different constraints, modalities, and architectures, with the traditional goal of pushing the loss-frontier now complemented by optimizing the compute-optimal frontier (Anagnostidis et al. 2024). In practice, these considerations translate to decisions about resource efficiency (Wan et al. 2023).

While WS has been periodically explored across architectures and paradigms, it has not become standard practice in training pipelines, often remaining an academic topic rather than a common choice in DL pipelines (Gong et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023a). We identify three barriers to practical WS adoption:

1) Implementation complexity: existing methods require architecture-specific adaptations and may not generalize to modern components (Su et al. 2021; Gu and Dao 2023); 2) Unclear growth limits: practitioners lack guidance on how much a given checkpoint can be grown before WS becomes counterproductive (Xie et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023a); 3) Hyperparameter opacity under scaling: the interaction between WS methods, hyperparameters, and model and data scaling budgets is under-explored, creating tuning overhead that undermines efficiency gains (Fetterman et al. 2023; Dey et al. 2025).

Moreover, in scaling regimes where both models and data grow to improve final convergence loss, measuring relative early-stage convergence speed may not adequately capture practical requirements (Shin et al. 2024; Unlu 2026).

In this work, we attempt to systematically investigate when WS is truly effective, in a first-principles manner, to address these barriers. We limit the discussion scope, both for clarity and computation, for the case when a base model checkpoint is available to be WS-ed to a larger model scale. We assume that we are aware of the dataset the base was trained on, and that some of its training hyperparameters may be available, but we treat it as a black-box when it comes to the training compute invested in it (unlike the assumption made by Liew and Kato 2025). We believe this better reflects a real-world scenario where only model checkpoints are publicly released. Our contributions are:

  1. 1.

    We generalize warmstarting growth into shrink-zero-perturb (SZP), a simple architecture-agnostic operation, and find that exact function-preservation is not required for effective warmstarting.

  2. 2.

    We empirically establish an upper bound on the growth ratio of a given checkpoint, such that, beyond this bound, training the grown model from scratch converges as fast as warmstarting it.

  3. 3.

    Using scaling-law fits, we show that for a given warmstarting method and base model size, the convergence advantage over training from scratch persists only within a bounded token budget, and we predict where this crossover occurs.

The paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 reviews prior warmstarting methods and guidance; Section 3 introduces SZP and its design motivation; Section 4 reports hyperparameter transfer, growth limits, and scaling-law predictions; and Section 5 summarizes practical guidelines and limitations.

2 Related Works

Model growth as initialization is the broad class of WS techniques that derive the weights of a larger model from a smaller, typically well-trained checkpoint, before beginning training. Here, the aim is to transfer the knowledge learned by the smaller model to improve the larger model’s training efficiency. Net2Net (Chen et al. 2016) and Network Morphisms (Wei et al. 2016) are among the earliest works exploring model growth for Deep Learning. At the time, the scope was limited to convolutional networks, but the core idea was carried forward to evolving architectures (Gong et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2024; Samragh et al. 2024). The benefit lies in being a one-time operation before training, requiring only weights from a smaller network of the same architectural family. A central idea in designing WS operations for initialization is that of function-preservation (FP), wherein the grown model’s output at the first forward pass matches that of the base model checkpoint used for WS. Introduced by Chen et al. 2016 for Deep Learning, FP has dominated the width-scaling WS paradigm (Wang et al. 2023b; Samragh et al. 2024; Du et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2026). However, with new architectures, it typically requires special adaptation to remain function preserving (Chen et al. 2021; Samragh et al. 2024). We thus question if FP at initialization is a requirement for model growth.  Karp et al. 2024 already showed that loss after a short training budget correlates better than loss at initialization with growth operator rankings. We extend this to more practical training budgets.

Learned model growth is the other family of WS that does not fix the exact manner of WS operation but handles it dynamically.  Wang et al. 2023a introduces a Kronecker-factored linear operator that parameterizes the linear map from the small to the larger model, learned over a few steps of training (in the style of  Karp et al. 2024). Here, the Kronecker product constrains growth ratios but works for both width and depth.  Evci et al. 2022 looks at dynamic model growing during training by maximizing the gradient norms of the newly added neurons to enforce faster convergence, while the output-preservation in the function-preservation-style is still implied. The learned component here comes from the newly grown weights set through SVD over the gradient on the activations. This design looks to prevent dead neurons in grown models, which can lead to early rank collapse or not utilize the grown parameter capacity and move sufficiently away in the function hypothesis space from the base model. This connects to plasticity loss (Lyle et al. 2023), which correlates with dead (inactive) neurons, low effective rank representations, and growing weight magnitudes.  Dohare et al. 2023 addresses this for a trained model by selectively reinitializing a fraction of low-utility neurons, scored by their activations, after each training step to keep a model in a continual learning setup. In this work, we focus entirely on the more straightforward setup of initialization-only WS.

Full-state Warmstarting looks beyond the weights.  Wang et al. 2024 looks at the learning rate schedulers, Ma et al. 2026 and Yu et al. 2026 necessitate the WS of optimizer states too. These families aim to preserve loss (and thereby FP), while suitably inducing training dynamics through optimizer schedules and states, including hyperparameters. Our setup assumes that only the smaller model checkpoint is available and no other information on the optimizer or scheduler, especially their states. The framework of investigation we offer in our paper should apply to future studies extending to complete training state warmstarts, too.

Warmstarting in practical settings has been covered mostly in terms of operator selection (Karp et al. 2024), for scaling related recommendations involving scaling laws (Wang et al. 2023b; Liew and Kato 2025), and the role of width-depth in WS efficacy (Du et al. 2024). We note that most WS literature varies in their setups, especially with respect to growth factors gg (the ratio of target to base model parameters) and the budget involved in training. Our investigation in this work focuses specifically on width-scaling strategies. Typically, joint scaling of models along both dimensions requires independent choice of width- and depth-growth operators (Du et al. 2024). Our study here is compatible with any depth-growing and thereby joint-growing setups. We adopt a “black-box” assumption where the tokens spent on the base checkpoint are unknown, and we are concerned primarily with the question of how much we can grow this model, by training for how much budget, to ensure there is a benefit over training from scratch. In the remaining paper, we treat the terms Model Growth and Warmstarting (WS) synonymously.

Refer to Appendix A for additional literature on hyperparameter transfer and scaling laws.

3 Methodology

This section defines the WS (Warmstarting) setup for our empirical study (Section 4). We first introduce a compact design space for growth (Section 3.1) that abstracts common warmstarting operators. We then use this space to identify a robust, architecture-agnostic default in SZP (Section 3.2).

3.1 Design Space for Warmstarting

To study when Warmstarting is effective, we compare simple, architecture-agnostic WS operations against training from scratch. Function-preservation (FP) methods typically combine three ingredients: cloning existing weights, scaling grown weights, and adding noise, often through architecture-specific rules (Chen et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2021; Du et al. 2024; Samragh et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2026) (see Appendix G). We instead keep these interacting design choices independent and apply them uniformly across all components, both 11D and 22D weights.

We define our design space by extending the shrink-and-perturb (SnP) formulation from Continual Learning (CL) (Ash and Adams 2020). In its original form, target weights θtarget\theta_{\text{target}} are derived by rescaling the base weights and adding Gaussian noise:

θtargetl=λshrink⋅θbasel+𝒩(0,σperturb)2\theta_{\text{target}}^{l}~=~\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}\cdot\theta_{\text{base}}^{l}+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{perturb}}{}^{2}) (1)

where λshrink\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{} regulates the influence of inherited weights and σperturb\sigma_{\text{perturb}}{} controls the standard deviation of Gaussian noise, used as perturbation for all neurons.

To adapt this for model growth, where the target model is larger than the base, we augment (in blue) the formulation with a growth function, 𝒢\mathcal{G}:

θtargetl=λshrink⋅𝒢⁡(θbasel,p,q)¯+𝒩(0,σperturb)2,\theta_{\text{target}}^{l}~=~\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}\cdot\underline{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{G}(\theta_{\text{base}}^{l},p,q)}+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{perturb}}{}^{2}), (2)

where 𝒢:ℝm×n×ℕ×ℕ→ℝp×q\mathcal{G}{}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow{}\mathbb{R}^{p\times q} maps the base weights θbasel∈ℝm×n\theta_{\text{base}}^{l}~\in~\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} into the larger target shape p×qp\times q (p≥m,q≥np\geq m,q\geq n). Here, λshrink∈ℛ>0\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}\in\mathcal{R}>0 and σperturb∈ℛ\sigma_{\text{perturb}}{}\in\mathcal{R}, and l∈𝒩l\in\mathcal{N} denoting layer index.

Intuition and Motivation.

By separating 𝒢\mathcal{G}, λshrink\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}, and σperturb\sigma_{\text{perturb}}{} in a WS operations, it gives a lightweight abstraction of common FP-style operations without requiring component-specific rules for exact function preservation. Formally, Equation 2 can represent more complex operators like Net2Net through appropriate choices of 𝒢\mathcal{G}, λshrink\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}, and σperturb\sigma_{\text{perturb}}{}, per architecture component (Samragh et al. 2024), though deriving such mappings remains intentionally out of scope here.

Conceptually, Growth determines which structure is inherited from the base model. Perturbation is critical for symmetry breaking, that is, it ensures that newly added neurons can learn distinct features rather than simply duplicating inherited behavior. Finally, Shrinking reduces the magnitude of inherited weights, dampening the dominance of base representations, and allows the new neurons to participate meaningfully in learning. Together, these axes yield simple WS realizations that connect cleanly to established CL concepts while remaining easy to implement. We offer theoretical intuition in Appendix C.1 and perform a mechanistic analysis of this setup in Appendix C.2.

3.2 Choosing a Default Warmstarting Method

We compare representative realizations of the design space in Figure 1 to select a robust default for our scaling experiments. In Figure 1, Zeros and Clone show no significant difference in isolation, but each improves with perturbation noise (Zeros+Perturb, Clone+Perturb), and further with shrinking (Shrink+Zeros+Perturb, Shrink+Clone+Perturb) across growth ratios. We view these variants as streamlined realizations of WS that remain architecture-agnostic and easy to implement. A function-preservation analogue would instead set the scaling magnitude relative to grown sizes, apply a weight-type-specific cloning operation, and similarly adjust the perturbation magnitude (Chen et al. 2016; Samragh et al. 2024).

Notably, all WS variants recover the base-model loss within a few steps and converge faster than Scratch early in training. However, the differences between variants become clearer in sustained convergence, where SZP (Shrink+Zeros+Perturb) is the only variant consistently superior across model widths. We therefore use SZP as our main architecture-agnostic WS method.

While simplicity is subjective, SZP strikes a practical middle ground: it mirrors the structure of standard FP algorithms while loosening the strict constraint of preserving base-model outputs at initialization. We emphasize that SZP is not proposed as a new state-of-the-art warmstarting algorithm, but as a principled baseline we find effective for studying the scaling behavior of WS in Section 4.

4 Empirical Study

In this section, we use the SZP setup to study its scaling behavior, compare against popular function-preservation baselines such as Net2Net, observe the existence of an upper bound on the growth factor, and fit scaling laws to characterize when training from scratch outperforms Warmstarting (WS). We distill these findings into practical recommendations.

4.1 Experiment Setup

Following Qiu et al. 2025, we use the synthetic regression task as a controlled testbed for scaling and WS (refer to Appendix B for more details). This setting is computationally light to afford dense hyperparameter sweeps at both base and target widths, for all run types: Scratch, and WS runs (Net2Net and SZP). The language modeling setup, used for the broader empirical comparison, is described below. We report the corresponding compute estimates in Appendix E.

4.1.1 Hyperparameter Selection

When treating WS as an initialization, it is expected to reshape the loss landscape over hyperparameters. Prior comparisons of WS operations have under-explored this. This matters particularly when WS to much larger models, where re-tuning costs can be prohibitive. Here, we use the synthetic MLP regression task to compare tuning strategies across WS operations.

Training Hyperparameters.

The tuned hyperparameters are detailed in Table 2 and summarized in Figure 2, where violins show the final validation loss distribution from a dense grid search at each target width for Scratch, Net2Net, and SZP. For transfer, we take the best configuration at the base width of 4848 and transfer it as (1) unchanged (static transfer), or (2) with width-aware μ​P\mu\text{P} scaling rules (Yang et al. 2021). We refer readers to Appendix B.4 for background and details on μ​P\mu\text{P}.

Figure 2 shows that dense tuning at the target width usually yields the best configuration when the budget allows. The effectiveness of transfer, however, depends on the initialization method. Static transfer worsens with growth factor for both Scratch and SZP, while Net2Net’s function-preservation aspect makes it more robust. Crucially, μ​P\mu\text{P} transfer is consistently near-optimal relative to the dense grid and outperforms static transfer across all run types. This motivates us to use μ​P\mu\text{P}{} transfer for all subsequent compute-heavy experiments, especially the language model runs reported in later sections. Notably, the best attainable losses across dense grid search over Scratch, Net2Net, and SZP converge as the growth factor increases. This hints at the practical limits of WS that we examine more closely in Section 4.2.

SZP Design Choices.

In function-preservation-based methods, the perturbation noise and scaling applied to base or new neurons depend on the architectural component. We instead apply Equation 2 uniformly across all weights and thus fix the scaling variable and the perturbation noise form. For perturbation noise, we use the scale-dependent σperturb=1/width\sigma_{\mathrm{perturb}}=1/\sqrt{\mathrm{width}} applied under both Static transfer and μ\muP. This choice is common in Deep Learning and has also been used in FP-based setups (Ma et al. 2026). We provide an ablation on the perturbation noise in Appendix C.3, where we observe that the noise scale affects the convergence rate more than the final generalization loss.

Just as perturbation is essential for new neurons to break symmetry inherited from the base model and use the full target capacity (Yu et al. 2026), shrinking is essential for reducing weight norms, increasing effective ranks, and maintaining plasticity. We follow Ash and Adams 2020 and fix λshrink=0.4\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}=0.4 in our experiments to isolate the effect of other variables. Our ablation in Appendix C.4 confirms that λshrink∈{0.2,0.4}\lambda_{\text{shrink}}{}\in\{0.2,~0.4\} offers improved performance over training from scratch for reasonable growth factors. We additionally provide a simple theoretical sketch in Appendix C.1 for why zero-padding, perturbation, and shrinkage play complementary roles.

4.1.2 Language Model Setup

All language-model experiments train decoder-only GPT-2 style models (Radford et al. 2019) using the open-source LitGPT implementation (LitGPT 2023) on the SlimPajama corpus (Soboleva et al. 2023). We perform only width-scaling here, consistent with our scope of studying FP-based WS. All runs used AdamW and a warmup-stable-decay learning rate scheduler with warmup and cooldown lengths set to fixed fractions of the total training budget. Each run’s training budget is set to 10,20,or​3010,~20,~\text{or}~30 tokens/parameter. We train models ranging from 1414M to 1.21.2B parameters. Except for dense grid searches at multiple base scales, all Scratch and WS runs at scales larger than the base use μ​P\mu\text{P}{} transfer for learning rates, and we scale the base-scale optimal batch size. Additional training and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix D. We additionally present ablations of SZP performance on the LLaMa architecture in Appendix F.1.

4.2 Practical Warmstarting

In this section, we focus on language model experiments and address our primary question of how and where to find gains when Warmstarting.

Empirical Performance of SZP.

We report performance scaling for SZP up to 1.21.2B parameters in Figure 3 (top), including downstream task performances in Appendix F.2. Both rows show that SZP converges faster than Scratch and Net2Net. Most notably, the relative performance gains of the WS operations (Net2Net and SZP) diminish as the growth factor gg grows. Figure 3 (rightmost, both rows) shows all the learning curves trained for 10,20,and​3010,~20,~\text{and}~30 tokens/parameters, for different base-target pairings, with identical learning rate schedules, confirming that faster convergence holds across scales for both comparisons. The difference in final loss is more subtle and is discussed more in later sections, where we fit scaling laws and systematically characterize the regimes in which WS outperforms training from Scratch, and SZP outperforms Net2Net.

The variance in performance difference across growth factors is a central observation of this work. The base model used to derive grown weights is typically well-trained, with its learned function occupying a subspace of the weight space, and exploiting this subspace is where WS’s efficiency gains originate (signal preservation). For function-preservation methods, which typically rely on clone operations, this subspace can be hard to escape due to inherent symmetries in the grown weights (Yu et al. 2026). Figure 7 (right) (see, Appendix C.2) reflects this in the lower effective rank of Net2Net compared to SZP. Decomposing weight movement into radial (along the initialization) and angular (orthogonal) components, we find that SZP’s attention layers show both rotation and scaling of the weights at initialization, while Net2Net’s radial component remains much more stable and weight adjustment is dominated by rotations than scaling the initialization vector. The practical impact of this may show up in too well-trained base models saturating WS performance (Liew and Kato 2025) or growing a base checkpoint too large such that gains diminish. In the next section, we observe and discuss this in more detail.

The Bounded Effective Growth Factor.

For practitioners with a fixed base checkpoint seeking to scale up (as scoped in Section 1), a key question is how much the model can grow while preserving WS’s efficiency gains over Scratch. Prior work (Du et al. 2024) shows that WS performance depends on the growth factor gg, defined as the ratio of target to base model parameters, and characterizes this via the optimal growth factor. This optimal factor, which we term goptg_{\text{opt}}, is estimated by fitting quadratic models to IsoFLOP losses and minimizing them. goptg_{\text{opt}} is meant to be an optimal growth factor such that it maximizes the relative gains of WS over training from Scratch. We argue this optimization-based characterization is hard to support from sparse evaluations, since reliably identifying goptg_{\text{opt}} requires substantially denser measurements, especially for g∈(1,gopt)g\in(1,g_{\text{opt}}{}). Instead, we propose reporting gupperg_{\text{upper}}, which we observe as the largest growth factor under which WS still improves over Scratch’s final validation loss. This can be extracted by drawing a horizontal line from g=1g=1 for each IsoFLOP line, cutting the parabola at a g>1g>1. We posit that gupperg_{\text{upper}} offers a more flexible and practical recommendation for WS in practice, and assess relative gains of different WS operations.

While such a bound is implicit in prior results Du et al. 2024, it has not been reported as a quantity of interest. In Figure 4, we reproduce these results for SZP and Net2Net in the LLM setting (MLP included as verification), modeling IsoFLOP lines with quadratic fits across growth factors (g=1g=1 denotes Scratch). Both goptg_{\text{opt}} (blue) and gupperg_{\text{upper}} (red) are read off the fit. gupperg_{\text{upper}} is observed across all settings. The exception is Net2Net in the LLM setting, where gupper=1g_{\text{upper}}=1 (the Scratch run) at the final IsoFLOPs eventually beats Net2Net by the end of the scheduled budget (when including the learning rate decay). This is consistent with Du et al. 202422 2 Du et al. 2024 reports that width-only scaling with FP underperforms, while their IsoFLOP analysis on growth factors for width-only scaling also exhibits a gupperg_{\text{upper}} bound. and highlights the shortcomings of function-preservation methods on more complex architectures (refer to Appendix G). For complete IsoFLOPs results, refer to Appendix H.1.

One interpretation is that Warmstarting places the target model in a region of the loss landscape shaped by the base model’s learned representations. For small gg, this initialization provides a favorable basin for efficient convergence. As gg grows, the base model occupies a diminishing fraction of the target architecture, and the initialization increasingly resembles a random one. Beyond gupperg_{\text{upper}}, the constraints imposed by the inherited structure outweigh its benefits, anchoring the warmstarted model in a suboptimal region. Consistent with this, our mechanistic analysis (Figure 13) shows that warmstarted models at higher gg increasingly resemble Scratch across interpretability metrics, suggesting gradual dilution of the base model’s structure. The stability of gupperg_{\text{upper}} across model scales further supports this. The rate at which warmstarted representations converge to Scratch-like behavior depends primarily on the architectural ratio gg, and not on absolute model size.

In summary, we observe a bound on the effective growth factor gupperg_{\text{upper}}, consistent with Du et al. 2024, and argue that identifying and reporting it is crucial for practical adoption of WS. When budget does not permit investigating gupperg_{\text{upper}}, we recommend g=2g=2 as a conservative default. Much of the WS literature restricts to 2×2\times growth (Samragh et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2026), leaving gupperg_{\text{upper}}{} undiscussed. For fair comparison of WS operations, gupperg_{\text{upper}}{} should be discussed and, where possible, investigated.

Effective Warmstarting Across Scale.

Scaling a language model involves growing both the model and the data budget. As discussed, diminishing gains are expected from WS, so studying its effectiveness at a single token budget and sparse model sizes provides only a limited, possibly misleading, signal. While Liew and Kato 2025 identified that overtraining base models diminishes WS gains, we highlight the effect of gupperg_{\text{upper}} on parameter scaling. Since we assume the base model’s training budget is unknown, we focus on the practical requirement of specifying the target budget.

We subset our collected data for g≈2g\approx 2 (Figure 4) and follow Approach 3 from Hoffmann et al. 2022 to fit the power-law parametric form predicting loss as a function of parameters NN and tokens DD,

L⁡(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ,L(N,D)=E+\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D^{\beta}},

where E,A,α,B,βE,A,\alpha,B,\beta are fit by optimizing the Huber loss (δ=10−3\delta=10^{-3}) on log⁡L\log L with L-BFGS-B. We fit scaling laws for Scratch, SZP, and Net2Net in both LLM and MLP settings. Appendix H.2 reports the parameters and R2R^{2} values, all above 0.990.99, confirming fit quality. In Figure 5, we visualize the difference in modeled loss between WS methods and Scratch fits as Isolines (contour lines of equal loss difference), marking the zero-difference contour in green as the boundary for our analysis. First, the existence of this boundary across MLPs and LLMs indicates regimes where training from Scratch can be preferred over WS. Second, the boundary’s position relative to the Chinchilla-optimal tokens/parameter front (solid black line) suggests that training for fewer tokens/parameter is more likely to retain WS gains. Importantly, this bound is predictive, subject to the quality of the fits.

These scaling-law fits and Iso-Loss Difference contours provide a wider picture that corroborates our initial hypothesis on the necessity of function-preservation for WS. In the MLP setup, Net2Net provides a clear front of WS gains with stronger final performance than Scratch. This is an expected result, as function-preservation was designed for MLPs. However, SZP provides a larger range of WS efficacy, with the loss-difference front pushed further from the Chinchilla-optimal front. As a contrasting case, for LLMs we observe that Net2Net shows only a small region of predicted gains over Scratch, consistent with the trends in Figure 4. For LLMs, SZP offers a reasonable front, indicating scaling trends for when WS becomes worse than Scratch training.

In summary, shorter training budgets (≤20​tokens/parameter\leq 20~\textit{tokens/parameter}{}) are more likely to yield WS gains over Scratch. For fair comparison of WS operations, the scaling pattern must be assessed and the front beyond which WS loses its advantage characterized, yielding prescriptive and predictive guidance.

5 Conclusion

We have characterized the upper bound on the growth factor within which Warmstarting (WS) remains computationally advantageous over training from Scratch. Beyond gupperg_{\text{upper}}, efficiency gains diminish significantly. Our analysis reveals that this limit is fundamentally tied to the training budget. We distill our contributions into a set of principled guidelines for the community:

We show that SZP (Shrink-Zero-Perturb), which augments zero-padding with a shrink-and-perturb strategy, is architecture-agnostic and more broadly applicable than function-preserving methods. SZP is easier to interpret, control, and study across architectures and setups, and our work here offers future WS literature both a framework to assess limits and make fairer comparisons of WS operations.

Limitations.

Our empirical study is constrained by the computational costs of language model training and the need for controlled comparisons. To reflect realistic scenarios where only model checkpoints are available, we treat the base model as a black box regarding its training history. While this distinguishes our work from studies that assume full access to base training compute (Liew and Kato 2025), it limits our ability to analyze the joint compute-optimal frontier across base size, target size, and tokens invested in both training phases. However, our findings at sub-11B scales align with trends reported at 11B+ scales in prior work, suggesting transferability to larger regimes. Additionally, exhaustive hyperparameter sweeps across all growth operators remain computationally prohibitive at the language model scale. Our comparison focuses on SZP vs. Net2Net to establish a representative baseline, with the understanding that SZP can serve as a modular alternative for width-scaling methods extending Net2Net. Especially, combining with depth-scaling (Du et al. 2024) for jointly growing both depth and width.

Future Directions.

Several promising avenues emerge from this work. First, benchmarking SZP across diverse architectures and data modalities would validate its generality beyond language models. Second, understanding the individual contributions of shrinking and perturbation within shrink-and-perturb could reveal whether these components can be predictively scaled using fixed rules or scaling law fits, and extending to WS of optimizers too. Third, given the existence of upper bounds on growth factors, exploring progressive growing and optimal growth schedules becomes a natural next step. Fourth, extending the scaling law to jointly model loss as a function of parameters, tokens, and growth factor would remove the need to fix the growth factor across experiments, enabling predictions for arbitrary growth factors from a single unified fit. More broadly, as specialized model checkpoints become increasingly available, strengthening the practical impact of warmstarting methods will be critical for improving resource efficiency across the field.

Appendix A Supporting Related Work

Scaled parameterizations refer to a set of rules that determine how certain parameters can be scaled with respect to one or more scaling dimensions [Everett et al. 2024]. This family of parameterizations describes scaling factors for the weights, the learning rate, and the standard deviation of the initialization. Each of these scaling variables can be defined separately for the input, hidden, and output layers (see Tables 1, 2, 333 3 and the μ​P\mu\text{P}{} Github page: https://github.com/microsoft/mup/blob/main/README.md in Yang and Hu 2021). The maximal update parameterization or μ​P\mu\text{P}{} is one such parameterization that showed its scaling rules to allow for stable training of larger networks without requiring re-tuning of the variables (such as learning rate) being scaled [Yang et al. 2021], i.e., feature learning does occur at infinite-width scaling [Yang and Hu 2021], extending the Neural Tangent Kernel [Jacot et al. 2018] theory demonstrating lazy-training (fixed features) when the network is scaled to infinite-width. Follow-up works exploited the symmetry in these parameterizations to provide alternative scaling rules [Blake et al. 2025], extended to meta learning [Thérien et al. 2024], and applied to newer optimizers [Essential AI et al. 2025]. Generally, in practice, a network where the learning rate is adequately tuned on a small-width network can be scaled using μ​P\mu\text{P}{} to be used as the learning rate for a larger width (theoretically, ∞\infty-width), and still exhibit stable training dynamics (no blowup of L1 norms of pre-activations or output logits). While μ\muP does not inherently predict loss values, its ability to preserve feature learning remains effective across diverse empirical settings [Vyas et al. 2023]. Consequently, hyperparameter transfer is well-aligned with the predictable loss trajectories observed in language model training [Dey et al. 2024, Bjorck et al. 2025].

Scaling laws formalize these trajectories by deriving parametric power-law fits of optimal loss against compute, dataset size, or parameter count [Kaplan et al. 2020, Hoffmann et al. 2022, Sorscher et al. 2022, Alabdulmohsin et al. 2023, Caballero et al. 2023]. Given a pretraining dataset and model architecture type, multiple models are trained varying the number of tokens seen, the model hyperparameters (including batch size), and the model sizes (width-depth). The resulting Pareto front characterizes the loss-compute trade-off, which is approximately linear on log-log axes and is commonly modeled with a power-law fit. This fit can then be used to predict the tokens required for compute-optimal loss given a fixed model, or the model size required for compute-optimal loss given a fixed token set to process. Chinchilla [Hoffmann et al. 2022] notably showed that this fit is sensitive to the hyperparameter choices, e.g., the length of cosine decay in the learning rate schedule. Subsequent works have looked at such a role of hyperparameters when scaling models [Porian et al. 2024], and also the suitability of certain hyperparameters to allow for compute reuse while scaling [Hägele et al. 2024]. Often, the role of optimal hyperparameters is highlighted as crucial but inadequately reported. Hyperparameter transfer methods become a key ingredient of a scaling recipe, and also influence novel observations for the scaling regime, such as scaling collapse [Qiu et al. 2025]. Bergsma et al. 2025 look at how μ​P\mu\text{P}, collapsed scaling curves, and novel parametric forms for scaling relationships together can be leveraged for early stopping learning curves.

B.4 Details on μ​Parameterization\mu\text{Parameterization}

Changing the width of a neural network changes not only its parameter count, but also the effective scale of activations, gradients, logits, and parameter updates. Therefore, hyperparameters tuned at one width need not transfer reliably to another. This creates a practical confounder for WS experiments: if each target width is retuned independently, improvements may come from better target-scale hyperparameters rather than from the growth operator itself.

μ​Parameterization\mu\text{Parameterization} [Yang et al. 2021] addresses this issue by prescribing width-dependent initialization and learning-rate scalings. Its goal is to keep models of different widths in a comparable feature-learning regime, so that the effect of parameter updates on activations remains stable as width changes. Equivalently, μ​P\mu\text{P}{} can be viewed as a hyperparameter-transfer rule from a small base model to a larger target model.

Given a base-width configuration

hn0=(η,λwd,B,wwarmup,wcooldown,…),h_{n_{0}}=(\eta,\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}},B,w_{\mathrm{warmup}},w_{\mathrm{cooldown}},\ldots),

we obtain the target-width configuration by

hn=Tμ​P​(hn0,n0,n),h_{n}=T_{\mu\text{P}}(h_{n_{0}};n_{0},n),

where Tμ​PT_{\mu\text{P}{}} applies parameter-type-specific scaling rules to initialization and optimizer hyperparameters. The main rules are summarized in Table 3. The key point is that different tensors can have different width dependencies: input weights, hidden weights, readout weights, embeddings, normalization parameters, and attention-logit scaling are not all treated in the same way. In practice, we specify base shapes for the model, identify which dimensions grow with width, and apply the corresponding μ​P\mu\text{P}{} optimizer rules.

We use μ​P\mu\text{P}{} only as a practical mechanism for hyperparameter transfer. In the synthetic MLP benchmark, we can compare it directly against dense target-width tuning and against static transfer, where the best base-width hyperparameters are reused unchanged. In Figure 2, stars denote μ​P\mu\text{P} transfer and open circles denote static transfer. For language modeling, full target-scale sweeps are too expensive, so we tune at the smallest relevant scale and transfer with μ​P\mu\text{P}{} throughout.

Appendix C Design Intuition and Ablations for SZP

This appendix complements the empirical setup in Section 4.1 by analyzing the main design choices in SZP. The method combines three simple ingredients: zero-padding, perturbation, and shrinkage. Zero-padding embeds the trained base model into the larger model, perturbation activates the newly added parameters, and shrinkage reduces the dominance of the copied base-model path. We first give a simple theoretical intuition for these roles, then provide a mechanistic interpretability analysis of their effect on weight statistics during training, and finally ablate the two nontrivial design choices: the perturbation scale and the shrinkage factor.

C.1 Theoretical Intuition for SZP

Zero-padding as an Exact Embedding.

Consider a feed-forward network with hidden states

hℓ+1=ϕ⁡(Wℓ​hℓ+bℓ),h_{\ell+1}=\phi(W_{\ell}h_{\ell}+b_{\ell}),

where ϕ⁡(0)=0\phi(0)=0. Let P⁡(θ)P(\theta) denote the width expansion from width nn to width m>nm>n by adding new neurons and setting every parameter touching them to zero. If we embed each hidden state as

h~ℓ=[hℓ0],\tilde{h}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{\ell}\\ 0\end{bmatrix},

then the widened layer acts as

h~ℓ+1=ϕ⁡([Wℓ000]​[hℓ0]+[bℓ0])=[ϕ⁡(Wℓ​hℓ+bℓ)0]=[hℓ+10].\tilde{h}_{\ell+1}=\phi\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}W_{\ell}&0\\ 0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}h_{\ell}\\ 0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}b_{\ell}\\ 0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(W_{\ell}h_{\ell}+b_{\ell})\\ 0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{\ell+1}\\ 0\end{bmatrix}.

By induction over layers,

FP⁡(θ)​(x)=fθ​(x)for all ​x.F_{P(\theta)}(x)=f_{\theta}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x.

Thus, in this idealized unnormalized setting, zero-padding preserves the pretrained function exactly. For normalized architectures such as Transformers with LayerNorm or RMSNorm, exact function preservation may additionally require coordinated rescaling of normalization parameters or adjacent weights – without this, zero-padding should be viewed as an embedding intuition rather than a strict function-preserving operation.

However, this exact embedding is also restrictive. Let

ℳ:={P⁡(θ):θ∈Θsmall}\mathcal{M}:=\{P(\theta):\theta\in\Theta_{\mathrm{small}}\}

be the embedded small-model manifold. At points in ℳ\mathcal{M}, each newly added neuron has zero activation and zero outgoing weight. By the chain rule of backpropagation, because the outgoing weights are exactly zero, the error signal propagated back to these new neurons is zero. Consequently, the gradients with respect to all newly added parameters vanish, and under standard gradient descent,

∇Θnewℒ​(P⁡(θ))=0.\nabla_{\Theta_{\mathrm{new}}}\mathcal{L}(P(\theta))=0.

Thus, pure zero-padding can keep optimization on the embedded small-model manifold, preventing the wider model from using its additional capacity.

The SZP initialization can be written as

Θ0=λshrink​P​(θ⋆)+E,0<λshrink≤1,\Theta_{0}=\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}}P(\theta^{\star})+E,\qquad 0<\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}}\leq 1,

where θ⋆\theta^{\star} is the pretrained base-model solution and EE is a perturbation.

For one widened layer, partition old and new coordinates and write the input as

h~=[hhnew],\tilde{h}=\begin{bmatrix}h\\ h_{\mathrm{new}}\end{bmatrix},

where hnew=0h_{\mathrm{new}}=0 for pure zero-padding and hnew=O⁡(‖E‖)h_{\mathrm{new}}=O(\|E\|) after perturbations from preceding layers. The following calculation keeps only first-order terms in the perturbation magnitude. Let the perturbation matrix and bias vector be conformably partitioned into blocks corresponding to the old (o) and new (n) dimensions:

Shrinkage plays a complementary role. It reduces the copied old-path signal from ‖W​h+b‖\|Wh+b\| to approximately λshrink​‖W​h+b‖\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}}\|Wh+b\|, while the new-path signal remains controlled by the perturbation.

Crucially, a larger relative preactivation ensures that the newly added weights generate correspondingly larger gradients during backpropagation, actively pulling the optimization trajectory away from the restrictive manifold ℳ\mathcal{M}. In summary, zero-padding preserves the pretrained model, perturbation activates the new dimensions, and shrinkage accelerates adaptation by breaking the optimization dominance of the inherited narrow solution.

C.2 Interpretability

In Figure 7 (left), we trace weight statistics for SZP vs. Zeros+Perturb (SZP without shrinking) across the input-output layers and the middle layer’s attention and MLP matrices. The CL literature highlights shrinking’s role in preserving the learned function via weight-norm clamping, which we observe here and correlate with improved plasticity [Lyle et al. 2023, Clare 2025] and loss reduction (Figure 1). As a more informative proxy than L1L_{1} or L2L_{2}, we track the R​M​S→L∞RMS\to L_{\infty} operator norm — the maximum row-wise L2L_{2} norm scaled by the square root of the input width — which is crucial for transferring learning dynamics across scales [Filatov et al. 2025, Pethick et al. 2025]. Shrinking also improves utilization of new neurons, evidenced by the larger effective rank of the attention layer, and enables a larger shift from initialization (‖Wt−W0‖2||W_{t}-W_{0}||_{2}), letting the grown model move further from its base weights. This matters because zero-padding or cloning alone may not break sufficient symmetry between old and new neurons [Yu et al. 2026], leaving the grown model too close to the base in function space, which manifests as loss saturation in Figure 1.

C.3 Perturbation Scale Ablation

Perturbation is used to activate the newly added neurons after zero-padding. To study its sensitivity, we sweep the perturbation scale σperturb\sigma_{\mathrm{perturb}} on a 32​M→286​M32\mathrm{M}\rightarrow 286\mathrm{M} transfer, keeping the remaining SZP settings fixed. The default setting uses

σperturb=1width.\sigma_{\mathrm{perturb}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{width}}}.

Table 4 reports the final validation loss for each perturbation scale, while Figure 8 shows the corresponding learning curves. Without perturbation, performance is substantially worse, suggesting that pure zero-padding does not effectively activate the added capacity. Nonzero perturbations improve both convergence and final loss, with the default σperturb=1/width\sigma_{\mathrm{perturb}}=1/\sqrt{\mathrm{width}} matching the best final performance in this sweep. The learning curves further show that the default reaches the low-loss region earlier than most fixed perturbation scales, which supports using it as a simple scale-aware choice rather than tuning σperturb\sigma_{\mathrm{perturb}} separately for each transfer.

C.4 Shrinkage Ablation

Shrinkage controls the strength of the copied base-model path in SZP. In the initialization Θ0=λshrink​P​(θ⋆)+E\Theta_{0}=\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}}P(\theta^{\star})+E, smaller values of λshrink\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}} reduce the contribution of the inherited narrow solution, while the perturbation EE activates the newly added coordinates. As discussed in Section C.1, this makes it easier for the new neurons to compete with the copied subnetwork after growth.

This role of shrinkage is consistent with recent work on plasticity in warm-started networks. DASH [Shin et al. 2024] also uses shrinkage to mitigate plasticity loss, but does so in a direction-aware manner: weights associated with useful learned features are preserved more strongly, while components attributed to memorized noise are shrunk more aggressively. In contrast, SZP intentionally uses a single global shrinkage factor, making it architecture-agnostic and easy to combine with arbitrary growth operators. Relatedly, weight clipping has been proposed as a simple way to control weight magnitudes and improve plasticity in continual and reinforcement learning settings [Elsayed et al. 2024]. Our use of shrinkage can be viewed as a one-shot initialization-time analogue of this broader norm-control principle.

We ablate λshrink\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}} while keeping the remaining SZP settings fixed. Figure 9 reports final validation loss across three base checkpoints, three token budgets, and multiple target scales. Across settings, the best values are concentrated in the low-to-moderate shrinkage regime, typically around λshrink∈{0.2,0.4}\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}}\in\{0.2,0.4\}. Setting λshrink\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}} too close to 11 corresponds to little or no shrinkage, leaving the grown model more dominated by the inherited base-model path. Conversely, setting it too close to 00 removes too much of the pretrained signal and makes the initialization closer to perturbation-only growth. The broad minimum around 0.20.2–0.40.4 suggests that shrinkage is useful, but does not require precise tuning.

We use λshrink=0.4\lambda_{\mathrm{shrink}}=0.4 in the main experiments as a stable default in this low-loss region. This choice is also consistent with the post-hoc analysis in Figure 7: compared with perturbation alone, adding shrinkage improves effective rank and permits larger movement from initialization, indicating that the grown model better uses its added capacity rather than remaining anchored to the base model.

Appendix D Language Modeling Task Details

D.1 Model Scales

Table 5 summarizes the language-model scales used across our experiments.

In this work, we focus only on width-scaling, specifically the setting where the head size, i.e., the output dimension of each attention head, remains fixed across scales. Equivalently, this form of width-scaling adds more heads of the same size to each layer. This approach is standard in the literature, especially when studying feature learning under width-scaling [Everett et al. 2024]. We note that this form of width-scaling provides the fairest setup for comparing WS methods. The query, key, and value weights are grouped per head within a tensor.

For a simple mental illustration, a 11-head network with width 11 will have an attention tensor with 33 dimensions, one each for query, key, and value, assuming no weight sharing. When grown to width 22 by scaling the head dimension, the flattened attention tensor has 66 dimensions, 22 each for query, key, and value. This introduces a subtle layout inefficiency: under index-wise growth, the second dimension of the query block in the larger model corresponds to the key dimension of the smaller model. This correspondence is maintained cleanly when width-scaling is implemented by increasing only the number of heads while keeping the head size fixed.

For the subset overlapping with Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard v1, we follow the corresponding fixed few-shot settings: ARC-Challenge 2525-shot, HellaSwag 1010-shot, MMLU 55-shot, and WinoGrande 55-shot [Hugging Face 2024a, Hugging Face 2024b]. We additionally include OpenBookQA and PIQA as standard LightEval multiple-choice tasks in the 00-shot setting. Across all six benchmarks, SZP improves over the corresponding Scratch baseline, suggesting that the optimization advantage observed in the pretraining curves carries over to downstream behavior.

Since these results are based on a single checkpoint per method and do not include multiple training seeds or statistical significance testing, we interpret them as a sanity check rather than a definitive downstream-performance claim.

Appendix G Net2Net

Net2Net [Chen et al. 2016] is a seminal function-preserving growth method: it expands a trained base network into a larger target network by duplicating/splitting hidden units (width) and/or inserting identity layers (depth), such that the target initially computes (approximately) the same function as the base. In recent standardized evaluations of model growth for LLM pre-training, a Net2Net-style direct width-expansion operator is used as the canonical function-preserving width-growth baseline (often referred to as a “direct” growth operator) [Du et al. 2024]. We therefore adopt Net2Net as our primary width warmstarting baseline.

G.1 Why Net2Net?

We use Net2Net [Chen et al. 2016] because it (1) targets width expansion, which is the focus of this work, (2) is (approximately) function-preserving at initialization, and (3) requires no changes to the training objective or additional optimization stages. This makes it a clean and widely used baseline for isolating the effect of the warmstarted initialization. Moreover, in the large-scale growth benchmark of Du et al. 2024, the Net2Net-style direct widening operator is the strongest width-expansion baseline among the compared growth operators, motivating its use as our primary width warmstarting baseline.

Why not other Methods?

We exclude several alternative strategies by design. Learned growth and distillation-based methods (for example, linear transformations [Wang et al. 2023a], progressive module training [Gong et al. 2019], or teacher–student frameworks [Qin et al. 2021]) introduce additional stages (e.g., auxiliary alignment losses or intermediate supervision), which makes it harder to attribute gains specifically to initialization quality. Importantly, our method could be composed with such techniques; however, such extensions are outside the scope of this work.

Intuition.

Width growth in Net2Net can be viewed as splitting a hidden unit into several identical copies, then rescaling the outgoing weights so that the combined contribution of the copies matches the original unit. Noise for Symmetry Breaking.

Because duplicated units start identically, we optionally add small Gaussian noise to the newly created parameters (or to a subset of duplicated rows/columns) to break symmetry, following standard Net2Net practice [Chen et al. 2016]. In our experiments this noise is small enough to keep the initialization close to function-preserving while avoiding perfectly identical copies.

G.3 Implementation in our Experiments

MLPs.

In the synthetic MLP experiments, we apply the widening rule above to each hidden layer: we duplicate hidden units according to π\pi and rescale the corresponding outgoing weights by the copy counts, optionally injecting small Gaussian noise for symmetry breaking.

Transformers.

In the transformer language-model experiments, we apply the same function-preserving principle when expanding width. Concretely, we grow matrices whose inner dimensions scale with model width (e.g., token embeddings and the projection matrices in attention/MLP blocks) via the same “copy columns / copy rows + rescale” recipe in Equation 8. For multi-head attention, because we keep the head size fixed and grow width by adding heads, copying is performed at the level of complete heads: duplicated WqW_{q}, WkW_{k}, and WvW_{v} rows are left unscaled, while the copy-count rescaling is applied only to the corresponding WoW_{o} columns. This preserves the duplicated heads’ softmax computation while averaging their combined contribution through the output projection. This yields an initialization that matches the base model computation as closely as possible at the moment of expansion, while remaining a drop-in replacement that does not modify the objective or training loop.

All comparisons use identical token budgets and optimization settings to isolate the effect of initialization.

G.4 Scope and Follow-up Work

While our study focuses on width-only growth, Net2Net [Chen et al. 2016] also proposes depth-expanding transformations (e.g., inserting identity layers). In transformers, bert2BERT [Chen et al. 2021] extends Net2Net-style ideas to BERT, and combines them with layer stacking to grow depth. More broadly, depth expansion can be paired with transformer-specific stacking strategies, such as those explored in Stacking Your Transformers [Du et al. 2024].

Our empirical study isolates width scaling (following Everett et al. 2024) because our emphasis on hyperparameter transfer and scaling behavior requires extensive sweeps across widths, training horizons, and transfer settings. Nevertheless, extending our warmstarting analysis to depth is a natural next step, and should be compatible with depth-stacking mechanisms and depth-aware scaling rules (e.g., depth μ​P\mu\text{P} variants and recipe-based transfers such as completeP [Dey et al. 2025]). Ultimately, this points toward studying warmstarting under compound scaling; we first establish controlled evidence for hyperparameter transfer under width growth [Everett et al. 2024, Dey et al. 2025, Essential AI et al. 2025].

Further Applications.

So far, we have used scaling-law fits to characterize when warmstarting is effective compared with training from scratch. The same approach can also be used for comparing Warmstarting methods against each other. In Figure 18, we compare SZP and Net2Net by plotting isolines of the difference between their fitted loss surfaces across model sizes and training budgets.
