Abstract

Diffusion language models (DLMs) are promising alternatives to autoregressive language models (ARMs), yet the intrinsic differences in their generated text remain underexplored. We first find empirically that off-the-shelf DLMs exhibit lower nn-gram entropy, higher semantic coherence, and higher semantic diversity. To understand the cause, we conduct controlled experiments that decouple the effects of training objectives and decoding algorithms. Results suggest that the DLM training objective contributes to the increases in semantic coherence and semantic diversity, but has a minor influence on entropy. These differences are primarily driven by the bidirectional context; other components in the training objective, such as input masking, label masking, and the weighting function, have a much weaker influence. Further, our experiments demonstrate that the reduction in entropy stems from DLMs’ decoding algorithms, particularly confidence-based remasking strategies. We provide a theoretical understanding for this entropy reduction phenomenon. Together, our work uncovers key mechanisms underlying the differences between DLMs and ARMs in text generation, and informs future design of training objectives and decoding algorithms in DLMs.11 1 Our code is available at https://github.com/imzzy1201/DifferenceDiffusionAutoregressive.

1 Introduction

Diffusion language models (DLMs) have recently emerged as promising alternatives to autoregressive language models (ARMs) for natural language generation (28; 52). They offer distinct advantages, such as higher data efficiency and faster text generation (40; 22; 29). With comparable model sizes and training budgets, DLMs achieve performance competitive with ARMs across various tasks, such as language understanding, mathematical reasoning, and code generation (48; 22; 70).

However, beyond comparisons on efficiency and benchmark performance, the differences in the intrinsic properties of text generated by DLMs and ARMs remain underexplored. In this work, we evaluate the generated texts using three metrics: entropy, semantic coherence, and semantic diversity (defined in §3.2), which capture text properties at the token, sentence, and document levels, respectively. As shown in Figure 1, our evaluation of 20 off-the-shelf DLMs and ARMs reveals that DLMs tend to produce texts with lower nn-gram entropy, higher semantic coherence, and higher semantic diversity (nn-gram entropy serves as a proxy for full entropy; see §3.3 for details). These findings provide insights into how the generation behaviors of DLMs and ARMs differ.

We conduct controlled experiments to isolate and analyze the mechanisms underlying these differences. Unlike ARMs, DLMs model the language distribution through mask prediction from noised inputs pθ​(x|xnoised)p_{\theta}(x|x_{\text{noised}}) (3; 46; 44). Moreover, DLMs employ a variety of flexible decoding strategies (36; 10; 57; 6; 66). Consequently, both the training objective and the decoding algorithm could contribute to the observed metric differences.

To decouple their effects, we perform two sets of experiments: (1) on the training objectives, comparing DLMs and ARMs under a shared decoding setup; and (2) on the decoding algorithms, comparing various decoding strategies within DLMs. To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

  • •

    We evaluate the generation behaviors of 20 off-the-shelf DLMs and ARMs on nn-gram entropy, semantic coherence, and semantic diversity, providing insights into the differences between their generated corpora (§3.3).

  • •

    Through controlled experiments on training objectives, we find that the DLM training objective contributes to the increase in semantic coherence and semantic diversity, but minimally affects entropy. By interpolating the objectives between DLMs and ARMs, we identify bidirectional context as the dominant factor responsible for these increments, whereas other components, such as input masking, label masking, or weighting functions, have a much smaller effect (§4).

  • •

    Through detailed experiments and analyses on decoding algorithms, we demonstrate that the choice of remasking strategy is critical. Specifically, DLMs’ entropy drops under confidence-based remasking strategies, resulting in lower entropy than ARMs; under other strategies, DLMs’ entropy remains approximately unchanged and stays at ARMs’ level. We also provide theoretical understanding for this effect (§5).

2 Related work

Diffusion language models.

Inspired by the success of diffusion models in image generation (43; 19), early work establishes a viable path from continuous to discrete domains, introducing diffusion over discrete tokens for language modeling (3; 31). Building on subsequent theoretical developments (46; 37; 44), recent efforts scale these methods to large language models, such as LLaDA (36) and Dream (66), demonstrating strong scalability and promising reasoning capabilities.

To further improve efficiency, various approaches are proposed, including autoregressive initialization and block-wise diffusion (58; 66; 16; 10; 57). Concurrently, the flexible decoding order of DLMs motivates specialized decoding strategies, including random selection, confidence-based or entropy-based remasking (8; 36; 23; 66; 5), dynamic scheduling (58; 10; 57; 6), and other related techniques.

Comparison between DLMs and ARMs.

Prior works comparing DLMs and ARMs can be organized into three aspects: training objective and dynamics, decoding mechanism, and downstream task performance.

In terms of training objective and dynamics, prior works examine how different training formulations shape model behavior, such as in representation structure, generalization, and robustness. Specifically, DLMs may exhibit early-layer redundancy and reduced recency bias (15), achieve higher data efficiency (40; 14; 33), and mitigate the reversal curse (25; 47).

Regarding decoding mechanism, DLMs enable flexible, any-order generation and theoretically support more efficient parallel sampling (26; 21). However, empirical results show that these advantages are not always realized in practice (65; 34), as performance could be influenced by decoding strategies. Other works discover that DLMs often determine their answers at early decoding stages (27), and can be halted early (53). Diffusion-specific safety issues are also explored (41; 69; 56).

For downstream task performance, prior works generally find that DLMs are competitive with similarly sized ARMs (35; 28), and that DLMs show advantages in tasks requiring bidirectional reasoning or global context understanding (67; 55; 60). Further, some studies also demonstrate trade-offs between generation quality and generation speed (13; 11).

Overall, existing studies primarily characterize differences in efficiency and downstream task performance, leaving the intrinsic properties of the generated text itself underexplored.

3 Preliminaries and results for off-the-shelf models

3.1 Diffusion language models

Let 𝒱\mathcal{V} be the vocabulary. A text sequence of length LL is denoted by x∈𝒱Lx\in\mathcal{V}^{L}, where xix^{i} is the ii-th token. We denote by 𝒟data\mathcal{D}_{\text{data}} the data distribution over 𝒱L\mathcal{V}^{L}.

DLMs learn to reconstruct clean text from noised inputs by predicting the ground-truth tokens for randomly masked positions (3; 36):

ℒ(θ)=−𝔼x0,t,xt[ωt∑i=1L𝟏[xti=MASK]logpθ(x0i∣xt)].\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},t,x_{t}}\left[\omega_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=\texttt{MASK}]\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t})\right]. (1)

Here, a clean sequence x0∼𝒟datax_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{data}} is noised into xtx_{t} by converting each token to MASK token with probability tt, where t∼Uniform​(0,1)t\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1) represents the noise level. A weighting function ωt\omega_{t}, commonly set to 1/t1/t, balances the loss across noise levels. The objective trains the model to recover the original token x0ix_{0}^{i} at each masked position in xtx_{t}.

Unlike the left-to-right sequential decoding of ARMs, DLMs start generation with a sequence of MASK tokens, iteratively refining it into actual text. A common practice is block-wise decoding (2), where the sequence is divided into equal-length blocks that are refined consecutively. We focus on this setting.

Within each block, the model performs multiple denoising steps to gradually decode all masked positions before moving to the next block. At each step, the model predicts a token for every masked position in the block, then remasks a subset of them, i.e., reverting them to MASK, before proceeding to the next step. The choice of which positions to remask is determined by a remasking strategy. Assume a block length of BB and a target number of NN denoising steps. In this work, we investigate four commonly used remasking strategies:

  • •

    Low-confidence remasking (8; 36): This strategy remasks tokens with the lowest confidence values, i.e., their predicted probabilities, leaving exactly B/NB/N most confident tokens to be decoded at each step.

  • •

    Dynamic low-confidence remasking (10; 57; 6): At each step, this strategy first attempts to remask tokens with confidence lower than τ\tau. If the number of decoded tokens does not reach the target B/NB/N, it falls back to low-confidence remasking. Consequently, the total number of decoding steps for a block may be fewer than NN, since more than B/NB/N tokens may be decoded in a single step.

  • •

    High-entropy remasking (66): This strategy evaluates the entropy of the predicted token distribution at each position, and remasks positions with the highest entropy values so that exactly B/NB/N tokens are decoded at each step.

  • •

    Random remasking (36): This strategy uniformly randomly selects tokens to remask so that exactly B/NB/N tokens are decoded at each step.

We refer to the first two approaches collectively as confidence-based remasking strategies, as they select remasking positions based on the confidence values of the predicted tokens.

3.2 Metrics for comparing generated text

We aim to understand the different generation behaviors of DLMs and ARMs across multiple granularities, progressing from the smallest token level, to the intermediate sentence level, and finally to the overall document level. Accordingly, we adopt three metrics, entropy, semantic coherence, and semantic diversity. The underlying intuition motivating this choice is that each metric captures how the generated corpus “disperses” at its corresponding granularity. Specifically, higher entropy, lower semantic coherence, and higher semantic diversity reflect more dispersed generation behaviors at the token, sentence, and document levels, respectively.

3.3 Evaluation on off-the-shelf models

To establish a practical foundation for our work and motivate our subsequent analysis, we begin by evaluating the generation behaviors of off-the-shelf DLMs and ARMs across the three metrics in §3.2. We compare 10 DLMs and 10 ARMs from diverse model families, with comparable model sizes ranging from 3B to 8B (listed in Appendix A). To estimate these metrics, we sample texts from these models on 1000 different prompts from the Fineweb dataset (39), using their default sampling configurations.

Regarding entropy, the non-sequential generation nature of DLMs prevents computing the log probability of a sequence; thus, we adopt nn-gram entropy as a proxy (45). To evaluate semantic coherence and semantic diversity, we extract embeddings using bge-m3 (9). As shown in Figure 1, DLMs on average exhibit lower trigram entropy, higher semantic coherence, and higher semantic diversity than ARMs. Unigram and bigram entropy exhibit the same trends as trigram entropy. See Appendix A for more details.

While the results on off-the-shelf models reveal clear trends, there is no strict control over their training setups, which may compromise the robustness of the observed phenomena and hinder rigorous analysis. Therefore, in §4 and §5, we train models from scratch and conduct controlled experiments to isolate and analyze the mechanisms underlying these differences. Since DLMs and ARMs differ in both their training objectives and decoding algorithms, we decouple these effects through two sets of experiments: in §4, we compare the training objectives under a shared decoding scheme; in §5, we evaluate various decoding strategies on the same model.

4 Controlled experiments on training objectives

As initially observed in off-the-shelf models (Figure 1), DLMs and ARMs exhibit distinct generation behaviors. We first investigate whether these differences are intrinsically driven by their training objectives.

4.1 Decomposition of objective components

To analyze the intrinsic effects of training objectives, we begin by decomposing each objective into its constituent components. Recall from §3.1 that the objective of DLMs is

ℒDLM(θ)≔−𝔼x0,t,xt[ωt∑i=1L𝟏[xti=MASK]logpθ(x0i∣xt)],where ωt=1/t.\mathcal{L}_{\text{DLM}}(\theta)\coloneqq-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},t,x_{t}}\left[\omega_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=\texttt{MASK}]\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t})\right],\quad\text{where }\omega_{t}=1/t. (5)

We can express ARM’s training objective in a similar format:

ℒARM​(θ)≔−𝔼x0​[∑i=1Llog⁡pθ​(x0i∣x0<i)].\mathcal{L}_{\text{ARM}}(\theta)\coloneqq-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{0}^{<i})\right]. (6)

The differences between these two objectives can be decomposed into four components:

  • •

    Input masking. DLMs take the noised sequence xinput=xtx_{\text{input}}=x_{t} as input, while ARMs take the raw sequence xinput=x0x_{\text{input}}=x_{0}.

  • •

    Label masking. DLMs accumulate the loss only over masked tokens (𝟏[xti=MASK]\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=\texttt{MASK}]), while ARMs accumulate it over all tokens.

  • •

    Weighting function. The diffusion process of DLMs yields a time-dependent weight (ωt=1/t\omega_{t}=1/t), whereas ARMs use a constant weight.

  • •

    Context scope. DLMs condition on bidirectional context pθ​(x0i∣xinput)p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{\text{input}}), whereas ARMs condition on unidirectional context pθ​(x0i∣xinput<i)p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{\text{input}}^{<i}).

To investigate how each individual component affects the resulting model, we design six intermediate training objectives, as detailed in Figure 2. See Appendix B.1 for more details on the choice of training objectives. These objectives serve as an interpolation between the ARM objective and the DLM objective. Starting from the ARM objective, we gradually change each component until we obtain the DLM objective. Consequently, by observing at which step the differences in metrics emerge, we can isolate the influence of specific components.

For instance, the uc+im objective in Figure 2 takes a randomly masked prefix xt<ix_{t}^{<i} as input and is trained to predict the ground truth x0ix_{0}^{i} at all positions, using the loss −𝔼x0,t,xt​[∑i=1Llog⁡pθ​(x0i∣xt<i)]-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},t,x_{t}}[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{<i})]. Building on this, the uc+im+lm objective trains on x0ix_{0}^{i} only for xti=MASKx_{t}^{i}=\texttt{MASK}, utilizing the loss −𝔼x0,t,xt[∑i=1L𝟏[xti=MASK]logpθ(x0i∣xt<i)]-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},t,x_{t}}[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=\texttt{MASK}]\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{<i})].

4.2 Experimental setup

We train eight models from scratch, each optimized with one of the training objectives defined in Figure 2. All models use an identical 120M-parameter LLaMA architecture (50). Each model is trained on the Fineweb (39) dataset for 2B tokens using a context length of 512. Each experiment is repeated with three different random seeds.

For evaluation, to isolate the effect of decoding algorithms, we adopt a unified decoding procedure across all models. Specifically, we evaluate every model using standard sequential autoregressive decoding. For DLMs, this setup is equivalent to setting the decoding block length to one. At each checkpoint, we evaluate the models by generating 512 sequences, each 512 tokens long, conditioned on distinct 32-token prompts sampled from the evaluation split of the dataset.

Due to the sequential decoding, we can directly estimate entropy by autoregressive factorization:

ℋ⁡(p𝒢)=𝔼(c,x)∼p𝒢​[1L​∑i=1Llog⁡p𝒢​(xi|x<i,c)].\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathcal{G}})=\mathbb{E}_{(c,x)\sim p_{\mathcal{G}}}\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log p_{\mathcal{G}}(x^{i}|x^{<i},c)\right]. (7)

To ensure robustness, we evaluate both semantic coherence and semantic diversity using four different embedding models: bge-m3 (9), embeddinggemma-300m (54), all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (42), and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (68). See Appendix B.2 for more details on experimental setups.

4.3 Results

To ensure a fair comparison, we evaluate the models at checkpoints that achieve the same next-token prediction loss on the validation set (see Appendix B.2 for details). Since all models are restricted to sequential generation, this loss is exactly the cross-entropy between each model’s generation distribution and the data distribution, and thus reflects generation quality. Consequently, this alignment ensures that models trained with different objectives have equivalent capabilities.

Under this controlled setup, we observe a clear clustering effect across the metrics (Figure 2). The eight models are divided into two distinct groups. The cluster containing the DLM objective consistently achieves higher semantic coherence and higher semantic diversity than the cluster containing the ARM objective. This matches the trends in Figure 1. Meanwhile, we observe no significant difference in entropy across the evaluated models.

We attribute this two-cluster separation to the difference in context scope: those utilizing bidirectional context form the cluster with higher semantic coherence and higher semantic diversity, while those using unidirectional context form the other. This separation suggests that bidirectional context is the dominant factor driving these differences, whereas input masking, label masking, and the weighting function exert a much weaker influence.

Specifically, we emphasize two pairs of experiments in Figure 2, where each pair differs only in context scope: (uc+im+lm, bc+im+lm) and (uc+im+lm+wf, bc+im+lm+wf). For each pair, the two experiments show a large gap in semantic coherence and semantic diversity, indicating the dominant effect of bidirectional context.

Last but not least, we verify that all phenomena in this section are robust across different datasets (Fineweb and TinyStories) and architectures (LLaMA and Qwen2). See Appendix B.3 for details.

5 Analyses on decoding algorithms

Apart from left-to-right decoding, DLMs can generate tokens in the order guided by an arbitrary remasking strategy, as mentioned in §3.1. At the same time, the experiments in §4 show that the training objective alone does not lead to an entropy difference between DLMs and ARMs, while Figure 1 points to potentially lower entropy in DLMs. We therefore investigate the influence of remasking strategies on the generation behaviors of DLMs.

5.1 Experimental setup

We use sequential decoding as a baseline to evaluate the influence of these remasking strategies. Our evaluation builds on the configuration from §4.2, using the final checkpoints of models trained in §4 with the ℒDLM\mathcal{L}_{\text{DLM}} objective, modifying only the decoding algorithm. For all strategies, we set the target number of denoising steps per block NN equal to the block length BB, which targets decoding one token per step. For the dynamic low-confidence remasking strategy, following 10, we set the confidence threshold to τ=0.9\tau=0.9. We evaluate three block lengths: 2, 8, and 32. Following §4, we conduct ablations across seeds, datasets and architectures. They are detailed in Appendix C.3.

5.2 Results

Entropy reduction phenomenon.

Following §3.3, we use nn-gram entropy to approximate full entropy. As shown in Figure 3, confidence-based strategies consistently lower nn-gram entropy compared to sequential decoding, whereas the other strategies have a comparatively minor influence. This suggests that confidence-based decoding reduces entropy.

To verify this, we establish the following inequality chain, comparing the full entropy under a confidence-based decoding strategy ℋ⁡(pcon)\mathcal{H}(p_{\text{con}}) with that under sequential decoding ℋ⁡(pseq)\mathcal{H}(p_{\text{seq}}):

for some non-negligible δ>0\delta>0, where ℋ⁡(pcon)\mathcal{H}(p_{\text{con}}) and ℋ⁡(pseq)\mathcal{H}(p_{\text{seq}}) are defined in Eq.(2), and the intermediate term denotes the cross-entropy:

As shown in Figure 3, for confidence-based strategies, the cross-entropy ℋ⁡(pcon,pseq)\mathcal{H}(p_{\text{con}},p_{\text{seq}}) consistently falls far below the sequential decoding entropy ℋ⁡(pseq)\mathcal{H}(p_{\text{seq}}). Understanding of entropy reduction.

One possible explanation for this entropy reduction is a distributional bias. In theory, even for a perfectly trained DLM, confidence-based decoding yields a biased generation distribution.

As mentioned in §3.1, confidence-based strategies first sample from the predicted distribution, then select remasking positions based on the confidence values of these sampling outcomes. By contrast, other strategies select positions based solely on the predicted distribution (i.e., by the distribution entropy or at random) before actual sampling occurs. Consequently, the outcome-dependence in confidence-based strategies introduces bias, whereas other strategies remain unbiased.

To further explore this explanation, we provide two supporting observations:

  • •

    Theoretical illustration under factorization assumption. To understand how this bias connects to entropy reduction, we first examine a single denoising step. Confidence-based strategies prefer positions with higher confidence values, i.e., predicted probabilities. As a result, they sharpen the predicted distribution by amplifying high probabilities and suppressing low ones, thereby reducing per-step entropy.

    Building upon this, we then formally prove that entropy reduction holds for the final generation under a simplified factorization assumption (Theorem 1).

  • •

    Bias elimination experiments. As an empirical validation, we apply a simple fix to the confidence-based strategy to eliminate this bias. After the strategy determines the positions to decode, we resample the tokens at these positions. This modification breaks the correlation between the remasking position selection and the sampled token outcomes, thereby eliminating the bias. As shown in Figure 3, this fix effectively restores the nn-gram entropy to a level similar to that of sequential decoding and invalidates inequality (ii). Details can be found in Appendix C.2.

Other influences.

We further evaluate how remasking strategies affect semantic coherence and semantic diversity. Our results show that confidence-based strategies are relatively unstable: while they consistently increase semantic coherence across our multiple experiments, their influence on semantic diversity is highly dataset-dependent. Specifically, in our main experiments on the Fineweb dataset, confidence-based strategies increase semantic diversity, but ablation experiments on the TinyStories dataset show decreases in semantic diversity, suggesting a relatively unpredictable behavior.

In contrast, other strategies have comparatively negligible effects on both metrics. For confidence-based strategies, applying bias elimination also removes their effects. These results suggest that the bias-based distinction may also determine whether a strategy affects semantic coherence and semantic diversity. See Appendix C.4 for more details.

6 Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we investigate the different generation behaviors of DLMs and ARMs by decoupling the effects of their training objectives and decoding procedures. Our control experiments identify two key mechanisms: First, the DLM training objective, particularly through the bidirectional context, drives the model toward higher semantic coherence and higher semantic diversity, while maintaining an entropy similar to that of ARMs. Second, the DLM decoding algorithms, specifically confidence-based remasking strategies, reduce entropy relative to autoregressive decoding.

It is also worth noting that our observations imply that DLMs can achieve higher semantic diversity at the same or lower entropy levels than ARMs. This suggests that DLMs may relax the conventional text generation trade-off between diversity and certainty, pointing to their potential for diverse yet controllable text generation. Together, our findings offer insights into the selection between DLMs and ARMs for different scenarios and may inform the design of training objectives and decoding algorithms in DLMs.

7 Limitation and future work

We conclude by outlining several limitations and directions for future work. Our experiments are limited to relatively small models; it remains to be verified that our findings generalize to larger models and datasets. Our theoretical analysis of the decoding procedure relies on several simplifying assumptions. Future work may relax these assumptions and extend the study to broader DLM architectures and decoding algorithms.

These models all have different architectures and training procedures, so adopting the default generation setting is a fair basis for comparison since it demonstrates how each model is “intended” to be used.

Data filtering and preprocessing. Since degenerate data severely interfere with our analysis, we filter out degenerate samples with extremely short length or excessively repeated content. The number of samples remaining after filtering is reported in Table 1.

Appendix B Additional details for controlled experiments on training objectives

B.1 Training objectives selection

We select all possible combinations of the four decomposed components listed in §4.1 subject to two rules. First, when bidirectional context is used, input masking and label masking are necessarily included: without them, the model would have access to the full clean sequence at both input and output, reducing the task to identity mapping rather than prediction. Second, the weighting function is only varied when label masking is present, since ωt=1/t\omega_{t}=1/t serves to normalize the loss magnitude. Specifically, it compensates for the fact that the number of loss terms (masked positions) is proportional to tt.

B.2 Experimental setup details

For the main experiments, we use a 120M-parameter LLaMA architecture (50), configured with a hidden size of 768, 12 attention heads, and 12 layers. We optimize all models using AdamW (30) optimizer with a global batch size of 1024 and a peak learning rate of 1e-3, which warms up over the first 100 steps and remains constant thereafter. Text generation is performed using standard sequential autoregressive decoding with a temperature of 1.01.0, without top-pp or top-kk sampling. This setup ensures that the generated outputs faithfully reflect the learned distribution, without introducing additional distortion.

As a control variable, we also evaluate the next-token prediction loss at each checkpoint. We compute it using a uniform procedure: sample x∼𝒟datax\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{data}}; for each i∈{2,…,L}i\in\{2,\dots,L\}, mask all tokens xjx^{j} with j≥ij\geq i to obtain x~\tilde{x}; then feed x~\tilde{x} into the model and compute the negative log likelihood of predicting xix^{i} from x~\tilde{x}. Averaging this quantity over ii and then taking expectation over xx gives the next-token prediction loss used in evaluation.

B.3 Robustness concern

As an additional robustness check, we repeat the main controlled experiments in §4 with three random seeds. Apart from the main experiments, we also verify the results across different model architectures and datasets. For the architecture ablation, we train another set of models using a 30M-parameter Qwen2 architecture (63), configured with a hidden size of 512, 8 attention heads, and 8 layers. Other hyperparameters remain the same as those in §4. For the dataset ablation, we train a set of models on the TinyStories dataset (12) for 5 epochs each. Because the texts in the TinyStories dataset are shorter, we reduce the context length to 128 and, accordingly, the learning rate to 5e-4.

All additional experiments exhibit the same phenomena as in §4: clustering effects are observed in semantic coherence and semantic diversity, while entropy shows no significant differences, and context scope remains the primary driver. These results further confirm the robustness of our conclusion.

Appendix C Supplementary analyses of decoding algorithms

C.1 Proof of entropy reduction

We formally prove that confidence-based strategies reduce full entropy, assuming the data distribution factorizes into independent position-wise distributions, and the DLM is optimally trained.

Throughout this section, we use superscripts to index positions within a sequence. To contextualize the proof, we briefly recall the decoding mechanisms of confidence-based strategies. We assume a block length BB and a target number of denoising steps NN. Let pθp_{\theta} be the trained DLM. At each denoising step, given current partially decoded sequence xtx_{t}, the model samples a token from pθ​(x0i∣xt)p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t}) for each masked position ii within the block.

Low-confidence remasking decodes B/NB/N tokens within this block that have the highest prediction probabilities pθ​(x0i∣xt)p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t}) (i.e. the confidence values), remasking the others. Dynamic low-confidence remasking extends this by additionally decoding any token in the block whose confidence value strictly exceeds a predefined threshold τ\tau, meaning that the number of tokens decoded per step can dynamically increase. Consistent with our experimental design, we set N=BN=B, which targets decoding one token per step. Once all tokens within the current block are decoded, the generation process proceeds to the next block.

Theorem 1.

Assume that the data distribution at each position is independent of the others, so that the full distribution factorizes as

pdata​(x)=∏i=1Lpdatai​(xi)=:∏i=1Lqxii,p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{\mathrm{data}}^{i}(x^{i})=:\prod_{i=1}^{L}q^{i}_{x^{i}}\ , (11)

and that the DLM is optimally trained, i.e., for any masked position ii of a noised input xtx_{t},

pθ​(x0i∣xt)=pdatai​(x0i)=qx0ii.p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t})=p_{\mathrm{data}}^{i}(x_{0}^{i})=q^{i}_{x_{0}^{i}}. (12)

Let plcrp_{\mathrm{lcr}} and pdlcrp_{\mathrm{dlcr}} denote the generation distributions induced by low-confidence remasking and dynamic low-confidence remasking, respectively. Then

ℋ⁡(plcr)≤ℋ⁡(pdata),ℋ⁡(pdlcr)≤ℋ⁡(pdata).\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{lcr}})\leq\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{data}}),\qquad\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{dlcr}})\leq\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{data}}). (13)
Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that q1i≥q2i≥⋯≥q|𝒱|iq_{1}^{i}\geq q_{2}^{i}\geq\cdots\geq q_{|\mathcal{V}|}^{i} for all ii. By definition, low-confidence remasking is a special case of dynamic low-confidence remasking when τ=1\tau=1. Thus, it suffices to prove ℋ⁡(pdlcr)≤ℋ⁡(pdata)\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{dlcr}})\leq\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{data}}).

We assume the model starts generating from an empty prompt, as our proof can generalize the starting state to any sequence.

Let X∼pdlcrX\sim p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}, X=(X1,X2,…,XL)X=(X^{1},X^{2},...,X^{L}). By the chain rule of entropy,

ℋ(pdlcr)=ℋ(X1)+ℋ(X2∣X1)+⋯+ℋ(XL∣X1:L−1).\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{dlcr}})=\mathcal{H}(X^{1})+\mathcal{H}(X^{2}\mid X^{1})+\cdots+\mathcal{H}(X^{L}\mid X^{1:L-1}). (14)

It is sufficient to show that for every position ii and possible prefix sequence x1:i−1x^{1:i-1}, we have

∀k,∑c=1kpdlcr(Xi=c∣X1:i−1=x1:i−1)≥∑c=1kqci.\forall k,\ \sum_{c=1}^{k}p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}(X^{i}=c\mid X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1})\geq\sum_{c=1}^{k}q_{c}^{i}. (15)

This is a majorization relation (32). Since the entropy function is Schur-concave, the majorization implies

ℋ(Xi∣X1:i−1=x1:i−1)≤ℋ(qi)=ℋ(pdatai),\mathcal{H}(X^{i}\mid X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1})\leq\mathcal{H}(q^{i})=\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{data}}^{i}), (16)

and taking expectation over x1:i−1x^{1:i-1} gives

ℋ(Xi∣X1:i−1)≤ℋ(pdatai).\mathcal{H}(X^{i}\mid X^{1:i-1})\leq\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{data}}^{i}). (17)

Summing over ii then yields our desired inequality

ℋ⁡(pdlcr)≤ℋ⁡(pdata).\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{dlcr}})\leq\mathcal{H}(p_{\mathrm{data}}). (18)

We prove Eq.(15) by introducing latent variable ZZ, where ZiZ^{i} denotes the decoding step at which XiX^{i} is generated. It is sufficient to show that for any configuration z1,…,zLz^{1},\ldots,z^{L} and any realization of the other tokens x1,…,xi−1,xi+1,…,xLx^{1},\dots,x^{i-1},x^{i+1},\dots,x^{L} such that

pdlcr(X1:i−1=x1:i−1,Xi+1:L=xi+1:L,Z1:L=z1:L)>0,p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}(X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1},X^{i+1:L}=x^{i+1:L},Z^{1:L}=z^{1:L})>0, (19)

we have for all kk,

∑c=1kpdlcr(Xi=c|X1:i−1=x1:i−1,Xi+1:L=xi+1:L,Z1:L=z1:L)≥∑c=1kqci.\sum_{c=1}^{k}p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}\!\left(X^{i}=c\,\middle|\,X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1},X^{i+1:L}=x^{i+1:L},Z^{1:L}=z^{1:L}\right)\geq\sum_{c=1}^{k}q_{c}^{i}. (20)

This is sufficient because the conditional distribution given X1:i−1X^{1:i-1} can be expressed as a mixture over the remaining variables, and majorization is preserved under such mixtures.

Let

S={j:zj=zi},T={j:(zj≥zi)∧(j​ and ​i​ are in the same block)},S=\{j:z^{j}=z^{i}\},\qquad T=\{j:(z^{j}\geq z^{i})\land(j\text{ and }i\text{ are in the same block})\}, (21)

be the set of positions decoded at step ziz^{i} and the set of positions within the same block as ii that are not decoded before step ziz^{i}. We consider the following two cases.

Case 1: |S|=1|S|=1.

In this case, the decoding step of XiX^{i} decodes exactly one position, namely ii. The event that XiX^{i} is the only token that is decoded at this step occurs if and only if all positions in T∖{i}T\setminus\{i\} are remasked at the same step. Under the optimal model assumption, decoding Xi=cX^{i}=c requires that for every j∈T∖{i}j\in T\setminus\{i\}, the sampled token at position jj has confidence no larger than qciq_{c}^{i} and does not exceed the threshold τ\tau. pdlcr(Xi=c|X1:i−1=x1:i−1,Xi+1:L=xi+1:L,Z1:L=z1:L)∝qci∏j∈T∖{i}∑r:qrj≤min⁡(τ,qci)qrj.\begin{gathered}p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}\!\left(X^{i}=c\,\middle|\,X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1},X^{i+1:L}=x^{i+1:L},Z^{1:L}=z^{1:L}\right)\\ \propto q_{c}^{i}\prod_{j\in T\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{r:\,q_{r}^{j}\leq\min(\tau,q_{c}^{i})}q_{r}^{j}.\end{gathered} (22)

To understand this proportionality, note that by Bayes’ theorem, the conditional probability is proportional to the joint probability:

pdlcr(Xi=c|X1:i−1=x1:i−1,Xi+1:L=xi+1:L,Z1:L=z1:L)∝pdlcr(Xi=c,X1:i−1=x1:i−1,Xi+1:L=xi+1:L,Z1:L=z1:L).\begin{gathered}p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}\!\left(X^{i}=c\,\middle|\,X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1},X^{i+1:L}=x^{i+1:L},Z^{1:L}=z^{1:L}\right)\\ \propto p_{\mathrm{dlcr}}\!\left(X^{i}=c,X^{1:i-1}=x^{1:i-1},X^{i+1:L}=x^{i+1:L},Z^{1:L}=z^{1:L}\right).\end{gathered} (23)

C.2 Bias elimination experiments

In §5.2, we suggest that the entropy reduction observed in confidence-based remasking strategies stems from a theoretical distributional bias. To support this explanation, we introduce a simple modification to the confidence-based strategy: after selecting the decoding positions based on confidence values, we resample the decoded tokens at these positions. This breaks the correlation between position selection and the sampled tokens, thereby eliminating the bias.

As shown in Figure 3, this modification restores nn-gram entropy to the baseline level and invalidates inequality (ii) in Eq.(8). This confirms that the bias causes the nn-gram entropy reduction. These phenomena are consistent across seeds, datasets and architectures (Figure 8, Figure 9 and Figure 10).

Beyond entropy, bias elimination also removes the influences of confidence-based strategies on semantic coherence and semantic diversity (Figure 11, Figure 12, and Figure 13). This suggests that the bias-based distinction could be generalized to these metrics as well.

C.3 Ablations across seeds, datasets and architectures

Consistent with Appendix B.3, we further validate our main findings by repeating the main experiments with two additional random seeds, using the Qwen2 architecture, and on the TinyStories dataset. As shown in Figure 8, Figure 9, and Figure 10, we consistently observe entropy reduction, including decreases in nn-gram entropy and the validity of inequality (ii). We also observe that bias elimination remains effective. These results confirm the robustness of our findings.

C.4 Effects on semantic coherence and semantic diversity

We also evaluate semantic coherence and semantic diversity across all remasking strategies and configurations. As shown in Figure 11, Figure 12, and Figure 13, non-confidence-based strategies have much smaller effects on both metrics compared to confidence-based strategies. Confidence-based strategies consistently increase semantic coherence, while their effect on semantic diversity depends highly on the dataset. As shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12, they increase semantic diversity on the FineWeb dataset, consistent across seeds and architectures. However, when we switch to the TinyStories dataset (Figure 13), confidence-based strategies reduce semantic diversity.
