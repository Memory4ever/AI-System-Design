Abstract

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) traditionally relies on a sparse, outcome-based signal. Recent work shows that providing a fine-grained, model-intrinsic signal—rewarding the confidence growth in the ground-truth answer—effectively improves language reasoning training by providing step-level guidance without costly external models. While effective for unimodal text, we find that naively applying this global reward to vision-language (V-L) reasoning is a suboptimal strategy, as the task is a heterogeneous mix of sparse visual perception and dense textual reasoning. This global normalization creates mixture-induced signal degradation, where the training signal for visual steps is statistically distorted by the predominant textual steps. We propose Perception-Decomposed Confidence Reward (PDCR), a framework that solves this by aligning the reward structure with the task’s heterogeneous nature. PDCR first performs an unsupervised skill decomposition, introducing a model-internal Visual Dependence Score to quantify visual reliance and applying a clustering algorithm to separate perception and reasoning steps. Based on this, PDCR computes a decomposed advantage by normalizing confidence gains within each skill cluster. This intra-cluster normalization provides a stable, correctly-scaled signal for both perception and reasoning. We demonstrate that PDCR outperforms the naive, global-reward formulation and sparse-reward baselines on key V-L reasoning benchmarks. The code is publicly accessible at https://github.com/hee-suk-yoon/PDCR.

1 Introduction

Pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit strong performance on complex, multi-step reasoning tasks Liu et al. 2023; Team et al. 2023; 202 2023; Bai et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2025a. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a leading approach for further improving these capabilities, using a programmatically checkable terminal metric (e.g., correctness of the final answer) as the reward Yang et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025; Shao et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2025b; Xia et al. 2025. While effective, the standard RLVR formulation supplies a sparse terminal accuracy signal, offering no guidance for intermediate steps and thus creating a credit assignment problem.

To address this, recent work has explored fine-grained, step-level supervision. One prominent approach is training an external Process Reward Model (PRM) Yoon et al. 2024a; Wang et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025; Ong et al. 2025b; Luo et al.. However, PRMs are often costly to train, data-hungry, and prone to misalignment. In contrast, a compelling, model-intrinsic alternative from the language domain is to reward the ground-truth confidence growth Yoon et al. 2025a. This method provides step-level guidance using the model’s own probabilities of the ground-truth answer, avoiding the need for an external reward model to evaluate each reasoning step.

While effective for unimodal text, we find that naively applying this global reward to vision-language (V-L) reasoning is a suboptimal strategy. As illustrated in Figure 1, the core challenge is that V-L reasoning is not a homogeneous process; it is a heterogeneous mixture of two distinct, high-level skills: (i) sparse, but critical, visual perception steps and (ii) dense, predominant textual reasoning steps. This statistical imbalance, as we show in Section 4, leads directly to mixture-induced signal degradation: the reward signal for critical visual steps is statistically distorted by the predominant textual steps.

To this end, we propose Perception-Decomposed Confidence Reward (PDCR), a framework that solves this by aligning the reward structure with the task’s heterogeneous nature. As illustrated in Figure 4, PDCR operates in two stages:

  1. 1.

    Unsupervised Skill Decomposition: We first introduce a Visual Dependence Score, a model-internal signal that quantifies a step’s reliance on the visual input. We then apply an optimal clustering algorithm (Otsu’s method) to automatically separate steps into visual perception and textual reasoning groups.

  2. 2.

    Perception-Decomposed Advantage: Based on this, PDCR computes a decomposed advantage by normalizing confidence gains within each respective skill cluster, ensuring a stable, intra-cluster comparison.

This decomposed approach, when paired with the standard terminal reward, provides a more robust and correctly-scaled training signal. By calculating a separate advantage for visual perception and textual reasoning, PDCR ensures that each skill is evaluated against its own statistical baseline, allowing visual perception steps to receive a stable, well-scaled signal that is not distorted by the predominant textual reasoning steps.

In detail, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

  • •

    Through a series of observations, we identify the mixture-induced signal degradation problem in vision-language reasoning, showing that naive, model-intrinsic rewards are suboptimal due to skill heterogeneity.

  • •

    We propose an unsupervised skill decomposition method using a Visual Dependence Score and optimal clustering, which requires no external labels during training.

  • •

    We introduce PDCR, a decomposed reward framework that uses this clustering to compute stable, intra-cluster advantages, resolving the signal degradation problem.

  • •

    We demonstrate through experiments on key vision-language reasoning benchmarks that our PDCR framework outperforms both sparse-reward baselines and the naive, global-reward formulation.

2 Related Works

2.1 Reasoning in Multimodal Models

Improving the reasoning capabilities of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) is an active area of research Kim et al. 2021; Yoon et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2025b; Xia et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025a; Fan et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025b; Sarch et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025; Qiu et al. 2025; Ong et al. 2025a; Chu et al. 2025; Yoon et al. 2024b. A common paradigm is to fine-tune MLLMs using Reinforcement Learning (RL) with verifiable rewards Guo et al. 2025; Shao et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024. However, this approach typically relies on a sparse, outcome-based reward (i.e., whether the final answer is correct), which is inefficient for training complex, multi-step tasks.

To overcome this, several works have introduced external Process Reward Models (PRMs) Wang et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025; Ong et al. 2025b; Luo et al.. PRMs provide a dense, step-level training signal, but are notoriously costly to train, often requiring extensive and difficult dataset curation. This cost has motivated a recent shift towards finding reward signals that are model-intrinsic or self-generated.

2.2 Model-Intrinsic and Self-Rewarding Methods

To address the costs of external PRMs, an alternative direction is self-rewarding reinforcement learning, which sources rewards from the model’s own internal signals Yuan et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024; Yoon et al. 2025b. These signals often include logit-based self-certainty Zhao et al. 2025, self-confidence Li et al. 2025a, or majority-voting Zhou et al. 2025.

A particularly relevant framework in the language domain is the Progressively Ascending Confidence Reward (PACR) Yoon et al. 2025a. PACR provides a dense, step-level reward by calculating the increase in the model’s log-probability of the ground-truth answer, guided by the inductive bias that confidence should ascend during a correct reasoning trace. This approach has proven effective for text-only reasoning but has not been sufficiently adapted for the unique challenges of multimodal tasks.

2.3 Decomposed Reward Modeling

A parallel line of research focuses on decomposing complex reward signals. In text-only LLMs, this is often done by evaluating a response from various perspectives, such as helpfulness, safety, or correctness Wu et al. 2023; Cui et al.; Zhang et al. 2025. This concept is also being explored in MLLMs, where the reward signal is often denser due to the multimodal inputs Xia et al. 2025; Özdemir and Akagündüz 2024.

However, existing work on vision-language decomposition primarily focuses on generating explicit textual descriptions (e.g., image captions) to serve as the basis for the reward Li et al. 2025b; Liu et al. 2025b; Ding et al. 2025. Our work diverges from this approach by decomposing the original reasoning steps directly via an internal, process-level signal, rather than relying on the generation of additional, explicit text like an image caption.

We argue that visual and linguistic capabilities are heterogeneous skills with distinct distributions, and therefore benefit from independent normalization. Analogous concepts have been explored in concurrent works in other domains, such as multi-reward Liu et al. 2026 or tool-use agentic tasks Zhu et al. 2025.

3 Background and Problem Setup

This section first describes the standard sparse-reward RL framework (GRPO) for reasoning training. It then discusses its sparse-reward limitations, followed by the recent, model-intrinsic signal of ground-truth confidence growth Yoon et al. 2025a which addresses them.

3.1 Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

For each trajectory τ(i)\tau^{(i)}, a sparse, terminal outcome reward R(i)R^{(i)} is assigned based on the final answer’s correctness:

This sparse reward is then used to compute the outcome-based advantage AO(i)A_{O}^{(i)} by normalizing it with the group’s mean and standard deviation:

AO(i)=R(i)−mean​({R(j)}j=1N)std​({R(j)}j=1N).A_{O}^{(i)}=\frac{R^{(i)}-\text{mean}(\{R^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N})}{\text{std}(\{R^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N})}. (2)

This single, trajectory-level advantage AO(i)A_{O}^{(i)} is then applied to all tokens within all reasoning steps hk(i)h_{k}^{(i)} of the trajectory τ(i)\tau^{(i)}. This sparse signal offers no step-specific guidance, thus exacerbating the credit assignment problem.

3.2 Ground-Truth Confidence Growth as a Model-Intrinsic Process Reward

To address the limitations of this sparse, trajectory-level signal, recent work in the language domain Yoon et al. 2025a introduced a dense, model-intrinsic reward based on ground-truth confidence growth. This approach is guided by the inductive bias that a model’s confidence in the correct answer Yg​tY_{gt} should progressively increase as it generates a well-formed reasoning trajectory.

To operationalize this inductive bias as a computable reward, each trajectory τ(i)\tau^{(i)} is first segmented into its discrete reasoning steps {hk(i)}k=1Ki\{h^{(i)}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{i}} (see Appendix 14 for segmentation details). As shown in the overall pipeline in Figure 2, this segmentation allows for a process-level reward. At each step kk of trajectory τ(i)\tau^{(i)}, the model’s confidence in the ground truth ck(i)c^{(i)}_{k} is evaluated as its log-probability given the current prefix:

ck(i)=log⁡πθ​(Yg​t|𝐈,𝐪,H≤k(i)),c^{(i)}_{k}=\log\pi_{\theta}(Y_{gt}|\mathbf{I},\mathbf{q},H^{(i)}_{\leq k}), (3)

where H≤k(i)=(h1(i),…,hk(i))H^{(i)}_{\leq k}=(h^{(i)}_{1},\dots,h^{(i)}_{k}). The core reward signal is the stepwise confidence gain gk(i)g^{(i)}_{k}, defined as the change in confidence induced by the addition of step hkh_{k}:

gk(i):=ck(i)−ck−1(i),g^{(i)}_{k}:=c^{(i)}_{k}-c^{(i)}_{k-1}, (4)

where c0(i)=log⁡πθ​(Yg​t|𝐈,𝐪)c^{(i)}_{0}=\log\pi_{\theta}(Y_{gt}|\mathbf{I},\mathbf{q}). A positive gain gk(i)>0g^{(i)}_{k}>0 indicates that step hkh_{k} made the ground truth more probable.

This combined signal provides fine-grained, step-specific guidance, effectively solving the credit assignment problem of sparse-only rewards in the language domain, while avoiding the need for external reward models.

4 The Challenge of Heterogeneous Skills in Vision-Language Reasoning

While the dense reward signal from ground-truth confidence growth (Eq. 5) is effective for unimodal language tasks, its naive application to vision-language (V-L) reasoning is suboptimal. The core issue, which we validate in this section, is that V-L reasoning is not a homogeneous process. Instead, it is a heterogeneous mixture of two functionally distinct, high-level skills: (1) sparse, but critical, visual perception steps and (2) dense, predominant textual reasoning steps.

[Observation 1]: V-L Reasoning as a Heterogeneous Skill Mix.

The two primary skills in a V-L reasoning trajectory serve different purposes. As illustrated in the example in Figure 3-(a), visual perception involves extracting evidence from the visual input 𝐈\mathbf{I} and translating it into textual observations. This includes identifying objects, their attributes, or their spatial relationships (e.g., “The image shows a person standing at a counter, actively using a POS terminal.”). In contrast, textual reasoning involves logical deduction, calculation, or conclusion-drawing based on previously established facts (e.g., “The act of operating a POS terminal in a commercial setting is the primary role of a cashier.”).

To empirically validate this functional distinction, we analyze the model’s internal attention patterns. We first annotated a representative set of reasoning trajectories, categorizing each step as either visual perception or textual reasoning (see Appendix 15 for full annotation details). We then measured the average attention each step paid to the input visual tokens. As shown in the plot in Figure 3-(b), the two skills exhibit distinct attention profiles. Visual perception steps exhibit high attention to the input visual tokens, as they are responsible for converting visual information into a textual format. Conversely, textual reasoning steps attend almost exclusively to the preceding text tokens, using the information established by prior steps to perform logical inference. This shows that a V-L reasoning trajectory is not a homogeneous process, but a heterogeneous mixture of these two distinct capabilities.

[Observation 2]: Statistical Imbalance of Skills.

Beyond their functional differences, we find a statistical imbalance between the two skills. Analyzing the same ground-truth annotated set from [Observation 1], we observe that visual perception steps are inherently sparse, accounting for approximately 30% of the total reasoning steps, while the textual reasoning steps form the dense majority (approx. 70%), as shown in Figure 3-(c). This sparsity is a key driver of the normalization problem we describe next.

[Observation 3]: Mixture-Induced Signal Degradation from Skill Imbalance.

This statistical imbalance poses a significant challenge for the global normalization in Eq. 5. We term this issue mixture-induced signal degradation, as the global statistics (min,max\min,\max) are computed from a pool overwhelmingly dominated by the returns of textual steps. This global baseline is, therefore, unrepresentative of the sparse perception steps.

Figure 3-(d) (left) illustrates this effect: the naive global normalization compresses and misaligns the perception advantage distribution, which could lead to ill-scaled credit for these critical actions. In contrast, Figure 3-(d) (right) shows the effect of decomposed normalization. By normalizing each skill cluster independently, this mixture-induced signal degradation is mitigated. The resulting advantage distributions for both skills appear better scaled and aligned, aiming to provide a more stable and representative training signal.

5 Method: Perception-Decomposed Confidence Reward (PDCR)

Our method, Perception-Decomposed Confidence Reward (PDCR), directly addresses the core problem identified in Section 4: that a single, homogeneous reward is suboptimal for heterogeneous V-L skills. As illustrated in Figure 4, PDCR operates in two stages. Specifically, it (i) performs unsupervised skill decomposition to automatically cluster steps into visual perception and textual reasoning groups, and (ii) computes a decomposed advantage by normalizing confidence gains within each respective cluster, ensuring a more stable, intra-cluster comparison.

5.1 Unsupervised Skill Decomposition via Visual Dependence

To decompose the reward signal during training, we must categorize each reasoning step hk(i)h_{k}^{(i)} as visual perception or textual reasoning. While our analysis in Section 4 used externally-annotated labels (via GPT) to validate the problem, such labels are not available in a practical training setup. Therefore, to achieve a scalable and self-contained decomposition, we introduce the Visual Dependence Score, Vk(i)V_{k}^{(i)}: a model-internal signal that quantifies a step’s reliance on the visual input.

This score quantifies how much the generation of a specific step hk(i)h_{k}^{(i)} depended on the actual visual input 𝐈\mathbf{I}, as opposed to a non-informative, blank (i.e., white) image 𝐈white\mathbf{I}_{\text{white}}. We define the standard log-probability of the step given the real image as pk(i)=log⁡πθ​(hk(i)|𝐈,𝐪,H<k(i))p_{k}^{(i)}=\log\pi_{\theta}(h_{k}^{(i)}|\mathbf{I},\mathbf{q},H_{<k}^{(i)}) and the white-image log-probability as pw,k(i)=log⁡πθ​(hk(i)|𝐈white,𝐪,H<k(i))p_{w,k}^{(i)}=\log\pi_{\theta}(h_{k}^{(i)}|\mathbf{I}_{\text{white}},\mathbf{q},H_{<k}^{(i)}). The Visual Dependence Score Vk(i)V_{k}^{(i)} is then the log-likelihood ratio of these two:

Vk(i)=pk(i)−pw,k(i).V_{k}^{(i)}=p_{k}^{(i)}-p_{w,k}^{(i)}. (7)

A high Vk(i)V_{k}^{(i)} score indicates that the model relied heavily on the visual input 𝐈\mathbf{I} to generate the step hk(i)h_{k}^{(i)}, marking it as a visual perception step. A score near zero indicates the step was primarily driven by the text prefix H<k(i)H_{<k}^{(i)}, marking it as a textual reasoning step.

To separate steps, we dynamically find an optimal threshold c∗c^{*} that partitions the set of all visual dependence scores 𝒱={Vk(i)}(i,k)∈I\mathcal{V}=\{V_{k}^{(i)}\}_{(i,k)\in I}. We determine c∗c^{*} using Otsu’s method Otsu 1979, a classic and provably optimal 1D clustering algorithm. This method is widely used in image segmentation to find a threshold that best separates a pixel intensity into two classes (e.g., foreground and background). We apply it here to our 1D distribution of visual dependence scores to find the optimal split that minimizes the total intra-cluster variance.

First, let 𝒱sorted={v1,v2,…,vM}\mathcal{V}_{\text{sorted}}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{M}\} be the set of all M=|I|M=|I| visual dependence scores, sorted in ascending order. We then iterate through all possible split points k∈[1,M−1]k\in[1,M-1]. Each kk partitions the sorted data into two clusters: C1​(k)={v1,…,vk}C_{1}(k)=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\} and C2​(k)={vk+1,…,vM}C_{2}(k)=\{v_{k+1},\dots,v_{M}\}.

For each potential split kk, we compute the cluster means μ1​(k)=1k​∑i=1kvi\mu_{1}(k)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i} and μ2​(k)=1M−k​∑i=k+1Mvi\mu_{2}(k)=\frac{1}{M-k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{M}v_{i}. We then calculate the total within-cluster sum of squares (SSE) for that split:

S​S​E​(k)=∑i=1k(vi−μ1​(k))2+∑i=k+1M(vi−μ2​(k))2.SSE(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(v_{i}-\mu_{1}(k))^{2}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{M}(v_{i}-\mu_{2}(k))^{2}. (8)

The optimal split point k∗k^{*} is the one that minimizes this SSE:

k∗=arg⁡min𝑘​S​S​E​(k).k^{*}=\underset{k}{\arg\min}\,SSE(k). (9)

The final threshold c∗c^{*} is set as the value of the score at this optimal split, c∗=vk∗c^{*}=v_{k^{*}}. This threshold c∗c^{*} partitions the set of all step indices II into two disjoint subsets:

ℐvisual\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\text{visual}} ={(i,k)∈I∣Vk(i)≥c∗},\displaystyle=\{(i,k)\in I\mid V_{k}^{(i)}\geq c^{*}\}, (10)

Validating Dynamic Thresholding.

We validate this dynamic, data-driven threshold c∗c^{*} against a simpler top-K thresholding baseline. This naive approach simply classifies a fixed percentage kk of steps with the highest Vk(i)V_{k}^{(i)} scores as visual perception. As shown in Figure 5, we measure the classification accuracy of both methods against the ground-truth annotated labels from Section 4. The Top-K method (purple bars) is highly sensitive to the choice of kk and achieves a peak accuracy of 67.5% at k=30k=30. Our dynamic Otsu’s method (pink dashed line) is parameter-free and significantly outperforms even the best Top-K baseline, achieving 76.2% accuracy. This confirms that our dynamic approach is a more robust and accurate method for separating the two skill distributions.

5.2 Perception-Decomposed Advantage Calculation

With the step indices now decomposed, we compute a separate, normalized advantage for each skill cluster. We take the discounted returns Gk(i)G_{k}^{(i)} (as defined in Section 3.2) and normalize them using min-max scaling, but only against other returns from the same cluster.

As shown in Figure 4, this yields two distinct process-level advantages. For visual steps, the advantage is:

(12)

For textual steps, the advantage is:

AT,k(i)=Gk(i)−min(j,k′)∈ℐtextual⁡Gk′(j)max(j,k′)∈ℐtextual⁡Gk′(j)−min(j,k′)∈ℐtextual⁡Gk′(j).A_{T,k}^{(i)}=\frac{G_{k}^{(i)}-\min\limits_{(j,k^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{textual}}}G_{k^{\prime}}^{(j)}}{\max\limits_{(j,k^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{textual}}}G_{k^{\prime}}^{(j)}-\min\limits_{(j,k^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{textual}}}G_{k^{\prime}}^{(j)}}. (13)

This decomposed normalization solves the “mixture-induced signal degradation” problem. The gain of a perception step is now evaluated only against its peers, resulting in a stable, well-scaled reward signal for both skills.

5.3 Final PDCR Advantage

Finally, the total advantage At​o​t​a​l,k(i)A_{total,k}^{(i)} for any given step is the weighted sum of the sparse outcome advantage AO(i)A_{O}^{(i)} (from Eq. 2) and its corresponding decomposed process advantage Adecomposed,k(i)A_{\text{decomposed},k}^{(i)}:

At​o​t​a​l,k(i)=λO​AO(i)+λP​Adecomposed,k(i),A_{total,k}^{(i)}=\lambda_{O}A_{O}^{(i)}+\lambda_{P}A_{\text{decomposed},k}^{(i)}, (14)

where Adecomposed,k(i)A_{\text{decomposed},k}^{(i)} is selected based on the step’s cluster:

Adecomposed,k(i)={AV,k(i),if ​(i,k)∈ℐvisualAT,k(i),if ​(i,k)∈ℐtextual.A_{\text{decomposed},k}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}A_{V,k}^{(i)},&\text{if }(i,k)\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{visual}}\\ A_{T,k}^{(i)},&\text{if }(i,k)\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{textual}}.\end{cases} (15)

This final advantage At​o​t​a​l,k(i)A_{total,k}^{(i)} is then applied to all tokens comprising the step hk(i)h_{k}^{(i)} during the policy update.

6 Experimental Setup

6.1 Models and Datasets

Our experiments utilize two open-source VLMs: Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. We train all models on the Vision-SR1 dataset Li et al. 2025b. Vision-SR1 is a diverse training corpus of approximately 47K examples, all of which include verifiable answers suitable for GRPO training. The dataset is specifically balanced to cover three key reasoning domains: Mathematical Reasoning, Commonsense Knowledge, and General Visual Understanding.

6.2 Evaluation Benchmarks

General Visual Understanding.

We evaluate on MMMU Yue et al. 2024, a 11.5K-question college-level benchmark for cross-disciplinary knowledge; its more rigorous successor, MMMU-Pro Yue et al. 2025, which increases the difficulty; RealWorldQA xAI 2024, 700 real-world images with spatially-grounded questions; and VisNumBench Weng et al. 2025, a 1.9K question benchmark targeting numerical estimation and counting.

Multimodal Mathematical Reasoning.

We use two specialized benchmarks: MathVerse Zhang et al. 2024, which evaluates Chain-of-Thought (CoT) correctness on 2.6K diagram-centric problems; and MATH-Vision Wang et al. 2024, 3K competition-grade problems across 16 disciplines.

Hallucination Diagnosis.

We use HallusionBench Guan et al. 2024, an image-context reasoning benchmark specifically designed to test a model’s hallucination and visual illusion errors.

6.3 Baselines

We compare our proposed PDCR against several key baselines on both model backbones. For a direct and fair comparison, we train using identical system prompts and hyperparameters (details in Appendix 16): GRPO Shao et al. 2024; DAPO Yu et al. 2025, which solves the vanishing advantage problem of GRPO (for this baseline, we add only dynamic sampling to standard GRPO, without incorporating other improvements from DAPO such as a higher clip rate.); and PACR Yoon et al. 2025a, which applies a naive, globally-normalized ground-truth confidence growth process reward.

7 Experimental Results and Ablations

Main Results.

Table 1 presents the main experimental results on both the Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbones. Across both model scales, our PDCR framework outperforms all baselines.

On the 7B model, PDCR achieves a final average score of 52.9, surpassing the next-best method, PACR (52.2), as well as the GRPO baseline (51.5). This demonstrates the clear benefit of our decomposed reward. Notably, PDCR shows consistent improvements over the naive, globally-normalized PACR, particularly on complex understanding tasks like MMMU-Pro (42.5 vs. 41.5) and MMMU (51.5 vs. 50.5), as well as on MathVerse (55.0 vs. 54.3). This performance gain is consistent on the 3B model, where PDCR (45.2) achieves the highest average score, outperforming the naive PACR (44.4) and the sparse GRPO (43.6).

We also note a key distinction from GRPO. A well-known issue of GRPO is the vanishing advantage problem: if all NN trajectories in a group are either correct or incorrect, the outcome advantage AO(i)A_{O}^{(i)} becomes zero for that sample, providing no learning signal. The DAPO baseline is designed to mitigate this by re-sampling until a mixed-outcome group is obtained. Our PDCR, by definition, solves this as well, since its process-level reward AP,k(i)A_{P,k}^{(i)} is non-zero. A key question, therefore, is whether PDCR’s gains are merely from stabilization. However, our results show that PDCR also consistently outperforms DAPO (e.g., 52.9 vs. 52.0 and 45.2 vs. 44.1). This comparison validates that our perception-decomposed reward provides a meaningful, high-quality training signal, and its contribution goes beyond just mitigating the vanishing advantage problem of GRPO.

Efficacy of Skill Decomposition.

To isolate the impact of our unsupervised skill decomposition (Section 5.1), we compare with a Random Decomposition baseline.

This baseline follows the same PDCR framework, including the decomposed advantage calculation (Eq. 12 and 13). However, instead of using our Visual Dependence Score (Vk(i)V_{k}^{(i)}) and dynamic thresholding to cluster the steps, it simply assigns each step to the ℐvisual\mathcal{I}_{\text{visual}} or ℐtextual\mathcal{I}_{\text{textual}} cluster at random.

The results are presented in Table 2. On the 7B backbone, the Random Decomposition baseline (52.3) performs significantly worse than our full PDCR method (52.9) and only slightly better than the naive PACR (52.2). This demonstrates that simply decomposing the reward is not enough. The performance gain of our method comes from correctly identifying and separating the heterogeneous skills. By using a data-driven, unsupervised clustering method, our PDCR framework provides a meaningful decomposition that effectively trains both perception and reasoning.

Training Dynamics.

We analyze the training dynamics in Figure 6-(a). The plot, which tracks average accuracy over training steps, shows that our PDCR method consistently outperforms all baselines throughout the entire training process. While the sparse GRPO baseline shows the slowest improvement, the naive PACR and our Random Decomposition baseline perform very similarly, reinforcing our earlier finding that a random decomposition offers minimal benefit.

Training Cost and Efficiency.

We analyze computational overhead in Figure 6-(b). PACR incurs a ∼\sim1.5x cost increase over GRPO, as it requires an extra forward pass to compute ckc_{k} (Eq. 3). Our PDCR adds a minimal further overhead, requiring one additional forward pass per trajectory to compute the VkV_{k} (Eq. 7), compared to PACR. Crucially, this training cost is offset by a significant gain in generation efficiency as shown in Figure 6-(c). This gain is driven by the fact that both PACR and our PDCR learn to produce shorter, more concise reasoning traces over time, as shown in Figure 6-(d).

8 Conclusion

We introduced PDCR to address the challenge of heterogeneous skills in Vision-Language reasoning. By clustering steps via a model-internal Visual Dependence Score, PDCR decomposes the confidence growth reward in an unsupervised manner. This intra-cluster normalization resolves mixture-induced signal degradation, providing stable advantages for both perception and reasoning. Ultimately, PDCR improves strong RLVR baselines, such as GRPO and PACR, using a self-contained signal.

14 Segmentation Detail

A prerequisite for a process-based reward framework is the segmentation of the reasoning trajectory τ(i)\tau^{(i)} into a discrete sequence of steps {hk(i)}k=1Ki\{h^{(i)}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{i}}. The step is the fundamental unit to which a reward or advantage is assigned.

Previous work in process-reward modeling has adopted several strategies to define this unit:

  • •

    Supervised Formatting via SFT: Some approaches utilize Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to enforce a highly structured output format, such as training the model to generate explicit step delimiters (e.g., “Step 1:”, “Step 2:”). While this simplifies segmentation, it necessitates an SFT stage to instill these formatting constraints. As detailed in our implementation (Appendix 16), our training adopts a R1-style pipeline Guo et al. 2025 that bypasses SFT to initiate reasoning training directly via RLVR. Consequently, strategies relying on pre-learned formatting are not applicable to our setting.

  • •

    Confidence-based Segmentation: Some approaches leverage intrinsic model signals to dynamically segment reasoning trajectories. For instance, Liu et al. 2025a proposes, a method that divides reasoning steps based on the model’s confidence in predicting the next token. This approach aims to identify true decision points by observing how the model’s uncertainty changes, avoiding arbitrary segmentation based on fixed lengths or rule-based heuristics that may not align with the model’s internal reasoning flow.

  • •

    Rule-Based Segmentation: A more common, lightweight, and model-agnostic approach is to use simple heuristics to parse the generated text. This is the strategy adopted by the original PACR framework Yoon et al. 2025a and other process-reward based methods Yang et al. 2025b; Zeng et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025. The most common heuristic is to define a new step at each newline character (\n), or after sentence-terminating punctuation (e.g., a period followed by a space). In this work, we adopt this strategy due to its simplicity and to ensure a fair comparison with PACR Yoon et al. 2025a.

16 Implementation Details

16.1 Training Framework and Hyperparameters

We perform all experiments using the EasyR1 framework. Consistent with the R1-Zero style training Guo et al. 2025, we apply Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) directly on the base model, bypassing any Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) stage. This ensures that the reasoning behaviors we observe are genuine results of the reinforcement learning process, rather than artifacts inherited from supervised fine-tuning.

All models (including baselines) are trained on the Vision-SR1-47K dataset for 1 epoch. To ensure a strictly fair comparison, we maintain a consistent configuration across all methods (GRPO Shao et al. 2024, DAPO Yu et al. 2025, PACR Yoon et al. 2025a, and our PDCR). Common hyperparameters for the optimizer, RL process, and evaluation are detailed in Table 6. All models were trained on a single node with 8 ×\times NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.

Format reward.

Given that our training pipeline bypasses SFT, the model does not inherently possess the structural priors to format its reasoning steps. To address this, we incorporate a format reward alongside the outcome accuracy reward. A trajectory is assigned a format score RfmtR_{\text{fmt}} based on the valid enclosure of the reasoning process within <think> and </think> tags, and the final answer within \boxed{}. Specifically, we augment the outcome reward R(i)R^{(i)} (Eq. 1) with a fixed bonus of Rfmt=0.1R_{\text{fmt}}=0.1 for compliant trajectories, while assigning Rfmt=0R_{\text{fmt}}=0 to invalid ones.

Details on DAPO baseline.

For the DAPO baseline Yu et al. 2025, we adopted a specific configuration to isolate the impact of reward stability. The full DAPO framework originally introduces three modifications to GRPO: (1) Clipping to promote diversity and prevent entropy collapse, (2) Dynamic Sampling to solve the vanishing advantage problem (where a batch has 0 variance in rewards), and (3) a Token-Level Policy Gradient loss to mitigate length bias.

As discussed in Section 7, our primary goal in comparing with DAPO is to determine if PDCR’s performance gains are merely a result of stabilizing the training signal against the vanishing advantage problem. Therefore, our implementation of the DAPO baseline specifically applies only Dynamic Sampling on top of the standard GRPO framework. This controlled configuration allows for a direct, isolated comparison regarding reward signal stability.

17 Ablation Study on Visual Dependence Calculation for Skill Decomposition

In Eq. 7 of the main text, we introduced the Visual Dependence Score (Vk(i)V_{k}^{(i)}) as the log-likelihood ratio between the model’s probability given the original image 𝐈\mathbf{I} versus a non-informative baseline 𝐈white\mathbf{I}_{\text{white}}. Following the analysis in Huang et al. 2025a, which examined token-level visual dependency, we examine the optimal strategy to divide the visual grounding steps using our dynamic thresholding. We evaluate each perturbation strategy on the Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct backbone by measuring the skill decomposition accuracy achieved using our dynamic thresholding (Eq. 9). All results are validated against the ground-truth labeled dataset described in Appendix 15. Our findings offer a clear insight into the nature of visual perception in large multimodal models:

  • •

    Information Destruction is Necessary: The strategies that effectively destroy visual semantics (i.e., White, Gaussian Blur, and Gaussian Noise) perform similarly well, achieving decomposition accuracies in the range of 75.8% - 76.2%. This suggests that the specific type of noise matters less than the fact that the visual information is removed. We opted for the White Image in PDCR simply because it is the most computationally efficient and reproducible method to achieve this ‘blinding’ effect.

  • •

    Spatial Transformation is Insufficient: In contrast, the Rotation strategy yields significantly poorer decomposition accuracy (65.9%). Thus, to accurately identify if a model is ‘seeing’, we should distort the visual signal entirely rather than merely transforming it. This confirms that information-destroying baselines are essential for robust skill decomposition, validating our choice of the White Image strategy in our experiments.

19 Limitations and Future Works

Our work presents a promising direction for self-rewarding V-L models, but has limitations that offer avenues for future research.

Computational Cost.

The primary limitation of our framework is the computational overhead during the training phase. As shown in Figure 6-(b) of main paper, both PDCR and the dense-reward baseline (PACR) Yoon et al. 2025a require additional computation to estimate the step-level confidence gains (and in our case, an additional visual dependence scores). This results in a higher per-step training cost compared to standard sparse-reward methods like GRPO Shao et al. 2024. However, since our decomposition mechanism is lightweight, the cost of PDCR is comparable to that of PACR.

Crucially, we argued that this training overhead is a favorable trade-off for inference efficiency. As shown in Figure 6-(c)&(d), PDCR induces significantly more concise reasoning traces compared to GRPO. Consequently, while PDCR requires more GPU-hours to train, it yields a more efficient model for deployment. Future work could explore internal activation-based approximations to obtain these confidence scores without requiring full additional forward passes, potentially bridging the training cost gap.

Binary Skill Decomposition.

Our method makes a simplifying assumption that V-L reasoning steps are a heterogeneous mixture of two distinct and separable skills. We then use Otsu’s method to enforce a single, hard threshold c∗c^{*} between them. In reality, some steps may be hybrid (e.g., complex spatial reasoning that is both perceptual and logical). Our binary decomposition forces these hybrid steps into one of the two clusters, which may not be optimal. Future research could explore soft-clustering assignments or the identification of three or more distinct skill types.
