Temper and Tilt Lead to SLOP: Reward Hacking Mitigation with Inference-Time Alignment

Ye Wang & Jing Liu & Toshiaki Koike-Akino Affiliation: Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories (MERL) Affiliation: 201 Broadway, Cambridge, MA 02139, USA Email: {yewang,jiliu,koike}@merl.com
Abstract

Inference-time alignment techniques offer a lightweight alternative or complement to costly reinforcement learning, while enabling continual adaptation as alignment objectives and reward targets evolve. Existing theoretical analyses justify these methods as approximations to sampling from distributions optimally tilted toward a given reward model. We extend these techniques by introducing reference-model temperature adjustment, which leads to further generalization of inference-time alignment to ensembles of generative reward models combined as a sharpened logarithmic opinion pool (SLOP). To mitigate reward hacking, we propose an algorithm for calibrating SLOP weight parameters and experimentally demonstrate that it improves robustness while preserving alignment performance.

1 Introduction

Alignment through post-training with reinforcement learning (RL) is a widely used and crucial technique for realizing effective foundation models (Christiano et al. 2017; Ziegler et al. 2019; Ouyang et al. 2022; Korbak et al. 2022a; Bai et al. 2022; Rafailov et al. 2023; Shao et al. 2024). Inference-time alignment methods, such as Best-of-N (BoN) (Stiennon et al. 2020; Nakano et al. 2021), provide a lightweight alternative or complement to computationally expensive RL methods, while achieving competitive performance (Gao et al. 2023; Dubois et al. 2023). Further, inference-time methods enable flexible, continual adaptation to evolving alignment objectives.

Common across many RL approaches is the optimization of the policy π\pi to maximize expected reward, subject to Kullback–Leibler (KL) regularization with respect to a reference policy pp, i.e., maxπ𝔼π[r]−λKL(π∥p)\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r]-\lambda\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|p). Korbak et al. 2022b observed that, in principle, the optimal policy is a tilted distribution in the form π∗∝p​exp⁡(r/λ)\pi^{*}\propto p\exp(r/\lambda), which is also known from information projection problems in classical information theory literature (Kullback and Khairat 1966; Csiszár 1975). This perspective has been a key to a line of research (Yang et al. 2024b; Mroueh and Nitsure 2025; Verdun et al. 2025; Khalaf et al. 2025) that has considered BoN and other related inference-time methods, while providing theoretical analysis of how they approximate sampling the optimal tilted distribution policy.

Our work extends upon these inference-time alignment methods, by introducing reference model temperature adjustment, which is closely related to a proposal of Jinnai et al. 2025, and further generalizing this framework to ensembles of generative reward models as a form of logarithmic opinion pool (LOP) (Heskes 1997). This perspective, while bridging RL objectives, sampling-based methods, and ensemble modeling, also reveals the impact of correlation and the benefits of diversity, when combining models. We experimentally demonstrate the effectiveness of calibrating the ensemble weights to mitigate reward hacking, while preserving alignment performance, for visual question answering and math reasoning tasks.

2 Formulation, background, and methods

2.1 Tempered reward maximization

We consider a generalization of the KL-regularized reward maximization problem, given by

πα,β∗(y∣x)=argmaxπ𝔼Y∼π(⋅∣x)[βr(x,Y)+(α−1)logp(Y∣x)]−KL(π(y∣x)∥p(y∣x)),\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}(y\mid x)=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\beta r(x,Y)+(\alpha-1)\log p(Y\mid x)\big]-\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(y\mid x)\|p(y\mid x)\big), (1)

where r:𝒳×𝒴→ℝr:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R} is a given (proxy) reward model, p⁡(y∣x)p(y\mid x) is a given reference model, and parameters α,β∈ℝ\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R} control the regularization. Typically, β>0\beta>0 is used, but we allow for β≤0\beta\leq 0 to consider situations where the proxy reward model may be severely misaligned with the ideal reward.

The standard KL-regularized reward maximization problem is the special case of (1) with α=1\alpha=1, where the reference model log-likelihood term disappears from the expectation, and the optimal solution is shown by Korbak et al. 2022b to be

π1,β∗​(y∣x)=p⁡(y∣x)​exp⁡(β​r​(x,y))Cβ,x,\pi_{1,\beta}^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{p(y\mid x)\exp(\beta r(x,y))}{C_{\beta,x}}, (2)

where Cβ,x:=∫p⁡(y∣x)​exp⁡(β​r​(x,y))​𝑑yC_{\beta,x}:=\int p(y\mid x)\exp(\beta r(x,y))\,dy is a normalizing constant.11 1 For discrete 𝒴\mathcal{Y}, the normalizing constants throughout the paper are instead defined via summation. The tilted distribution solution of (2) is also known in classical information theory literature (Kullback and Khairat 1966; Csiszár 1975). Note that as β→∞\beta\to\infty, the optimal policy is plain reward maximization over yy in the support of the reference model, while for β=0\beta=0, the optimum is just the reference model, i.e., π1,0∗=p\pi_{1,0}^{*}=p, due to regularization. Sweeping the value of β\beta, these tilted distributions achieve the optimal reward maximization versus KL divergence (from the reference model) trade-off, which is the target for many RL training methods. Characterizing the general problem of (1) follows as a corollary.

Corollary 1.

The problem given above in (1) is equivalently written and solved as

πα,β∗​(y∣x)\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}(y\mid x) =argmaxπ𝔼Y∼π⁡(y∣x)[βr(x,Y)]−KL(π(y∣x)∥p(y∣x)α/Cα,x)\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi(y\mid x)}\big[\beta r(x,Y)\big]-\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(y\mid x)\|p(y\mid x)^{\alpha}/C_{\alpha,x}\big) (3)
=p​(y∣x)α​exp⁡(β​r​(x,y))Cα,β,x,\displaystyle=\frac{p(y\mid x)^{\alpha}\exp(\beta r(x,y))}{C_{\alpha,\beta,x}}, (4)

where Cα,x:=∫p​(y∣x)α​𝑑yC_{\alpha,x}:=\int p(y\mid x)^{\alpha}\,dy and Cα,β,x:=∫p​(y∣x)α​exp⁡(β​r​(x,y))​𝑑yC_{\alpha,\beta,x}:=\int p(y\mid x)^{\alpha}\exp(\beta r(x,y))\,dy are normalizing constants.

Corollary 1 states that augmenting the reward with an additional reference model log-likelihood term in (1) is equivalent to adjusting the temperature of the reference model in (3). These equivalent problems are both solved by (4), which we call the tempered and tilted distribution, as α\alpha is essentially an inverse temperature applied to the reference model, while β\beta controls the exponential tilting towards the reward model. Note that as α→∞\alpha\to\infty, with a fixed, finite β\beta, the solution approaches the maximum likelihood output of the reference model, whereas α=0\alpha=0 makes the reference model uniform and removes its impact, and values of α<0\alpha<0 inverts the reference model, amplifying less likely outputs.

2.2 Approximate inference-time alignment techniques

While the tilted (and tempered) distributions of (2) and (4), in principle, express closed-form solutions for the regularized reward maximization problems given by (1) and (3), handling these formulations directly is often computationally intractable. Even with finite sets, where the integration for determining the normalizing constants are replaced with summation, these calculations become infeasible due to the enormous size of the output space (e.g., all token sequences up to a max length). Hence, this subsection reviews some practical inference-time methods that approximate sampling the tilted distribution of (2), i.e., for the special case of α=1\alpha=1, while requiring only the ability to draw independent samples of the reference model and to evaluate the reward model on those samples.

Best-of-N (BoN) is a widely adopted heuristic (see (Stiennon et al. 2020) and (Nakano et al. 2021) for early influential examples) to augment or replace RL training. It simply consists of generating nn independent samples, y1,…,yny_{1},\ldots,y_{n}, from the reference model p⁡(y∣x)p(y\mid x), evaluating each with the reward model, and outputting the sample that maximizes the reward, i.e., arg⁡maxyi⁡r⁡(x,yi)\arg\max_{y_{i}}r(x,y_{i}). Under certain simplifying assumptions, Yang et al. 2024b established that BoN asymptotically approaches the optimal tilted distribution. However, in more general finite regimes, which are characterized by Mroueh and Nitsure 2025, BoN does not necessarily approximate the tilted distribution. Best-of-Poisson (BoP) (Khalaf et al. 2025) is an extension of BoN, where, instead of a fixed nn, the number of samples is drawn from a Poisson distribution, which results in a close approximation of the tilted distribution, under the assumption of uniformly distributed rewards.

Soft Best-of-N (SBoN) (Verdun et al. 2025) similarly generates nn independent samples and evaluates the reward, ri=r⁡(x,yi)r_{i}=r(x,y_{i}), for each sample. However, instead of selecting the maximizer, SBoN randomly selects sample yiy_{i} with probability exp⁡(β​ri)/∑jexp⁡(β​rj)\exp(\beta r_{i})/\sum_{j}\exp(\beta r_{j}), i.e., the distribution over the nn samples formed by the softmax of their rewards, with inverse temperature β\beta. Verdun et al. 2025 characterize the accuracy of SBoN, which includes establishing that KL(π1,β∗∥π~β,n)=O(1/n)\textrm{KL}(\pi_{1,\beta}^{*}\|\tilde{\pi}_{\beta,n})=O(1/n), where π~β,n\tilde{\pi}_{\beta,n} denotes the distribution sampled by SBoN. Reward Augmented Decoding (RAD) (Deng and Raffel 2023) employs a similar concept, but, instead of selecting among entire response candidates, RAD utilizes the reward model to augment the sampling of each token, within the iterative process of auto-regressive generation.

Jinnai et al. 2025 propose methods to augment the reward model for regularizing BoN to mitigate reward-hacking. Their main proposal, inspired by minimum Bayes risk decoding, involves a proximity regularizer that aims to minimize the Wasserstein distance with respect to the reference policy. As an ablation, Jinnai et al. 2025 also consider what they call KL-regularized BoN, which essentially augments the reward with the reference model log-likelihood, and is analogous to the (α−1)​log⁡p​(y∣x)(\alpha-1)\log p(y\mid x) term in the tempered reward maximization of (1).

2.3 SLOP: sharpened logarithmic opinion pooling

The proxy reward model rr implicitly defines the alignment distribution, given by

q⁡(y∣x):=exp⁡(r⁡(x,y))/Rx,\displaystyle q(y\mid x):=\exp(r(x,y))/R_{x}, (5)

where Rx:=∫exp⁡(r⁡(x,y))​𝑑yR_{x}:=\int\exp(r(x,y))\,dy is a normalizing constant. The tempered and tilted distribution, given in (4), can be rewritten as

πα,β∗​(y∣x)\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}(y\mid x) =p​(y∣x)α​q​(y∣x)β∫p​(z∣x)α​q​(z∣x)β​𝑑z\displaystyle=\frac{p(y\mid x)^{\alpha}q(y\mid x)^{\beta}}{\int p(z\mid x)^{\alpha}q(z\mid x)^{\beta}\,dz} (6)
=:softmax⁡(α​log⁡p⁡(y∣x)+β​log⁡q⁡(y∣x)),\displaystyle=:\mathrm{softmax}\big(\alpha\log p(y\mid x)+\beta\log q(y\mid x)\big), (7)

where softmax⁡(⋅)\mathrm{softmax}(\cdot) denotes exponentiation and normalization, such that we have valid distributions over y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y}, for each x∈𝒳x\in\mathcal{X}. This form makes it clear that the tempered and tilted distribution is essentially a logarithmic opinion pool (LOP) (Genest and Zidek 1986; Heskes 1997), which is also related to the concept of product of experts (Hinton 2002). However, unlike literature that typically considers LOP with non-negative weights that sum to one, we instead employ arbitrary α,β∈ℝ\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}, which allows for concentrating probability mass on the most confident outputs (e.g., converging towards proxy reward maximization as β→∞\beta\to\infty) or even inverting the contribution of anti-aligned models (e.g., if the negated proxy reward is closer to the gold reward, then β<0\beta<0 may be more effective). We emphasize this aspect by calling this form of model as a Sharpened LOP (SLOP).22 2 Admittedly, also for the sake of utilizing a memorable and ironic acronym.

Inspired by this logarithmic pooling perspective, we can also generalize to a pool of mm experts (i.e., generative and/or proxy reward models), denoted by s1,…,sms_{1},\ldots,s_{m}, representing the score contributed by each expert, where si​(x,y)=log⁡pi​(y∣x)s_{i}(x,y)=\log p_{i}(y\mid x) for a generative model and si​(x,y)=ri​(x,y)≡log⁡qi​(y∣x)s_{i}(x,y)=r_{i}(x,y)\equiv\log q_{i}(y\mid x) for a reward model.33 3 Due to later normalization via softmax, the normalizing constant of the alignment distribution may be omitted. These experts are combined, with weights ω:=(ω1,…,ωm)∈ℝm\omega:=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}, to form the SLOP given by

πω∗​(y∣x)=softmax⁡(∑i=1mωi​si​(x,y))∝∏i=1mpi​(y∣x)ωi,\displaystyle\pi_{\omega}^{*}(y\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}s_{i}(x,y)\right)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(y\mid x)^{\omega_{i}}, (8)

where, for notational convenience, each pip_{i} denotes either a generative model or the corresponding alignment distribution qiq_{i} of a reward model.

Directly sampling the SLOP distribution from (8) is often intractable, when the output space 𝒴\mathcal{Y} is very large. However, (8) can also be interpreted as the solution of a regularized reward maximization problem, where the proxy reward is a weighted ensemble of expert scores, which allows us to approximately sample from the SLOP via the following extension of SBoN (Verdun et al. 2025). By convention, we set the generative reference model as the first expert p1p_{1}. For a given prompt xx, we sample nn candidates y1,…,yn∼iidp1(⋅∣x)y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}p_{1}(\cdot\mid x), evaluate candidate rewards as function of expert scores, as given by,

r′​(x,yj):=(ω1−1)​log⁡p1​(yj∣x)+∑i=2mωi​si​(x,yj),\displaystyle r^{\prime}(x,y_{j}):=(\omega_{1}-1)\log p_{1}(y_{j}\mid x)+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\omega_{i}s_{i}(x,y_{j}), (9)

and select one candidate according to

π^ω,n​(yj∣x)=softmax⁡(r′​(x,yj))∝exp⁡(∑i=1mωi​si​(x,yj)−s1​(x,yj)),\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{\omega,n}(y_{j}\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}(r^{\prime}(x,y_{j}))\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}s_{i}(x,y_{j})-s_{1}(x,y_{j})\right), (10)

where softmax⁡(⋅)\mathrm{softmax}(\cdot) denotes exponentiation and normalization over the set of nn candidates. Note that the first expert weight is set to (ω1−1)(\omega_{1}-1), which accounts for the candidates being already sampled from the first expert.

Corollary 2.

Let π^ω,n\widehat{\pi}_{\omega,n} denote the distribution realized via the above extension of SBoN, by selecting from nn sampled candidates y1,…,yn∼iidp1(⋅∣x)y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}p_{1}(\cdot\mid x), according to (10). Expanding r′r^{\prime}, we have p1​exp⁡(r′)=∏i=1mexp⁡(ωi​si)p_{1}\exp(r^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\exp(\omega_{i}s_{i}), and thus π=πω∗\pi=\pi_{\omega}^{*}. ∎

In the special case of one reference model and one reward model, this approximates tempered-and-tilted sampling, with candidate likelihoods proportional to exp⁡((α−1)​log⁡p⁡(yj∣x)+β​r​(x,yj))\exp\big((\alpha-1)\log p(y_{j}\mid x)+\beta r(x,y_{j})\big), where (α,β)(\alpha,\beta) correspond to (ω1,ω2)(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

2.4 Reward hacking mitigation via calibrated model pooling

The motivation for considering regularized reward maximization is that neither the reference model pp nor the (proxy) reward model rr is perfect. The ultimate objective is to attain a policy that maximizes a gold reward or closely approximates an ideal distribution, neither of which may be readily available or precisely known. Intuitively, optimizing toward an inaccurate proxy reward model rr may be misleading and counter-productive to the ultimate objective. This is a well-known phenomenon commonly referred to as reward hacking (Pan et al. 2022), for which the recent works of (Mroueh and Nitsure 2025; Huang et al. 2025; Aminian et al. 2025; Khalaf et al. 2025) have provided theoretical characterizations and related mitigation strategies. In particular, our approach extends upon the HedgeTune concept of (Khalaf et al. 2025) by utilizing a small amount of calibration samples to optimize the SLOP weights ω\omega.

The ultimate objective (in principle) of our reward hacking mitigation approach is to maximize the expected gold reward, over the choice of SLOP weight parameters, as given by

supω𝔼x∼𝒟,x∼πω∗​(y∣x)​[g⁡(x,y)],\displaystyle\sup_{\omega}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},x\sim\pi_{\omega}^{*}(y\mid x)}[g(x,y)], (11)

where the expectation is over both sampling xx from some input distribution 𝒟\mathcal{D}, and sampling yy from the SLOP πω∗\pi_{\omega}^{*}. We note that the optimization problem of (11) does not always have a maximizer with finite weights, as sometimes the supremum reward may only be approached by sharpening the model towards a hard deterministic output with diverging weights. See Appendix B for further discussion.

In practice, we generally cannot arbitrarily evaluate the gold reward, which limits us to empirically estimate the objective in (11) via a small number of calibration samples. We assume that we are given kk input samples, x1,…,xkx_{1},\ldots,x_{k}, drawn i.i.d. from the input distribution 𝒟\mathcal{D}, and that for each xix_{i}, we can query the gold reward for up to nn samples. In practice, for situations with a verifiable reward, instead of querying the gold model up to k​nkn times, this assumption may instead be realized by just having the gold reference responses corresponding to the kk input samples (e.g., for math problems, responses can be checked against reference solutions to assign a correctness reward). Algorithm 1 uses these calibration samples to calculate the following empirical estimate of the objective in (11), upon which it performs gradient ascent, as given by

J^​(ω)=1k​∑i=1k∑j=1nπ^ω,n​(yi​j∣xi)​g​(xi,yi​j)−λ​‖ω‖2,\displaystyle\widehat{J}(\omega)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widehat{\pi}_{\omega,n}(y_{ij}\mid x_{i})g(x_{i},y_{ij})-\lambda\|\omega\|^{2}, (12)

where λ≥0\lambda\geq 0 is optionally used to regularize weight optimization and avoid divergence towards fully sharpened hard outputs. Given calibrated weights ω\omega, we can either approximately sample the SLOP distribution via the method given by (10), or instead employ a hard selection that simply picks the candidate yjy_{j} that maximizes the weighted SLOP score ∑iωi​si​(x,yj)−s1​(x,yj)\sum_{i}\omega_{i}s_{i}(x,y_{j})-s_{1}(x,y_{j}).

2.5 Optimal weights for jointly Gaussian model scores

Consider the binary output scenario, with 𝒴={0,1}\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}, while 𝒳\mathcal{X} remains some arbitrary, large set of possible inputs (e.g., all questions that seek a verifiable binary response). For any x∈𝒳x\in\mathcal{X}, we let yxy_{x} denote the corresponding correct response, and the gold reward is given by g⁡(x,y)=1−|y−yx|g(x,y)=1-|y-y_{x}|. Thus, the gold reward maximization objective is to maximize the probability of sampling the correct response. For each model pip_{i} and x∈𝒳x\in\mathcal{X}, let the log-posterior ratio (evaluated on the correct response) be denoted by

Li​(x):=log⁡pi​(yx∣x)pi​(1−yx∣x).\displaystyle L_{i}(x):=\log\frac{p_{i}(y_{x}\mid x)}{p_{i}(1-y_{x}\mid x)}. (13)

For this illustrative example, we assume that, over x∼𝒟x\sim\mathcal{D}, L⁡(x):=(L1​(x),…,Lm​(x))L(x):=(L_{1}(x),\ldots,L_{m}(x)) are jointly Gaussian with mean vector μ¯\bar{\mu} and positive-definite covariance Σ\Sigma, to depict a pool of models with potentially varying and correlated reliability. Let the weighted sum of these ratios be denoted by Z⁡(x):=ωT​L​(x)Z(x):=\omega^{T}L(x), which is a scalar Gaussian with mean μ=ωT​μ¯\mu=\omega^{T}\bar{\mu} and variance σ2=ωT​Σ​ω\sigma^{2}=\omega^{T}\Sigma\omega. Classical detection theory (Kay 1998) establishes that setting ω=c​Σ−1​μ¯\omega=c\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu}, for any c>0c>0, maximizes the detection margin (μ/σ)=μ¯T​Σ−1​μ¯(\mu/\sigma)=\sqrt{\bar{\mu}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu}}. Note that the same analysis and result also applies to the heavier-tailed Cauchy distribution, as discussed in Appendix C.

In this binary example, the SLOP evaluated on the correct solution reduces to πω∗​(yx∣x)=ρ⁡(Z⁡(x))\pi_{\omega}^{*}(y_{x}\mid x)=\rho(Z(x)), where ρ⁡(z):=1/(1+exp⁡(−z))\rho(z):=1/(1+\exp(-z)) is the sigmoid function. Thus, the expected gold reward is given by

𝔼x∼𝒟,y∼πω∗​(y∣x)​[g⁡(x,y)]=𝔼x∼𝒟​[πω∗​(yx∣x)]=𝔼Z∼𝒩⁡(μ,σ2)​[ρ⁡(Z)]≈Φ⁡(μ8π+σ2),\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\omega}^{*}(y\mid x)}[g(x,y)]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\pi_{\omega}^{*}(y_{x}\mid x)]=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}[\rho(Z)]\approx\Phi\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}+\sigma^{2}}}\right), (14)

where Φ\Phi is the probit function (standard normal CDF) and this classical approximation is seen in (Spiegelhalter and Lauritzen 1990; MacKay 1992), with further recent analysis in (Mucsányi et al. 2025). Under this approximation, and setting ω=c​Σ−1​μ¯\omega=c\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu}, the expected gold reward is given as

𝔼x∼𝒟,y∼πω∗​[g⁡(x,y)]⟶Φ⁡(μ¯T​Σ−1​μ¯),as ​c⟶∞.\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\omega}^{*}}[g(x,y)]\longrightarrow\Phi\left(\sqrt{\bar{\mu}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu}}\right),\quad\text{as }c\longrightarrow\infty. (15)

Note that saturating the weights is necessary to maximize accuracy, since we are sampling from πω∗\pi_{\omega}^{*}, which only approaches a hard decision as c→∞c\to\infty. However, instead of sampling, if we make a hard decision directly as arg⁡maxy​πω∗​(y∣x)\arg\max_{y}\pi_{\omega}^{*}(y\mid x), with any fixed c>0c>0, which is equivalent to the optimal log-likelihood ratio test, then the accuracy would be exactly Pr[Z>0]=Φ(μ¯T​Σ−1​μ¯)\Pr[Z>0]=\Phi\left(\sqrt{\bar{\mu}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu}}\right).

2.5.1 Example impact of diversity vs. correlation

Consider the simple example with μ¯=𝟏m\bar{\mu}=\mathbf{1}_{m} and Σ=σ02​((1−η)​Im+η​𝟏m​𝟏mT)\Sigma=\sigma^{2}_{0}((1-\eta)I_{m}+\eta\mathbf{1}_{m}\mathbf{1}_{m}^{T}), where 𝟏m=[1,…,1]T\mathbf{1}_{m}=[1,\ldots,1]^{T} is an all-ones vector of size mm and η∈[0,1)\eta\in[0,1) is the correlation factor. Then, we have

μ¯T​Σ−1​μ¯\displaystyle\bar{\mu}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu} =mσ02​(1+η⁡(m−1)),\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\sigma^{2}_{0}(1+\eta(m-1))}, (16)

which demonstrates that with uncorrelated (η=0\eta=0) experts, the accuracy Φ⁡(m/σ02)\Phi(\sqrt{m/\sigma^{2}_{0}}) approaches one as mm grows. However, with correlation η>0\eta>0, the accuracy saturates to Φ⁡(1/η​σ2)\Phi(\sqrt{1/\eta\sigma^{2}}) for m≫1m\gg 1. Thus, it is beneficial to ensemble diverse experts that have low correlation, and ideally independent error patterns.

3 Experimental results

3.1 Reward-guided visual question answering

This experiment considers the task of multiple-choice, visual question answering, evaluated with the ScienceQA (SQA) (Lu et al. 2022) benchmark dataset. The gold reward is one for the correct answer and zero otherwise. Thus, the objective is to maximize accuracy. To investigate the impact of imprecise reward models, we take a pretrained VLM as the generative reference model and pair it with a synthetic proxy reward model that may instead reward incorrect answers, at some given error rate. Since SQA multiple-choice questions contain only two to five answer options, we constrain VLM generation to single-token decoding over the small response set 𝒴={A,B,C,D,E}\mathcal{Y}=\{\text{A},\text{B},\text{C},\text{D},\text{E}\}. This simplification allows for exact sampling of the tempered-and-tilted (two-expert SLOP) distribution and focuses this controlled experiment on weight optimization for inaccurate proxy reward models. The (α,β)(\alpha,\beta) weights are optimized with 200200 calibration samples from the SQA test split, while the remaining 40414041 samples of the SQA test split are held-out for evaluation. See Appendix D for further details and additional results with the Gemma-3-4B (Gemma Team et al. 2025) and Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai et al. 2025) VLMs, which yield similar findings.

Figure 1(a) plots the results for the LLaVa-1.5-7B (Liu et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024) VLM paired with a synthetic proxy reward model at varying accuracies. The “optimized” curve is the result of freely optimizing α,β∈ℝ\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}, while the “ablation” curves constrain with α=1\alpha=1 and/or β≥0\beta\geq 0. Two baselines ignore the proxy reward model (β=0\beta=0): sampling the “reference” model (α=1\alpha=1, 63.4%63.4\% accuracy) and “greedy” token selection (α=∞\alpha=\infty, 66.1%66.1\% accuracy). On the other hand, ignoring the reference model and purely following the “proxy” reward (α=0\alpha=0, β=∞\beta=\infty) yields the proxy reward accuracy, which works well for accurate proxies, but quickly degrades (along the slope-one line, falling off the plot) for inaccurate proxies, which depicts the effect of hacking with a faulty reward model.

The SLOP curves generally dominate the performance of the individual reference and proxy models, by effectively combining the two. For the ablations with β≥0\beta\geq 0, performance gracefully degrades back to the baselines as the proxy reward model becomes more unreliable. The optimized and ablation curves, with unconstrained β\beta, yield increased performance for low proxy reward accuracies, since below 40%40\% accuracy (which is roughly the random guessing accuracy for SQA), the inaccurate proxy more reliably indicates an incorrect answer, providing a useful signal that can be exploited with weight β<0\beta<0. This can be seen in Figure 1(b), which plots the optimized (α,β)(\alpha,\beta) weights across varying proxy reward accuracies, showing how the confidence weight for each model correspondingly varies. At high proxy accuracies, the optimal α\alpha is close to zero, as following the proxy suffices. However, at middling proxy accuracies, α\alpha is larger, showing more reliance on the confidence of the reference model. For comparison, it also plots the optimal β\beta for the ablation (with fixed α=1\alpha=1 and β≥0\beta\geq 0), which simply falls back to 00 at 40%40\% proxy reward accuracy or below. We note that the ablation with fixed α=1\alpha=1 and β≥0\beta\geq 0 is essentially the HedgeTune method (Khalaf et al. 2025).

3.2 Math reasoning with generative model pooling

This experiment explores the task of math reasoning, evaluated on the GSM8K (Cobbe et al. 2021) benchmark, with a SLOP that ensembles four LLMs: Gemma-3-1B (Gemma Team et al. 2025), Qwen2-1.5B (Yang et al. 2024a), Qwen3-0.6B (Yang et al. 2025), and Phi-3.5-mini (Abdin et al. 2024). In this task, the input prompt is a grade school math word problem, and responses with the correct numerical solution receive a gold reward of one, and zero otherwise. Thus, the objective is to maximize accuracy.

We apply the approximate SLOP sampling method of (10), with one of the LLMs serving as the reference model to sample up to 200200 responses, and the expert scores given by the token-averaged log-likelihoods of each LLM, i.e., si​(x,y)=(1/Ly)​log⁡p⁡(y∣x)s_{i}(x,y)=(1/L_{y})\log p(y\mid x), where LyL_{y} is the number of tokens in the response yy and is introduced to avoid length bias. We apply Algorithm 1 to calibrate the SLOP weights using 200200 calibration examples from the GSM8K test split, while the remaining 11191119 samples are held-out for evaluation.

The BoN baselines select from the candidates using the individual LLM expert scores or the simple sum of the expert scores as the proxy reward. SLOP sampling tends to outperform the baselines, with more stable, monotonic improvement as the number of samples increase. We also evaluate “hard selection” of the candidate with the highest weighted SLOP score, which further improves performance over SLOP sampling. 3.3 SLOP weight selection from score statistics

This experiment continues with the same math reasoning setting of Section 3.2, and explores whether effective SLOP weights can be estimated from expert score statistics rather than optimization, by applying the insights and Gaussian model assumption of Section 2.5. Since this setting is not limited to binary responses, we instead apply this assumption to model the relative scores for any pair of correct and incorrect responses. We estimate the mean μ¯\bar{\mu} and covariance Σ\Sigma of these relative scores from all such sampled pairs across the 200200 calibration questions. Then, we assign weights as ω=Σ−1​μ¯\omega=\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mu}, which we call the inverse covariance SLOP. To avoid a potentially ill-conditioned inverse, we also consider the diagonalized covariance SLOP, which first drops the off-diagonal terms of the covariance matrix before performing the inverse. As another ablation, we also compare with the simple fixed weight strategy with ω=𝟏\omega=\mathbf{1}.

Table 1 summarizes the average performance of these three ablations for SLOP weight selection, alongside the baseline reference model sampling, SLOP with calibrated weights, and the top-performing BoN baseline. Performance plots are found in Figures 10, 11, 12, and 13 in Appendix E. Table 2 lists the optimized SLOP weights, along with the cosine similarity scores between these weights and each of the three ablation methods for assigning weights, and also reports the condition number of the relative score covariance matrix. Generally, the covariance-based methods improve upon the reference model sampling baseline (except the inverse covariance case with Gemma-3, which seems to suffer from ill-conditioning), and can be competitive against the calibrated SLOP.

4 Discussion

We develop inference-time alignment methods intended to improve robustness to reward hacking and to better combine signals from multiple generative or reward models. Potential positive impacts include more effective deployment of foundational models, especially when proxy rewards are imperfect. Potential negative impacts include misuse of inference-time methods to make models better at satisfying flawed, biased or harmful objectives, or amplification of undesirable model behaviors when the calibration signal is misaligned. Misuse risks are challenging to fully mitigate, however we emphasize the importance of calibration with gold or verifiable rewards.

4.1 Limitations

A limitation of our work is the somewhat narrow set of experiments that we conducted. As alignment is a fundamental topic with broad applications, there is certainly much more to be investigated. Larger and more capable models should be considered, as well as different modalities, beyond LLMs and VLMs, such as multimedia generation or vision-language-action models for embodied AI applications. Our empirical study was limited to question answering and math reasoning tasks, however broader datasets, benchmarks, and tasks should also be explored. Besides tasks with verifiable rewards, alignment for other objectives, such as safety and trust (e.g., robustness to jailbreaking attacks, or reducing hallucinations and toxicity) are also interesting directions to be evaluated.

However, our aim was mainly to provide theoretic insights that inspire new developments, which we tested with focused and controlled experiments. We were also limited by the modest compute resources (a single desktop GPU) available for this study, as discussed in Appendix G.

4.2 Concluding remarks and future work

We provide a generalized inference-time alignment framework that flexibly ensembles multiple generative and/or reward models. In this perspective, continual adaptation and reward-hacking mitigation take the form of calibrating the weights of this ensemble, which depends not only on individual expert quality, but also on the correlation among experts. Our future work will broaden the empirical evaluation beyond our current proof-of-concept experiments, in new directions, as discussed above. We will further investigate the roles of correlation and diversity in expert ensembles, and how they can be cultivated to improve performance.

Appendix G Computational resource usage

All experiments were run on a single desktop workstation, equipped with an Intel Core i7-13700K CPU, 128 GB of system RAM, and an Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of VRAM. The storage footprint for all models, datasets, and results generated by our experiments is approximately 48 GB.

In total, our experiments used approximately 150150 to 200200 hours of compute on this machine. The vast majority of this time was used by the math reasoning experiments, discussed in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, and Appendix E, specifically response generation and scoring. The visual question answering experiments of Section 3.1 and Appendix D consisted of no more than 33 hours of the total compute, since our approach for these multiple-choice required only computing single next-token likelihoods.
