GeoFlowVLM: Geometry-Aware Joint Uncertainty for Frozen Vision-Language Embeddings

Abstract

Standard dual-encoder vision-language models that map images and text to deterministic points on a shared unit hypersphere through ℓ2\ell_{2} normalization typically expose neither aleatoric uncertainty (cross-modal ambiguity) nor epistemic uncertainty (lack of training-distribution support). Existing post-hoc methods either recover at most one of the two uncertainty components, or ignore the hyperspherical geometry of these models’ embeddings. We propose GeoFlowVLM as a post-hoc adapter that learns the joint distribution of paired ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalised dual-encoder VLM embeddings on the product hypersphere 𝕊d−1×𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1} via Riemannian flow matching with a single masked velocity field. A consistency result shows that, in the population limit, the trained network exposes the joint flow and both cross-modal conditional flows as valid Riemannian flow-matching velocity fields on their respective domains. We derive two quantities from this single model: a conditional retrieval entropy that quantifies aleatoric ambiguity with a decision-theoretic interpretation via a Fano-type bound, and a marginal-typicality epistemic score justified by an exact chain-rule decomposition of the joint NLL. This decomposition isolates a cross-modal pointwise-mutual-information term that is structurally discriminative rather than epistemic, and is empirically the only consistently uninformative standalone component. Empirically, the entropy tracks Recall@1 with near-ideal monotonic calibration across three retrieval benchmarks in both directions, and the marginal-typicality sum yields consistently calibrated selective accuracy across four zero-shot classification benchmarks.

1 Introduction

Dual-encoder vision-language models (VLMs) such as CLIP [31] and SigLIP [43] project images and text to a shared unit hypersphere via ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalisation, reducing cross-modal compatibility to cosine similarity. This deterministic point representation is effective for retrieval and zero-shot transfer, but provides no estimate of prediction uncertainty, neither the aleatoric ambiguity arising from the intrinsically one-to-many nature of vision-language correspondence [8], nor the epistemic uncertainty on queries falling outside the regions of embedding space seen during training. Quantifying both kinds of uncertainty without retraining the underlying encoder is the central challenge of post-hoc uncertainty quantification (UQ) over frozen vision-language embeddings.

Existing post-hoc UQ methods (Table 1) split along two axes: the type of uncertainty captured, and whether the method respects the hyperspherical geometry of these embeddings. Aleatoric methods attach a distribution to each modality. Of these, ProbVLM [39], GroVE [40], and BayesVLM [2] all operate in ambient Euclidean space and do not account for the ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalisation constraint that defines the considered VLM representation space; AsymVLM [19] respect the sphere, but only for the text modality. Density-based epistemic methods over frozen encoders are scarcer: MC Dropout [15] ignores embedding geometry, while REPVLM [20] learns a density per modality on the sphere but provides no aleatoric estimate. ProbVLM is the only method that exposes both kinds of uncertainty, yet it does so heuristically: its epistemic estimate is MC Dropout over its adapter, measuring uncertainty of the adapter rather than the backbone, and its generalised Gaussian model misses the hyperspherical structure. The proposed GeoFlowVLM addresses the gap in the bottom-right cell of Table 1: a post-hoc method exposing aleatoric and density-based epistemic readouts from a single coupled density while respecting the hyperspherical geometry of these models’ embeddings.

Contributions. (i) GeoFlowVLM: a post-hoc probabilistic adapter trained via Riemannian flow matching with a single masked velocity field on the product hypersphere 𝕊d−1×𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}, yielding joint, marginal, and cross-modal conditional densities without encoder retraining. An ablation against an otherwise-identical Euclidean variant establishes hyperspherical geometry as structurally necessary.

(ii) A conditional-posterior-entropy aleatoric estimator: GeoFlowVLM’s cross-modal conditional density, evaluated and normalised over the retrieval gallery, defines a per-query categorical posterior whose entropy quantifies retrieval ambiguity and, via Fano’s inequality, certifies a lower bound on MAP Bayes risk. Empirically it tracks retrieval difficulty with near-monotonic calibration across three benchmarks in both directions, outperforming all baselines in image-to-text retrieval and matching the strongest directional baseline in text-to-image retrieval.

(iii) A chain-rule decomposition of the joint NLL into per-modality marginal typicality and cross-modal pointwise mutual information (PMI). PMI is structurally discriminative rather than epistemic while marginal typicality is the reliable epistemic component. This explains the effectiveness of per-modality methods such as REPVLM and frames jointly trained marginals as a coherent extension.

2 Related Work

GeoFlowVLM draws on four bodies of work: post-hoc probabilistic adapters for frozen VLMs, Riemannian generative modelling, the geometric structure of normalised VLM embeddings, and density-based epistemic scoring.

Post-hoc UQ for frozen VLMs. The post-hoc baselines surveyed in Sec. 1 differ mainly in the form of distribution they attach to a frozen encoder. ProbVLM [39] trains lightweight heteroscedastic-Gaussian adapters per modality and reads out epistemic uncertainty via MC Dropout over those same adapters; GroVE [40] couples the two modalities through a shared latent variable with Gaussian-process mappings; BayesVLM [2] obtains a Gaussian over embeddings via a last-layer Laplace approximation; AsymVLM [19] replaces the ambient-Gaussian assumption with directional (vMF / power-spherical) distributions but treats each modality in isolation; REPVLM [20] fits a Riemannian-flow density per modality on the sphere and uses its log-likelihood as an epistemic score; and MC Dropout [15] reduces uncertainty to forward-pass variance. Training-time probabilistic VLMs such as PCME [8], PCME++ [9], and ProLIP [7] introduce stochastic embeddings during pre-training and are therefore outside the frozen-encoder regime we adopt; they form a natural “ceiling” for what post-hoc methods can hope to recover, and we treat them as out-of-scope rather than as baselines.

Aleatoric/epistemic decomposition over frozen encoders. The classical predictive-uncertainty decomposition into aleatoric and epistemic components [21, 17] relies on a posterior over model parameters, which is unavailable when the encoder is frozen. In the frozen-encoder regime, the standard surrogate is density-based: aleatoric ambiguity is summarised by the entropy of a predictive posterior, and epistemic support by typicality under a learned density [20]. We adopt this surrogate explicitly: throughout, “epistemic score” refers to a typicality-based density readout, not a Bayesian-posterior variance, and we flag the distinction at the points it matters (Sec. 4.2).

Generative modelling on Riemannian manifolds. Continuous-time generative models have been extended from Euclidean space to general Riemannian manifolds. Riemannian continuous normalising flows [28] and score-based models [12] rely on costly heat-kernel approximations or simulation; flow matching [27] sidesteps this by regressing onto analytic conditional vector fields, extended to Riemannian geometries with closed-form geodesic targets by Chen and Lipman [5]. Minibatch optimal transport coupling further straightens trajectories [37]; concurrently, Lee et al. [23] show that spherical flow matching outperforms Euclidean baselines for natural image generation. In our setting, the closest precedent is REPVLM [20], which fits a Riemannian flow density to a single VLM modality at a time. GeoFlowVLM departs from this line by learning a coupled density on the product hypersphere with a masked velocity field, so that conditional posteriors, marginals, and the joint likelihood are all read out from one trained model (Theorem 1).

Geometry of VLM embeddings. Contrastive training projects VLM embeddings onto the unit hypersphere via ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalisation, and recent analyses split by whether they study the embeddings before or after this projection. Unnormalised-space works characterise the raw encoder outputs: Levi et al. [24] reveal a double-ellipsoid structure in pre-normalisation CLIP, Betser et al. [3] use whitened embeddings as a likelihood surrogate for paired images and captions, and Draganov et al. [14] argue that embedding norms carry self-supervised signal. Normalised-space works operate directly on the sphere: Liang et al. [25] document a persistent modality gap, complementing alignment–uniformity analyses of contrastive representations [42].

Density-based out-of-distribution detection. Our density-based epistemic UQ estimate connects to likelihood-based OOD scoring; the core observation that raw likelihood is coarse and that typicality refinements improve discrimination directly motivates the family parameterisation in Sec. 4.2.

3 Joint Density on the Product Hypersphere

Problem setup.

Let 𝒳\mathcal{X} denote the image space and 𝒴\mathcal{Y} the space of text captions, with I∈𝒳I\in\mathcal{X} and T∈𝒴T\in\mathcal{Y} denoting an image-text pair. Given any contrastive VLM with image encoder fI:𝒳→ℝdf_{I}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d} and text encoder fT:𝒴→ℝdf_{T}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{d} producing ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalised embeddings (e.g., CLIP [31], SigLIP [43], EVA-CLIP [36]), the pair (I,T)(I,T) maps to paired unit-normalised embeddings (𝐞I,𝐞T)(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T}) on the unit hypersphere 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}. We learn a probabilistic model pϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T}) of their joint distribution that respects this geometry, from which both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty are then derived in Sec. 4 without retraining fIf_{I} or fTf_{T}. We build on Riemannian flow matching [5], which on simple manifolds such as the sphere regresses onto a closed-form geodesic-tangent target and trains simulation-free; sampling integrates the learned ODE numerically, with periodic projection onto the manifold to prevent drift.

Joint formulation.

We model concatenated pairs 𝐳=[𝐞I;𝐞T]∈ℳ=𝕊d−1×𝕊d−1\mathbf{z}=[\mathbf{e}_{I};\mathbf{e}_{T}]\in\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1} as i.i.d. samples from a single joint distribution p⁡(𝐳)p(\mathbf{z}) on the product manifold ℳ\mathcal{M}, resulting in the model GeoFlowVLM (Geodesic Flow Matching for Vision-Language Models). The key design choice is to expose joint generation, T→IT\!\to\!I, and I→TI\!\to\!T inference through a single velocity field 𝐯tϕ​(𝐳t,𝐦)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{m}) conditioned on a block-structured binary mask 𝐦∈{0,1}2​d\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^{2d}: 𝐦k=1\mathbf{m}_{k}=1 marks dimensions transported by the flow, 𝐦k=0\mathbf{m}_{k}=0 marks dimensions held at their observed values with the corresponding stream’s time fixed to 00. The three masks 𝐦T→I=[𝟏d;𝟎d]\mathbf{m}_{T\to I}=[\mathbf{1}_{d};\mathbf{0}_{d}], 𝐦I→T=[𝟎d;𝟏d]\mathbf{m}_{I\to T}=[\mathbf{0}_{d};\mathbf{1}_{d}], and 𝐦joint=[𝟏d;𝟏d]\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{joint}}=[\mathbf{1}_{d};\mathbf{1}_{d}] select the three modes; this design enables the conditional posteriors and joint density required for uncertainty quantification (Sec. 4) to be read off a single trained model.

The mask restricts supervision to the generated dimensions, giving the masked CFM objective,

3.1 Consistency of Masked Riemannian Conditional Flow Matching (CFM)

Although the masked velocity field is trained as a single object, each conditional mask reduces it, in the population limit, to a valid Riemannian conditional flow on the corresponding marginal sphere.

Theorem 1 (Masked CFM recovers consistent Riemannian velocity fields).

Assume the joint data distribution pp admits a bounded density on ℳ=𝕊d−1×𝕊d−1\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}, that the per-mask time schedule holds as described in Sec. 3, and that the hypothesis class is sufficiently expressive to attain the population minimum of ℒGeoFlowVLM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GeoFlowVLM}}. Then any minimiser ϕ⋆\phi^{\star} satisfies:

  1. 1.

    (Joint flow.) 𝐯tϕ⋆​(𝐳,𝐦joint)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\phi^{\star}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{joint}}) is the marginal Riemannian CFM velocity transporting p0p_{0} to p1p_{1} on ℳ\mathcal{M}, almost everywhere in (t,𝐳)(t,\mathbf{z}).

  2. 2.

    (Conditional flows.) For any 𝐞I∗\mathbf{e}_{I}^{*} in the support of the image marginal, the second-block component of 𝐯tϕ⋆​(⋅,𝐦I→T)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\phi^{\star}}(\cdot,\mathbf{m}_{I\to T}) is the marginal Riemannian CFM velocity transporting p0p_{0} to p(⋅∣𝐞I∗)p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}^{*}) on 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}, almost everywhere in (t,𝐞T)(t,\mathbf{e}_{T}). The symmetric statement holds for 𝐦T→I\mathbf{m}_{T\to I}.

Proof sketch. Restricted to mask 𝐦I→T\mathbf{m}_{I\to T}, the loss decouples: the image block vanishes because the image stream is held fixed at t=0t=0, and the text block reduces to the standard Riemannian CFM objective on 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1} with 𝐞I\mathbf{e}_{I} as auxiliary input. The conditional CFM identity [27, 5] then characterises the unique pointwise minimiser as the marginal velocity transporting p0p_{0} to p(⋅∣𝐞I)p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}). The joint case follows analogously. Full proof in Appendix C.4. □\square

Remark 1 (Velocity-field uniqueness).

The minimiser ϕ⋆\phi^{\star} need not be unique as a parameter vector, but the induced velocity fields are: the marginal CFM velocity is the unique pointwise minimiser of each reduced objective. Distinct parameter vectors achieving the population minimum therefore induce the same velocity fields almost everywhere.

Remark 2 (Parameter coherence across masks).

The masking scheme induces a multi-task objective in which each mask defines an independent CFM target. The theorem states that at the joint population minimum, each task is simultaneously at its own individual population minimum—there is no trade-off between masks at the population level. A single ϕ⋆\phi^{\star} therefore realises the joint flow and both cross-modal conditional flows without any auxiliary consistency loss enforcing agreement between them; this follows directly from the mask-conditioned loss decoupling in the proof.

Remark 3 (Scope).

The theorem is a population-limit result; finite-sample, discretisation, and approximation errors are open theoretical directions, with step-count and probe-count ablations (Appendix D) serving as empirical proxies.

Practical consequences.

Two direct practical consequences follow. All three log-densities are evaluable on the same trained model via reverse-time integration under the corresponding mask. The per-modality marginals follow via Bayes’ identity,

log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)=log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T)−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T∣𝐞I),\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})\;=\;\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})\;-\;\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}), (2)

and symmetrically for 𝐞T\mathbf{e}_{T}; this coherence enables the uncertainty decomposition in Sec. 4.2.

4 Uncertainty Quantification from the Joint Density

The trained joint density of Sec. 3 delivers two uncertainty estimates from a single model: a conditional retrieval entropy that captures aleatoric ambiguity (Sec. 4.1), and a marginal-typicality epistemic score motivated by an exact chain-rule decomposition of the joint NLL that isolates a structurally discriminative cross-modal term (Sec. 4.2).

4.1 Aleatoric Uncertainty: Retrieval Entropy

Cross-modal retrieval is intrinsically one-to-many: a given image is compatible with many semantically valid captions and a caption with many plausible images. Aleatoric uncertainty in this setting is the irreducible variability of valid targets given the input, representing the variability that no amount of additional training data can remove [21]. The conditional posterior pϕ​(𝐞T∣𝐞I)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}) exposed by the joint flow (Theorem 1) is the model’s learned distribution over valid targets given the query; its Shannon entropy is therefore our aleatoric score. Mismatch between pϕp_{\phi} and the true conditional, arising e.g. from sparse coverage or distribution shift, would manifest as miscalibration of this score, and is scored separately via the typicality readout of Sec. 4.2.

In retrieval, we estimate this entropy through the gallery itself. Posterior samples drawn from the conditional flow are kernel-aggregated to a softmax distribution 𝝅=(π1,…,πN)\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N}) over the NN gallery items, and we report its Shannon entropy as the aleatoric score,

H=−∑j=1Nπjlogπj.H\;=\;-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}\log\pi_{j}. (3)

A symmetric construction with pϕ​(𝐞I∣𝐞T)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}\mid\mathbf{e}_{T}) yields the score for image-to-text retrieval. Low HH corresponds to a sharply constrained query (posterior mass on few candidates); high HH to one consistent with many plausible matches. We note that HH is the Shannon entropy of a discrete distribution 𝝅\boldsymbol{\pi} obtained by vMF kernel-density aggregation of MM posterior samples over the NN-item gallery, not the differential entropy of the continuous posterior pϕ​(𝐞T∣𝐞I)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}) itself; calibration accordingly depends on κ\kappa, MM, and NN, whose effects we analyse in Appendix C.5 and Sec. D.

Bayes-risk interpretation.

The score (3) has a decision-theoretic interpretation. Treating the gallery as a finite classification problem: true item X∈[N]X\in[N], query 𝐞I\mathbf{e}_{I} as observation, and 𝝅\boldsymbol{\pi} as the per-query posterior P⁡(X∣𝐞I)P(X\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}); the MAP predictor y^=arg⁡maxj⁡πj\hat{y}=\arg\max_{j}\pi_{j} incurs 0/10/1 Bayes risk r=1−maxj⁡πjr=1-\max_{j}\pi_{j}. Applying Fano’s inequality [10] to this per-query classification problem gives,

H⁡(𝝅)≤H2​(r)+r​log⁡(N−1),H(\boldsymbol{\pi})\;\leq\;H_{2}(r)+r\log(N-1), (4)

where H2H_{2} is binary entropy. The function f⁡(r)=H2​(r)+r​log⁡(N−1)f(r)=H_{2}(r)+r\log(N-1) is strictly increasing on [0,(N−1)/N][0,(N-1)/N], so the bound is invertible: a query with H⁡(𝝅)≥cH(\boldsymbol{\pi})\geq c satisfies r≥f−1​(c)r\geq f^{-1}(c), yielding a rigorous lower bound on MAP Bayes risk from entropy alone. The bound achieves equality when the non-mode mass is spread uniformly, i.e. πj=r/(N−1)\pi_{j}=r/(N-1) for all j≠y^j\neq\hat{y}; for other posterior shapes, and for large galleries where log⁡(N−1)\log(N-1) dominates, the bound is conservative. Consequently, HH should be read as a monotone proxy for posterior risk—it certifies a lower bound on rr but does not estimate rr precisely. We stress that rr is Bayes risk under 𝝅\boldsymbol{\pi}, which is a kernel-density approximation to pϕp_{\phi} on the gallery rather than the true data conditional; whether HH tracks true retrieval difficulty is an empirical question addressed in Sec. 5.

4.2 Density-Based Epistemic Scores

In the post-hoc setting we consider, the encoder is frozen and there is no Bayesian posterior over its parameters; the natural surrogate is typicality under the learned density, following standard practice for likelihood-based OOD scoring [20]. We use “epistemic score” in this density-based sense throughout and do not claim a Bayesian decomposition of total uncertainty.

Decomposition into typicality and cross-modal matching.

The chain rule applied to the joint log-density gives an exact decomposition that separates two categorically different signals:

−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T)=[−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T)]⏟epistemic typicality​−PMIϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T)⏟cross-modal matching,-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})\;=\;\underbrace{\bigl[-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T})\bigr]}_{\text{epistemic typicality}}\;\underbrace{-\,\mathrm{PMI}_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})}_{\text{cross-modal matching}}, (5)

where PMIϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T):=log⁡[pϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T)/(pϕ​(𝐞I)​pϕ​(𝐞T))]\mathrm{PMI}_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T}):=\log[p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})/(p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})\,p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}))] is the pointwise mutual information under the learned joint. The first term measures how typical each embedding is under the learned per-modality distributions: low typicality signals that the model has poor coverage of that region, which is the standard density-based epistemic proxy. The second term is the log-likelihood ratio between the joint and the product of marginals: it measures the cross-modal compatibility of a specific image-text pair, and is structurally discriminative rather than epistemic. The distinction is most concrete in zero-shot classification: PMIϕ\mathrm{PMI}_{\phi} there measures image–class compatibility rather than distributional coverage—it is confidence-like, not epistemic—and is detrimental as a standalone score on every benchmark (Sec. 5.2). We consequently use the marginal typicality sum as the epistemic score and validate this choice empirically in Sec. 5.2,

uep​(𝐞I,𝐞T)=−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T).u_{\mathrm{ep}}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})\;=\;-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}). (6)

Estimation via the joint flow.

Both marginals in Eq. (6) are obtained from the same trained model without additional parameters or retraining. Using Eq. (2),

−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)=−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I,𝐞T)+log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T∣𝐞I),-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})\;=\;-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})\;+\;\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}), (7)

and symmetrically for −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T)-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}). Computing uepu_{\mathrm{ep}} therefore requires three probability flow ODE solves on the same model: one joint and two conditional, each via reverse-time integration of the manifold change-of-variables identity with a tangent-projected Hutchinson trace estimator. These are numerical approximations whose quality depends on solver step count and probe count; we study their sensitivity in Appendix D; Appendix C.6 provides the algorithm and an unbiasedness lemma.

5 Experiments

We evaluate GeoFlowVLM as a post-hoc probabilistic layer operating entirely in embedding space without modifying the underlying encoder. The experiments in the main text are instantiated on a frozen OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 (laion2b_s32b_b79k) [31, 6] backbone, one of the strongest publicly available hyperspherical dual-encoder VLMs; we additionally evaluate on SigLIP (ViT-L-16-SigLIP-384) [43] in Appendix D to probe backbone generality; evaluating at larger scales and on EVA-CLIP is left to future work. Following the ALIGN paradigm [18], which demonstrated that large-scale noisy web supervision can produce strong vision-language representations, we train all models once on the Conceptual Captions dataset (CC-1M; 1M image–text pairs) with no task-specific fine-tuning, then evaluate zero-shot on seven held-out benchmarks spanning three retrieval datasets (both retrieval directions) and four zero-shot classification datasets, covering in-distribution, fine-grained, and viewpoint-shifted domains. Full details of the optimisation, architecture, and training curriculum are provided in the supplementary material (Sec.  C). We probe two complementary uncertainty readouts from the single trained model:

(Q1) Does GeoFlowVLM’s retrieval entropy, derived from the conditional posteriors pϕ​(𝐞T∣𝐞I)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}) and pϕ​(𝐞I∣𝐞T)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}\mid\mathbf{e}_{T}), reliably predict query difficulty in both retrieval directions across unseen datasets?

(Q2) Does the marginal typicality sum uep=−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T)u_{\mathrm{ep}}=-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}) serve as a reliable density-based epistemic score, yielding monotonically increasing selective accuracy as coverage decreases across unseen datasets?

5.1 Retrieval Entropy as Aleatoric Uncertainty (Q1)

Q1 probes whether the retrieval entropy HH defined in Sec. 4.1, computed from GeoFlowVLM’s conditional posteriors, is a calibrated estimator of cross-modal aleatoric ambiguity that tracks retrieval difficulty symmetrically in both directions.

Evaluation setup. Following 39, queries are ranked by HH and partitioned into ten equal-frequency bins, with Recall@1 computed within each bin. A calibrated score produces a monotone decrease from low to high entropy bins, summarised by Spearman ρ\rho and R2R^{2} of a linear fit to bin-wise error. We compare with ProbVLM [39], AsymVLM-PSD and AsymVLM-vMF [19], and GroVE [40], all trained on CC1M and evaluated zero-shot on MS-COCO, Flickr, and CUB-200. Dataset and baseline details are available in Appendices A.1 and A.2.

Results. Table 2 and Figure 1 show that GeoFlowVLM achieves negative monotonicity across all datasets and both directions, with the best ρ\rho and R2R^{2} in I2T and on Flickr30k and CUB-200 in T2I; on MS-COCO T2I, AsymVLM-vMF’s vMF prior yields higher R2R^{2} (0.9260.926 vs. 0.8950.895). ProbVLM’s ρ\rho is strongly positive in I2T, indicating its uncertainty rises on correctly retrieved queries; AsymVLM and GroVE show unstable trends in some cases, with R2R^{2} collapsing on CUB-200. The bin-wise curves confirm a monotone decrease for GeoFlowVLM while baselines plateau, oscillate, or invert. These results indicate that the conditional posterior entropy is a calibrated and direction-symmetric estimator of cross-modal aleatoric uncertainty that transfers from web-caption training to fine-grained OOD retrieval.

5.2 Likelihood-Based Epistemic Uncertainty (Q2)

Density-based epistemic uncertainty reflects the absence of support for a test sample under the joint distribution learned during training. As GeoFlowVLM is trained on CC1M and evaluated zero-shot downstream, samples with different visual statistics or label spaces are likely to lie in weakly supported regions of pϕp_{\phi}, motivating uepu_{\mathrm{ep}} as an epistemic signal. We note that this is a typicality-based notion of epistemic uncertainty; we do not estimate a posterior over model parameters.

Evaluation setup. Following the protocol of REPVLM [20], samples are ranked by their epistemic score in ascending order and filtered at coverage levels c∈{100%,90%,…,10%}c\in\{100\%,90\%,\ldots,10\%\}, retaining the fraction cc of least uncertain samples at each level. A useful epistemic score should yield monotonically increasing accuracy as coverage decreases, since rejecting high-uncertainty samples should preferentially remove misclassified examples. All methods are trained on CC1M and evaluated zero-shot on ImageNet-1K [13], Food-101 [4], CIFAR-100 [22], and ObjectNet [1] using 10,00010{,}000 samples per dataset. We focus on density-based epistemic scores, which measure typicality under a learned distribution, and compare against REPVLM [20], which scores negative log-density on the hyperspherical embedding manifold, and ProbVLM [39], which estimates uncertainty through MC dropout over its probabilistic adapter. BayesVLM [2] obtains a Gaussian covariance over embeddings via last-layer Laplace approximation; because it does not expose a pointwise log-density score for individual test embeddings, it does not admit the typicality-based evaluation protocol used here and is not included. Confidence-based proxies such as maximum cosine similarity over class prompts measure prediction sharpness rather than distributional support and address a different question; we leave such comparisons to future work.

Component analysis. We validate the chain-rule decomposition in Eq. (5) by evaluating each term as a standalone epistemic score across the four datasets (Figure 2 and Table 3). Both marginal-typicality terms produce strong signals: text typicality −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T)-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}) leads on three of four benchmarks while image typicality −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}) leads on Food-101, with small absolute differences in all but one case. The exception is ObjectNet, where viewpoint shift moves image embeddings away from the training support and image typicality drops sharply (0.6000.600 vs. 0.6910.691 for text). The PMI term is, by a clear margin, the weakest signal on every benchmark, and is the only term whose selective-prediction curves trend downward (Figure 2)—it preferentially rejects correct predictions, consistent with its structurally discriminative rather than epistemic status (Sec. 4.2). Because the two marginals individually carry the epistemic signal but neither uniformly dominates, we use their sum uepu_{\mathrm{ep}} for all benchmark comparisons below; the sum also enhances robustness against the modality-specific failure mode evident on ObjectNet.

Results. Since no single component dominates uniformly across datasets (Table 3), we use the marginal typicality sum uep=−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T)u_{\mathrm{ep}}=-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}) as the epistemic score for all benchmark comparisons. Figure 3 shows that GeoFlowVLM produces a consistent monotone increase in accuracy with rejected fraction across all four datasets. Performance is numerically comparable to REPVLM across most benchmarks with neither method dominating uniformly. We do not claim a numerical advantage over REPVLM on this task: the epistemic contribution of GeoFlowVLM is primarily analytical. The chain-rule decomposition (Eq. 5) explains why marginal typicality is the correct epistemic signal and why PMI is not—providing principled grounding for the per-modality scoring approach that REPVLM uses empirically. Concretely, marginals derived from the jointly trained model are coherent by construction (via Eq. 2), whereas REPVLM trains two independent flows whose individual densities carry no cross-modal consistency guarantee; the same joint model delivers aleatoric uncertainty without additional training or parameters. ObjectNet provides a case where this structural difference matters empirically: under SigLIP’s more isotropic embeddings, GeoFlowVLM maintains a stronger advantage over REPVLM on ObjectNet specifically (Appendix D). Viewpoint-shifted images may not appear atypical under an image marginal trained in isolation—SigLIP’s flatter geometry suppresses that signal—but their departure from typical training-time image–text co-occurrences remains detectable via the jointly trained marginal, which inherits cross-modal structure that a per-modality flow cannot encode.

Ablations.

We ablate four design choices (full results in Appendix D). (i) A Euclidean baseline identical to GeoFlowVLM in architecture, training data, and optimisation, but operating in ℝd×ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d} without sphere projection or geodesic interpolation—underperforms GeoFlowVLM on both aleatoric and epistemic metrics across all datasets, confirming hyperspherical geometry is necessary (Appendix D.1). (ii) Aleatoric calibration recovers sharply at λ≥1\lambda\geq 1 and plateaus; we use λ=3\lambda=3 (Appendix D.4). (iii) Reliable uncertainty estimates do not require many integration steps or probes: quality is stable across N∈{10,…,50}N\in\{10,\ldots,50\} ODE steps (Appendix D.2) and nprobe∈{1,…,5}n_{\mathrm{probe}}\in\{1,\ldots,5\} Hutchinson probes (Appendix D.3); we adopt N=50N=50 and nprobe=1n_{\mathrm{probe}}=1 as conservative defaults, although the ablations show that NN as low as 1010 is sufficient at no measurable cost in estimation quality. (iv) SigLIP (ViT-L-16-SigLIP-384) probes a qualitatively different embedding geometry: its sigmoid loss scores pairs independently without CLIP’s cross-pair softmax denominator, producing more isotropic distributions on 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}. The architecture is otherwise unchanged (d=1024d=1024): aleatoric calibration transfers consistently, while epistemic discrimination degrades on ImageNet-1K for density-based methods but not ProbVLM, attributed to flatter learned density and weaker typicality-based OOD contrast under more uniform embeddings (Appendix D.5).

6 Conclusions, Limitations, Discussion and Broader Impact

GeoFlowVLM demonstrates that a geometry-aware joint density model over frozen hyperspherical VLM embeddings trained once on large-scale data and applied zero-shot—suffices to recover both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty across diverse downstream tasks, addressing a gap left by methods that do not account for the underlying hyperspherical geometry or treat modalities independently. The joint distribution on the product hypersphere is strictly richer than any per-modality model: its conditional posteriors expose cross-modal aleatoric ambiguity inaccessible to per-modality flows, while the chain-rule decomposition of the joint NLL identifies marginal typicality as the right epistemic signal, explains why PMI is not, and accounts for the empirical effectiveness of approaches such as REPVLM. We hope this work encourages joint embedding modelling as a general strategy for richer probabilistic reasoning over vision-language representations.

As a post-hoc generative adapter, GeoFlowVLM requires one-time training on large-scale data (unlike training-free approaches such as BayesVLM [2]) and three ODE solves per query for uepu_{\mathrm{ep}}; both uncertainty estimates depend on kernel bandwidth, step count, and proxy-corpus match, and calibration is bounded by encoder quality. The epistemic scores are density-based rather than Bayesian—measuring typicality under the learned joint, not parameter-posterior variance—and the consistency theorem (Theorem 1) is a population-limit result; quantitative finite-sample bounds remain open. SigLIP experiments (Appendix D) confirm aleatoric calibration transfers across backbones and GeoFlowVLM outperforms REPVLM on three of four epistemic benchmarks; degradation of both density-based methods on ImageNet-1K (while ProbVLM is unaffected) may be attributed to SigLIP’s sigmoid objective producing more isotropic embeddings [42, 43], which flatten the learned density and weaken typicality-based OOD contrast. As the method operates over frozen embeddings without generating new content, direct misuse risk is limited; improved VLM uncertainty quantification has clear value in safety-critical applications such as medical retrieval and autonomous systems.

A.1 Datasets

All models are trained on Conceptual Captions 1M (CC1M) [34], a web-crawled image-caption dataset providing one caption per image. Embeddings for all datasets are precomputed using OpenCLIP with a ViT-H/14 backbone initialized from the laion2b_s32b_b79k checkpoint, with standard OpenCLIP image preprocessing and matching text tokenization – and similarly for the SigLIP (ViT-L-16-SigLIP-384) [43] backbone for additional experiments presented in Sec. D.5. All embeddings are ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalized before use. Precomputing and caching embeddings allows all training and evaluation to operate directly in the shared multimodal embedding space without repeatedly running the encoder.

Table 4 summarises all datasets. For aleatoric uncertainty (Q1), we use three cross-modal retrieval benchmarks capped at 5,000 test samples each: MS-COCO [26] and Flickr [30] provide five captions per image, while CUB-200 [41] provides ten human-written captions per image [33] and introduces fine-grained visual ambiguity across 200 bird species alongside a domain shift from CC1M. For epistemic uncertainty (Q2), we use four zero-shot classification benchmarks capped at 10,000 test samples each: ImageNet-1K [13], Food-101 [4], CIFAR-100 [22], and ObjectNet [1].

A.2 Baselines

We compare against four post-hoc probabilistic baselines for frozen vision-language models. The descriptions below follow the original papers; when used in our experiments, these methods are instantiated on frozen CLIP embeddings under a common training protocol for fair comparison. Table 5 summarises the main modeling choices most relevant to our Q1 and Q2 evaluations.

ProbVLM [39]

ProbVLM is a post-hoc probabilistic adapter that converts deterministic frozen VLM embeddings into probabilistic embeddings through separate image and text adaptors. The method can model both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. It captures aleatoric uncertainty by predicting the parameters of a heteroscedastic Generalized Gaussian Distribution (GGD) for each embedding, while training combines intra-modal reconstruction with cross-modal alignment. It captures epistemic uncertainty by keeping dropout active at inference and measuring the variance across multiple stochastic forward passes through the adaptors.

AsymVLM [19]

AsymVLM is a post-hoc probabilistic adaptation method designed for the hyperspherical geometry of pretrained VLM embeddings. Its main formulation is asymmetric: text embeddings are modeled probabilistically using directional distributions on the unit hypersphere, while image embeddings remain deterministic. The paper studies two principal directional choices, von Mises–Fisher (vMF) and Power Spherical (PS), and optimizes a likelihood-based objective derived from an InfoNCE-style formulation. AsymVLM is primarily designed to model aleatoric uncertainty in language rather than density-based epistemic uncertainty.

GroVE [40]

GroVE is a post-hoc probabilistic embedding method based on a multimodal Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model (GPLVM). It learns a shared low-dimensional latent representation for each paired image–text example and uses two sparse variational Gaussian Processes, one per modality, to map this latent representation back to the observed embedding spaces. GroVE primarily models aleatoric uncertainty, capturing ambiguity in image–text correspondence through a predictive Gaussian embedding whose covariance arises from uncertainty in the inferred latent representation and the GP prediction.

REPVLM [20]

REPVLM explicitly models density-based epistemic uncertainty. It learns the conditional embedding density p⁡(𝐳∣c)p(\mathbf{z}\mid c) on the hypersphere using conditional Riemannian flow matching, where 𝐳\mathbf{z} is a pretrained VLM embedding and cc is a discrete modality label indicating whether the embedding comes from the image or text encoder. Its epistemic uncertainty score is the conditional negative log-likelihood −log⁡p⁡(𝐳∣c)-\log p(\mathbf{z}\mid c), so embeddings in low-density regions of the manifold are interpreted as regions of model ignorance.

Appendix B GeoFlowVLM Architecture

The joint velocity field 𝐯tϕ​(𝐳t,𝐦)\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{m}) is parameterised by a symmetric dual-stream architecture, illustrated in Figure 4. The noisy joint embedding 𝐳t\mathbf{z}_{t} is split into 𝐳I\mathbf{z}_{I} and 𝐳T\mathbf{z}_{T}, each projected into a shared HH-dimensional latent space by modality-specific Image and Text Projection encoders, each consisting of a linear layer followed by LayerNorm. In parallel, the per-stream flow times tI,tTt_{I},t_{T} and the discrete flow direction dir∈{0,1,2}\mathrm{dir}\in\{0,1,2\} are processed by the CondMLP module: each time signal is encoded via Random Fourier Features [32] with frozen random frequencies, providing broader spectral coverage over t∈[0,1]t\in[0,1] than a fixed sinusoidal grid, and summed with a learned direction embedding before being passed through a per-stream two-layer MLP with SiLU activations. This produces conditioning vectors 𝐜I,𝐜T∈ℝH\mathbf{c}_{I},\mathbf{c}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{H} that are computed once and shared across all subsequent blocks.

The projected hidden states 𝐡I,𝐡T\mathbf{h}_{I},\mathbf{h}_{T} are then refined by LL stacked DualStreamBlocks (DSBs). Each DSB applies two sequential sub-layers to both streams. In the first sub-layer, each stream hidden state is modulated by its conditioning vector via DiT-style AdaLN-Zero [29], which applies an affine-free LayerNorm followed by a learned shift and scale derived from 𝐜I\mathbf{c}_{I} or 𝐜T\mathbf{c}_{T}, with the projection zero-initialised so that conditioning begins as the identity and is learned incrementally. The modulated state is then passed through a multi-head Cross Gate, where the hidden space is partitioned into nheadsn_{\mathrm{heads}} independent subspaces and in each subspace the receiving stream selectively absorbs information from the sending stream:

CrossGate⁡(𝐱,𝐬)=σ⁡(Wgate​𝐱)⊙Wval​𝐬,\mathrm{CrossGate}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\sigma\!\left(W_{\mathrm{gate}}\,\mathbf{x}\right)\odot W_{\mathrm{val}}\,\mathbf{s}, (8)

where σ\sigma is the sigmoid function, WgateW_{\mathrm{gate}} operates on the receiving stream to produce a data-dependent gate, and WvalW_{\mathrm{val}} transforms the sending stream independently. This enforces the inductive bias that CLIP features factor into independent semantic subspaces while allowing asymmetric cross-modal alignment. All WvalW_{\mathrm{val}} projections are zero-initialised for stability. The cross-gate output is scaled by LayerScale [38] initialised at 10−410^{-4} and added back to the stream via a residual connection. In the second sub-layer, another AdaLN-Zero modulates the updated hidden state before a SwiGLU feed-forward network [35], which computes SiLU⁡(Wgate​𝐱)⊙Wval​𝐱\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{\mathrm{gate}}\mathbf{x})\odot W_{\mathrm{val}}\mathbf{x} and consistently outperforms GELU at equal parameter count, again followed by a LayerScale-weighted residual connection.

After all DSBs, the Image and Text Decoders map each stream through a LayerNorm and a zero-initialised linear projection back to the original embedding dimension dd. The two outputs are concatenated to form the full joint velocity 𝐯tϕ∈ℝ2​d\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}. Zero initialisation of the decoders ensures near-zero initial velocities, preventing the model from displacing embeddings off ℳ\mathcal{M} at the start of training and promoting stable geodesic transport.

For conditional generation under either of the two conditional masks, classifier-free guidance [16] can be applied at inference by linearly interpolating between conditional and unconditional velocity fields,

where λ≥0\lambda\geq 0 is the guidance scale and the unconditional field is obtained by substituting the conditioning stream with its OT-coupled noise source 𝐳0cond\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\mathrm{cond}}, consistent with the CFG dropout used during training. Increasing λ\lambda sharpens the generated distribution towards the conditioning signal.

C.3 Product Hypersphere Geometry and Masked CFM Mechanics

Because CLIP embeddings are ℓ2\ell_{2}-normalized, each image embedding 𝐞I\mathbf{e}_{I} and text embedding 𝐞T\mathbf{e}_{T} lies on the unit hypersphere 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}. For paired embeddings we therefore work on the product manifold,

ℳ=𝕊d−1×𝕊d−1,𝐳=(𝐞I,𝐞T),\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\qquad\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T}),

rather than a single sphere formed by concatenation. This matches the Riemannian flow matching setting on simple manifolds and their product spaces.

Base distribution.

We take p0p_{0} to be the uniform distribution on ℳ=𝕊d−1×𝕊d−1\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{d-1} (with respect to the product of round volume measures). Concretely, samples 𝐳0=(𝐞I0,𝐞T0)∼p0\mathbf{z}_{0}=(\mathbf{e}_{I}^{0},\mathbf{e}_{T}^{0})\sim p_{0} are obtained by drawing each block from a unit Gaussian in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} and renormalising to 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1}, independently across blocks. Under this choice log⁡p0\log p_{0} is a constant equal to the negative of the log-volume of ℳ\mathcal{M}, which simplifies the change-of-variables identity used for log-density evaluation in Sec. C.6.

Tangent spaces and projection.

The tangent space factorizes as,

T𝐳​ℳ=T𝐞I​𝕊d−1×T𝐞T​𝕊d−1,T𝐞​𝕊d−1={𝐯∈ℝd:⟨𝐯,𝐞⟩=0}.T_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{M}=T_{\mathbf{e}_{I}}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times T_{\mathbf{e}_{T}}\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\qquad T_{\mathbf{e}}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}\rangle=0\}.

Accordingly, if the network outputs a raw ambient-space velocity (𝐯I,𝐯T)(\mathbf{v}_{I},\mathbf{v}_{T}), we project it blockwise onto the tangent space,

Π𝐳​(𝐯I,𝐯T)=((I−𝐞I​𝐞I⊤)​𝐯I,(I−𝐞T​𝐞T⊤)​𝐯T).\Pi_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{v}_{I},\mathbf{v}_{T})=\bigl((I-\mathbf{e}_{I}\mathbf{e}_{I}^{\top})\mathbf{v}_{I},\,(I-\mathbf{e}_{T}\mathbf{e}_{T}^{\top})\mathbf{v}_{T}\bigr).

In exact continuous-time dynamics, a tangent vector field keeps trajectories on ℳ\mathcal{M}. In practice, numerical solvers may still use reprojection to control drift.

Geodesics, exponential maps, and logarithmic maps.

Under the product metric, geodesics decouple across the two sphere factors. For each sphere,

d𝕊​(𝐱,𝐲)=arccos⁡⟨𝐱,𝐲⟩,d_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\arccos\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle,
exp𝐱⁡(𝐯)=cos⁡(‖𝐯‖)​𝐱+sin⁡(‖𝐯‖)​𝐯‖𝐯‖,log𝐱⁡(𝐲)=θsin⁡θ​(𝐲−cos⁡θ​𝐱),θ=d𝕊​(𝐱,𝐲).\exp_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{v})=\cos(\|\mathbf{v}\|)\,\mathbf{x}+\sin(\|\mathbf{v}\|)\,\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|},\qquad\log_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})=\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\bigl(\mathbf{y}-\cos\theta\,\mathbf{x}\bigr),\quad\theta=d_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Using the geodesic-distance premetric and κ⁡(t)=1−t\kappa(t)=1-t, the conditional RFM path is the constant-speed geodesic,

𝐳t=exp𝐳1⁡((1−t)​log𝐳1⁡(𝐳0)),\mathbf{z}_{t}=\exp_{\mathbf{z}_{1}}\!\bigl((1-t)\log_{\mathbf{z}_{1}}(\mathbf{z}_{0})\bigr),

where the exponential and logarithmic maps are applied componentwise on the two sphere factors.

Masked interpolant.

Writing 𝐠t\mathbf{g}_{t} for the per-block geodesic operator and decomposing 𝐳=[𝐞I;𝐞T]\mathbf{z}=[\mathbf{e}_{I};\mathbf{e}_{T}], the masked interpolant used in ℒGeoFlowVLM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GeoFlowVLM}} is,

𝐳t=(𝟏2​d−𝐦)⊙𝐳1+𝐦⊙𝐠t​(𝐳0,𝐳1),𝐠t​(𝐚,𝐛)=sin⁡((1−t)​ω)sin⁡ω​𝐚+sin⁡(t​ω)sin⁡ω​𝐛,\mathbf{z}_{t}\;=\;(\mathbf{1}_{2d}-\mathbf{m})\odot\mathbf{z}_{1}\;+\;\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{1}),\qquad\mathbf{g}_{t}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\;=\;\tfrac{\sin((1-t)\omega)}{\sin\omega}\,\mathbf{a}+\tfrac{\sin(t\omega)}{\sin\omega}\,\mathbf{b},

with ω=arccos⁡⟨𝐚,𝐛⟩\omega=\arccos\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle. Conditioned dimensions (𝐦k=0\mathbf{m}_{k}=0) are pinned at 𝐳1\mathbf{z}_{1} for all tt; generated dimensions (𝐦k=1\mathbf{m}_{k}=1) follow the geodesic from 𝐳0\mathbf{z}_{0} to 𝐳1\mathbf{z}_{1}.

Target velocity.

The flow-matching regression target on the generated dimensions is the tangent vector of the geodesic,

𝐯t​(𝐳t∣𝐳1)=𝐦⊙ωsin⁡ω​[cos⁡(t​ω)​𝐳1−cos⁡((1−t)​ω)​𝐳0],\mathbf{v}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{t}\mid\mathbf{z}_{1})\;=\;\mathbf{m}\odot\frac{\omega}{\sin\omega}\bigl[\cos(t\omega)\,\mathbf{z}_{1}-\cos((1-t)\omega)\,\mathbf{z}_{0}\bigr],

with ω\omega computed per block. On conditioned dimensions (𝐦k=0\mathbf{m}_{k}=0) the target is identically zero, so the mask in ℒGeoFlowVLM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GeoFlowVLM}} removes any supervision there.

OT Coupling.

Given a data sample 𝐳1=[𝐞I1,𝐞T1]∼p⁡(𝐳)\mathbf{z}_{1}=[\mathbf{e}_{I}^{1},\mathbf{e}_{T}^{1}]\sim p(\mathbf{z}) on the product sphere 𝕊dI−1×𝕊dT−1\mathbb{S}^{d_{I}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d_{T}-1}, we draw a source sample 𝐳0=[𝐞I0,𝐞T0]\mathbf{z}_{0}=[\mathbf{e}_{I}^{0},\mathbf{e}_{T}^{0}] by projecting independent Gaussian samples onto each sphere factor. To reduce the variance of the flow matching objective and encourage more coherent geodesic trajectories, we pair source and target samples within each minibatch via entropic optimal transport [11, 37], using the sum of geodesic arc distances across both modalities as the transport cost,

c⁡(𝐳0,𝐳1)=d𝕊​(𝐞I0,𝐞I1)+d𝕊​(𝐞T0,𝐞T1),d𝕊​(𝐚,𝐛)=arccos⁡(⟨𝐚,𝐛⟩).c(\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{1})=d_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{e}_{I}^{0},\mathbf{e}_{I}^{1})+d_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{e}_{T}^{0},\mathbf{e}_{T}^{1}),\qquad d_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\arccos(\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle). (10)

Given uniform marginals, an entropically regularized transport plan is computed via Sinkhorn’s algorithm with regularization ε=0.05\varepsilon=0.05, and converted to a deterministic assignment via row-wise argmax, which approximates a hard coupling. Because targets are assigned as paired product-space samples (𝐞I1,𝐞T1)(\mathbf{e}_{I}^{1},\mathbf{e}_{T}^{1}), the coupling preserves the empirical image-text pairing structure during minibatch matching, unlike separate per-modality OT which could assign inconsistent image and text targets to the same source. We use a large minibatch size of B=8192B=8192 to reduce OT estimation noise; smaller batches produce less stable couplings and worse training behaviour.

Per-mask time scheduling.

Times are sampled per stream. Under the joint mask 𝐦joint\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{joint}} both streams share a single t∼𝒰⁡[0,1]t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]. Under 𝐦I→T\mathbf{m}_{I\to T} the text stream receives t∼𝒰⁡[0,1]t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1] while the image stream is fixed at t=0t=0; under 𝐦T→I\mathbf{m}_{T\to I} the roles are reversed. The conditional reduction in Theorem 1 is driven by the mask zeroing the loss on conditioned dimensions and the interpolant pinning those dimensions to 𝐳1\mathbf{z}_{1}, so the population-limit theorem is robust to the time-schedule choice. We adopt the fixed-t=0t{=}0 convention on conditioned streams as a deliberate operational choice: it ensures the network sees a clean conditioner, matching how the model is queried at inference, and aligns the training and inference evaluation regimes, simplifying generalisation arguments under finite samples and finite capacity.

Riemannian divergence on embedded submanifolds.

For ℳ⊂ℝ2​d\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d} embedded as in Sec. 3, write Π𝐳∈ℝ2​d×2​d\Pi_{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d} for the orthogonal projector onto T𝐳​ℳT_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{M} (the blockwise projector diag⁡(I−𝐞I​𝐞I⊤,I−𝐞T​𝐞T⊤)\mathrm{diag}(I-\mathbf{e}_{I}\mathbf{e}_{I}^{\top},\,I-\mathbf{e}_{T}\mathbf{e}_{T}^{\top})). Let 𝐯~t:ℝ2​d→ℝ2​d\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d} be any smooth ambient extension of 𝐯t\mathbf{v}_{t} that agrees with 𝐯t\mathbf{v}_{t} on ℳ\mathcal{M} and takes tangent values there. The Riemannian divergence of 𝐯t\mathbf{v}_{t} on ℳ\mathcal{M} admits the ambient form,

divℳ𝐯t(𝐳)=tr(Π𝐳∇𝐯~t(𝐳)Π𝐳),\mathrm{div}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{v}_{t}(\mathbf{z})\;=\;\mathrm{tr}\!\bigl(\Pi_{\mathbf{z}}\,\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{z})\,\Pi_{\mathbf{z}}\bigr), (11)

where ∇𝐯~t\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{t} is the ambient Jacobian. This is the standard expression for the divergence of a tangent vector field on an embedded submanifold and will be used in Sec. C.6.

On the simultaneous attainability of all three minimisers by a single network.

A reader might worry that since one network must minimise three objectives that share parameters, the population minima of the three sub-objectives might not be jointly attainable. They are, in the population limit, because each pointwise minimiser is determined by a disjoint conditioning event: the masked CFM target depends on (𝐦,𝐳t,t)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{z}_{t},t), and the three masks 𝐦joint,𝐦I→T,𝐦T→I\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{joint}},\mathbf{m}_{I\to T},\mathbf{m}_{T\to I} partition the input space into disjoint regions (the network observes the mask explicitly as input). Therefore the three pointwise minimisers do not compete at any single (𝐦,𝐳t,t)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{z}_{t},t). Realising the population minimum requires only that the function class is large enough to represent the union of three target functions on disjoint domains, which is implied by (A3).

Remark on finite samples and finite capacity.

The theorem is a population-limit identity assuming a sufficiently expressive function class so that (13) is attained. In practice, the trained network only approximates (13), and the gap between the empirical conditional flow and the true conditional distribution is controlled by the generalisation gap of ℒI→T\mathcal{L}_{I\to T}. We do not provide a quantitative bound; consistency arguments for CFM under finite capacity and finite samples remain an active area of theoretical work and are outside the scope of this paper.

C.5 Retrieval Entropy: Computational Details

We give the full computational pipeline for the aleatoric score HH defined in Sec. 4.1. We describe the text-to-image direction; the image-to-text case is symmetric.

Posterior sampling.

Given a text query 𝐞T\mathbf{e}_{T}, we sample MM image embeddings from the conditional flow by integrating the Riemannian ODE under mask 𝐦T→I\mathbf{m}_{T\to I},

𝐞I(0,m)∼p0,d​𝐞I(t,m)d​t=[𝐯tϕ​((𝐞I(t,m),𝐞T),𝐦T→I)]I,\mathbf{e}_{I}^{(0,m)}\sim p_{0},\qquad\frac{d\mathbf{e}_{I}^{(t,m)}}{dt}=\bigl[\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\phi}\!\bigl((\mathbf{e}_{I}^{(t,m)},\mathbf{e}_{T}),\mathbf{m}_{T\to I}\bigr)\bigr]_{I},

projecting onto 𝕊d−1\mathbb{S}^{d-1} after each step to prevent manifold drift. We collect the endpoint samples 𝐞^I(m):=𝐞I(1,m)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{I}^{(m)}:=\mathbf{e}_{I}^{(1,m)} for m=1,…,Mm=1,\dots,M. By Theorem 1 these are samples from pϕ​(𝐞I∣𝐞T)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}\mid\mathbf{e}_{T}) in the population limit.

Gallery aggregation.

For each gallery item 𝐠j∈𝕊d−1\mathbf{g}_{j}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}, j=1,…,Nj=1,\dots,N, we aggregate the MM posterior samples into a single similarity score via a vMF-style log-sum-exp,

sj=log⁡1M​∑m=1Mexp⁡(κ​𝐠j⊤​𝐞^I(m)).s_{j}\;=\;\log\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\exp\!\bigl(\kappa\,\mathbf{g}_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{I}^{(m)}\bigr).

This is the log-density of an unnormalised vMF kernel-density estimate of pϕ​(𝐞I∣𝐞T)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}\mid\mathbf{e}_{T}) at 𝐠j\mathbf{g}_{j}, with concentration parameter κ>0\kappa>0 controlling kernel sharpness: at κ→0\kappa\to 0, sjs_{j} becomes constant in jj and HH saturates at log⁡N\log N; larger κ\kappa sharpens discrimination between gallery items.

Softmax normalisation and entropy.

Normalising across the gallery yields a posterior 𝝅=(π1,…,πN)\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N}) with πj=esj/∑kesk\pi_{j}=e^{s_{j}}/\sum_{k}e^{s_{k}}, whose Shannon entropy,

H=−∑j=1NπjlogπjH\;=\;-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}\log\pi_{j}

is reported as the aleatoric score for the query.

Hyperparameters and their roles.

Three quantities govern the score: the number of samples MM, the kernel concentration κ\kappa, and the gallery size NN. We use M=50M=50 samples per query and κ=100\kappa=100 throughout (Table 6). The estimator’s variance for a single probe is bounded by ∥Π𝐳∇𝐯~t(𝐳)Π𝐳∥F2\|\Pi_{\mathbf{z}}\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{z})\Pi_{\mathbf{z}}\|_{F}^{2} for Rademacher probes (and twice that for Gaussian probes), which is finite for our smooth network outputs and decreases as 1/K1/K with the number of probes; see [37, Sec. A]. Pipeline.

Putting everything together, the joint NLL of an image–text pair (𝐞I,𝐞T)(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T}) is computed by: (i) setting 𝐳1=(𝐞I,𝐞T)\mathbf{z}_{1}=(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T}); (ii) integrating the augmented reverse-time ODE (17) from t=1t=1 to t=0t=0 using the learned velocity field 𝐯t\mathbf{v}_{t} under the joint mask 𝐦joint\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{joint}}, with divergence estimated via (21); (iii) reading off s0s_{0}; and (iv) reporting uepjoint=−(C+s0)u_{\mathrm{ep}}^{\mathrm{joint}}=-(C+s_{0}), equivalently −s0-s_{0} up to a dataset-level constant. Marginal estimation in zero-shot classification.

For zero-shot classification we evaluate −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}) via the Bayes identity by pairing the test image embedding 𝐞I\mathbf{e}_{I} with the predicted class prompt 𝐞T(y^)\mathbf{e}_{T}^{(\hat{y})}, and conversely for −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T(y^))-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}^{(\hat{y})}). In the population limit the identity holds for any 𝐞T\mathbf{e}_{T} and the choice is immaterial; under finite-step Euler integration with a single Hutchinson probe, however, the joint and conditional path integrals only cancel in expectation, so different choices of 𝐞T\mathbf{e}_{T} yield numerically different marginal estimates and couple the marginal estimate to the prediction. Our ablations on NN and nproben_{\mathrm{probe}} (Sec. D.2, Sec. D.3) show that the resulting AUSAC is stable to the precision of the path integrals at the values we use. The coupling of −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}) to the prediction is therefore a numerical artifact of finite-step integration; the coupling of −log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T(y^))-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}^{(\hat{y})}) to the prediction is structural but intentional, reflecting that distinct class prompts occupy regions of the text manifold with different training-distribution support.

D.4 Effect of Guidance Scale λ\lambda on Aleatoric UQ

The guidance scale λ\lambda controls how strongly conditional sampling is steered toward the query-conditioned posterior. When λ=0\lambda=0, sampling reduces to the unconditional flow and ignores query-specific cross-modal structure, so the resulting posterior samples scatter across the gallery and the entropy H=−∑jπjlogπjH=-\sum_{j}\pi_{j}\log\pi_{j} carries no information about query difficulty. As λ\lambda increases, the sampled distributions pϕ​(𝐞T∣𝐞I)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}\mid\mathbf{e}_{I}) and pϕ​(𝐞I∣𝐞T)p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I}\mid\mathbf{e}_{T}) concentrate around modes compatible with the conditioning embedding: easy queries produce low-entropy posteriors concentrated on few gallery items, while hard queries remain spread out, making HH a meaningful aleatoric signal. We ablate λ∈{0,1,2,3,4,5,7}\lambda\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5,7\} on MS-COCO, Flickr8k, and CUB using 1,000 images per dataset, evaluating the Spearman correlation ρ\rho between per-query entropy and retrieval difficulty in both directions with all other factors fixed. As shown in Figure 9, ρ\rho is near zero or positive at λ=0\lambda=0, recovers strongly at λ≥1\lambda\geq 1, and remains stable across larger values. The T2I direction converges cleanly across all three datasets; I2T exhibits higher variance for CUB due to its dense 10-caption annotation structure, which destabilises per-bin recall estimates at this evaluation scale. Since retrieval rankings are derived from frozen CLIP embeddings, λ\lambda affects only the uncertainty estimates and not retrieval scores. We use λ=3\lambda=3 in all experiments as a conservative default within the stable regime.

D.5 Backbone Robustness: SigLIP

GeoFlowVLM is designed as a post-hoc probabilistic adapter over any frozen encoder that produces L2-normalized embeddings on the unit hypersphere. To verify that our results are not specific to the CLIP backbone used in the main experiments, we evaluate on SigLIP [43] (ViT-L-16-SigLIP-384, pretrained on WebLI). Crucially, SigLIP embeddings share the same dimensionality as CLIP ViT-H-14 (d=1024d=1024), so the GeoFlowVLM architecture and all baselines remain identical with no modification. We evaluate both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty following the same protocol as the main paper (Q1 and Q2 respectively).

Aleatoric Uncertainty (Q1).

Table 10 reports mean calibration performance under the SigLIP backbone, and Figure 10 shows the corresponding per-bin trends averaged over 5 independent runs. GeoFlowVLM achieves the best or near-best Spearman correlation and R2R^{2} across all three datasets in both retrieval directions, with AsymVLM variants competitive on MS-COCO T2I by a narrow margin. These results are consistent with the main paper findings on the CLIP backbone, confirming that the conditional retrieval entropy derived from the joint density generalizes to the SigLIP embedding space despite the differences in pretraining objective.

Epistemic Uncertainty (Q2).

Figure 11 reports selective accuracy curves under the SigLIP backbone. GeoFlowVLM’s epistemic score is uep​(𝐞I,𝐞T)=−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞I)−log⁡pϕ​(𝐞T)u_{\mathrm{ep}}(\mathbf{e}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{T})=-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{I})-\log p_{\phi}(\mathbf{e}_{T}), where both marginals are obtained via the chain-rule decomposition of the learned joint density without additional parameters; REPVLM uses the same functional form but fits each marginal independently.
