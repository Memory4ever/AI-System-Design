Independent Researcher\nL73: ###### Abstract\nL74: Interactive video generation commonly relies on small bidirectional chunks to reduce response latency. Yet our preliminary experiments show that larger chunks improve video quality and better utilize GPU parallelism, allowing more latents to be sampled with only a modest increase in sampling time. Motivated by these observations, we propose Speculative Sampling, a strategy that combines the modeling power of large bidirectional windows with the low-latency interaction of small ones.\nL75: It expands the modeling window by appending noise latents as padding and caches the resulting predictions for reuse when the condition remains unchanged. Predictions are delivered incrementally, while the uncommitted future remains replaceable. Our latency analysis shows that this removes the waiting-time lower bound imposed by chunk boundaries, shifting the optimization focus from reducing chunk size to accelerating per-chunk sampling.\nL76: Bidirectional window size thus becomes a decision variable for jointly optimizing visual quality and sampling time, rather than a necessary constraint on low-latency interaction. This opens a path to serving large bidirectional models in interactive streaming systems without complex streaming post-training. Project page: cite58†https://yubo-shankui.github.io/speculative_sampling†yubo-shankui.github.io .\nL77: ## 1 Introduction\nL78: \nL79: Interactive video generation requires newly arriving conditions to influence ongoing playback promptly. A viewer may change a camera command or action instruction at any time, while existing video continues to play. Generation throughput alone does not capture this requirement: a fast model can still leave the viewer waiting for a generation or playback boundary.\nL80: A natural route is fine-grained autoregressive generation. Advances in rollout training and few-step distillation have made this route increasingly practical (cite59†Huang et al., 2025 ; cite60†Zhao et al., 2026 ; cite61†Yang et al., 2025 ). Smaller generation chunks provide more frequent opportunities to incorporate new conditions, enabling fine-grained interaction, but may limit joint temporal modeling and parallel efficiency.\nL81: This raises our central question: must low-latency interaction come at the cost of a smaller bidirectional modeling window?\nL82: We introduce Speculative Sampling, a strategy that combines the modeling power of large bidirectional windows with the low-latency interaction of small ones. It expands the modeling window with padding latents and caches their predictions for reuse when the condition remains unchanged, while allowing the predicted future to be revised when new conditions arrive (Figure cite62†1 ).\nL83: Figure 1: Fine-grained interaction without shrinking generation chunks. Our method preserves large-chunk joint sampling while allowing updates beyond the protected prefix, rather than only at chunk boundaries. Gray/green denote old/new conditions; the dashed line marks playback position at condition arrival, and thick green edges mark updated output. Positions represent video content, not computation time.\nL84: We first formulate a serving model that separates condition arrivals, generation, output commitment, and playback. Generation is the computation needed for deliverable video output; for the latent diffusion models studied here, it includes DiT sampling and required VAE decoding. The model distinguishes generation time from phase-dependent random-arrival waiting imposed by scheduling and playback boundaries, and identifies replaceable outputs.\nL85: When new output can enter playback only at chunk boundaries, the chunk duration imposes a waiting floor even if generation is fast.\nL86: We next examine chunk size $K$, the number of latent frames jointly sampled in one DiT sampling call. Changing $K$ affects joint temporal modeling and parallel efficiency, as well as the time needed to generate each chunk. Our chunk-size ablation shows higher aggregate video quality for larger-chunk configurations than their frame-wise counterparts in the evaluated settings (Section cite22†4 ).\nL87: Larger chunks also better utilize GPU parallelism, allowing more latents to be sampled with only a modest increase in sampling time. Together with the system model, this motivates retaining the benefits of joint generation while reducing the waiting imposed by coarse output commitment.\nL88: Guided by the system model and chunk-size ablation, we develop our method to combine large-chunk joint generation with fine-grained delivery. We expand the bidirectional modeling window by appending noise latents as padding. Rather than discarding the resulting predictions, we cache them for reuse when the condition remains unchanged, while keeping the uncommitted future replaceable.\nL89: We formulate the controller as a two-state finite-state machine that coordinates joint generation and incremental consumption of cached predictions, while playback continues independently. This decouples DiT sampling calls from individual deliveries: completed predictions are reused between condition updates, avoiding repeated sampling merely to support finer-grained delivery.\nL90: The resulting design points to an alternative route to interactive video generation: shrinking chunks need not be a prerequisite for low interaction latency. Instead, the optimization focus can shift to accelerating high-quality joint generation. DiT sampling acceleration and parallel acceleration can support this route. We further demonstrate this route by serving a large bidirectional model in a low-latency interactive streaming system without Self-Forcing-style post-training.\nL91: Our contributions are fourfold:\nL92: \nL93:   1. 1.\nL94: \nL95: A simple serving-system model for interactive video generation. We formalize condition arrivals, generation, output commitment, and playback, distinguishing generation time from the additional waiting imposed by scheduling and playback boundaries (Section cite11†3.1 ).\nL96: \nL97:   2. 2.\nL98: A systematic analysis of chunk size. We study how chunk size affects video quality, generation time, and redundant computation, clarifying the trade-offs between joint modeling and responsiveness (Section cite22†4 ; Appendices cite37†A –cite41†B ).\nL99: \nL100:   3. 3.\nL101: Recomputation-efficient speculative sampling. Guided by this model and analysis, we propose a speculative sampling scheme formulated as a two-state finite-state machine. It combines large-chunk joint generation with fine-grained output commitment, reusing predictions and replanning only a feasible suffix when conditions change (Section cite10†3 ).\nL102: \nL103:   4. 4.\nL104: An alternative route to interactive video generation. By separating chunk size from output commitment, we shift the optimization focus from shrinking chunks to accelerating high-quality joint generation, making per-chunk generation time a central target for improving responsiveness.\nL105: ## 2 Related work\nL106: #### Autoregressive video generation.\nL107: Autoregressive video generation exploits temporal structure by predicting new content conditioned on the past (cite63†Yan et al., 2021 ). This provides a natural interface for extending videos, including segment-wise continuation under changing prompts (cite64†Villegas et al., 2022 ). Bringing sequential prediction to diffusion models (cite65†Chen et al., 2024 ) leaves two practical challenges: the cost of repeated sampling and errors accumulated through generated history.\nL108: Recent progress therefore targets efficient few-step causal generation (cite66†Yin et al., 2024 ; cite60†Zhao et al., 2026 ) and training aligned with self-generated, long-horizon rollouts (cite59†Huang et al., 2025 ; cite61†Yang et al., 2025 ). Related rolling and bidirectional-window approaches retain joint temporal processing during sequential output (cite67†Kim et al., 2024 ; cite68†Kodaira et al., 2025 ; cite69†Liu et al., 2025 ; cite70†Rui et al., 2026 ).\nL109: NFD additionally describes speculative multi-frame sampling on a frame-causal backbone to improve throughput, but retains a causal mask within the enlarged window and therefore does not exploit bidirectional modeling across it (cite71†Cheng et al., 2025 ). Related control mechanisms include reuse or replanning of fully denoised futures with ground-truth observation feedback (cite72†Lillemark et al., 2026 ) and management of generation–playback lead (cite73†Bai et al., 2026 ).\nL110: Our method studies a complementary interface: retaining temporally bidirectional joint generation within each chunk while committing outputs at a finer granularity under newly arriving conditions.\nL111: #### Accelerating video generation.\nL112: Acceleration targets several components of generation cost. Few-step distillation (cite66†Yin et al., 2024 ; cite60†Zhao et al., 2026 ) and adversarial post-training (cite74†Lin et al., 2025 ) reduce DiT sampling steps. Pyramid Flow performs much of its DiT sampling below full spatial resolution and compresses history through a temporal pyramid (cite75†Jin et al., 2024 ); FramePack reduces the historical-context token budget (cite76†Zhang et al., 2025a ).\nL113: FasterCache and TeaCache reuse intermediate computations across diffusion steps (cite77†Lv et al., 2024 ; cite78†Liu et al., 2024 ), whereas Sliding Tile Attention and Sparse VideoGen reduce attention work through local or spatial–temporal sparsity (cite79†Zhang et al., 2025b ; cite80†Xi et al., 2025 ). USP addresses distributed attention through sequence parallelism (cite81†Fang and Zhao, 2024 ). These methods have distinct training and inference requirements.\nL114: Our perspective shifts the emphasis from shrinking generation chunks to accelerating their generation. By decoupling generation from output commitment, our method highlights per-chunk generation time, rather than chunk size alone, as a key determinant of interaction latency. This places greater importance on accelerating joint generation instead of requiring increasingly fine-grained autoregressive models.\nL115: ## 3 Method\nL116: ### 3.1 Serving-System Model\nL117: An interactive video serving system generates video into a buffer while the player consumes previously generated content. New conditions arrive online and must influence subsequent playback promptly.\nL118: For the latent diffusion models considered here, the chunk size $K$ counts the latents jointly sampled with bidirectional temporal modeling.^{1}^{1} 1 Throughout this paper, our chunk-based generation setting uses bidirectional temporal modeling within each chunk and autoregressive continuation across chunks, conditioned on retained past content.\nL119: Let $f$ denote the video’s temporal sampling frame rate, used for generation and playback at its intended speed, and $r$ the pixel frames represented by each latent. A latent spans $\\delta=r/f$ seconds, so a chunk covers $T=K\\delta=Kr/f$ seconds.\nL120: cite82†Image: Refer to caption L121: \nL122: Figure 2: Generation speed alone does not determine response latency. In this periodic reference schedule, the highlighted update waits $R$ for generation, takes $C$ to produce deliverable output, and waits $Q$ for playback. Each chunk is prepared for the next playback period; $V_{0}$ is prebuffered.\nL123: Figure cite83†2 illustrates why response latency is not just DiT sampling time. A condition arrives at $t_{\\mathrm{arrival}}$, waits until generation starts at $t_{\\mathrm{start}}$, and yields a first output group ready at $t_{\\mathrm{ready}}$ and displayed at $t_{\\mathrm{playback}}$. For a displayed update, these stages give\nL124:  | $\\displaystyle R$  | $\\displaystyle=t_{\\mathrm{start}}-t_{\\mathrm{arrival}},$  | $\\displaystyle C$  | $\\displaystyle=t_{\\mathrm{ready}}-t_{\\mathrm{start}},$  |  | (1)\nL125:  | $\\displaystyle Q$  | $\\displaystyle=t_{\\mathrm{playback}}-t_{\\mathrm{ready}},$  | $\\displaystyle L$  | $\\displaystyle=R+C+Q.$  |\nL126: Here $R$ and $Q$ are the waits before generation and playback, respectively. Generation time $C$ covers the critical path to the first deliverable output, including the required DiT sampling and VAE decoding.\nL127: For a conventional interface that admits newly conditioned output only at fixed chunk playback boundaries, the expected combined waiting satisfies $\\mathbb{E}[R+Q]\\geq T/2$ under fixed generation time and isolated, uniformly phased arrivals (Section cite18†3.4 ). Since $T=Kr/f$, two direct ways to reduce this waiting floor at fixed $r$ are smaller chunks and higher temporal frame rates. Both approaches reduce latency by shortening the chunk period $T$.\nL128: We instead ask: can we eliminate the chunk-period waiting floor by decoupling joint generation from output commitment? This would remove the need to shrink chunks or increase the model’s temporal frame rate merely to obtain finer-grained interaction, shifting the optimization focus to generation time rather than chunk duration.\nL129: ### 3.2 Speculative Sampling via a Finite-State Machine\nL130: Our design separates how much the model jointly predicts from how much the system commits at each delivery. Delivery granularity $q$ is the number of latents whose pixel frames are transferred together; our method uses $q=1$, with each regular group spanning $\\delta$ seconds. For each sampling call, we append $K-1$ noise latents as padding to the next latent to be delivered, forming a $K$-latent bidirectional modeling window.\nL131: Rather than discarding the resulting padding predictions, we retain them in a prediction cache for reuse while the condition remains unchanged. Output commitment makes content non-replaceable and may precede actual playback. A large chunk supplies a reusable future; the controller keeps that future replaceable until needed. This is the role of speculative sampling, distinct from the individual DiT sampling calls it schedules.\nL132: #### Joint predictions and retained state.\nL133: \nL134: At a branch position $j$, let $\\mathcal{H}_{j}$ be the conditioning state derived from the retained prefix, and let $c$ denote the current condition. One DiT sampling call produces a $K$-latent prediction $\\widetilde{Y}_{j}$:\nL135: \nL136:  | $$\\widetilde{Y}_{j}\\sim p_{\\theta}(\\,\\cdot\\mid\\mathcal{H}_{j},c\\,),\\qquad|\\widetilde{Y}_{j}|=K.$$  |  | (2)\nL137: The next latent and the padding latents are sampled jointly with bidirectional temporal attention. Their predictions are cached with source/playback mappings and condition versions, independently of the state used to condition subsequent calls. For a chunk-wise AR model, $\\mathcal{H}_{j}$ includes prefix KV; Section cite16†3.3 defines an alternative for bidirectional clip models. Completed latents require the appropriate VAE decoding before pixel delivery.\nL138: #### Control states and events.\nL139: \nL140: The controller is an extended finite-state machine with two control states, $\\mathcal{S}=\\{\\mathsf{Produce},\\mathsf{Consume}\\}$. $\\mathsf{Produce}$ means a generation job is active; $\\mathsf{Consume}$ means no job is active and predictions can be reused. Playback continues in both states. Registers $x$ store the prediction cache, conditioning and VAE state, condition versions, playback and commitment positions, and the active job $J$.\nL141: The events are condition arrival, output/state readiness, job completion, playback tick, and refill/retry wake-up. An event $e$ at time $t$ first updates the registers to $\\bar{x}=U_{e}(x)$. The dispatch guard $g(\\bar{x},t)$ requires an idle worker, pending-update or refill demand, and a feasible branch boundary. The transition is\nL142:  | $$(z^{+},x^{+})=\\begin{cases}(\\mathsf{Produce},\\operatorname{Launch}(\\bar{x})),&g(\\bar{x},t),\\\\\nL143: (\\mathsf{Produce},\\bar{x}),&\\bar{J}\\neq\\varnothing,\\\\\nL144: (\\mathsf{Consume},\\bar{x}),&\\text{otherwise}.\\end{cases}$$  |  | (3)\nL145: \nL146: Dispatch requires $\\bar{J}=\\varnothing$, so the first two guards are disjoint. Only $\\operatorname{Launch}$ starts a DiT sampling call.\nL147: #### Guarded replacement and refill.\nL148: A feasible boundary lies beyond the protected prefix, has the required conditioning inputs, preserves a valid VAE continuation, and lets the old prefix cover computation until the new content can meet its display deadlines. On an update, $\\operatorname{Launch}$ selects the earliest such boundary, locks the prefix, and replaces only the eligible suffix with a new complete chunk. It does not shorten the new sample to the old chunk’s remaining length.\nL149: Without an update, it appends a chunk when remaining playable coverage approaches the estimated compute lead, before exhaustion. Infeasible requests remain pending without discarding useful predictions.\nL150: Arrivals during $\\mathsf{Produce}$ update the pending condition but not the active job. Completion validates its results and either returns to $\\mathsf{Consume}$ or immediately launches another eligible job. Multiple pending updates coalesce to the latest version, with superseded events recorded. Playback ticks consume ready groups in either state; they do not themselves require DiT sampling.\nL151: Output readiness may precede job completion, so time $C$ is not necessarily the full $\\mathsf{Produce}$ residence time. Figure cite84†3 illustrates these actions; Appendix cite44†C specifies the guards and event updates.\nL152: cite85†Image: Refer to caption L153: \nL154: Figure 3: Speculative Sampling serving system and control. (a) Joint generation populates a prediction cache for fine-grained delivery and playback; playback progress guides refill. (b) The finite-state machine coordinates generation in Produce and prediction reuse in Consume, illustrated for $q=1$. Condition updates trigger feasible replanning without interrupting active generation; playback continues in both states.\nL155: ### 3.3 Adapting Bidirectional Models for Streaming\nL156: Bidirectional teacher models often define the quality target for causal streaming students, yet their full-sequence generation interfaces are not designed for interactive serving. Directly converting the same backbone to causal generation can also introduce a quality gap without careful adaptation (cite66†Yin et al., 2024 ).\nL157: This motivates a further extrapolation of chunk size: can a large-chunk bidirectional model itself serve an interactive video stream? We preserve bidirectional joint sampling within each call and adapt its conditioning and output interfaces, allowing successive calls to continue from retained video while outputs are committed incrementally.\nL158: #### Continuation interface.\nL159: Bidirectional video generators commonly provide native image- or video-conditioned continuation interfaces, allowing successive jointly sampled chunks to be connected without modifying within-chunk attention. A practical complication arises with temporally compressive 3D VAEs: the continuation interface often expects a temporally uncompressed first-frame latent, whereas an interior cached frame belongs to a temporally compressed latent group.\nL160: We therefore decode the retained boundary to pixels and re-encode its final frame through the VAE’s first-frame path before the next DiT sampling call. This produces the latent format expected by the pretrained continuation interface. Alternatively, the bidirectional model can be fine-tuned to accept compressed latent continuation directly.\nL161: ### 3.4 Latency and Recomputation Analysis\nL162: #### Removing coarse playback alignment.\nL163: \nL164: Consider uniform latent groups, fixed generation time $C$, and an isolated update uniformly distributed over a chunk period $T=K\\delta$. If new output can enter playback only at chunk boundaries, then\nL165: \nL166:  | $$t_{\\mathrm{playback}}^{\\min}=T\\left\\lceil\\frac{t_{\\mathrm{arrival}}+C}{T}\\right\\rceil,\\qquad\\mathbb{E}[L]\\geq C+\\frac{T}{2}.$$  |  | (4)\nL167: Since $L=R+C+Q$ and $C$ is fixed, this also gives $\\mathbb{E}[R+Q]\\geq T/2$. It bounds the mean combined waiting, not each request or each component separately; Appendix cite37†A gives the derivation. This boundary-imposed waiting persists whether it occurs before or after generation. With a ready prefix, no additional buffering constraint, and fine-grained commitment, the earliest latent-aligned display is\nL168:  | $\\displaystyle t_{\\mathrm{playback}}$  | $\\displaystyle=\\delta\\left\\lceil\\frac{t_{\\mathrm{start}}+C}{\\delta}\\right\\rceil,$  |  | (5)\nL169:  | $\\displaystyle Q$  | $\\displaystyle=t_{\\mathrm{playback}}-(t_{\\mathrm{start}}+C),\\qquad 0\\leq Q<\\delta.$  |\nL170: \nL171: Thus alignment is tied to delivery granularity rather than chunk size.\nL172: #### Generation time and prefix waiting.\nL173: \nL174: Fine commitment does not remove computation or its data dependencies. For one indivisible $C$-second job every $T$ seconds, with $C\\leq T$, an isolated uniform arrival is blocked with probability $C/T$ and waits $C/2$ on average when blocked, provided that job supplies the required prefix. Hence\nL175: \nL176:  | $$\\mathbb{E}[R]=\\frac{C^{2}}{2T},\\qquad\\mathbb{E}[L]=C+\\frac{C^{2}}{2T}+\\mathbb{E}[Q].$$  |  | (6)\nL177: Reducing $C$ lowers both generation time and this conditional residual wait (Figure cite86†4 ). For nonuniform completion phases, $\\mathbb{E}[Q]$ is determined by the actual pipeline timing (Appendix cite37†A ).\nL178: \nL179: Figure 4: At fixed $T$, direct generation scales as $C/T$, while residual wait scales as $\\mathbb{E}[R]/T=(C/T)^{2}/2$ under the stated periodic model.\nL180: #### Cost of revocable predictions.\nL181: \nL182: Without condition changes, prediction reuse preserves the matched chunk-wise baseline’s complete DiT sampling-call count over steady-state output. Normal refill remains necessary; finer delivery does not cause resampling. Updates introduce extra work by replacing unused predictions. For equal-cost calls producing $K$ usable latent groups each, let $w$ be the fraction ultimately discarded rather than played. Relative DiT sampling work satisfies\nL183:  | $$A\\simeq\\frac{1}{1-w}.$$  |  | (7)\nL184: Infrequent updates can keep this overhead small, but update frequency alone does not determine $w$: chunk size and the selected branch also matter. VAE decoding, adapter preparation, overlap trimming, aborted jobs, and cache reconstruction require separate cost accounting. Appendix cite41†B gives the assumptions and a discrete update model. Together, these results motivate accelerating high-quality joint generation rather than shrinking chunks solely for responsiveness.\nL185: DiT sampling acceleration and parallel acceleration target the generation path; their benefit is measured through $C$ and total resource cost.\nL186: ## 4 Experiments\nL187: \nL188: We first characterize how chunk size affects generation quality and computational efficiency within and across GPUs. We then compare conventional chunk commitment and Speculative Sampling in end-to-end interactive serving.\nL189: \nL190: ### 4.1 Experimental Setup\nL191: #### Models and quality evaluation.\nL192: For Wan1.3B, we follow CF++ (cite60†Zhao et al., 2026 ) and train on Toy6K, $K\\in\\{1,3,7,21\\}$ models with 1/2/4-step sampling. VBench (cite87†Huang et al., 2023 ) evaluates 81-frame, 16-fps videos at $832\\times 480$ and $1280\\times 720$ over 946 prompts and five seeds. For LTX-2.3 (cite88†HaCohen et al., 2026 ), we train $K\\in\\{1,3,9,15,45\\}$ models with three DiT forwards under schedule $[999,757,522,0]$, where 0 is the terminal boundary, on an internal 8K-video training set (Internal8K).\nL193: We evaluate on an internal 32-case set (Internal32), with matched seeds and 361-frame outputs at $832\\times 448$ and 24 fps.
