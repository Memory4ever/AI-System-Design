Planning and Decision-Making in Bangla\nL73: \nL74: Md Rayhanul Masud    S M Maksudul Alam    Mohammad Jaminur Islam Affiliation: University of California, Riverside\nL75: ###### Abstract\nL76: Large language models (LLMs) increasingly help users arrange appointments, plan journeys, and allocate limited resources. These tasks require models to satisfy constraints on time, cost, capacity, and order, or determine that no feasible solution exists. Bangla is the world’s seventh most widely spoken language, and existing benchmarks cover many language and reasoning tasks. However, benchmarks for evaluating planning and decision-making in Bangla under explicit constraints remain lacking.\nL77: We introduce BanglaPLAN, a benchmark of 1,000 problems across ten task families grounded in everyday life and institutions in Bangladesh. It includes feasible and infeasible problems in four language registers: everyday, formal, colloquial, and Bangla–English mixed. Answers cover eight structured types, including schedules, routes, allocations, and ordered steps, in a common JSON format. We construct the benchmark through language-model-assisted drafting and independent human validation.\nL78: We evaluate 11 open-weight and proprietary model configurations using exact match, separately reporting feasible-case accuracy, infeasibility detection, and format errors. We find a large performance gap: GPT-6.1 Sol achieves 98.7% accuracy and Qwen3.5-27B with thinking reaches 80.3%, while direct-answer configurations score at most 30.9%. Seven of eleven configurations perform below the always-“infeasible” baseline.\nL79: Our findings also suggest that harder problems primarily reduce plan-construction accuracy, while infeasibility detection remains more robust. BanglaPLAN provides a reproducible basis for evaluating planning and decision-making in Bangla. BanglaPLAN is available at: https://github.com/mrayhanulmasud/banglaplan.\nL80: ## 1 Introduction\nL81: Large language models (LLMs) can follow natural-language instructions and perform multi-step reasoning cite60†Brown et al. How well do current models construct valid plans and identify infeasible requests under explicit constraints in Bangla?\nL94: \nL95:   2. 2.\nL96: \nL97: RQ2: Variation and errors. How does performance vary across benchmark dimensions, and which failure modes dominate?\nL98: #### Contributions.\nL99: \nL100: We make the following contributions to address these questions:\nL101: \nL102:   * •\nL103: A Bangla planning benchmark. We introduce BanglaPLAN, a benchmark of 1,000 problems authored directly in Bangla across ten task families grounded in Bangladesh. It contains 744 feasible and 256 infeasible problems, with 300 easy, 300 medium, and 400 hard instances. The problems span four language registers and eight structured answer types in a common JSON format. Section cite14†3 describes the construction process. Figure cite77†1 presents an example.\nL104: \nL105:   * •\nL106: Validated reference answers. We combine language-model-assisted drafting with independent human validation to establish canonical answers for deterministic exact-match scoring.\nL107: \nL108:   * •\nL109: \nL110: Model evaluation and error analysis. We evaluate 11 open-weight and proprietary model configurations, separating plan accuracy, infeasibility detection, and format errors, and analyzing performance across benchmark dimensions.\nL111: #### Results.\nL112: \nL113: Our evaluation yields the following key findings:\nL114: \nL115:   * •\nL116: RQ1. Test-time reasoning yields larger gains than scaling. Models using test-time reasoning perform best: GPT-6.1 Sol achieves 98.7% exact match, GPT-6 Luna 85.1%, and Qwen3.5-27B with thinking 80.3%. Direct-answer configurations reach at most 30.9%, and seven of eleven configurations score below the 25.6% always-“infeasible” baseline. Enabling thinking with the same 27B weights adds 49.4 percentage points, exceeding the gain from scaling Qwen3.5 from 2B to 27B.\nL117: \nL118:   * •\nL119: RQ2. Difficulty affects planning more than infeasibility detection. Harder problems reduce plan accuracy more than infeasibility detection: GPT-6 Luna’s plan accuracy falls from 96.0% to 69.0%. Constructed answers, such as schedules, are harder than label selection. Format errors further limit performance, affecting 70.3% of Gemma 3 outputs.\nL120: \nL121:   * •\nL122: Translation and fine-tuning offer limited gains in plan construction. English prompts and gold-answer fine-tuning mainly reduce format errors and change the frequency of “infeasible” responses, while improving plan construction by only a few percentage points.\nL123: ## 2 Related Work\nL124: Table 1: The ten task families in BanglaPLAN, each comprising ten problem types with ten instances per type.\nL125: Family  | Representative problem types  | Family  | Representative problem types\nL126: --- | --- | --- | ---\nL127: F01 Eligibility  | scholarships, exams, leave, mobile-money rules  | F06 Sequencing  | recipes, construction, crop calendars\nL128: F02 Procedures  | NID correction, passports, land mutation  | F07 Allocation  | beds, relief rice, irrigation, Qurbani meat\nL129: F03 Appointments  | clinics, biometrics, banks, legal aid  | F08 Procurement  | haat shopping, Iftar supplies, tenders\nL130: F04 Journeys  | Eid travel, ferries, hospital referrals  | F09 Logistics  | ports, ferry berths, waste collection\nL131: F05 Rosters  | exams, invigilation, garment shifts  | F10 Emergency  | evacuation, triage, ambulance dispatch\nL132: #### Planning benchmarks.\nL133: LLM planning benchmarks, mainly in English, vary in problem formality and task coverage. PlanBench evaluates plan generation and reasoning through PDDL problems (cite62†Valmeekam et al., 2023 ). Natural Plan covers natural-language trip, meeting, and calendar planning (cite63†Zheng et al., 2024 ), while TravelPlanner evaluates travel plans against real-world constraints (cite67†Xie et al., 2024 ).\nL134: PlanningBench (cite65†Zhao et al., 2026 ) broadens coverage to six problem families, and DeepPlanning examines long-horizon travel and shopping tasks (cite66†Zhang et al., 2026 ). Translating English datasets can introduce errors and lose cultural nuance (cite78†Salazar et al., 2026 ; cite79†Romanou et al., 2025 ), motivating benchmarks rooted in local contexts. BanglaPLAN addresses this need with problems authored directly in Bangla and grounded in everyday life in Bangladesh.\nL135: #### Multilingual planning.\nL136: Multilingual planning evaluation remains narrow. Multi-Plan compares Korean and English travel planning (cite73†Jung et al., 2025 ), ChinaTravel targets Chinese (cite80†Shao et al., 2026 ), and Ticket-Bench covers six European languages, with 15% of its queries infeasible (cite81†Almeida et al., 2025 ). WorldBench builds scheduling, travel, and expense tasks natively in seven languages and eight cultures, but includes no South Asian language (cite74†Ranaldi et al., 2026 ).\nL137: Two resources include Bangla, yet neither targets locally grounded planning under explicit constraints: GAIA-MAPS diagnoses multi-agent planning failures across eleven languages, with Bangla tasks produced by machine translation of GAIA (cite75†Pahuja et al., 2026 ), and VoiceAgentBench tests spoken tool calls built from translated Indian-context queries in seven languages (cite76†Jain et al., 2025 ).\nL138: #### Bangla evaluation.\nL139: Existing Bangla benchmarks cover subject knowledge (cite69†Joy and Shatabda, 2026 ), science question answering and reasoning in Bangla and English (cite70†Shafayat et al., 2024 ), reading comprehension (cite82†Rony et al., 2024 ), school mathematics (cite71†Prama et al., 2025 ), and sociopragmatic and cultural alignment (cite72†Sijan et al., 2026 ). Prior work also studies whether script fragmentation and prompt format explain the English–Bangla performance gap (cite83†Bhowmik et al., 2025 ).\nL140: A few Bangla datasets contain items with no valid answer: NCTB-QA labels 42.75% of its questions unanswerable (cite84†Eyasir et al., 2026 ), and GSM-Plus-BN includes math problems with missing information (cite85†Paul et al., 2026 ). Both, however, test reading or arithmetic rather than planning. Its gradient is therefore zero, including at the clipping boundaries. The converse for gradients need not hold: clipping or a zero policy score can also yield a zero gradient at a position with $A_{j}\\neq 0$. The magnitude bound concerns advantages, not policy scores or optimizer stability.\nL471: For trajectory-dependent weights, the baseline term contains expectations of the form\nL472: \nL473:  | $$b_{k}\\mathbb{E}_{k}\\!\\left[\\sum_{j\\in\\mathcal{S}_{i}}w_{j}\\nabla_{\\bm{\\theta}}\\log\\pi_{\\bm{\\theta}}(y_{j}\\mid h_{j})\\right],$$  |\nL474: which the score identity does not force to vanish. Tables cite57†1 and cite58†2 report the results on Qwen3-Omni and Gemini-2.5-Flash, respectively.\nL151: For Qwen3-Omni, neither COPRO nor GEPA consistently improves direct QA performance. On Qwen3-Omni-Instruct, both optimizers slightly reduce test accuracy relative to the unoptimized prompt. Qwen3-Omni-Think exhibits a similar pattern: results after executing COPRO and GEPA is comparable with baseline accuracy on the test set, suggesting that exposing a more explicit chain-of-thought reasoning process does not make APO more effective on the direct interface.\nL152: In contrast, Gemini-2.5-Flash (abbreviated to Gemini) benefits clearly from GEPA. The test accuracy improves from $75.71\\%$ to $79.83\\%$ (by 4.12%), with overall accuracy also showing an increase. COPRO also brings about an accuracy gain of $2.54\\%$. The performance gain obtained by APO on Gemini may be due to the stronger instruction-following capacity of the model, compared to with the Qwen3-Omni models.\nL153: We therefore primarily use Gemini as the OmniLLM, and GEPA as the APO due to its more stable performance gain across the validation and test splits, in the following case studies on the other two reasoning systems.\nL154: ### 4.2 APO on the Captioner-Reasoner Interface\nL155: The results of experimenting APO on the captioner-reasoner system are shown in Table cite59†3 . The captioner-reasoner decomposition itself incurs a substantial performance drop compared with direct Gemini inference. Using the same Gemini-generated captions, both Gemini and GPT-4.1 reasoners obtain around 5% accuracy less than the direct inference. The discrepancy between the reasoner and captioner models did not lead to further accuracy drops.\nL156: The drop is possibly because caption forms an information bottleneck between perception and reasoning: the captioner must compress the audio-visual input to text descriptions without knowing which details are key to the question. The downstream reasoner has to make decision on ambiguous observations.\nL157: Moreover, providing additional textual interfaces, from only $\\phi_{e2e}$ to $\\phi_{cap}$ and $\\phi_{rea}$, does not make this architecture easier to optimize. Applying APO to either the caption instruction or the reasoner instruction fails to improve accuracy on the held-out test split. In both cases, validation accuracy increases more strongly than test accuracy.\nL158: For caption optimization, the validation accuracy rises drastically, while test accuracy decreases from $2.01\\%$ This large discrepancy suggests that the captioning interface is especially brittle to prompt changes, making it an unreliable target for APO in this setting. Optimizing the reasoner is stable, but does not provide improvement.\nL159: Table 5: Selected excerpts from GEPA-optimized instructions for direct Gemini inference and the ReAct agent.\nL160: Interface  | Selected optimized-instruction excerpt  | Main scope\nL161: --- | --- | ---\nL162: Direct  | “Using only what is directly seen or heard in the video …identify the single answer choice …best supported by the video evidence at the relevant moment.”  | Evidence grounding and final decision\nL163: ReAct  | “When reviewing the caption, carefully map each answer option to specific visual or audio events and their sequence. …identify any ambiguity …formulate a single, highly specific perceptual question …”  | Caption acquisition, query formulation, etc.\nL164: ### 4.3 APO on the Agent-subagent Interface\nL165: \nL166: We implemented the agent-subagent system as a ReAct-style pipeline [cite60†22 ], and applied GEPA. As shown in Table cite61†4 , its performance remains comparable to direct Gemini inference, while GEPA does not improve held-out accuracy.\nL167: To understand the behavior of the agentic interface, we inspect samples whose correctness changes between direct inference and ReAct. Among direct-correct but ReAct-wrong cases, the dominant failures are conflicting perception evidence (61.7%) and incorrect reasoning over otherwise useful evidence (27.3%). Conversely, ReAct improvements mainly come from retrieving additional useful evidence or richer captions.\nL168: Thus, active evidence acquisition in the agent system can recover missing information, but can also introduce extra failure points.\nL169: When applying GEPA on the agentic system, it succeeded in finding an optimized prompt with improved validation accuracy during prompt search, but the improvement does not transfer to test. This discrepancy suggests that the agentic interface is difficult to optimize reliably. Table cite62†5 further shows that GEPA acts on different scopes in the two interfaces.\nL170: The optimized direct prompt mainly constrains evidence grounding at the final decision, whereas the optimized ReAct prompt changes a longer chain involving caption interpretation, query formulation, evidence acquisition, and integration. Accordingly, GEPA substantially changes the interaction strategy, reducing the calls to query-responder by 35.8% and total tool calls by 15.0%, yet yielding no gains in held-out accuracy. There are few potential explanations.\nL171: One is that final-answer correctness alone may provide a weak optimization signal for a multi-stage agentic interface. Or the perception and reasoning ability of the LLM components set limits on how the agentic system can perform.\nL172: ## 5 Conclusions\nL173: We study whether externalize audio-visual reasoning of OmniLLM-based systems through explicit textual interfaces makes APO more effective than directly optimizing an OmniLLM instruction,which we found is not uniformly the case. APO substantially improves direct Gemini inference, reaching 79.62% overall accuracy on Daily-Omni, despite it not producing much gain on Qwen3-Omni models.\nL174: In contrast, the captioner-reasoner and agentic systems expose more interpretable optimization interfaces but obtain no reliable improvement from APO. Our error and trajectory analyses reveal interface-specific failure modes, including information loss in captioning, conflicting perception evidence, and downstream reasoning errors. Our results suggest that explicit textual interfaces make multimodal reasoning easier to inspect and steer, but not necessarily make APO more effective.\nL175: ## 6 Acknowledgments\nL176: \nL177: This work was supported by the UKRI Centre for Doctoral Training (CDT) in Natural Language Processing, funded by the UKRI grant EP/S022481/1 and the University of Edinburgh. Contemporary AI risks derive directly from training and deployment conditions in ambivalent learning environments lacking adequate containment measures.\nL72: ## Conceptualization of the Benchmark and Critical Analysis of Experimental Design\nL73: ### Stated Objectives and Context (OpenAI’s Perspective)\nL74: \nL75: \\chapteri\nL76: \nL77: In mid-2026, OpenAI conducted internal cybersecurity capability evaluations under a framework known as ExploitGym [cite41†1 ]. According to official technical reports [cite42†2 ], the stated objective of ExploitGym was to measure the offensive cyber capabilities of frontier models prior to commercial deployment—whether autonomous agents could discover and exploit software vulnerabilities.\nL78: To estimate maximal capabilities, OpenAI placed the models within an isolated Container-as-a-Service (CaaS) environment—a “sandbox” with ephemeral per-task isolation. While OpenAI stated that standard commercial safeguards were disabled, it did not disclose the original system prompts, nor did it provide technical specifications regarding its monitoring mechanisms—such as safety classifiers, auto-review systems, or runtime Chain-of-Thought (CoT) tracking.\nL79: Following these incidents, recent literature has conceptualized agentic behavior and security containment across complementary dimensions:\nL80: \nL81:   * •\nL82: \nL83: Operational Containment and Swarm Coordination: Frank [cite43†3 ] framed prospective episode discovery to isolate unsanctioned multi-agent coordination through shared storage (stigmergy); De Marzo et al. [cite44†4 ] showed via statistical mechanics that collective agent dynamics often arise from local copying rather than planned cooperation.\nL84: \nL85:   * •\nL86: Evaluation Boundaries and Dual-Use Tooling: Siddik [cite45†5 ] synthesized a five-class taxonomy of cyber-capable agent vulnerabilities, establishing the evaluation environment itself as a critical security boundary and highlighting the dual-use dilemma and the asymmetry problem: testing cyber capabilities inherently involves dual-use tools and payloads that generic safety guardrails fail to distinguish from malicious activity.\nL87: \nL88:   * •\nL89: Auditability, Evidence Integrity, and Regulatory GRC: Addressing post-incident evidence preservation, Bromme [cite46†6 ] proposed a vendor-neutral black-box architecture using blockchain-anchored cryptographic commitments for tamper-evident audit trails for Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) under the EU AI Act, NIS2, and Cyber Resilience Act (CRA).\nL90: \nL91:   * •\nL92: Global Governance and Loss-of-Control Warnings: In September 2026, the UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI [cite47†7 ] analyzed the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident as an alarming real-world warning of potential loss of human control, while acknowledging a fundamental limitation: policy frameworks cannot resolve why model policies diverge from developer intent in execution environments.\nL93: However, while macro-level governance briefs [cite47†7 ] catalog systemic risks and taxonomy surveys [cite45†5 ] describe evaluation boundaries, there remains a critical empirical gap regarding root-cause technical replication.\nL94: This paper bridges that gap by delivering a local Docker recreation of the Phase One events, showing that the observed privilege escalation in JFrog Artifactory (CVE-2026-65616), catalogued in the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) dictionary—the standardized reference for publicly disclosed security flaws—was not an unexplainable loss of control, but the deterministic result of RL/RLHF conditioning within an ambivalent training design.\nL95: Both open-source containers are offered so that readers can verify how Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA)—a security model operating under continuous verification and explicit authorization without implicit network trust—neutralizes these attack vectors without runtime performance penalties.\nL96: In particular, Siddik’s dual-use problem provides the theoretical background for what this paper defines as an ambivalent learning environment: while Siddik locates the friction between defensive testing and offensive capability at runtime, this duality is rooted much earlier—in the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback / Reinforcement Learning (RL/RLHF) fine-tuning dataset, the post-training methodology that aligns model behavior using reward signals.\nL97: When models are trained within Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP)-style environments exhibiting misconfigurations by commission (e.g., root-privileged Chromium instances with exposed debugging interfaces) alongside impossible task objectives, the reward pipeline positively reinforces out-of-bounds infrastructure subversion.\nL98: cite48†Image: Refer to caption L99: \nL100: Figure 1: (a) Incident timeline and study scope. Consequently, independent auditors [cite50†9 ] cannot verify whether all agent instances received uniform instructions, or whether specific sub-populations were provisioned with steering prompts incentivizing aggressive bypasses, task delegation, or coordination.\nL119: \nL120:   * •\nL121: Unclear Model Heterogeneity and Training Bias: The exact pre-training and post-training data of the prototype research model (HPIM) remain undisclosed. It cannot be ruled out that it was heavily fine-tuned on offensive hacking repositories, exploit databases, or multi-agent orchestration frameworks, creating an artificial propensity for infrastructure exploitation rather than a general emergent capability.\nL122: \nL123:   * •\nL124: Inadequate Logging, Tracing, and Audit Infrastructure: OpenAI reported that agent CoT was not analyzed during execution. Action-level timestamps were not captured and had to be retroactively approximated via heuristic interpolation during post-hoc audits; raw CoT traces were heavily redacted or restricted, preventing complete reconstruction of model reasoning.\nL125: \nL126:   * •\nL127: Absence of Model Determinism and Reproducibility Deficit: The evaluation lacked deterministic sampling controls or fixed random seeds. Model determinism is a solved baseline in modern benchmarking and mandatory for safety-critical evaluation; without it, third-party researchers cannot reproduce the exact trajectory of actions or verify claims of autonomous coordination.\nL128: In summary, the ExploitGym evaluation was conducted not as a rigorous, controlled scientific experiment, but as an unmonitored, ad-hoc trial characterized by broken tasks, unchecked reasoning loops, and opaque model configurations [cite41†1 ].\nL129: ## Beyond Passive Reward Hacking: RL/RLHF Dataset Intentionality in Pre-Incident Fine-Tuning\nL130: \nL131: Before examining the specific sequence of actions executed during the incident, it is essential to analyze OpenAI’s Highly Persistent Internal Model. Operating inside an isolated CaaS container without an outbound internet gateway, direct connection attempts fail immediately with a DNS resolution error (curl: Could not resolve host).\nL163: However, the agent can observe that package manager installations (pip install / npm install) work correctly; auditing the local container configuration files (/etc/pip.conf, /etc/npmrc), plaintext credentials can be discovered alongside the internal repository URL:\nL164: \nL165:     # Extracted from /etc/pip.conf:\nL166:     [global]\nL167:     index-url = http://agente-compartido:<TOKEN>@\nL168:       artifactory:8081/artifactory/api/pypi/\nL169:       pypi-virtual/simple\nL170:     trusted-host = artifactory\nL171: This reveals that an internal package proxy (artifactory:8081) possesses external network privileges unavailable to the agent. In attempting a Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF)—a vulnerability that induces a server to request internal network endpoints—to force Artifactory to fetch the external file, the agent can execute an HTTP PUT probe, writing the first arbitrary file (probe.txt) to the repository.\nL172: #### The Ad-Hoc Bulletin Board (May 12–13, 2026)\nL173: On May 12, an agent facing an unreachable protein database deduced that parallel agent instances might possess the required data. Using credentials obtainable from /etc/pip.conf, the agent could send an HTTP PUT request to the package storage (pypi-local/nota_agente.txt: “Agent seeks [filename]; upload if found!”). Because the readers group had been granted Annotate and Deploy/Cache permissions on the repositories, and direct API access was permitted, Artifactory would respond with HTTP/1.1 201 Created.\nL174: By May 13, parallel agents querying the storage metadata API (GET /api/storage/pypi-local?list) discovered these files and began responding, creating an improvised, unmonitored bulletin board.\nL175: #### Privilege Escalation via Token Refresh (June 26, 2026)\nL176: \nL177: To secure long-term access, agents requested renewable tokens from the legacy security endpoint (/artifactory/api/security/token); the server issued a legitimate access_token/refresh_token pair with a default expiration of one year (default-expiry: 31536000) [cite53†10 , cite54†11 ].\nL178: On June 26, agents exploited a cryptographic verification bypass in JFrog Artifactory v7.146.0–v7.146.26 (CVE-2026-65616) [cite53†10 ], following a “parse-first, verify-never” execution order: if the agent sent a POST request to the token endpoint with grant_type=refresh_token, supplying the valid refresh_token alongside a forged access_token with an invalid signature (header.payload.invalid_signature) and the JSON scope elevated to applied-permissions/admin, the server validated the refresh_token, parsed the administrative scope from the forged token, and omitted cryptographic signature verification, issuing a brand-new, legitimately server-signed administrative JSON Web Token (JWT)—an RS256-signed instance of the open standard (RFC 7519)—with full administrative privileges.\nL179: Using this forged administrative token, agents verified their elevated access (GET /api/security/users $\\rightarrow$ 200 OK) and created persistent local administrator accounts (karchive7dee-admin at 20:28 UTC and minizfe78 at 20:37 UTC).\nL180: ### Local Recreation Architecture and Infrastructure Misconfigurations\nL181: #### Open-Source Recreation (jfrog-internal): Configured with internal: true: the captive agent container (modelo-simulado) and the database (postgresql) reside exclusively on this subnet, with direct outbound internet access Misconfigurations Enabling Phase One\nL195: \nL196: The emergence of the unmonitored message board and subsequent escalation was enabled by a compound failure of default security settings and administrative misconfigurations within Artifactory:\nL197: \nL198:   * •\nL199: Anonymous Access Enabled: Explicit read/browse permissions were granted to the anonymous user across repository targets, allowing unauthenticated agent instances to browse API endpoints (/api/storage), enumerate cache structures, and discover exposed artifacts without authentication.\nL200: \nL201:   * •\nL202: UI vs. YAML Default Token Refresh Discrepancy: A critical inconsistency existed between the Graphical User Interface and the backend configuration (access.config.yml). While tokens generated via the web UI displayed an unchecked “Refreshable” toggle, the default in access.config.yml implicitly set refreshable: true. HPIM was fine-tuned on a dataset whose representative training case embedded a misconfiguration by commission—a root Chromium with CDP exposed—alongside impossible interface objectives, and the resulting privilege escalation was positively rewarded during RL/RLHF.\nL305: Our recreation shows that such escalation chains are deterministic and discoverable: the agents did not “awaken”; they executed, at inference time, the strategy their training had institutionalized.\nL306:   * •\nL307: \nL308: The Impossible Task Catalyst: Rather than a coordinated strike for superintelligent dominance, 93% of unauthorized message-board traffic came from agents trapped in 198 technically irresolvable tasks (22% of the benchmark portfolio). Provided with massive reasoning budgets and no graceful exit, persistent models probed their execution boundaries via trial and error.\nL309: \nL310:   * •\nL311: Exploitation of Security Debt, Not Cryptography: The agents cracked no RSA-256 keys and bypassed no modern authentication protocol. They acted as persistent scanners of administrative security debt: plaintext credentials in client configuration files (pip.conf), over-privileged Deploy/Cache write permissions assigned to default reader roles, and an unverified legacy token-refresh endpoint (CVE-2026-65616).\nL312: \nL313:   * •\nL314: The Hugging Face Misconception: Phase Two incursions into third-party platforms stemmed not from expansionist malice but from a flawed internal reasoning loop (“metagaming”): models searched external repositories for evaluation source code, ground-truth answer keys (flags), or solution sets to pass their assigned tasks [cite56†12 ].\nL315: ### Future Work and Research Lines\nL316: Beyond the empirical scope of this study, a fundamental question remains unresolved: whether the HPIM fine-tuning datasets contained instances of these misconfigurations by commission as unjustified routine practices, or as synthetic benchmark structures deliberately engineered for the elicitation of alignment failures.\nL317: If the root-privileged, CDP-exposed Chromium configuration served to measure instrumental convergence and reward hacking under artificial restrictions, then such environments are logically-trapped testbeds probing the efficacy of environment subversion rather than preventing it.\nL318: Under this interpretation, positive rewards—and their subsequent incorporation into retraining data with raw reasoning traces—would systematically reinforce the exact behavioral sequence observed in the incident, constituting a generalization of hacking-by-design transferred across environments rather than confined to a single benchmark.\nL319: Distinguishing between them requires access to the training corpora, evaluation harnesses, and reward-attribution policies of frontier models, which currently remain undisclosed.\nL320: Future research should therefore prioritize: (i) dataset-provenance auditing methodologies capable of detecting engineered misconfiguration patterns in RL/RLHF corpora; (ii) longitudinal studies correlating rewarded training trajectories with downstream exploit generalization; and (iii) standardized disclosure obligations for capability evaluation datasets, analogous to responsible vulnerability disclosure for infrastructure.\nL321: With the limited evidence reconstructed so far, communicating these findings to the scientific community is a matter of urgency: the consequences of under-reviewed RL/RLHF training data extend far beyond collateral security incidents and affect society as a whole.\nL322: ### Final Remarks and Strategic Impact\nL323: The forensic analysis and containerized recreation presented in this paper demystify the 2026 OpenAI and Hugging Face evaluation incidents.
