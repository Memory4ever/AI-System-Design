University of Technology Tsinghua University    Haoyue Zhang    Delu Zeng    Jiahao Wang Affiliation: The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Noah Research Institute    Junmei Yang    Pan tiewen    Jingfeng Liu Affiliation: Shenzhen Technology University LinkSprite    Zhiheng Zhou    Bo Li    Min Chen\nL70: ###### Abstract\nL71: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains language models from checked task outcomes, but assigning a terminal reward to individual reasoning tokens or actions remains difficult. Group-relative methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) sample several attempts per prompt, but lose their reward-based learning signal when all attempts receive the same outcome.\nL72: We propose Hindsight-Privileged RLVR (HP-RLVR), a single-rollout method that turns a completed trajectory’s own future into token-level update weights. The generating policy snapshot scores each eligible token under its causal history and under a hindsight context containing the realized future and verifier feedback.\nL73: A normalized Jensen–Shannon divergence between the two views weights the reward relative to a historical baseline, yielding bounded token credit for both successful and failed attempts, with no reference solution or learned critic. In paired Qwen3-8B runs over a shared $100$-step schedule, HP-RLVR trains $1.21\\times$, $1.46\\times$, and $2.30\\times$ faster than GRPO on mathematics, code generation, and ALFWorld, using one eighth the rollouts per prompt.\nL74: Accuracy is comparable overall, with HP-RLVR ahead on mathematics at all three sizes and GRPO ahead on code and ALFWorld. In ALFWorld, $43.2\\%$ (4B) and $49.0\\%$ (8B) of GRPO’s gradient-norm records are zero, versus none for HP-RLVR.\nL75: ## 1 Introduction\nL76: Figure 1: Qwen3-8B training cost at four prompts per step and $100$ steps. HP-RLVR uses group size $G{=}1$, versus GRPO’s $G{=}8$. Bars are relative to GRPO, with labels above each pair showing speedup. Figure 2: Overview of HP-RLVR’s single-rollout training pipeline with training-only hindsight. After generation, the frozen generating policy scores the trajectory under causal and hindsight contexts, without additional sampling.\nL77: The paired profiles show full-vocabulary distributions at the same token position. Normalized JSD weights the historically centered verifier reward to produce token credit. Bars are schematic, and dashed frames group stages.\nL78: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains language models through outcomes checked by symbolic verifiers, test suites, or interactive environments (cite50†Guo et al., 2025 ). Terminal verification supplies supervision without labeling every intermediate decision. Turning that supervision into efficient learning, however, requires addressing both the cost of obtaining an update signal and its allocation within a trajectory.\nL79: First, group-relative learning obtains its signal through repeated attempts. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) compares multiple responses to each prompt, replacing a learned critic with a baseline computed from group rewards (cite51†Shao et al., 2024 ). This comparison requires multiple rollouts, yet homogeneous outcomes give zero reward advantages (cite52†Yu et al., 2025 ). Additional sampling therefore incurs cost without necessarily supplying an informative comparison.\nL80: Second, comparisons between complete attempts leave credit coarse within each attempt. Outcome-supervised GRPO broadcasts a trajectory’s advantage to its tokens (cite51†Shao et al., 2024 ), despite the different roles of intermediate decisions in reasoning and multi-turn interaction (cite53†Lightman et al., 2023 ; cite54†Feng et al., 2025 ).\nL81: Process supervision supplies intermediate labels (cite53†Lightman et al., 2023 ), implicit process rewards learn dense signals (cite55†Yuan et al., 2024 ; cite56†Cui et al., 2025 ), and intermediate-value estimation can require additional continuations (cite57†Kazemnejad et al., 2025 ). These approaches motivate a complementary question: can a single verified attempt, whether successful or failed, itself supply a finer learning signal?\nL82: A completed attempt’s realized future provides context for revisiting earlier decisions (cite58†Harutyunyan et al., 2019 ; cite59†Mesnard et al., 2021 ). We introduce Hindsight-Privileged RLVR (HP-RLVR), which uses this future as training-only privilege (cite60†Fig. 2 ). The generating policy compares token distributions with and without the future and verifier feedback.\nL83: Historical reward centering directs the update and can give failures negative credit, while normalized Jensen–Shannon divergence supplies bounded, nonnegative token weights. This yields local credit from one rollout per prompt, without a reference solution or learned critic (cite12†Sec. 3 ).\nL84: We evaluate Qwen3 models (1.7B/4B/8B) (cite61†Yang et al., 2025 ) on mathematics, code generation, and ALFWorld (cite62†Shridhar et al., 2020 ). cite63†Figure 1 summarizes the Qwen3-8B cost benefit at a shared prompt and step budget, using one eighth as many rollouts per prompt. Task accuracy and training-signal diagnostics assess the resulting single-rollout updates (cite64†Figs. 3 and cite65†4 ).\nL85: \nL86: Our contributions are:\nL87: \nL88:   * •\nL89: A single-rollout hindsight construction using the generating policy and its own realized future, without reference solutions or a learned critic (cite14†Sec. 3.2 ).\nL90: \nL91:   * •\nL92: \nL93: A bounded token-credit rule for both successful and failed trajectories, combining historical reward centering with normalized distributional divergence (cite15†Sec. 3.3 ).\nL94: \nL95:   * •\nL96: \nL97: Lower training cost at a shared step schedule, with accuracy comparisons and protocol diagnostics (cite17†Sec. 4 ).\nL98: ## 2 Background and Related Work\nL99: \nL100: ### 2.1 Related work\nL101: #### Outcome-based and hindsight credit assignment.\nL102: Methods for assigning local credit differ in how they obtain information beyond a terminal reward. Learned value baselines estimate future returns (cite66†Schulman et al., 2016 ), and RUDDER redistributes delayed rewards through a return predictor (cite67†Arjona-Medina et al., 2019 ).\nL103: In language-model reasoning, process supervision supplies intermediate labels (cite53†Lightman et al., 2023 ), implicit process rewards learn dense signals from outcome supervision (cite55†Yuan et al., 2024 ; cite56†Cui et al., 2025 ), and VinePPO estimates intermediate values through additional continuations (cite57†Kazemnejad et al., 2025 ). Hindsight credit assignment instead uses observed future information to revisit past actions (cite58†Harutyunyan et al., 2019 ).\nL104: Counterfactual credit assignment conditions value functions on suitable future events to reduce policy-gradient variance (cite59†Mesnard et al., 2021 ).\nL105: This distinction also matters for multi-turn agents. GiGPO augments episode-level group advantages with step-level comparisons at repeated states across sampled trajectories (cite54†Feng et al., 2025 ). HCAPO uses the language model as a post-hoc critic and combines hindsight action evaluation with multi-scale advantages (cite68†Tan et al., 2026 ).\nL106: HP-RLVR draws on the same idea of revisiting decisions after completion, but constructs its weights from the full-vocabulary divergence between two views of the generating snapshot.\nL107: #### Privileged supervision and failures.\nL108: On-policy distillation scores student-generated sequences with a teacher (cite69†Agarwal et al., 2024 ). OPSD conditions the same model on privileged reference information (cite70†Zhao et al., 2026 ). Feedback-based self-distillation obtains privilege from interaction (cite71†Hübotter et al., 2026 ; cite72†Liu et al., 2026 ; cite73†Yeo et al., 2026 ). H^{2}SD uses verified student responses to refine successful trajectories and reference hints to correct failures (cite74†Cai et al., 2026 ).\nL109: HP-RLVR instead uses an unsigned divergence to weight reward-based updates, including updates on failures without a corrective reference. Negative reinforcement can itself improve reasoning (cite75†Zhu et al., 2025 ), but update dynamics depend on weighting (cite76†Chen et al., 2026 ), loss normalization, and length dynamics (cite77†Liu et al., 2025 ; cite52†Yu et al., 2025 ).\nL110: HP-RLVR and GRPO use trajectory-balanced loss reduction, and we report training-signal availability under this shared protocol (cite24†Sec. 4.4 ).\nL111: ### 2.2 Problem formulation\nL112: \nL113: Let $\\pi_{\\bm{\\theta}}$ generate a response or interactive trajectory ${\\bm{y}}$ for a prompt ${\\bm{x}}\\sim\\mathcal{D}$. A verifier assigns $R({\\bm{x}},{\\bm{y}})\\in[0,1]$, binary for task success or graded for partial credit. The objective is\nL114: \nL115:  | $$\\mathcal{J}_{\\mathrm{RLVR}}(\\theta)=\\mathbb{E}_{{\\bm{x}}\\sim\\mathcal{D},\\,{\\bm{y}}\\sim\\pi_{\\bm{\\theta}}}[R({\\bm{x}},{\\bm{y}})].$$  |  | (1)\nL116: For interactive trajectories, this expectation also includes environment transitions, but only policy-generated tokens contribute policy scores. With a parameter-independent verifier and environment, the score-function gradient is $\\mathbb{E}[R\\nabla_{\\bm{\\theta}}\\log\\pi_{\\bm{\\theta}}({\\bm{y}}\\mid{\\bm{x}})]$.\nL117: \nL118: GRPO samples $G$ trajectories per prompt and assigns\nL119: \nL120:  | $$\\hat{A}_{i}=\\frac{R_{i}-\\overline{R}}{\\sigma_{R}+\\epsilon_{\\mathrm{stab}}},\\qquad\\overline{R}=\\frac{1}{G}\\sum_{i=1}^{G}R_{i},$$  |  | (2)\nL121: where $\\sigma_{R}$ is the group reward standard deviation and $\\epsilon_{\\mathrm{stab}}>0$ stabilizes the denominator. The same advantage applies to every generated token of trajectory $i$. Homogeneous groups give $\\hat{A}_{i}=0$ for all members. Appendix cite29†A summarizes notation.\nL122: ## 3 Hindsight-Privileged RLVR\nL123: ### 3.1 From trajectory rewards to token credit\nL124: \nL125: With a single rollout, group-relative credit vanishes: $\\overline{R}=R_{i}$ and $\\hat{A}_{i}=0$ (cite78†Eq. 2 ). A baseline fixed before sampling can instead supply a reward comparison without requiring another attempt at the same prompt. The following standard identity provides the basis for this construction.\nL126: \nL127: {lemma}\nL128: [Historical baseline invariance] Condition on the completed history $\\mathcal{H}_{k}$, and let $\\mathbb{E}_{k}$ denote expectation over a fresh prompt from $\\mathcal{D}$ and a matched on-policy rollout. Assume the task distribution, verifier, and environment are parameter-independent, and the trajectory law has differentiable, fixed support with integrably dominated derivatives as specified in appendix cite31†B . Let $g_{\\theta}$ be the sum of policy scores over all generated tokens.\nL129: Any $\\mathcal{H}_{k}$-measurable baseline $b_{k}\\in[0,1]$, held fixed when differentiating, satisfies\nL130:  | $$\\mathbb{E}_{k}[(R-b_{k})g_{\\theta}]=\\nabla_{\\bm{\\theta}}\\mathcal{J}_{\\mathrm{RLVR}}(\\theta).$$  |  | (3)\nL131: \nL132: Thus a single on-policy trajectory yields an unbiased estimator of the unweighted reward gradient under these assumptions. We choose the mean reward of earlier trajectories. For batch $k$, let $\\mathcal{C}_{k}$ contain trajectories completed before that batch, and define\nL133:  | $\\displaystyle b_{k}$  | $\\displaystyle=\\frac{1}{|\\mathcal{C}_{k}|}\\sum_{i^{\\prime}\\in\\mathcal{C}_{k}}R_{i^{\\prime}},$  |  | (4)\nL134:  | $\\displaystyle s_{i}$  | $\\displaystyle=R_{i}-b_{k}.$  |\nL135: \nL136: When history is empty, use an initial $b_{\\mathrm{init}}\\in[0,1]$ fixed before sampling. The current batch enters the running sum and count only after its advantages are constructed.\nL137: Broadcasting $s_{i}$ still assigns the same credit to every token. For generated position $j$, we therefore separate trajectory evaluation from local allocation:\nL138: \nL139:  | $$A_{j}=\\underbrace{(R_{i}-b_{k})}_{\\text{trajectory evaluation}}\\,\\underbrace{w_{j}}_{\\text{local allocation}}.$$  |  | (5)\nL140: Here, $w_{j}\\in[0,1]$ is a token weight. The completed trajectory’s future offers additional context for revisiting earlier decisions. We use its effect on the generating policy’s predictions to construct $w_{j}$ (cite60†Fig. 2 ).\nL141: ### 3.2 Hindsight-conditioned scoring\nL142: \nL143: Let $h_{j}$ be the history visible when token $y_{j}$ was generated, $F_{j}$ the allowed future from the same trajectory, and $O$ the textual verifier report. With the generating snapshot frozen, the two scoring distributions are\nL144: \nL145:  | $\\displaystyle p_{j}$  | $\\displaystyle=\\pi_{\\theta_{\\mathrm{old}}}(\\cdot\\mid h_{j}),$  |  | (6)\nL146:  | $\\displaystyle q_{j}$  | $\\displaystyle=\\pi_{\\theta_{\\mathrm{old}}}(\\cdot\\mid h_{j},F_{j},O).$  |\nL147: Both views score the same position. $F_{j}$ excludes $y_{j}$ and contains the allowed continuation or later agent transitions. Only the training scorer receives $F_{j}$ and $O$ (appendix cite38†C.1 ). Both views reuse the same completed trajectory, so no additional continuations are sampled (cite79†Algorithm 1 ). Their distributional change supplies the local weight defined below.\nL148: A task adapter renders the verifier report as an outcome descriptor $O$, for example “the completed trajectory was unsuccessful” or “the solution passed $7$ of $10$ tests.” The adapter restates the report without adding an independent correctness judgment. An eligible future $F_{j}$ contains only allowed information from the same realized trajectory and excludes the scored token itself.\nL149: For a single response it is the allowed continuation after that token; for an agent it comprises the subsequent executed actions and observations, starting at the next transition. Scoring turn $t$ therefore uses transitions from $(\\mathrm{action}_{t+1},\\mathrm{observation}_{t+2})$ onward, excluding the immediate observation that could trivially reveal the current action. Future information is available only after completion, and never during rollout generation or deployment.\nL150: ### 3.3 Outcome-centered token credit\nL151: \nL152: The local allocation should vanish when the causal and hindsight views agree, and be positive when they differ. Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) satisfies this criterion (cite80†Lin, 1991 ).\nL153: \nL154: {lemma}\nL155: \nL156: [Zero divergence] Let $p,q$ be probability mass functions on a finite vocabulary $\\mathcal{V}$, and let $m=(p+q)/2$. With natural logarithms and zero-mass terms interpreted as zero,\nL157:  | $$\\operatorname{JSD}(p,q)=\\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\\mathrm{KL}}(p\\|m)+\\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\\mathrm{KL}}(q\\|m)$$  |\nL158: \nL159: is nonnegative and equals zero if and only if $p(v)=q(v)$ for every $v\\in\\mathcal{V}$.\nL160: \nL161: Motivated by cite15†Sec. 3.3 , we use the normalized divergence as the token weight. With $m_{j}=(p_{j}+q_{j})/2$, define\nL162: \nL163:  | $\\displaystyle w_{j}$  | $\\displaystyle=\\frac{\\operatorname{JSD}(p_{j},q_{j})}{\\log 2}\\in[0,1],$  |  | (7)\nL164: \nL165: The normalization gives the following bound on the resulting credit.\nL166: \nL167: {proposition}\nL168: [Bounded credit] Let $p_{j},q_{j}$ be probability mass functions on $\\mathcal{V}$, $R_{i},b_{k}\\in[0,1]$, and define $w_{j},A_{j}$ by cite81†Eqs. 7 and cite82†5 . Every scored position satisfies\nL169: \nL170:  | $$0\\leq w_{j}\\leq 1,\\qquad|A_{j}|\\leq|R_{i}-b_{k}|\\leq 1.$$  |\nL171: \nL172: Moreover, $w_{j}=1$ if and only if $p_{j}$ and $q_{j}$ have disjoint supports.\nL173: The advantages are detached before optimization, and positions with no eligible future are masked out. For scored positions, cite15†Sec. 3.3 gives $A_{j}=0$ exactly when $R_{i}=b_{k}$ or $p_{j}=q_{j}$. Otherwise, the centered reward determines the sign and the divergence determines the relative magnitude within a trajectory. A zero-advantage position contributes zero to the credit-weighted surrogate gradient.\nL174: Although reward enters explicitly as one scalar factor, $w_{j}$ also depends on its rendered descriptor $O$.\nL175: cite13†Section 3.1 applies to the unweighted trajectory score, before hindsight weighting, token-length normalization, and clipping. Since $w_{j}$ depends on the sampled trajectory, its baseline contribution need not have zero expectation. HP-RLVR therefore optimizes a weighted surrogate. Proofs of the two lemmas and the proposition appear in appendix cite30†B .\nL176: ### 3.4 Training objective and reduction\nL177: \nL178: Let $\\mathcal{S}_{i}$ be the eligible generated positions of trajectory $i$, $N_{i}=|\\mathcal{S}_{i}|$, and $B$ the number of contributing trajectories. We maximize the empirical PPO-style surrogate (cite83†Schulman et al., 2017 )\nL179:  | $\\displaystyle\\widehat{\\mathcal{J}}_{\\mathrm{HP}}(\\theta)$  | $\\displaystyle=\\frac{1}{B}\\sum_{i=1}^{B}\\frac{1}{N_{i}}\\sum_{j\\in\\mathcal{S}_{i}}\\ell_{j}(\\theta),$  |  | (8)\nL180:  | $\\displaystyle\\ell_{j}(\\theta)$  | $\\displaystyle=\\min\\bigl(r_{j}A_{j},\\operatorname{clip}(r_{j},1-\\epsilon,1+\\epsilon)A_{j}\\bigr),$  |\nL181:  | $\\displaystyle r_{j}(\\theta)$  | $\\displaystyle=\\frac{\\pi_{\\bm{\\theta}}(y_{j}\\mid h_{j})}{\\pi_{\\theta_{\\mathrm{old}}}(y_{j}\\mid h_{j})}.$  |\nL182: Only the current policy receives gradients, with prompts, observations, and hindsight context excluded from the policy loss. We use one optimization epoch and $\\epsilon=0.2$.\nL183: Both methods average over valid tokens within each trajectory and then over trajectories. For multi-turn data stored as rows, a row with $N_{it}$ valid tokens receives weight $N_{it}/N_{i}$ within its trajectory. Successful episodes can terminate before failed episodes, so equal turn-row weights can give longer episodes greater aggregate weight. This reduction avoids that extra weighting by turn count. Implementation checks are described in appendix cite19†4.1 .\nL184: cite79†Algorithm 1 summarizes one rollout/training step. Sampling and scoring follow cite13†Secs. 3.1 and cite14†3.2 . We study sequence-level verification in mathematics and code generation, and sparse terminal rewards in multi-turn ALFWorld interaction.\nL215: ### 4.1 Experimental setup\nL216: We evaluate Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B (cite61†Yang et al., 2025 ), comparing HP-RLVR ($G{=}1$) with a critic-free GRPO control ($G{=}8$). Paired runs share task data, seed $42$, LoRA configuration (cite87†Hu et al., 2021 ), Adam settings (cite88†Kingma and Ba, 2014 ), and one PPO epoch per rollout step.
