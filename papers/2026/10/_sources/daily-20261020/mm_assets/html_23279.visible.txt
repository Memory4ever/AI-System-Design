Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) answer questions about whole images well, yet they still struggle with fine-grained region understanding. A key reason is that text-only supervision never specifies which regions support an answer. To close this gap, we present Xiaomi-Lens-0, a 4B MLLM built on Qwen3.5 that predicts masks from its own visual representations. Specifically, a lightweight decoder combines vision-encoder patch features with visual latents, the language model’s mask-token states, so mask supervision reaches the shared backbone without an additional segmentation encoder. Because each latent decodes to a mask, the model can interleave reasoning with region-specific latents and thus think with visual latents, while grounded reasoning traces supervise the regions it visits during chain-of-thought. As a result, across all eight RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg splits, Xiaomi-Lens-0 gains 5.6 points on average over Qwen3.5 in referring comprehension and achieves the highest segmentation cIoU among compared models. Moreover, it surpasses Qwen3.5 across all perception benchmarks.
Figure 1: Examples of Xiaomi-Lens-0 across six tasks: referring expression comprehension, referring expression segmentation, multi-instance grounding, reasoning segmentation, embodied referring, and fine-grained perception. The model localizes targets with boxes or masks and answers questions about counting, attributes, and spatial relations.

1 Introduction
Visual grounding links natural-language expressions to image regions and is a foundational capability of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [1, 79]. It supports embodied intelligence [28] and multimodal agent systems [6, 12]. Specifically, referring expression comprehension (REC) and referring expression segmentation (RES) evaluate grounding directly, requiring the model to output the referenced target as a bounding box or a pixel-level mask, respectively. Beyond these tasks, visual questions about object counting, attributes, and spatial relations also depend on grounding: answering them requires pinpointing the regions they refer to and perceiving visual details [59, 54].
However, current MLLMs still struggle with fine-grained region understanding, often miscounting nearby objects or misidentifying the attributes of small regions [45, 23]. We attribute this to text-only supervision, under which the image serves only as a conditioning input and the training signal comes entirely from the text answer. Such supervision does not explicitly specify which image regions support the answer or how far those regions extend. Segmentation masks provide explicit spatial targets by labeling which pixels belong to a referenced region [17], which makes them a direct source of supervision for region extent.
Although existing MLLMs for RES [60, 39, 75] already train with mask supervision, most obtain the spatial detail for mask prediction from an additional encoder: LISA [18] and Sa2VA [71] rely on a hierarchical segmentation encoder [17, 40], and Qwen3-VL-Seg [66] fuses multi-scale features from a lightweight supplementary image encoder. Others decode masks from the MLLM’s own visual features: PixelLM [41] decodes from multiple layers of its visual encoder, and MLLMSeg [50] fuses shallow visual encoder features with the language model’s visual tokens. However, both keep the vision encoder frozen, so the mask loss does not reach the shared visual features. Moreover, across both lines, mask supervision is treated as a means to segmentation and evaluated mainly on RES and grounding, leaving unexplored whether it can also improve fine-grained perception and visual reasoning. Therefore, we investigate whether dense mask supervision can improve fine-grained perception and visual reasoning by requiring the MLLM to predict masks from its own visual representations.
In this report, we develop Xiaomi-Lens-0, a 4B MLLM built on Qwen3.5 [37] that predicts region masks directly from its own visual representations, bringing pixel-level supervision into the backbone shared with language generation. Concretely, the model reads spatial features from the patch grid of its vision encoder [10], taken before the spatial merger, without an additional hierarchical visual encoder or multi-scale feature fusion. To specify the target region, it generates a special mask token [18] whose contextual state, termed a visual latent, represents the region requested by the instruction or referenced in the preceding response. A lightweight mask decoder combines this visual latent with the shared visual features to predict the corresponding mask. Joint language and mask training updates both the shared visual features and the language model states that identify target regions, thereby giving the representations used for perception and language generation explicit supervision over region extent.
In this architecture, the mask decoder also serves as a visual latent decoder. This allows the MLLM to interleave textual reasoning with region-specific visual latents [65, 21], each of which is decoded into a mask that explicitly grounds the corresponding reasoning evidence in the image. We refer to this formulation as thinking with visual latents. To train this capability, we construct grounded reasoning traces [42, 26] that pair intermediate region references with masks, and jointly supervise both the textual responses and the decoded masks. This extends pixel-level supervision to intermediate regions referenced in chain-of-thought (CoT) [55], making their spatial extent explicit and inspectable through decoded masks.
We evaluate Xiaomi-Lens-0 across six tasks (Figure 1): REC, RES, multi-instance grounding, reasoning segmentation, embodied referring, and fine-grained perception. It improves REC over its Qwen3.5 backbone on all eight splits of RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg, with an average gain of 5.6 points, and achieves the highest cIoU among the compared models on every RES split. For multi-instance grounding on COCO 2017 [24], it also achieves the highest F1 averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95. Beyond spatial prediction, it outperforms its backbone on every perception benchmark. To evaluate referring under conditions that existing benchmarks rarely cover, we also build LensBench, which crosses explicit and implicit instructions with single and multiple targets across general, aerial, driving, and panoramic imagery, with every paired unit human-reviewed for target completeness and instruction–mask agreement (Section 3.3). Xiaomi-Lens-0 achieves the highest cIoU among the compared models in the aerial, driving, and panoramic domains, although implicit instructions remain challenging, particularly when selecting multiple targets in panoramic scenes. Finally, ablations show that grounded reasoning traces further increase REC and RES scores.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:
•
Mask prediction from shared visual representations. We develop Xiaomi-Lens-0, a 4B MLLM that decodes region masks from the patch grid of its own vision encoder, taken before the spatial merger, and the visual latents produced by its language model, without an additional hierarchical visual encoder or multi-scale feature fusion. Joint language and mask training, therefore, supervises the extent of the region in the representations shared with language generation.
•
Thinking with visual latents. We interleave textual reasoning with visual latents that the mask decoder maps to masks, and construct grounded reasoning traces that extend pixel-level supervision to intermediate regions in CoT.
•
LensBench. We introduce a human-reviewed referring benchmark that pairs single and multiple targets with explicit and implicit instructions across four visual domains, enabling paired comparisons of target selection and mask prediction under varying instruction semantics.
•
Mask supervision also improves perception. Beyond the spatial gains above, Xiaomi-Lens-0 outperforms its Qwen3.5 backbone on every fine-grained perception benchmark. Moreover, ablations show that adding mask supervision to box supervision further improves all three evaluated general perception benchmarks and the REC validation splits of RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg.
2 Method
Xiaomi-Lens-0 predicts masks directly from its own visual representations, so mask supervision must reach the same features and states that support language generation. As shown in Figure 2, the shared vision encoder supplies merged tokens to the language model and its patch-level feature grid to a lightweight mask decoder. The decoder combines this grid with a region-specific visual latent from the language model, and joint training propagates the mask loss into both. We first motivate decoding from the shared encoder (Section 2.1), then describe the architecture and joint objective (Section 2.2), extend mask prediction to intermediate regions through thinking with visual latents (Section 2.3), and present the two-stage training recipe (Section 2.4).
Figure 2: Xiaomi-Lens-0 architecture. The language model consumes spatially merged visual tokens. Each generated <|mask|> token has a contextual state that queries the encoder’s patch-level features through a lightweight mask decoder. Patch-wise compatibility scores are reassembled into a spatial grid and upsampled with two transposed-convolution layers.
2.1 Mask Prediction from Shared Visual Features
Mask prediction requires spatial features that describe region extent and a query that identifies the target. In LISA and Sa2VA, the spatial features come from an additional hierarchical segmentation encoder, and Qwen3-VL-Seg fuses multi-scale features from a supplementary image encoder. Under these designs, the MLLM’s own visual representation is not required to encode the region detail that the mask loss supervises. To supervise the shared representation directly, we decode masks from a single patch grid obtained before the MLLM’s spatial merger, without additional encoder or multi-scale fusion. This choice preserves the encoder’s patch resolution and provides the mask loss with a direct path to the visual features used for language generation.
The patch grid of a Vision Transformer (ViT) [10] provides spatially indexed features enriched by attention across the image. Its residual structure expresses the final representation as the initial patch features plus successive transformations:
𝐱l=𝐱l−1+ℱl​(𝐱l−1),𝐱L=𝐱0+∑l=1Lℱl​(𝐱l−1),\mathbf{x}_{l}=\mathbf{x}_{l-1}+\mathcal{F}_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{l-1}),\qquad\mathbf{x}_{L}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{F}_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{l-1}),
(1)
where 𝐱0\mathbf{x}_{0} contains the initial patch features and ℱl\mathcal{F}_{l} is the residual transformation at layer ll. The identity mapping 𝐱l−1\mathbf{x}_{l-1} keeps the fine-grained signals produced by earlier layers from being overwritten by higher-level semantic abstractions. Instead, those signals propagate through the network and accumulate into 𝐱L\mathbf{x}_{L}, so the final representation retains a high-mutual-information pathway to the input image.
LongCat-Next formalizes this property as intrinsic information recovery and tests it by pairing frozen encoders with a lightweight pixel decoder [31]. Specifically, semantic encoders with residual pathways reconstruct coarse image structure despite receiving no reconstruction supervision, and a randomly initialized ViT even reconstructs better than its pretrained counterpart, which indicates that recoverability follows from the network structure rather than from learned semantics. Moreover, QwenViT [1], without its spatial merger, behaves like a plain ViT that maintains a single patch resolution and still recovers coarse image structure, whereas the merger degrades fidelity because its aggressive downsampling discards fine-grained, high-frequency detail. Because the patch grid is taken before the merger, it preserves exactly the spatial structure that mask prediction targets, and it remains part of the encoder’s shared computation. We therefore use the patch-level grid as the spatial input to the decoder. A contextual state from the language model identifies the target, so the same grid supports multiple region predictions.
2.2 Model Architecture
2.2.1 Shared Visual Features and Visual Latents
Language generation and mask prediction begin with the same image encoding. Given an image I∈ℝ3×H×WI\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W}, the vision encoder ℰv\mathcal{E}_{v} produces a spatial grid of patch features, which we flatten for notational convenience:
𝐕0=ℰv​(I)=[𝐯1,…,𝐯Np]∈ℝNp×dv,Np=hv​wv,\mathbf{V}^{0}=\mathcal{E}_{v}(I)=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{N_{p}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times d_{v}},\qquad N_{p}=h_{v}w_{v},
(2)
where (hv,wv)(h_{v},w_{v}) denotes the patch-grid resolution. Following the Qwen3.5 backbone [37], an s×ss\times s spatial merger groups neighboring patch features, and the visual projector ϕ\phi maps the merged features to the language-model width dld_{l}:
𝐕=ϕ⁡(Merges⁡(𝐕0))∈ℝNv×dl,Nv=Np/s2.\mathbf{V}=\phi\!\left(\operatorname{Merge}_{s}(\mathbf{V}^{0})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times d_{l}},\qquad N_{v}=N_{p}/s^{2}.
(3)
The autoregressive language model processes the merged tokens 𝐕\mathbf{V} together with the system and user tokens. At decoding step tt, the language-model head maps the contextual state 𝐡t∈ℝdl\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}} to a next-token prediction.
To mark each region for mask decoding, we add a special <|mask|> token to the output vocabulary. For its kk-th occurrence at response position tkt_{k}, we take the contextual state 𝐦k=𝐡tk∈ℝdl\mathbf{m}_{k}=\mathbf{h}_{t_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}} as the visual latent. The token identity is shared across occurrences, but each state depends on the image, the instruction, and the preceding response, so it represents the region requested by the instruction or referenced in the preceding response. A response can contain several mask tokens, each supplying the query for a separate mask.
2.2.2 Mask Decoding
The mask decoder scores each patch of the grid 𝐕0\mathbf{V}^{0} (Equation 2) for the requested target. A lightweight Transformer layer first refines these features with global visual context, and a linear projection then maps each patch to a mask feature space of width dmd_{m}:
𝐅=Pv​(𝒯mask​(𝐕0))=[𝐟1,…,𝐟Np]∈ℝNp×dm.\mathbf{F}=P_{v}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{mask}}(\mathbf{V}^{0})\right)=[\mathbf{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{N_{p}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}\times d_{m}}.
(4)
Next, we project the mask-token state into the same space, 𝐪k=Pm​(𝐦k)∈ℝdm\mathbf{q}_{k}=P_{m}(\mathbf{m}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}, and use it as a dynamic classifier over patch locations. Its compatibility with patch pp is
zk,p=𝐪k𝖳​𝐟pdm,p=1,…,Np.z_{k,p}=\frac{\mathbf{q}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}_{p}}{\sqrt{d_{m}}},\qquad p=1,\ldots,N_{p}.
(5)
Finally, we reassemble the NpN_{p} scores into the original patch layout and apply two transposed convolution layers to produce a dense mask logit map. Applying a sigmoid gives
𝐌^k=σ⁡(𝒰mask​(Reassemblehv,wv⁡(𝐳k)))∈[0,1]H×W,\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{k}=\sigma\!\left(\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{mask}}\bigl(\operatorname{Reassemble}_{h_{v},w_{v}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\bigr)\right)\in[0,1]^{H\times W},
(6)
where 𝒰mask\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{mask}} is the two-layer upsampling head and σ\sigma is the sigmoid function. Because all regions share the same image features, we compute 𝐅\mathbf{F} once per image and reuse it for every visual latent, changing only the query 𝐪k\mathbf{q}_{k}. Beyond the shared vision encoder, the decoder consists of one Transformer refinement layer and one upsampling path.
2.2.3 Joint Language and Mask Supervision
Pixel-level mask supervision gives each visual latent an explicit target region. For a training example with KK supervised <|mask|> occurrences, the kk-th occurrence is paired with a binary target mask 𝐌k\mathbf{M}_{k}. The mask objective combines pixel-wise binary cross-entropy and Dice loss [32]:
ℒmask=1K​∑k=1K[λbce​ℒbce​(𝐌^k,𝐌k)+λdice​ℒdice​(𝐌^k,𝐌k)],\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\lambda_{\mathrm{bce}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bce}}(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k})+\lambda_{\mathrm{dice}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dice}}(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k})\right],
(7)
where λbce\lambda_{\mathrm{bce}} and λdice\lambda_{\mathrm{dice}} weight the two terms. The cross-entropy term supervises individual pixels, and the Dice term supervises region overlap. Adding the mask objective to the next-token prediction loss gives the joint objective:
ℒ=ℒntp+ℒmask.\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ntp}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}}.
(8)
During joint training, the mask loss is propagated to the vision encoder via 𝐅\mathbf{F} and to the language model via 𝐦k\mathbf{m}_{k}, while ℒntp\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ntp}} supervises the response sequence. Region extent must therefore be encoded in the shared image features and in the contextual states that identify targets. Since text answers are generated from these same representations, mask supervision also improves fine-grained perception, as Section 4.4 shows.
2.3 Thinking with Visual Latents
Because each visual latent is a contextual state of the language model, the mask decoder also decodes visual latents produced during reasoning. Specifically, in thinking with visual latents, the response interleaves textual reasoning with <|mask|> tokens placed at region references. Each token’s contextual state represents the region discussed in the preceding text, and the decoder maps that state to a mask over the shared image grid. A counting trace, for example, can enumerate individual instances with one visual latent each, and a spatial comparison can assign separate latents to the objects being compared. Each latent is tied to an explicit image region.
We supervise these intermediate regions using grounded reasoning traces that pair region references with target masks (Section 3.2). The next-token loss supervises the reasoning text, spatial references, and final answer, while the mask loss supervises the extent of each annotated region. The joint objective in Equation 8 supervises both final segmentation targets and intermediate regions in a CoT.
During inference, mask decoding is an output branch alongside autoregressive generation: subsequent text is conditioned on the image and the preceding response tokens, and no crops or region features are fed back to the language model. Thus, the decoded masks make the regions behind intermediate statements inspectable, while training extends pixel-level supervision to the states that represent those regions.
2.4 Training Recipe
We train in two stages. Stage 1 pretrains the mask decoder to map frozen representations to regions, and Stage 2 jointly fine-tunes the backbone and decoder. Figure 3 summarizes the data mixtures. The in-house data, detailed in Section 3, provide masks for referring-expression targets and for intermediate regions in grounded reasoning traces.
Figure 3: Training data for Stage 1 mask decoder pretraining (left) and Stage 2 supervised fine-tuning (right). Donut charts show the proportions of public and in-house data; the adjacent lists give the public datasets and their sample counts.
2.4.1 Stage 1: Mask Decoder Pretraining
We freeze the pretrained backbone and optimize only the mask decoder with Equation 7, so that it learns to decode regions from fixed image features and visual latents. The training mixture combines in-house data with public sources covering objects, parts, and referring expressions: Objects365 [43], LVIS [13], PACO [38], PhraseCut [57], RefCOCO, RefCOCO+ [67], and RefCOCOg [30, 33]. These sources expose the decoder to regions at different scales and levels of specificity before joint adaptation.
2.4.2 Stage 2: Supervised Fine-Tuning
We then unfreeze the backbone and optimize the full model with Equation 8. The language loss supervises the response sequences, and the mask loss updates both the shared visual features and the visual latents. The data combine in-house referring-expression and grounded reasoning examples with COCO 2017 [24], RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, RefCOCOg, RoboRefIt [28], and ReasonSeg [18].
3 Data Pipeline
The data pipeline supplies the two forms of region supervision used in Section 2. Specifically, referring-expression data pairs with instructions, final target masks, and supervises REC and RES outputs (Section 3.1). Grounded reasoning data attach masks to the regions referenced at intermediate steps of a CoT and supervise thinking with visual latents (Section 3.2). Both pipelines fix or verify the region annotation before accepting the accompanying language. We then present LensBench (Section 3.3), which evaluates target selection and region extent as instruction semantics, target cardinality, and visual domain vary.
3.1 Referring-Expression Training Data
Referring-expression data provide explicit region targets for REC and RES. We first establish a box and mask for each candidate object, then generate instructions that describe it, and finally verify that each instruction selects the annotated region (Figure 4). Fixing the target before instruction synthesis allows several descriptions of the same object to share a single spatial annotation.
Figure 4: RES data pipeline. (a) Candidate objects are localized, their descriptions are checked against image crops, and masks are generated. (b) The masked view and original image guide referring-instruction synthesis. (c) Re-grounding and contextual verification check that each instruction identifies its intended target.
3.1.1 Visual Grounding
We establish candidate regions through object discovery and crop-level verification. Given an image II, object discovery returns boxes bib_{i} with short descriptions did_{i}. Before generating masks or instructions, a crop–text check 𝒱crop​(X,t)∈{0,1}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{crop}}(X,t)\in\{0,1\} verifies each description against its image crop. The accepted candidates are
𝒞I={(bi,di)∈𝒟⁡(I)|𝒱crop​(Crop⁡(I,bi),di)=1},\mathcal{C}_{I}=\left\{(b_{i},d_{i})\in\mathcal{D}(I)\;\middle|\;\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{crop}}\bigl(\operatorname{Crop}(I,b_{i}),d_{i}\bigr)=1\right\},
(9)
where 𝒟⁡(I)\mathcal{D}(I) is the set of discovered box–description pairs. For each (bi,di)∈𝒞I(b_{i},d_{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{I}, mask generation produces Mi=𝒮⁡(I,bi,di)∈{0,1}H×WM_{i}=\mathcal{S}(I,b_{i},d_{i})\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}.
3.1.2 Instruction Synthesis
We generate referring instructions from both the target appearance and its scene context. For each retained target, a masked view I~i=I⊙Mi\widetilde{I}_{i}=I\odot M_{i} preserves the target pixels and sets the background to black. The generator then receives this view to identify the referent and the original image to describe its relation to surrounding objects:
𝒬i={qi​j}j=1Ki=𝒢⁡(I,I~i),\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\{q_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{K_{i}}=\mathcal{G}(I,\widetilde{I}_{i}),
(10)
where KiK_{i} is the number of expressions generated for target ii. The expressions identify the target through location, appearance, action, function, or contrasts with neighboring objects; a blanket, for example, can be described by its pattern or its use for warmth. Concise descriptions, negation, and quantity references diversify the wording while preserving the single annotated target. A quantity reference can thus distinguish one object within a counted group.
3.1.3 Consistency Filtering
We retain instructions that agree with the target in both localization and meaning. Re-grounding each qi​jq_{ij} in the original image must return exactly one box, ℛ⁡(I,qi​j)={bi​j′}\mathcal{R}(I,q_{ij})=\{b^{\prime}_{ij}\}, whose intersection over union (IoU) with the candidate box meets a threshold. In addition, a separate check 𝒱ref​(I,I~i,qi​j)∈{0,1}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{ref}}(I,\widetilde{I}_{i},q_{ij})\in\{0,1\} uses the original image and masked view to assess whether the expression uniquely describes the target and its contextual relations. With an IoU threshold τ\tau, the retained instructions are
𝒬~i={qi​j∈𝒬i|ℛ(I,qi​j)={bi​j′},IoU(bi,bi​j′)≥τ,𝒱ref(I,I~i,qi​j)=1}.\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}=\left\{q_{ij}\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{R}(I,q_{ij})=\{b^{\prime}_{ij}\},\;\operatorname{IoU}(b_{i},b^{\prime}_{ij})\geq\tau,\;\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{ref}}(I,\widetilde{I}_{i},q_{ij})=1\right\}.
(11)
The semantic check screens for references to nearby instances or properties absent from the target, complementing the box-overlap criterion. For each retained instruction, the final box is the tight bounding box of the mask, box⁡(Mi)\operatorname{box}(M_{i}). The resulting sample (I,qi​j,box⁡(Mi),Mi)(I,q_{ij},\operatorname{box}(M_{i}),M_{i}) pairs the same referent with box and pixel-level supervision.
3.2 Grounded Reasoning Data
Grounded reasoning data supervise the intermediate regions used in thinking with visual latents (Section 2.3). They target the region-level failures that motivate this work, such as miscounting nearby objects and misidentifying the attributes of small regions. Each visual question answering (VQA) example pairs an image and question with a reasoning trace, a final answer, and masks for the trace’s region references. We first synthesize question–answer pairs, verify the answers independently, and then construct traces with mask-annotated region references (Figure 5). The spatial training signal thus extends from final segmentation targets to the regions discussed along the reasoning trace.
Figure 5: VQA data pipeline. (a) After image and region screening, an MLLM generates question–answer pairs with multiple-choice or numeric answers, optionally referring to regions by temporary IDs. (b) Rule-based filters remove flawed questions, options are shuffled, and an independent checker must reproduce the reference answer. (c) A reasoning model generates traces with box references. Traces that are complete, use valid boxes, and match the verified answer are paired with masks whose bounding boxes agree with the referenced boxes.
3.2.1 Question–Answer Synthesis
We synthesize questions about counting, attributes, and spatial relations, each of which requires perceiving specific regions. After screening images and available region annotations, we assign temporary IDs to retained boxes so that questions can distinguish similar instances or combine information across regions. An MLLM generates question–answer pairs with multiple-choice or numeric answers, providing a common format for exact-match verification.
3.2.2 Question–Answer Verification
We verify the answers before generating reasoning traces. Rule-based filters remove malformed or duplicate options, near-duplicate questions, and wording that reveals the answer; multiple-choice options are then shuffled to remove position cues. Next, an independent checker answers each remaining question from the image without seeing the reference answer, receiving region hints only when the question names them. We retain a pair when its predicted option label or numeric value exactly matches the reference. Final examples use the original image, retaining ID markers only for regions named in the question.
3.2.3 Trace Generation and Mask Annotation
We generate reasoning traces that explicitly reference the image regions discussed at each intermediate step. For each verified pair, a reasoning model produces candidate traces that interleave text with <bbox> references and end with an answer. We retain complete traces with valid box references and an answer matching the verified reference. When several traces pass, we select the shortest to limit redundancy; pairs with no passing trace are discarded.
After trace selection, each accepted box reference is paired with an existing instance mask or, when unavailable, a mask generated by SAM 3 [4]. We accept the mask when the IoU between its tight bounding box and the referenced box exceeds a threshold, and append a <|mask|> token to the reference. Its contextual state becomes the visual latent supervised by that mask through Equation 7. Figure 6 shows counting and spatial-relation traces with masks attached to the regions named at each step.

3.3 LensBench
Referring benchmarks such as RefCOCO pair each expression with a single target in everyday photographs and usually name the target’s category. To evaluate a broader range of referring conditions, LensBench examines how target selection and mask prediction vary when the instruction describes targets indirectly, requests several instances, or comes from a different visual domain. It covers general, aerial, driving, and panoramic imagery, with 300 complete evaluation units per domain. Each unit pairs one image with two target masks and four instructions crossing single and multiple targets with explicit and implicit wording (Figure 7). The benchmark contains 1,200 paired units and 4,800 instruction-level samples.
3.3.1 Benchmark Design
Each unit separates the number of requested instances from the way they are described. Single instructions select one instance, and multiple instructions select at least two distinct instances. Explicit instructions name the category and specify the target set through attributes, relations, or quantity. Implicit instructions describe the targets through function, purpose, or commonsense relations, omitting the category and direct synonyms so that selection requires an image-supported inference.
For an image II, let GG be a set of target instances from the same category, with |G|≥2|G|\geq 2, and let f∈Gf\in G be a distinguishable focus instance. The target masks are
Msingle=Mf,Mmultiple=⋁i∈GMi,M_{\mathrm{single}}=M_{f},\qquad M_{\mathrm{multiple}}=\bigvee_{i\in G}M_{i},
(12)
where ⋁\bigvee denotes pixel-wise logical OR. We generate one explicit and one implicit instruction for each mask, yielding four evaluation samples from the same image. The two instruction types share the same ground-truth mask within each cardinality, enabling paired comparisons.
3.3.2 Benchmark Construction
We fix the target instances and masks before generating instructions. Candidate annotations follow the visual-grounding procedure in Section 3.1.1; instance curation verifies identity and visible extent and resolves duplicates. Masks are stored at their original resolution using lossless run-length encoding.
Within each image, we group countable instances by category. Each group must contain every visible instance within its intended scope, without unrelated objects or duplicate physical instances. We then select a focus instance f∈Gf\in G and fix both target masks before generating instructions. The generator receives the original image, a crop containing the focus instance and its surroundings, and target metadata:
𝒬u={qu​j}j=14=𝒢⁡(I,Crop⁡(I,cf),zu),\mathcal{Q}_{u}=\{q_{uj}\}_{j=1}^{4}=\mathcal{G}\bigl(I,\operatorname{Crop}(I,c_{f}),z_{u}\bigr),
(13)
where u=(I,G,f)u=(I,G,f) indexes the unit and cfc_{f} expands box⁡(Mf)\operatorname{box}(M_{f}) to include surrounding context. The indices j=1,2,3,4j=1,2,3,4 correspond to single–explicit, single–implicit, multiple–explicit, and multiple–implicit, respectively. The metadata zuz_{u} gives the canonical category, group size, and focus bounding box box⁡(Mf)\operatorname{box}(M_{f}). Each instruction must preserve its assigned target set and semantic granularity.

3.3.3 Consistency Filtering
Automatic screening checks mask quality, instruction semantics, and agreement with independently re-grounded instances. The mask check 𝒱mask​(u)\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{mask}}(u) assesses identity and visible extent, detects duplicate physical instances, and verifies the union of individual masks. The instruction check 𝒱ref​(u,q)\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{ref}}(u,q) uses individual and union masks to assess whether the wording is natural, self-contained, precise about the target set, and consistent with its assigned explicit or implicit condition. The intended instance set is Gq={f}G_{q}=\{f\} for single and Gq=GG_{q}=G for multiple.
To check whether each instruction recovers the intended targets, re-grounding ℛ⁡(I,q)\mathcal{R}(I,q) receives only the original image and instruction, with no target annotations. Its output retains duplicate predictions. We match predicted boxes to target instances one to one, maximizing total IoU, and denote the prediction assigned to instance ii by bq​i′b^{\prime}_{qi}. Let δq\delta_{q} count pairs of identical normalized prediction boxes. With an IoU threshold τ\tau, the instructions passing all automatic checks are
𝒬~u={q∈𝒬u||ℛ(I,q)|=|Gq|,δq=0,mini∈Gq⁡IoU⁡(box⁡(Mi),bq​i′)≥τ,𝒱mask​(u)=1,𝒱ref​(u,q)=1}.\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{u}=\left\{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{u}\;\middle|\;\begin{aligned} &|\mathcal{R}(I,q)|=|G_{q}|,\quad\delta_{q}=0,\\
&\min_{i\in G_{q}}\operatorname{IoU}\bigl(\operatorname{box}(M_{i}),b^{\prime}_{qi}\bigr)\geq\tau,\\
&\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{mask}}(u)=1,\quad\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{ref}}(u,q)=1\end{aligned}\right\}.
(14)
A unit passes automatic screening when all four instructions pass, that is, 𝒬~u=𝒬u\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{u}=\mathcal{Q}_{u}. Uncertain judgments and failed requests are recorded separately to inform human review.
Finally, human reviewers make the final selection or revision decision for each complete unit. They inspect the original image, individual instance masks, both target masks, and all four instructions together to check target completeness and instruction–mask agreement. Only complete four-sample units enter LensBench, preserving the paired comparisons. Each sample is (I,q,box⁡(Mq),Mq)(I,q,\operatorname{box}(M_{q}),M_{q}), where Mq=⋁i∈GqMiM_{q}=\bigvee_{i\in G_{q}}M_{i} is the mask assigned to instruction qq.
4 Experiments
The experiments test the question raised in Section 1: whether dense mask supervision, applied to masks predicted from the MLLM’s own visual representations, improves fine-grained perception and visual reasoning. We address three questions. (1) Spatial prediction: without an additional encoder for spatial features, how well does Xiaomi-Lens-0 predict boxes and masks compared with methods that use one? (2) Fine-grained perception: do the gains extend to counting, attribute, and spatial-relation questions answered in text? (3) Attribution: what do mask supervision and grounded reasoning traces each contribute? Section 4.2 addresses the first two questions across six tasks, Section 4.3 examines instruction semantics and visual domains on LensBench, and Section 4.4 addresses the third.
4.1 Evaluation Setup
The evaluation covers REC, RES, multi-instance grounding, reasoning segmentation, embodied referring, and fine-grained perception. All scores are percentages, and differences are reported in percentage points. Main-model results use the equal-weight average of three Stage 2 checkpoints, with a generation limit of 16,384 tokens.
•
REC and RES. We use the validation, TestA, and TestB splits of RefCOCO and RefCOCO+ [67], and the validation and test splits of RefCOCOg [30, 33]. For REC, Prec@0.5 is the percentage of predicted boxes that have an IoU of at least 0.5 with the target box. A complementary box-grounding evaluation reports F1 at IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.95, and the mean F1 over thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05 (F1@mIoU). RES uses cIoU on the same eight splits.
•
Multi-instance grounding. COCO 2017 [24] evaluates localization of multiple object instances. We report recall, precision, and F1 at box IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.95, along with their mIoU aggregates.
•
Reasoning segmentation and embodied referring. ReasonSeg [18] evaluates target selection from reasoning-dependent instructions, and RoboRefIt [28] evaluates referring expressions in embodied scenes. We report gIoU and cIoU on the ReasonSeg validation and test sets and on RoboRefIt TestA and TestB, following the ReasonSeg convention, in which gIoU is the mean per-image IoU.
•
Fine-grained perception. The English and Chinese MME-RealWorld sets (MME-RW-en and MME-RW-cn) [77] and CV-Bench (CV-B) [47] assess general perception. V* [59] and the 4K and 8K HR-Bench sets (HR-4K and HR-8K) [54] assess high-resolution perception. CountQA [45] and ColorBench (ColorB) [23] assess counting and color recognition. All perception scores measure answer accuracy, with no box or mask output.
For NN image–instruction pairs with binary prediction and target masks PiP_{i} and GiG_{i} and nonzero unions, the benchmark definitions are
gIoU=1N​∑i=1N|Pi∩Gi||Pi∪Gi|,cIoU=∑i=1N|Pi∩Gi|∑i=1N|Pi∪Gi|,\mathrm{gIoU}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|P_{i}\cap G_{i}|}{|P_{i}\cup G_{i}|},\qquad\mathrm{cIoU}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}|P_{i}\cap G_{i}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}|P_{i}\cup G_{i}|},
(15)
where ii indexes image–instruction pairs. gIoU weights each pair equally, while cIoU pools pixel intersections and unions across the dataset and therefore weights large targets more heavily.
On LensBench (Section 3.3), we report mask cIoU and box Prec@0.5 by instruction type and visual domain.

4.4 Ablation Study
The ablations attribute the gains to the two components introduced in Section 2. The BBox-only variant is trained on the same Stage 2 grounding and reasoning data with the mask loss disabled, isolating pixel-level mask supervision. The Grounding-only variant keeps mask supervision but omits grounded reasoning data, isolating thinking with visual latents. Both tables include the original backbone as a reference.
Mask supervision and perception.
Box supervision alone already improves both REC and perception over the backbone (Table 8). The BBox-only variant gains 3.1, 4.9, and 2.2 validation REC points on RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg, and reaches 68.8 on MME-RW-en, 66.1 on MME-RW-cn, and 87.9 on CV-B, gains of 2.3, 5.8, and 2.5 points.
Adding mask supervision to this box-supervised setting raises all six metrics further. The full model’s REC advantage over BBox-only ranges from 1.1 to 2.4 points, and its perception advantage from 0.2 to 1.4 points. The REC advantage is largest on RefCOCO+, at 2.4 points, and smallest on RefCOCO, at 1.1 points. Since RefCOCO+ excludes absolute location words, mask supervision helps most where the target must be separated from its neighbors by appearance and shape, which a box encodes only coarsely. Because the two variants differ only in the mask loss, these differences answer the question posed in Section 1: supervising region extent in the MLLM’s own representations improves box localization and fine-grained perception expressed in text.

5 Related Work
Visual Grounding in MLLMs
MLLMs commonly ground language by generating box coordinates in the text stream, as in Shikra, Ferret, and CogVLM [5, 74, 52, 68, 69]; Florence-2 casts grounding and other vision tasks as sequence prediction [61]. Under this interface, spatial supervision reaches the model only through coordinate tokens, and the extent of the referenced region remains implicit. Youtu-VL extends autoregressive supervision to visual targets alongside language and examines how such supervision shapes multimodal learning [56]. For pixel-level grounding, RES makes region extent explicit by requiring a pixel-level mask for the referenced target.
Segmentation with MLLMs
Most MLLM-based segmentation systems obtain spatial features from a dedicated segmenter. LISA conditions a SAM-based branch on a generated token [18, 17]; LISA++ adds instance-aware outputs, and GSVA handles multiple or empty targets [64, 60]. GLaMM and OMG-LLaVA interleave masks with grounded conversation [39, 75], and Sa2VA couples an MLLM with SAM 2 for image and video tasks [71, 40]. Seg-Zero trains a reasoning model to prompt a frozen segmenter [27], and InstructSAM learns parallel instance queries that condition SAM 3 [72, 4]. Qwen3-VL-Seg replaces the segmenter with a lightweight supplementary encoder and combines multi-scale feature fusion with box-guided decoding and iterative mask refinement [66].
Alongside these dedicated segmentation branches, another line derives spatial predictions from the MLLM’s own representations or output interface. PixelLM decodes masks from features at multiple layers of its vision encoder [41], and MLLMSeg decodes them from shallow vision-encoder features fused with the language model’s visual tokens [50]; both keep the vision encoder frozen. ClawMachine refers to entities through groups of visual tokens, and PaDT maps patch-derived reference tokens to boxes or masks with a lightweight decoder [29, 44]. Text4Seg represents segmentation through textual descriptors, while UFO combines a language interface with embedding retrieval [20, 46]. Both lines evaluate the benefit of mask supervision mainly on segmentation and grounding. To study the effect beyond spatial prediction, Xiaomi-Lens-0 decodes masks from a single patch-level grid of the shared vision encoder and evaluates whether the resulting supervision also improves fine-grained perception, as measured by text answers.
Grounded and Latent Visual Reasoning
Fine-grained visual reasoning often requires details beyond a global image description. To capture such details, Cambrian-1 integrates high-resolution features through a spatially aware connector [47]; V* searches for relevant regions, and Divide, Conquer, and Combine processes high-resolution patches with textual summaries [59, 54]. OpenAI o3 thinks with images through cropping, zooming, and other image transformations [34], and DeepEyes, Pixel Reasoner, Chain-of-Focus, and Mini-o3 learn or control such image interactions during visual search [78, 49, 76, 19].
Complementing such image interactions, other work either grounds intermediate reasoning steps explicitly or carries them in visual form. Visual CoT supervises intermediate box references [42], and UniPixel links intermediate mask pointers to object-centric reasoning [26]. Meanwhile, Mirage interleaves latent visual tokens with text during multimodal reasoning [65], and LVR performs reasoning in the visual embedding space [21]. Thinking with visual latents connects these directions: each region reference produces a visual latent that the shared mask decoder maps to a mask, and the mask loss on that latent updates the backbone. Intermediate regions thus receive pixel-level supervision and remain inspectable through the decoded masks.
6 Conclusion
We presented Xiaomi-Lens-0, a 4B MLLM that decodes region masks from the patch-level grid of its shared vision encoder and from visual latents, the language-model states of generated mask tokens. The mask loss therefore reaches the representations used for language generation, and thinking with visual latents extends this pixel-level supervision to the regions named in a grounded chain of thought.
Without an additional spatial encoder, Xiaomi-Lens-0 achieves the highest cIoU on all eight RES splits, improves REC over its Qwen3.5 backbone on every split, and achieves the highest threshold-averaged F1 for multi-instance grounding on COCO 2017. Moreover, it surpasses the backbone on every fine-grained perception benchmark; the BBox-only ablation attributes a further gain on all evaluated REC and perception metrics to mask supervision, and grounded reasoning further raises REC and RES scores. On LensBench, it leads in cIoU on aerial, driving, and panoramic imagery, although implicit instructions remain harder, especially for multiple targets in panoramic scenes.

Appendix A Conversation Format and Examples
The examples below illustrate how spatial predictions are serialized in a response. A <bbox> block contains box coordinates, and a mask block contains the special <|mask|> token. The contextual state at this token is the visual latent passed to the decoder in Section 2.2.
Explicit Instruction: Box Output
The clothing description directly identifies the requested person. The response returns a single box after an empty reasoning block.
[
{
"role": "user",
"content": "<image>\nLocate all instances in the image that match the description \"the lady with the blue shirt\". Report bbox coordinates."
},
{
"role": "assistant",
"content": "<think>\n\n</think>\n\n<bbox>[244, 469, 557, 745]</bbox>"
}
]
Implicit Instruction: Box and Mask Outputs
The instruction specifies a protective function that requires the model to infer the target category. The response identifies and describes the selected helmet, then returns its box and a mask token whose visual latent represents that target.
[
{
"role": "user",
"content": "<image>\nLocate all instances in the image that match the description \"If we want to protect our heads when participating in contact sports like football or baseball, what object in the picture should we wear?\". Report bbox coordinates and masks."
},
{
"role": "assistant",
"content": "<think>\nTo protect the head in contact sports, the appropriate item to wear is a helmet. In the center of the image, sitting on a wooden shelf, is a glossy blue helmet featuring the number 99 and an Oilers logo. This object is clearly a piece of protective headgear. While there are other padded items visible on the left and right edges, the blue helmet in the middle is the most distinct and complete example of the required safety equipment. Therefore, this central blue region is the correct target to identify.\n</think>\n\n<bbox>[190, 169, 756, 924]</bbox><mask><|mask|></mask>"
}
]
Appendix B Qualitative Analysis
The qualitative examples connect the quantitative results in Section 4 to target selection, region extent, and intermediate region references. Grounding examples show the boxes and masks predicted for requested targets. Complementing these grounding examples, VQA examples show how thinking with visual latents makes intermediate regions inspectable: each region discussed in the reasoning trace is linked to its decoded mask and to the final answer. For these VQA visualizations, we replay the stored completion through the same model to obtain the contextual states used for mask decoding.
Referring Expression Grounding and Multi-Object Localization
Figures 9 and 10 illustrate target selection and region delineation from appearance, action, relative size, and spatial relations. The masks distinguish requested instances from nearby objects, including a particular fruit wedge and a cow beside another cow. Figure 11 shows multi-instance predictions, where individual boxes distinguish repeated or overlapping objects such as oranges in a bowl and surfboards in a rack.
Figure 9: Box predictions for referring expressions based on appearance, action, relative size, and spatial relations. Each orange box marks the instance selected by the model.
Figure 10: Mask predictions for targets identified by referring expressions. Orange overlays delineate the selected fruit wedge, laptop, kite, and cow. The accompanying expressions specify the intended referent in each scene.
Figure 11: Multi-object box predictions in scenes with repeated instances and multiple categories. Labels identify the predicted category and distinguish instances within each category.
Grounding Functional Descriptions
Figure 12 illustrates target selection from functional descriptions, including objects used to cut grass or add citrus flavor to seafood. The responses connect each requested function to visible candidates. For example, the spacecraft trace considers two candidates before selecting the foreground object, while the gift-packaging example returns both boxes.
Figure 12: Predictions from functional descriptions. Each example pairs the instruction and response with the predicted boxes and masks in orange. The spacecraft and packaging traces explicitly consider multiple candidate referents.
Object Selection in Cluttered Scenes
Figure 13 shows referring requests that combine object identity with attributes or relative position in cluttered scenes. These cues select a cup, a beverage can, a book, and a cleanser among nearby objects. Specifically, positional phrases distinguish the requested cup and cleanser from other instances of the same category, and the masks delineate the selected objects.
Figure 13: Object selection from requests that combine category, appearance, and position. Orange boxes and masks show the predictions alongside the reasoning and response.
Spatial Comparison and Color Recognition
Figure 14 illustrates visual latents associated with spatial comparison and color recognition. The first trace localizes chairs and bicycles before comparing their horizontal positions and selecting answer A. The second identifies a small vehicle in the wider scene and selects answer D, orange. Matching colors connect each region reference to its box and decoded mask, and an enlarged view shows the small target.
Figure 14: Region references in spatial comparison (top) and vehicle-color recognition (bottom). Matching colors link the response tokens to image regions. The enlarged view shows the small targets in the second example.
Counting with Instance References
Figure 15 illustrates instance-by-instance counting with visual latents. The traces enumerate five spatially separated climbing stones and six leaves, several of which overlap, and then return the corresponding counts. Matching colors link each enumerated item to its predicted box and mask, so the region behind each counting step can be inspected.
Figure 15: Instance-by-instance counting of climbing stones (top) and leaves (bottom). The traces contain five and six region references, respectively. Each colored token block corresponds to a box and a mask.
