Abstract
Video world models must capture how a scene evolves beyond the current camera view to generate consistent observations when its content reappears. Existing approaches rely on observation history, static point clouds, or image caches constructed from past frames. These representations are often expensive and, more importantly, cannot update dynamic scene content outside the field of view: generated vehicles and pedestrians may be forgotten after leaving the field of view or reappear in physically inaccurate states. We represent an evolving scene through synchronized videos from reference-camera rigs, each comprising eight cameras that jointly cover the surrounding scene. We connect these rigs in a graph and propagate video generation between neighboring rigs to coordinate scene dynamics across locations and time.
We introduce Momentum, a two-stage pipeline: WorldUpdate jointly generates reference-camera videos as a shared memory of scene dynamics, and WorldInteract grounds camera-controlled video generation in this evolving memory, allowing moving content to remain represented outside the target view and reappear consistently.
To support training and evaluation, we develop a CARLA pipeline that captures synchronized videos from reference camera rigs and a moving target camera, together with an evaluation framework measuring consistency within the generated world state and the target observations.
Experiments on generated street scenes show that grounding target videos in scene dynamics generated by Momentum improves dynamic scene consistency by 126.2%126.2\% in unseen scenes over static image conditioning, while also improving visual fidelity. Our models and source code will be made publicly available.

1 Introduction
Video world models simulate how visual environments evolve over time and respond to actions, supporting applications in game generation (Team et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2025b; Qian et al., 2026), robotic learning and control (Gao et al., 2026a; Ye et al., 2026), autonomous driving simulation (Wayve, 2026; Basant et al., 2026), and virtual-world exploration (Ball et al., 2025; Team et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025). In these settings, the generated video reveals only a partial view of an environment whose contents and dynamics extend beyond what is currently visible. As the viewpoint changes, the generated observations should remain consistent with both the scene’s spatial structure and the motion of objects within it.
A vehicle approaching an intersection should follow the same course whether the generated video follows it, shows it from another viewpoint, or turns away and later shows it again. This requires grounding generation to a world representation that evolves through time.
Existing memory mechanisms provide part of this grounding through observation history or persistent spatial representations. Conditioning on past frames preserves local continuity, but a finite context limits how much of the environment remains accessible (Zhang et al., 2026; Savov et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026). Image caches (Xiao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Yu et al., 2025a), depth maps (Ren et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026), and reconstructed point clouds (Ren et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025c) extend spatial memory by retaining information from previously observed locations. However, stored observations describe the scene at the time they were captured. Without a mechanism for advancing their contents, they cannot provide temporally updated grounding for a moving camera. The challenge is therefore to maintain a representation that specifies both what exists at a location and how its state changes over time.
Recent approaches have begun to model dynamics beyond the current camera view through explicit object-level motion plans or entity-centered simulation within a static background (Wang et al., 2026a; Duan et al., 2026). These advances support persistent dynamics for selected entities, while maintaining the evolution of general scene content remains relatively unexplored. We pursue a representation that evolves scene content across a coordinated network of reference cameras, without requiring explicit object trajectories, identity tracking, or a static-dynamic scene decomposition.
Seoul World Model (SWM) offers a useful foundation through image-based spatial grounding (Seo et al., 2026). It conditions camera-controlled video generation on retrieved street-view images, using geometric reprojection and semantic referencing to anchor generated observations to a particular environment. These references ground the scene’s spatial structure and appearance, while motion is synthesized within the generated video. We extend this principle to spatiotemporal grounding: synchronized videos from spatially distributed reference cameras provide a shared account of both scene appearance and motion. The target camera is thus grounded in the scene at the corresponding location and time, allowing different camera trajectories to observe the same unfolding dynamics.
We introduce Momentum, a two-stage pipeline in which WorldUpdate generates an evolving world state and WorldInteract uses it to ground camera-controlled video generation. The method is presented on Fig. 1. The state is organized as a graph of panoramic rigs, each comprising eight reference cameras.
WorldUpdate jointly generates videos for the eight cameras of each rig and propagates generation through the graph, conditioning each rig on geometrically reprojected video from an already generated neighbor. This couples the evolution of scene content across reference viewpoints. WorldInteract then generates video along a target camera trajectory using the resulting world rollout as spatiotemporal grounding. The reference videos therefore serve both as the simulation of scene dynamics and as their persistent representation, enabling motion outside the target camera’s view to remain part of the state used to generate subsequent observations.
To develop this pipeline, we build an SWM-based image-grounded model on Cosmos Predict 2.5 and a CARLA data-generation framework that provides training and evaluation data for both stages. The framework captures static reference images and synchronized videos from fixed reference-camera rigs to supervise world-state generation. It also records videos along diverse moving target-camera trajectories, providing supervision for camera-controlled generation grounded in the reference observations. These observations provide supervision for generating a dynamic world state and for rendering camera-controlled video consistent with that state. We develop an evaluation framework for dynamic grounding, combining visual, camera-control, and scene-consistency metrics to assess the evolving world representation and the consistency of target videos with its dynamics.
We evaluate our SWM-based baseline on real-world driving data and assess dynamic grounding on CARLA scenes from seen and unseen towns. Generating reference videos introduces an additional inference stage, trading computation for a shared representation of scene dynamics beyond the current view. This cost can be amortized across multiple target-camera trajectories grounded in the same world rollout, while improving dynamic consistency over static image grounding. With ground-truth reference videos, the Dynamically Grounded WM improves visual fidelity, camera control, and scene consistency over static grounding across single- and multi-rig settings. End-to-end, Momentum generates its own reference videos and improves dynamic scene consistency by 130.2%130.2\% in seen towns and 126.2%126.2\% in an unseen town compared with the Dynamically Grounded WM conditioned on static images.
Our contributions are threefold:
•
We introduce a spatiotemporal world representation comprising a graph of reference-camera rigs with synchronized multi-view videos, providing shared spatial and dynamic grounding across camera viewpoints and time.
•
We propose Momentum, a two-stage pipeline that generates scene dynamics through graph-propagated multi-view generation in WorldUpdate and grounds camera-controlled observations in those dynamics through WorldInteract.
•
We develop a CARLA data-generation pipeline for synchronized reference and target camera observations, an evaluation framework for assessing dynamic scene consistency in both the generated world representation and the resulting target videos, and an image-grounded world model based on SWM.
2 Related Work
2.1 Video World Models
Building on learned models of environment dynamics (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018), generative video world models increasingly support interactive visual environments across domains. Genie (Bruce et al., 2024; Ball et al., 2025), LingBot-World (Team et al., 2026), and HY-WorldPlay (Sun et al., 2025) enable controllable exploration. DIAMOND (Alonso et al., 2024), GameFactory (Yu et al., 2025b), and Matrix-Game 3.5 (Qian et al., 2026) develop interactive game-like simulation, while Pandora (Xiang et al., 2024) uses natural-language actions to guide video evolution. Driving models such as GAIA (Wayve, 2026) and OmniDreams (Basant et al., 2026) generate controllable observations for simulation and evaluation. In robotics, DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026a) learns action-conditioned simulation from human-video priors, DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026) jointly predicts future video and robot actions for control, and RoboWorld (Jeon et al., 2026) supports robot-policy evaluation through video simulation. Cosmos (Agarwal et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2026) provides pretrained world-model capabilities for physical AI; we build on Cosmos Predict 2.5 (Ali et al., 2025). Our focus is on organizing generated dynamics into a persistent world state that grounds observations across target-camera trajectories.
2.2 Memory and Dynamics in Video World Models
Memory mechanisms extend generation beyond a short observation window. StateSpaceDiffuser (Savov et al., 2026) uses recurrent state-space modeling, FramePack (Zhang et al., 2026) compresses frame history, and Mixture of Contexts (Cai et al., 2026) learns sparse attention over informative temporal contexts. WorldMem (Xiao et al., 2026), VRAG (Chen et al., 2026b), Context-as-Memory (Yu et al., 2025a), and MemCam (Gao et al., 2026b) retrieve historical observations. Spatial organization further supports consistency through 3D caches in GEN3C (Ren et al., 2025), surfel indexing in VMem (Li et al., 2025c), and geometric memory in SPMem (Wu et al., 2026), Captain Safari (Chou et al., 2026), and Lyra 2.0 (Shen et al., 2026). Latent Spatial Memory (Wang et al., 2026b) stores spatial information in diffusion latent space, while SWM (Seo et al., 2026) grounds generation in external street-view images. These mechanisms retain past evidence, but memory retention alone does not advance the represented scene through time.
Dynamic geometric methods such as NVFi (Li et al., 2023), FreeGave (Li et al., 2025b), and TRACE (Li et al., 2025a) predict future RGB views by fitting scene dynamics to recorded videos. We instead reuse a dynamics model across scenes and spatial regions without scene-specific fitting. InfiniCube (Lu et al., 2025) and WorldSplat (Zhu et al., 2026) generate dynamic environments under supplied object-motion controls; our model generates scene evolution without prescribed object trajectories.
Persistent dynamics beyond the target view remain relatively unexplored. HyDRA (Chen et al., 2026a) retrieves motion and appearance cues for hidden subjects, while MosaicMem (Yu et al., 2026) and Mem-World (Zheng et al., 2026) combine spatial memory with dynamic video generation. WorldDirector (Wang et al., 2026a) plans object trajectories through an LLM, and LiveWorld (Duan et al., 2026) advances active entities through virtual monitors against a static background. Momentum instead organizes its evolving state by spatial regions: coordinated reference-camera videos generate and represent scene dynamics, then ground target-camera observations. This formulation supports spatial extension through additional rigs without explicit entity tracking or a static–dynamic decomposition.

3.1 Problem Statement
Given a history of HH observations I1:HI_{1:H}, the corresponding camera poses c1:Hc_{1:H}, future camera trajectory cH+1:H+Tc_{H+1:H+T}, and a world grounding GG, a grounded camera-controlled video world model f(I1:H,c1:H+T,G)f(I_{1:H},c_{1:H+T},G) predicts the corresponding future observations I^H+1:H+T\hat{I}_{H+1:H+T}, where GG provides additional information about the scene beyond that contained in the observation history, such as spatial context or previously unobserved regions of the environment.
Existing grounded world models such as SWM use GG as a collection of static spatial references, which may originate from different points in time and therefore do not constitute a temporally evolving representation of the dynamic scene. We instead model the grounding as a time-indexed world state SH:H+TS_{H:H+T}, allowing the grounding itself to evolve throughout generation. The prediction can then be written as I^H+1:H+T=h(I1:H,cH+1:H+T,SH:H+T)\hat{I}_{H+1:H+T}=h(I_{1:H},c_{H+1:H+T},S_{H:H+T}), where the state sequence is obtained from an initial grounded state through SH:H+T=g(SH)S_{H:H+T}=g(S_{H}).
In our model, StS_{t} is represented by NN spatially distributed reference-camera rigs, each comprising eight co-located cameras with uniformly spaced orientations. Thus, St={Itn,k}n=1,…,N;k=1,…,8S_{t}=\{I_{t}^{n,k}\}_{n=1,\ldots,N;\,k=1,\ldots,8}. Unlike the static spatial references used in SWM, all reference cameras are temporally synchronized and generate video, so SH:H+TS_{H:H+T} forms a spatiotemporally evolving representation of the scene.
3.2 Data Generation Framework
Training and evaluating our models requires reference observations paired with target-camera trajectories, with synchronized video for dynamic grounding. No large-scale public corpus meets these requirements, and SWM’s real-world data is static and proprietary. We therefore develop an open CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017) framework for static and dynamic grounding, across six towns with randomized weather, illumination, vehicles, and pedestrians. Further details - in App. A.1.
Static grounding data.
We pair target videos with panoramic reference rigs covering the same maps. Each rig contains eight outward-facing cameras spaced 45∘45^{\circ} apart in yaw, positioned 22 m above the road. Rigs follow the road graph at 2020 m intervals with small random positional offsets. Captures are separated by 2.42.4 s, equivalent to 3030 km/h travel, allowing dynamic content to evolve between neighboring captures as in street-view acquisition. Target videos span 3636 s at 1010 fps along vehicle (autopilot), pedestrian (AI-controlled walker), or flyover (freely moving elevated camera) trajectories. References and targets are captured in independent sessions with independently sampled conditions, sharing static structure while differing in transient content.
Dynamic grounding data.
To supervise evolving reference views, we capture reference and target videos in a shared simulation rollout. A moving target camera traverses a randomly selected intersection covered by one panoramic rig (single-rig) or up to four spatially distributed rigs (multi-rig). Each session spans 300300 frames at 1010 fps, with synchronized observations depicting the same scene evolution. To reduce storage, rigs record new observations only when the target camera is within 2525 m; otherwise, their latest frames are repeated to retain temporal alignment. Active intervals provide eight synchronized streams per rig for world-state supervision, while target trajectories revisit shared content from different viewpoints and headings to supervise grounded camera-controlled generation.
We collect 2020 sessions per setting per town for training and two evaluation sets per setting of 3030 sessions each: Test 1 uses unseen intersections in training towns, and Test 2 uses a town excluded from training to assess generalization.
3.3 Static Grounding
Our Statically Grounded World Model follows the
SWM architecture (Seo et al., 2026). As it is currently not publicly available, we build an open implementation of it. Details appear in App. A.2.
Backbone and rollout.
The backbone is a Diffusion Transformer operating in the latent space of a causal 3D VAE, initialized from Cosmos Predict 2.5-2B (Ali et al., 2025). Generation proceeds autoregressively in chunks, denoising chunk ii conditioned on a text prompt P(i)P^{(i)} (we use a constant preset prompt), target-camera trajectory C(i)C^{(i)}, and HH history latents from chunk i−1i-1. Per-frame Plücker ray maps, encoded by a shallow convolutional encoder and added to latent tokens, provide camera poses for history, target, reference, and sink latents. We train under teacher forcing on the CARLA data of Sec. 3.2.
Retrieval-augmented grounding.
Each chunk retrieves up to KK reference images near the target trajectory, with camera poses and metric depth supplied by CARLA during training and estimated with Depth Anything V3 (Lin et al., 2025) at inference. Two pathways incorporate these references. Geometric referencing unprojects the nearest references for each target frame into a point cloud and forward-splats it into the target viewpoint; the resulting warped video is encoded and concatenated channel-wise with noisy target latents for dense spatial conditioning. Semantic referencing encodes each reference into a single latent, appended to the token sequence at a RoPE temporal position separated from the generation window by GrefG_{\mathrm{ref}}. Target latents attend to all KK references to recover appearance information lost through depth errors or occlusion.
A virtual lookahead sink adds a retrieved latent at RoPE position ΔVL\Delta_{\mathrm{VL}} beyond the chunk as a clean anchor, refreshed each generation step. References and targets come from different scene realizations, encouraging reliance on persistent structure rather than transient content. Sec. 4 compares this baseline with SWM’s reported scores.
3.4 Momentum
Momentum is a two-stage pipeline that generates an evolving world state and uses it to ground camera-controlled video. The architecture of our method is shown on Fig. 2. The state StS_{t} comprises the reference-camera rig views, advanced through time rather than held fixed. WorldUpdate implements the state transition SH:H+T=g(SH)S_{H:H+T}=g(S_{H}), generating synchronized videos across rigs to represent scene dynamics independently of the target-camera trajectory. WorldInteract implements the observation model hh, generating target-camera video conditioned on the observation history, camera trajectory, and corresponding world state. The reference videos thus serve both as the simulation of scene dynamics and as their persistent representation, grounding observations when moving content leaves and later re-enters the target view.
Dynamics are represented without explicit entity detection, identity tracking, or re-identification, so for a fixed rig configuration the generation workload does not grow with the number of moving entities. The two stages operate independently: we store completed world rollouts in the dataset format of Sec. 3.2, allowing WorldInteract to consume generated and recorded reference videos through the same interface. A single world rollout can ground multiple target-camera trajectories, be inspected independently, and be generated at a different resolution or on a different machine from the target video.
3.4.1 WorldUpdate
WorldUpdate is the component of Momentum responsible for advancing the world state through synchronized reference-camera video generation. It requires consistency both across cameras within a rig and across spatially distributed rigs observing shared content. To meet these requirements, we introduce the Multi-Rig Dynamically Grounded World Model (Multi-Rig DGWM) as an architecture for this component, combining joint multi-view generation with geometric conditioning between rigs.
Multi-view generation.
Multi-Rig DGWM builds on the pretrained multi-view video diffusion transformer of Cosmos-Predict 2.5 (Ali et al., 2025), trained on autonomous driving data. It extends its seven-view configuration to the eight cameras of our rigs by adding an extra learnable view embedding. View-stacked attention exchanges information across cameras during joint generation, supporting cross-view consistency. We incorporate the warped-video channels of geometric referencing and the Plücker camera residual from the grounded model. Semantic reference tokens, the virtual lookahead sink, and their associated attention masks are omitted: each fixed camera’s generated history anchors its viewpoint and appearance, while reprojected neighbor videos provide spatially aligned conditioning on scene evolution.
Multi-rig consistency.
Independent rig generation can produce conflicting depictions of shared content, including objects duplicating or disappearing between viewpoints. We therefore condition each rig on a neighbor’s synchronized video, unprojecting its views with depth and reprojecting them into the receiving rig using the known relative poses. During training, conditioning comes from the ground-truth video of the nearest other rig, allowing training samples to be processed independently. At inference, neighboring videos must themselves be generated, requiring a schedule that makes each conditioning source available before its dependent rig.
Graph propagation.
We construct a graph connecting rigs close within a range to provide useful reprojection conditioning. Generation proceeds in chunks of one model window, overlapping by the conditioning frames to maintain temporal continuity. Within each chunk, we traverse the graph breadth-first from a source rig, which generates from its own history without neighbor conditioning. Each subsequent rig conditions on the freshly generated video of its nearest already-generated neighbor(s), while rigs at equal depth are generated together. This propagates scene evolution across the graph over a shared temporal interval.
3.4.2 WorldInteract
WorldInteract is the component of Momentum that generates target-camera video grounded in the evolving world state. It must follow the specified camera trajectory while preserving the scene appearance and motion represented by the reference videos. For this component, we introduce the Dynamically Grounded World Model (Dynamically Grounded WM), retaining the architecture of the Statically Grounded World Model (Sec. 3.3) and fine-tuning it on synchronized dynamic grounding data (Sec. 3.2).
For each chunk, the model retrieves KK nearby rig views as semantic references and reprojects the nearest reference view into each target frame. Unlike static grounding, geometric conditioning uses the reference video frame at the corresponding target time, conveying the scene’s evolving appearance and motion. Semantic references are instead sampled at the chunk’s start, providing a common temporal anchor across the retrieved viewpoints.

4 Experiments
Experimental Setup.
We train Statically Grounded WM, Dynamically Grounded WM and Multi-Rig DGWM on the CARLA training set (Sec. 3.2) on 6 towns, according to the training schedule outlined in App. A.2 and App. A.3. We evaluate them in the roles of components of Momentum. We evaluate on Test 1 and Test 2, as collected in Sec. 3.2. Unless otherwise stated, we use the 4-rig version of each set and choose 2 views of 1 closest rig for reprojection. We evaluate visual fidelity - PSNR, LPIPS, camera trajectory
accuracy - rotation and translation error and scene consistency - SCS-static (Bagchi et al. (2026)), which we adapt to dynamic objects in SCS-dyn. See App. B.
Baseline Validation.
We evaluate our Statically Grounded World Model on the publicly available Ann Arbor driving data from MARS (Li et al., 2024), following SWM’s evaluation protocol, using masked versions of PSNR and LPIPS (see App. B). This comparison assesses whether our reimplementation provides a competitive baseline despite training exclusively on synthetic data, whereas SWM additionally uses video interpolated from proprietary real-world panoramas. As shown in Tab. 2, our model achieves higher mPSNR, lower mLPIPS, and lower rotation error than the reported SWM results, although translation error remains higher. These results establish a competitive image-grounded baseline for evaluating the benefits of dynamic grounding.
WorldInteract Consistency Evaluation.
We evaluate model choices for WorldInteract using ground-truth reference observations, isolating target-video generation from errors in WorldUpdate. We compare the Statically Grounded World Model conditioned on either fixed reference images from t=0t=0 or synchronized reference video against the Dynamically Grounded WM conditioned on video. Image conditioning reprojects the initial observations into each target viewpoint, while video conditioning uses time-aligned observations. Across scenes containing one or four reference-camera rigs, the Dynamically Grounded WM achieves the best visual fidelity, camera-trajectory accuracy, and static and dynamic scene consistency in both seen and unseen towns (Tab. 2). Supplying video conditioning to the statically trained model alone does not yield comparable gains, demonstrating the importance of training with synchronized dynamic grounding.

We next vary the number of rigs supplying conditioning within four-rig scenes, using either one or three source rigs with the same model and conditioning choices. The Dynamically Grounded WM outperforms both baselines with either source count (Tab. 3). In seen towns, one source provides better visual and semantic consistency, while three slightly improve camera-trajectory accuracy. In the unseen town, three sources improve all metrics, despite training with a single source rig, showing that the model can exploit additional reference coverage at inference.

We evaluate how visual fidelity changes throughout generation using per-frame PSNR on both test sets. As shown in Fig. 3, the Dynamically Grounded WM maintains visual fidelity more consistently over time than models using static grounding.

We evaluate Momentum from static reference images, without ground-truth reference videos. The baselines directly condition target-video generation on these images, while Momentum uses WorldUpdate to generate an evolving world state through graph propagation and WorldInteract to ground target observations in that state. 5 Conclusion
We introduced Momentum, a two-stage pipeline that grounds camera-controlled video generation in an evolving world state. Synchronized reference-camera videos represent scene appearance and dynamics beyond the target view, without explicit entity tracking or a static–dynamic decomposition. WorldUpdate advances this state through graph-propagated generation, while WorldInteract uses it to ground target-camera observations. We developed the Dynamically Grounded WM and Multi-Rig DGWM for these roles, together with a CARLA data-generation framework, an SWM-based Statically Grounded World Model, and an evaluation framework. Experiments in seen and unseen towns demonstrate improved visual fidelity, controllability and scene consistency through dynamic grounding. Compared with the Dynamically Grounded WM conditioned on static image references, Momentum improves dynamic scene consistency (SCS-dyn) by 130.2%130.2\% in seen towns and 126.2%126.2\% in the unseen town. These results open a path toward spatially extensible video world models in which multiple viewpoints and interactions share a consistently evolving scene.

Appendix A Appendix
A.1 Data Generation Implementation Details
All observations are rendered using 1280×7201280\times 720 pinhole cameras with a 90∘90^{\circ} field of view. Each capture stores RGB video together with per-frame camera extrinsics, intrinsics, and GNSS measurements; depth is stored losslessly. Data collection is configured declaratively, supports resuming interrupted sessions, and can be distributed across multiple CARLA servers.
Static grounding.
Panoramic rigs are placed at road waypoints with a random positional offset of up to 1.51.5 m. Each rig contains eight co-located cameras with yaw offsets spaced by 45∘45^{\circ} and records both RGB and depth. Target vehicle trajectories use an autopilot vehicle travelling at a randomized fraction below the local speed limit. Pedestrian trajectories use an eye-height camera at 1.651.65 m mounted to an AI walker with randomly sampled navigation goals. Flyover trajectories follow collision-checked random walks at 22–88 m altitude, with camera pitch sampled around −20∘-20^{\circ} with ±15∘\pm 15^{\circ} variation and additional yaw perturbations. We render 2020 sessions per town and trajectory type.
Dynamic grounding.
Each session is centred on a randomly selected intersection with at least three approach directions, bounding-box half-extent of at least 55 m, and sufficient nearby vehicle spawn points and pedestrian navigation area within 6060 m. In the multi-rig setting, up to four rigs are placed on road waypoints within 4040 m of the intersection and at least 1212 m apart, with one rig located at the junction centre. The single-rig setting retains only the central rig.
We spawn up to 1010 vehicles and 2525 pedestrians around the selected region. The target camera is placed at an eye height of 1.81.8–2.22.2 m and follows a smoothed, collision-checked trajectory visiting the rigs in randomized order. The path remains within 3030 m of the nearest reference rig in the multi-rig setting and 2020 m in the single-rig setting, promoting repeated observation of shared geometry from different headings, including U-turns.
Each session consists of a single synchronous CARLA rollout of 300300 frames at 1010 fps. Every reference rig records eight RGB and eight depth streams aligned with the target-camera timeline. To reduce rendering cost, reference rigs are active only while the target camera is within 2525 m; outside this range, the most recent frame is repeated to preserve temporal alignment. Test sessions use disjoint random seeds, and Test 1 additionally excludes intersections used during training.
A.2 Statically Grounded World Model Implementation Details
Architecture.
The backbone is the 22B Cosmos-Predict2.5 DiT: 2828 blocks of width 20482048 with 1616 heads, spatial patch size 22, AdaLN-LoRA of rank 256256, and 3D RoPE. The VAE compresses by 4×4\times temporally and 8×8\times spatially into 1616 latent channels. Three modifications are made. First, the input embedder is widened from 1616 to 3232 latent channels so that the warped video of the geometric pathway can be concatenated to the noisy target; with the condition and padding masks, the network sees 3434 channels. Second, separate patch embedders, weight-initialised from the base embedder, are added for reference and sink latents. Third, RoPE temporal indices are decoupled from position in the token sequence, allowing references and the lookahead sink to be placed outside the generation window, and attention masks are added for reference and sink padding. All remaining blocks are inherited unchanged.
Chunk geometry.
Each chunk covers 7777 pixel frames, which the causal VAE encodes to L=20L=20 latents, preceded by H=5H=5 history latents, for a 2525-latent generation window. We use K=5K=5 references, a reference gap of Gref=50G_{\mathrm{ref}}=50 latent positions, an inter-reference spacing of 11, and a lookahead offset of ΔVL=5\Delta_{\mathrm{VL}}=5 latent frames. Clean conditioning latents (history, references, and sink) are assigned timestep 00 under the base model’s per-frame AdaLN.
Training.
We fine-tune from the public Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B checkpoint for 1010K iterations at 704×1280704\times 1280 resolution with AdamW (lr=4.8×10−5\mathrm{lr}=4.8\times 10^{-5}, weight decay 0.0010.001, β=(0.9,0.999)\beta=(0.9,0.999)), 100100 warm-up steps, and gradient clipping at 0.10.1, under the base rectified-flow recipe (logit-normal timestep sampling, shift 55). Training is sharded with FSDP across 88 NVIDIA H100 GPUs at a per-GPU batch size of 11. To avoid over-reliance on any single conditioning signal, we perturb history latents with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.10.1 with probability 0.50.5, and drop the reference and warped-video pathways independently with probability 0.20.2 each.
Inference.
Chunks are rolled out sequentially with a rectified-flow ODE sampler using 3535 steps and classifier-free guidance of 3.03.0, with only the history latents carried forward; the camera trajectory, retrieved references, and sink are re-derived at every chunk.
A.3 Momentum Implementation Details
WorldUpdate: Multi-Rig DGWM.
The rig model is initialised from the released 22B multi-view Cosmos-Predict2.5 checkpoint, with an eighth view-embedding row appended to its seven-slot table and the warped-video latent appended to the input embedder, giving 4141 input channels (1616 noisy, 11 condition mask, 11 padding mask, 77 view, 1616 warped). Every pretrained column keeps its index, so the conversion only adds zero-initialised weights. All eight views of a rig are generated jointly at 192×352192\times 352 per view with 88 latent frames per view, corresponding to 2929 pixel frames per chunk. Generation is conditioned on a single history latent (h=1h=1), with the initial chunk conditioned on one ground-truth frame per view of each rig.
We fine-tune for 1010K iterations with AdamW (lr=3×10−5\mathrm{lr}=3\times 10^{-5}, weight decay 10−310^{-3}, β=(0.9,0.999)\beta=(0.9,0.999)), 100100 warm-up steps, and gradient clipping at 0.10.1, sharded with FSDP. The warped latent is dropped with probability 0.20.2 per sample, which both teaches the model to generate without a reprojection source and provides the branch without reprojection conditioning for classifier-free guidance. This rate is chosen to approximate how often a rig is the graph source at inference.
WorldInteract: Dynamically Grounded World Model.
The target-camera model is fine-tuned from the statically grounded checkpoint described in App. A.2 on the dynamic grounding sessions, retaining that model’s chunk geometry, optimiser settings, and conditioning dropout. It generates target-camera video at 704×1280704\times 1280 resolution in chunks of 7777 pixel frames (L=20L=20 latents), conditioned on H=5H=5 history latents. At inference, it uses the same 3535-step rectified-flow ODE sampler and classifier-free guidance of 3.03.0 as the statically grounded model.
References are bound to the first frame of the chunk’s window. The sink is read ΔVL=5\Delta_{\mathrm{VL}}=5 latent frames past the target end, clamped to the length of the available state. During training, a sample’s geometric pathway is disabled unless at least 95%95\% of its target frames fall within a window in which the source rig was actively rendering.
Rig connectivity and source selection.
Reference rigs actively render within a range of r=25r=25 m. This range is recorded with each session and also determines the edges of the rig graph used by WorldUpdate. For target-camera generation, WorldInteract reprojects the nearest in-range rig by default. Rigs outside the active rendering range hold their last frame and are excluded as reprojection sources.
Appendix B Metrics
B.1 Camera Trajectory Accuracy
RotErr & TransErr
These measure how well the camera motion of the generated video follows the target trajectory, using the protocol of Seo et al. (2026) (App. B.1). We estimate per-frame camera-to-world poses (𝐑t,𝐭t)(\mathbf{R}_{t},\mathbf{t}_{t}) of both the generated and the ground-truth video with Depth Anything V3 Lin et al. (2025) at a processing resolution of 504504 px. Estimating the reference trajectory with the same model, rather than using the simulator’s exact poses, reduces the estimator’s systematic bias and places both trajectories in the same camera convention. As in Seo et al. (2026), videos are processed in non-overlapping chunks of 3232 frames, and each chunk is scored in its own coordinate frame. Within a chunk cc with first frame t0t_{0}, poses are expressed relative to that anchor,
𝐑trel=𝐑t0⊤​𝐑t,𝐭trel=𝐑t0⊤​(𝐭t−𝐭t0),\mathbf{R}^{\mathrm{rel}}_{t}=\mathbf{R}_{t_{0}}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{t},\qquad\mathbf{t}^{\mathrm{rel}}_{t}=\mathbf{R}_{t_{0}}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{t}_{t}-\mathbf{t}_{t_{0}}\bigr),
(1)
and, since monocular pose estimation is only defined up to scale, each translation trajectory is normalised by its own maximum norm within the chunk, 𝐭¯t=𝐭trel/maxt′∈c⁡∥𝐭t′rel∥2\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{t}=\mathbf{t}^{\mathrm{rel}}_{t}/\max_{t^{\prime}\in c}\lVert\mathbf{t}^{\mathrm{rel}}_{t^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}. With ℱ\mathcal{F} the set of all non-anchor frames over all chunks of a clip, the rotation error is the mean geodesic distance on SO⁡(3)\mathrm{SO}(3) and the translation error the mean Euclidean distance between normalised translations:
RotErr=1|ℱ|​∑t∈ℱarccos⁡(tr⁡(𝐑^trel⊤​𝐑trel)−12),\mathrm{RotErr}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{F}}\arccos\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{\mathrm{rel}\,\top}_{t}\mathbf{R}^{\mathrm{rel}}_{t}\bigr)-1}{2}\right),
(2)
TransErr=1|ℱ|∑t∈ℱ∥𝐭¯^t−𝐭¯t∥2.\mathrm{TransErr}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{F}}\bigl\lVert\hat{\bar{\mathbf{t}}}_{t}-\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{t}\bigr\rVert_{2}.
(3)
The anchor frames, whose error is zero by construction, are excluded. RotErr is reported in radians and TransErr is unitless (it is relative to the chunk’s extent); lower is better for both.
B.2 Consistency Metrics
SCS static & dynamic
The Structural Consistency Score Bagchi et al. (2026) measures whether the generated video keeps the layout of the scene, independent of appearance, as the mean mask IoU between corresponding regions of the generated and ground-truth videos:
SCS=1N​T​∑j=1N∑t=1T|M^t,j∩Mt,j||M^t,j∪Mt,j|.\mathrm{SCS}=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\bigl|\hat{M}^{t,j}\cap M^{t,j}\bigr|}{\bigl|\hat{M}^{t,j}\cup M^{t,j}\bigr|}.
(4)
Masks are generated automatically with SAM 3.1 Carion et al. (2025) tracking over a fixed vocabulary of semantic categories, and Mt,jM^{t,j} is the union of all instances of category jj tracked in frame tt. The static and dynamic categories are:
•
SCS-static: {building, road,
sidewalk, vegetation, sky}, the persistent scene
structure, which the model must preserve and move consistently with the camera;
•
SCS-dynamic: {vehicle, pedestrian,
person, bicycle, animal}, moving entities, which additionally
test whether the model keeps them present and in the right place.
Frames in which a category is empty in both videos (IoU 0/00/0) are excluded. IoUs are pooled over all valid (clip, category, frame) triples. To bound the cost of SAM 3.1, both videos are temporally subsampled to 2 fps.
B.3 Video Fidelity Metrics
mPSNR & mLPIPS
Following Seo et al. (2026), we measure reconstruction fidelity only on the static part of the scene. We reuse SAM 3.1 to extract dynamic masks, as above, for the generated and the ground-truth video, giving D^t,Dt∈{0,1}H×W\hat{D}_{t},D_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}. A pixel counts as static only if it is static in both videos:
St=¬(D^t∨Dt).S_{t}=\neg\bigl(\hat{D}_{t}\lor D_{t}\bigr).
(5)
mPSNR is the PSNR over all static pixels and colour channels of a clip (peak value 11):
mPSNR=−10​log10​∑t∑pSt​(p)​∥I^t​(p)−It​(p)∥223​∑t∑pSt​(p).\mathrm{mPSNR}=-10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{t}\sum_{p}S_{t}(p)\,\lVert\hat{I}_{t}(p)-I_{t}(p)\rVert_{2}^{2}}{3\sum_{t}\sum_{p}S_{t}(p)}.
(6)
LPIPS Zhang et al. (2018) is a distance in feature space and cannot be restricted to an arbitrary pixel set, thus for mLPIPS we fill the dynamic regions of the generated frame with the ground truth, I~t=St⊙I^t+(1−St)⊙It\tilde{I}_{t}=S_{t}\odot\hat{I}_{t}+(1-S_{t})\odot I_{t}, and compute
mLPIPS=1|𝒯S|​∑t∈𝒯SLPIPSVGG​(I~t,It),𝒯S={t:∑pSt​(p)>0}.\mathrm{mLPIPS}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{S}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{S}}\mathrm{LPIPS}_{\text{VGG}}\bigl(\tilde{I}_{t},I_{t}\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{T}_{S}=\{t:\textstyle\sum_{p}S_{t}(p)>0\}.
(7)
B.4 Measures of Change
Staticness measures how much of the scene moves in a video. The exo rig cameras are fixed, so a change in where an object appears in the image can only come from the object moving. We reuse the SAM 3.1 tracks of the dynamic categories. Unlike SCS, masks are compared across time within one video rather than between the generated and ground-truth videos. For a clip vv, the staticness is the mean IoU between consecutive frames:
St⁡(v)=1|𝒫v|​∑(t,j)∈𝒫v|Mt,j∩Mt+1,j||Mt,j∪Mt+1,j|,𝒫v={(t,j):|Mt,j∪Mt+1,j|>0}.\mathrm{St}(v)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{v}|}\sum_{(t,j)\in\mathcal{P}_{v}}\frac{\bigl|M^{t,j}\cap M^{t+1,j}\bigr|}{\bigl|M^{t,j}\cup M^{t+1,j}\bigr|},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{v}=\bigl\{(t,j):\bigl|M^{t,j}\cup M^{t+1,j}\bigr|>0\bigr\}.
(8)
If no agents are present or moving, St=1\mathrm{St}=1. The score falls as a larger share of the agents’ area moves, appears or disappears between frames. As in SCS, both videos are temporally subsampled to 2 fps, so tt and t+1t{+}1 are 0.50.5 s apart. Pairs in which a category is empty in both frames are excluded.
We compute St\mathrm{St} for the generated video (St^\widehat{\mathrm{St}}) and for the ground truth (St\mathrm{St}), and average each over clips. SAM 3.1 masks jitter slightly from frame to frame, so even a perfectly frozen video scores somewhat below 11; the persistent baseline value is therefore the reference, not 11. A model can match the average staticness while freezing dynamic clips and animating static ones, so we also report the mean per-clip gap
Δ​St=1|𝒱|​∑v∈𝒱|St^​(v)−St⁡(v)|,\Delta\mathrm{St}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\bigl|\widehat{\mathrm{St}}(v)-\mathrm{St}(v)\bigr|,
(9)
where 𝒱\mathcal{V} is the set of clips for which both scores are defined.

Appendix C Additional Experiments
C.1 WorldUpdate Consistency
In this experiment we evaluate the multi-view abilities of our WorldUpdate model for generating video across rigs and views. To this end, we take the two closest to the current camera orientations of the neighboring rigs and reproject them to the current view. We evaluate how close the model predictions are to the ground truth of those views at that time. We compare our multi-view WorldUpdate with WorldInteract, which is dynamically grounded but not multi-view in design.
Results are shown in Tab. 5. A baseline that freezes the conditioning frame achieves the best PSNR and LPIPS. This is a property of the setting: the rig cameras are stationary, so most of the image is static in time and visual metrics reward generating no motion. SCS-dyn is affected in the same way. It is an overlap score, and over one evaluation step a moving object still overlaps most of itself, so a frozen frame is credited with the overlap the object retains, while a model that moves it is penalised wherever its motion departs from the single recorded future. Both must therefore be read together with the staticness gap. WorldInteract stays close to the frozen baseline in staticness, and inherits its PSNR, LPIPS and SCS-dyn from that. WorldUpdate has the lowest staticness gap in every configuration, moving the scene by roughly the amount the ground truth does, and pays for it on every metric that rewards freezing, while preserving static structure best of all generative models (SCS-st) and exceeding the frozen baseline on it in the seen towns. This experiment thus exposes a trade-off between a preference for static content and dynamic accuracy across models, and a bias of the metrics themselves towards the former.
