Abstract
Pixel-space diffusion models have recently re-emerged as a compelling alternative to latent diffusion, avoiding the lossy latent bottleneck and separately trained autoencoder. JiT shows that a vanilla diffusion transformer can generate directly in pixel space by replacing the commonly used velocity prediction (𝒖{\bm{u}}) with clean image prediction (𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}). In this work, we revisit this choice and introduce MFlow, an adaptive parameterization that learns to interpolate between 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} and 𝒖{\bm{u}} prediction across principal directions of the data, which can be learned end-to-end with diffusion model training. We find that the preferred parameterization varies systematically with the data representation and model capacity. Notably, increasing model capacity shifts preference toward 𝒖{\bm{u}} prediction from 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} prediction. We evaluate MFlow across ImageNet, UCF-101 video, and large-scale text-to-image generation, demonstrating competitive or improved generative performance across diverse settings.
In addition, we find that vanilla pixel diffusion transformers, irrespective of parameterization, retain a persistent high-frequency deficit relative to real images. To address this, we introduce ConvRefiner, a lightweight convolutional module trained post-hoc with adversarial loss while keeping the diffusion backbone frozen. With only a small amount of additional training, ConvRefiner restores the high-frequency statistics of generated images while preserving FID.

1 Introduction
Diffusion and flow-based models have become a leading paradigm for visual generation in recent years.
Modern versions of these models predominantly operate in the latent space, obtained from compressing the pixel-space data with a separately-trained autoencoder.
However, latent diffusion models (LDMs) (Rombach et al. (2022); Peebles and Xie (2023); Ma et al. (2024); Esser et al. (2024); Yu et al. (2025); Singh et al. (2026)) introduce their own limitations – a lossy bottleneck leads to unfaithful reconstruction after compression, and there is a need for a separately trained compression autoencoder, which is typically unaware of the downstream generative objective.
Recent works (Li and He (2026); Chen et al. (2026b); Yu et al. (2026); Mukhopadhyay et al. (2026); Chen et al. (2025b); Ma et al. (2026b); Ma et al. (2026c); Wang et al. (2026c)) have revived diffusion directly in pixel space for image generation, removing the
aforementioned drawbacks in LDMs.
In particular, JiT (Li and He (2026)), demonstrates that vanilla diffusion transformers can achieve competitive generative performance directly on raw pixels, eliminating the need for pretrained autoencoders or complicated architectural modifications.
Its aggressive patchification keeps the
sequence length manageable, e.g., its 16×1616\times 16 patchification produces
equivalent number of tokens as a 16×16\times spatial compression VAE followed
by 1×11\times 1 latent patchification.
At the same time, such aggressive patchification poses a challenge in pixel-space diffusion as each token must
model a high-dimensional block of raw pixels, raising two questions:
what prediction parameterization is best suited to such high-dimensional
patches, and can a vanilla large-patch transformer faithfully model
fine-scale image details? In this work, we aim to improve the
pixel-space diffusion paradigm along these axes.
JiT addresses the first question with clean-image (𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}) prediction.
Under the manifold assumption, clean data lies near a low-dimensional
manifold, whereas noise and velocity span the ambient space, making
𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} easier for an undercomplete network to predict. However, sampling requires converting 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} to 𝒖{\bm{u}} (velocity), which becomes ill-conditioned near the low-noise regime.
AsymFlow (Chen et al. (2026a)) reduces this problem by predicting
𝒖{\bm{u}} in a selected rank-rr PCA subspace and 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} in its
complement, keeping the prediction target effectively in a low dimensionality. However, this hard binary cutoff rank must be chosen for each setting through expensive training sweeps, e.g., AsymFlow needed 6 trainings to tune the rank-rr.
Where as Jin and Wang (2026) use a learnable but globally shared interpolation between 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} and 𝒖{\bm{u}}, not reducing the effective noise dimensionality.
We introduce MFlow, an adaptive parameterization with a
continuous gain mi∈[0,1]m_{i}\in[0,1] for every principal direction of data. Notably, this differentiable velocity-recovery formulation
lets us jointly train the generator and gains with the standard
velocity-space flow-matching loss, letting model learn the best combination of 𝒖{\bm{u}} and 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} per principal component. MFlow therefore
replaces the AsymFlow’s manually selected rank and binary mask with a
continuous parameterization that adapts to the data representation,
objective, and model capacity.
Our experiments show that MFlow can be adapted not only to pixel-space image generation, but also to diverse paradigms, like pixel-space video generation, text-to-image generation at multiple model capacities, and high-dimensional latents like RAEv2 (Singh et al. (2026)). Further visualization of the learned
direction-wise gains as 𝑴{\bm{M}}-curves, a plot of mim_{i}’s in decreasing direction variance order, reveal interesting insights, such as increasing model capacity
shifts more directions toward velocity-like prediction.
Prediction parameterization alone does not resolve all limitations of
vanilla pixel diffusion transformers. Irrespective of the parameterization,
we find that JiT-family models exhibit a persistent high-frequency deficit
relative to real images. This suggests a limitation
of the minimalist large-patch design, where patch tokens are projected
directly back to pixels using MLP-layers without an explicit local refinement pathway. We
introduce ConvRefiner, a lightweight residual convolutional
module trained post hoc with perceptual and adversarial supervision while
keeping the diffusion backbone frozen. With only 5% post-hoc training,
ConvRefiner brings the generated high-frequency spectrum closer to that of
real images while preserving FID.
Overall, this work presents adaptive prediction parameterization and fine-scale signal modeling
as complementary directions for improving vanilla pixel diffusion transformers.
To summarize, the key contributions are:
•
We introduce MFlow, a framework of continuous, direction-adaptive prediction
parameterization whose gains are learned jointly with the generator
under a standard velocity-space flow-matching objective. We show MFlow is a general framework and can be used for diverse domains like pixel-space video generation, high-dimensional latents (like RAEv2) in addition to pixel-space image generation.
•
We identify a high-frequency deficit in vanilla
JiT-style models and introduce ConvRefiner, a lightweight post-hoc
module that restores high-frequency details while preserving fidelity matching the frequency spectrum of real images.

2 Related Work
Pixel-space diffusion and prediction parameterization.
Early diffusion models generated directly in pixel space (Ho et al. (2020); Nichol and Dhariwal (2021); Dhariwal and Nichol (2021); Karras et al. (2022)), while subsequent works improved the scalability of end-to-end pixel generation through architectural and formulation advances (Jabri et al. (2023); Hoogeboom et al. (2023); Hoogeboom et al. (2025); Crowson et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025b); Mukhopadhyay et al. (2026)). Recent approaches have renewed interest in pixel-space generation using vanilla or specialized transformer architectures (Li and He (2026); Chen et al. (2026b); Yu et al. (2026); Guo et al. (2026)), with recent studies further exploring its scaling to text-to-image generation (Jiang et al. (2026); Wang et al. (2026c); Ma et al. (2026b); Yu et al. (2026); Ma et al. (2026c); Chen et al. (2025b)). In the context of our parameterization study, JiT (Li and He (2026)) advocates clean-image prediction for high-dimensional pixel modeling, an approach also adopted by recent large-scale pixel-space models such as Tuna-2 (Liu et al. (2026)). k-Diff Jin and Wang (2026) and AsymFlow Chen et al. (2026a) further explore adaptive combinations of clean-data and velocity prediction. Instead of deciding a predefined fixed formulation, which can vary across architectures and datasets, MFlow lets the model learn the best fit between clean image and velocity prediction.
Details and frequency modeling.
Several recent works study the difficulty of modeling fine-scale information in
vanilla pixel-space diffusion transformers with large spatial patch sizes, such as
16×1616\times 16–32×3232\times 32. Existing approaches use perceptual
supervision (Ma et al. (2026c)), frequency-aware objectives or
flows (Degeorge et al. (2026); Pavasovic et al. (2026); Fan et al. (2026)), frequency-specialized
architectures (Ma et al. (2026b); Ma et al. (2026a)),
or improved information pathways (Guo et al. (2026)). Concurrent work also shows
that a frozen JiT model can recover additional high-frequency detail through
self-guidance (Fu et al. (2026)). Although some of these approaches also improve
training efficiency (Degeorge et al. (2026); Ma et al. (2026b)), they generally introduce specialized
architectural components or modify the training objective, and their generated
frequency statistics can still exhibit a high-frequency gap relative to real
images (Degeorge et al. (2026); Ma et al. (2026b); Pavasovic et al. (2026)). In contrast, our lightweight ConvRefiner is trained
post hoc while keeping the pretrained diffusion backbone frozen. It recovers the
missing high-frequency statistics without modifying the backbone or its original
training procedure, while preserving FID.

Adaptive parameterization. Common non-adaptive parameterization/regression-targets include: noise ϵ\bm{\epsilon}-prediction, velocity 𝒖{\bm{u}}-prediction , and clean signal 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction. These are algebraically equivalent but differ in conditioning. EDM (Karras et al. (2022)) introduces an input/output preconditioning, where the network effectively predicts a noise-level dependent combination of clean data and noise. However, JiT (Li and He (2026)) demonstrates that such a preconditioning substantially underperforms 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction for high-dimensional pixel patches using a vanilla transformer backbone, motivating the use of standard clean image prediction. More recently, k-Diff (Jin and Wang (2026)) introduces a learnable scalar interpolation between prediction targets, showing that the preferred parameterization varies across pixel and latent representations. However, its globally shared coefficient cannot account for directional differences within a representation.
AsymFlow (Chen et al. (2026a)) addresses this limitation by assigning velocity 𝒖{\bm{u}}-prediction to a selected low-rank subspace while clean image 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction in its complement.
More concretely, let
𝑼=[𝒗1,…,𝒗D]{\bm{U}}=[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{D}] be the orthonormal patch-PCA eigenvectors of the data (variances
λ1≥⋯≥λD\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{D}), and let 𝑨=𝑼:,1:r{\bm{A}}={\bm{U}}_{:,1:r} be the top-rr directions with 𝑨T​𝑨=𝑰r{\bm{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\bm{A}}={\bm{I}}_{r}, then the orthogonal projector 𝑷=𝑨​𝑨T{\bm{P}}\;=\;{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{\mathrm{T}} has the properties 𝑷2=𝑷{\bm{P}}^{2}={\bm{P}} and (𝑰−𝑷)​𝑷=𝟎({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}){\bm{P}}=\bm{0}. i.e.,
predict the full clean signal, but only the low-rank projection of the noise.
Because 𝑷{\bm{P}} is
a projector, the target splits cleanly into the velocity inside the subspace 𝑷​𝒖A=𝑷⁡(𝒙0−ϵ)=𝑷​𝒖{\bm{P}}\,{\bm{u}}_{\mathrm{A}}={\bm{P}}({\bm{x}}_{0}-\bm{\epsilon})={\bm{P}}\,{\bm{u}}, and the clean data on the complement (𝑰−𝑷)​𝒖A=(𝑰−𝑷)​𝒙0({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}})\,{\bm{u}}_{\mathrm{A}}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}){\bm{x}}_{0}. Limitations of previous adaptive parameterizations. Chen et al. (2026a) note that the default 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction and k-Diff parameterization are less expressive.
On the other hand, AsymFlow makes hard-cutoff decision at a fixed rank rr, and this chosen subspace is dataset/modality dependent, requiring an expensive hyperparameter search over multiple parameterization setting.
Furthermore, restricting the noise term to the top-rr (highest-variance) directions is arbitrary – e.g., we found that PCA[:16]\mathrm{PCA}[9\!:\!16] can beat PCA[:8]\mathrm{PCA}[1\!:\!8] (FID 3.543.54 vs 3.593.59 on the B-level model trained on ImageNet).
Finally, the binary nature of the cutoff, while useful for interpretation, is not necessary: it extends naturally to a continuous, per-direction weighting that softly mixes the clean-signal (𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}) and velocity (𝒗{\bm{v}}) prediction. Building on these insights, we introduce MFlow, which learns a continuous, direction-dependent parameterization, allowing the prediction target to adapt across directions without requiring a hard subspace partition or costly search for parameterization.

4 Signal Modeling
4.1 Motivation
JiT lacks high-frequency but some other pixel diffusion models do not. JiT’s minimalist architecture lacks an explicit pathway dedicated to fine-grained signal details, like skip connections (Bao et al. (2023); Hoogeboom et al. (2023); Hoogeboom et al. (2025)) or parallel refinement networks (Chen et al. (2026b); Yu et al. (2026)), motivating an investigation into whether this simplicity introduces limitations in its generated frequency distribution.
We study the frequency spectrum of the images generated by models we trained which share the JiT architecture. We use radially averaged power spectrum (RAPS) for this analysis. For context, images on a pixel grid can have at most 0.50.5 cycles/pixel horizontal or vertical frequencies (fxf_{x},fyf_{y}). However, in the case of radial averaging, the radial frequencies frf_{r} can reach till the diagonal frequency of 0.7070.707 (=0.52+0.52=\sqrt{0.5^{2}+0.5^{2}}). Interestingly, as seen in Fig. 5, we observe that all these models lack radial frequencies in the range of 0.5−0.70.5-0.7. Recent concurrent works (Pavasovic et al. (2026); Degeorge et al. (2026); Fu et al. (2026)) have also observed this.
As seen in Fig. 6, we tried investigating other LDMs and pixel diffusion backbones. We find that neither VAE-reconstruction/generations of DiT (Peebles and Xie (2023)), REPA-E (Leng et al. (2025)), nor the generations of PixelDiT (Yu et al. (2026)) (which as a pixel level pathway) suffer from this issue, indicating that it might be a JiT backbone specific problem, more explicitely the use of high patchidication (and unpatchification) of 16−32×16-32\times without any intra-patch local operation.

4.2 ConvRefiner: A simple post-hoc fix
Based on our analysis we suspect that this high-frequency deficit might be specific to the JiT-based pixel diffusion architecture. However, as seen in the previous section, convolutional VAE decoders are able to recover high-frequency. Chen et al. (2025a) note that only finetuning the last few layers of a pretrained VAE with an adversarial loss can improve local details at a reduced training cost.
Motivated by this, we add a few lightweight convolutional blocks and post-hoc train only these blocks using an additional GAN loss apart from flow-matchting losses (Fig 7). We keep the JiT backbone frozen to keep the refinement light. The ConvRefiner predicts a residual over 𝒙{\bm{x}}-prediction of the network on which the perceptual and adversarial losses are then applied. For other parameterizations, the prediction is first converted to 𝒙{\bm{x}} (clean image) and then the ConvRefiner residual is applied on top of it.

We clamp the initializations to [0.05,0.95][0.05,0.95], as otherwise the sigmoid\mathrm{sigmoid} function will blow up 𝑴{\bm{M}}. 3.663.66 to 4.884.88. Training on 𝒖G{\bm{u}}_{\mathrm{G}} creates

improving the recovered transport field

Subsequently, by varying the 𝑴{\bm{M}} initialization to the normalized-variance (‘norm_var’) as described in 3.2, we see slight improvement over ’asym8’ initialization, FID reduces from 3.843.84 to 3.803.80. Given that ‘norm_var’ has a smoother dependence on the PCA direction’s variance, we choose it as default. We also experiment with learning-rate of 𝑴{\bm{M}} parameters, and see that having a higher learning rate multiplier seems to help a bit bringing FID down from 3.803.80 to 3.743.74.
We use this final setting (init ‘norm_var’, 𝐮{\bm{u}}-loss, learnable 𝐌{\bm{M}} w/ 10×\times{} lr-multiplier) as our default unless specified otherwise.

ConvRefiner Ablations.
Our preliminary control experiment showed that JiT trained normally for 5% more epochs does not improve the frequency spectrum. In Fig. 11 (left) we investigate the effect of number of blocks (b∈2,4,8b\in{2,4,8}) and the weight of the adversarial loss (w∈0.005,0.01,0.02w\in{0.005,0.01,0.02}).
Firstly, we see that only using the refiner without the adversarial loss helps a bit.
However, the frequency spectrum gets closer to real with more blocks and more GAN weight. Favoring a smaller lightweight network, we finally select b=4b=4 and w=0.02w=0.02 as our final setting. This improves pixel level details as seen in Fig. 23.
Ablating the choice of refiner family (Fig. 11 (right)), we see that just finetuning the unpatchify layer or using a residual attention refiner has negligible effect. Another readily available convolutional refiner choice DiP patch-detailer(Chen et al. (2026b)) does improve the high-frequency and even overshoots with the GAN loss. However, it degrades the base JiT’s FID 3.42 to 3.90 and 4.46 respectively, bolstering our choice of ConvRefiner.
6 Discussion
Figure 12: Learned M{\bm{M}} values. Variations in the MFlow gain curves across multiple axes: objectives – w/ vs w/o LPIPS, domains – images vs videos, representations – pixel vs latent 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction models, model capacities – 1B vs 13B-MoE.
𝑴{\bm{M}}-curve Analysis. Given the learned nature of the gain parameters mim_{i}, we analyze how they vary across objectives, domains, representations, and model capacities. In Fig. 12, we visualize the learned mim_{i} values ordered by decreasing PCA variance, which we call an 𝑴{\bm{M}}-curve. Here, mi=0m_{i}=0 corresponds to 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction and mi=1m_{i}=1 to 𝒖{\bm{u}}-prediction; the ‘norm_var’ initialization is shown in gray.
1.
On ImageNet (a), adding LPIPS drives all but the leading few directions toward 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction while also improving FID (Table 2), suggesting less need for 𝐮{\bm{u}}-like prediction beyond the dominant PCA directions.
2.
Moving to videos (b), the number of directions with non-negligible gains shrinks further, showing that the preferred parameterization varies across domains. We discuss in detail about the behaviour of LPIPS on 𝑴{\bm{M}}-curve in A.4.
3.
With RAEv2 (Singh et al. (2026)) (c), whose latent model uses 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction, the gains collapse almost entirely toward zero, recovering an approximately pure 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} parameterization. Yet forcing 𝒖{\bm{u}}-prediction on a small leading subspace changes FID only marginally (Table 10), indicating that a low-rank 𝐮{\bm{u}} subspace can still be tolerated.
4.
Finally, in text-to-image generation (d), increasing model capacity shifts more directions toward 𝒖{\bm{u}}-prediction. Together with the RAEv2 result, this suggests that model capacity influences how much 𝐮{\bm{u}}-prediction a model can accommodate, whereas the statistics of the data representation determine where that 𝐮{\bm{u}}-prediction is most useful.
In particular, for a fixed budget of NN u{\bm{u}}-predicted directions, the allocation should remain data-dependent, with the leading high-variance PCA directions providing a natural choice. MFlow can exploit increased capacity or adapt to domain change, without requiring a manually chosen rank.
7 Conclusion
We revisit two design questions in vanilla pixel diffusion transformers: how their prediction parameterization should adapt to the data, and whether their minimalist architecture captures high-frequency. We introduce MFlow, a direction-adaptive parameterization that learns a continuous interpolation between clean-data and velocity prediction across principal data directions, avoiding the need for manual subspace selection, saving parameterization search cycles.
Our analysis of the learned M-curves further shows that the preferred parameterization changes systematically with both data statistics and model capacity.
Beyond parameterization, we identify a persistent high-frequency deficit in JiT-style models and address it with ConvRefiner, a lightweight post-hoc convolutional module that restores high-frequency statistics while preserving generative quality. Together, these results suggest that both adaptive prediction targets and explicit treatment of fine-scale signal structure are important ingredients for scaling simple pixel-space diffusion transformers.

A.2 MFlow Implementation Details
All operators are diagonal in the fixed patch-PCA basis 𝑼{\bm{U}}, so every step is a change of basis
(img→\topatch coeffs @ U), a per-direction scalar op, and a change back (@ U⊤→{}^{\top}\to img).
Below, x is a batch of patch-token tensors, t∈\in[0,1] is flow matching timestep, U (D,D)
and lam (D,) are the precomputed PCA basis/variances. These mirror the JiT codebase.
⬇
# One-time setup: fixed gains from lambda, or learnable
# params. lam_n is the normalized variance (lam_max = 1).
def setup_gains(lam):
lam_n = lam.clamp(min=0) / lam.clamp(min=0).max()
m_fixed = lam_n / (lam_n + c)
return m_fixed
# Learnable variant (default): phi (D,) logits + scalar
# m_i = sigmoid(phi_i).
def gains():
if learnable:
m = sigmoid(phi)     # phi is init with torch.logit(m_fixed)
else:
m = m_fixed
return m
⬇
# Graded target u_G = x0 - M eps (Using Eq. 2), in the PCA basis.
def mflow_target(x0, eps, t):
m  = gains()
ce = to_patch_coeffs(eps) @ U        # eps in PCA coords
Me = (m * ce) @ U.T                  # M eps = U diag(m) U^T eps
return x0 - to_img(Me)               # u_G
⬇
# Exact recovery of x0-hat from the output u_G (Using Eq. 5, Using Eq. 6).
def mflow_to_x0(u_M, x_t, t):
m   = gains(t)
omt = clamp_min(1 - t, t_eps)        # (1 - t)
cu  = to_patch_coeffs(u_M) @ U       # u_M coeffs
cz  = to_patch_coeffs(x_t) @ U       # x_t coeffs
denom = clamp_min(omt + m * t, t_eps)  # Delta_i = (1-t)+m_i t
x0_c  = (omt * cu + m * cz) / denom  # x0-hat coeffs (eq. 11)
return to_img(x0_c @ U.T)
⬇
# Velocity for sampling (Using Eq. 6).
def mflow_to_velocity(u_M, x_t, t):
x0_hat = mflow_to_x0(u_M, x_t, t)
return (x0_hat - x_t) / clamp_min(1 - t, t_eps)
⬇
# Training step: velocity-space flow-matching loss.
def training_loss(net, x0, t):
eps = randn_like(x0)
x_t = t * x0 + (1 - t) * eps         # forward interpolant
u_M = net(x_t, t, cond)              # raw output ~= u_G
u_pred = mflow_to_velocity(u_M, x_t, t)  # -> full velocity
loss   = weight(t) * mse(u_pred, x0 - eps)  # Using Eq. 7
return loss
⬇
# Sampling: Euler/Heun on velocity, integrating t: 0 -> 1.
def sample(net, steps):
x = randn(...)                       # t = 0, pure noise
for t0, t1 in schedule(steps):
u = mflow_to_velocity(net(x, t0, cond), x, t0)
x = heun_step(x, u, t0, t1, net)
return x

A.4 Surprising behaviour LPIPS on the Learned 𝑴{\bm{M}}-Curve
Figure 12(a) shows that LPIPS substantially
changes the prediction parameterization learned by MFlow. Without
LPIPS, the gains decrease approximately monotonically with PCA variance:
high-variance directions receive more 𝒖{\bm{u}}-like prediction, while
lower-variance directions become increasingly 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-like. This behavior
is consistent with the normalized-variance initialization. With LPIPS,
however, the leading gains become non-monotonic. Using zero-based
indexing, PC0 and PC1 retain high gains, PC2–PC3 exhibit a pronounced
dip, and PC4 rises again.
Figure 13:
Leading modes of the uncentered patch-PCA basis.
We reshape the first 64 eigenvectors of the
16×16×316\times 16\times 3 patch-PCA basis into RGB patches; the value
λ\lambda denotes the variance of each direction. The leading
ordering interleaves patch-global or very low-frequency modes
with spatially varying modes. In particular, PC0 is dominated by
low-frequency patch-level tone, PC1 is an approximately uniform
chromatic mode, PC2–PC3 encode spatial gradients, and PC4 returns
to an approximately uniform chromatic mode. Eigenvector signs are
arbitrary, so the interpretation is based on spatial structure
rather than the displayed color polarity.
The PCA modes in Fig. 13 provide a structural
interpretation of this pattern. PC0 represents a patch-global,
low-spatial-frequency component, while PC1 is nearly constant across the
patch and primarily changes its color. In contrast, PC2 and PC3 contain
vertical and horizontal spatial gradients. PC4 then returns to a nearly
spatially uniform chromatic component. The leading sequence is therefore
approximately
patch-global,patch-global,spatial,spatial,patch-global,\text{patch-global},\quad\text{patch-global},\quad\text{spatial},\quad\text{spatial},\quad\text{patch-global},
which closely matches the learned gain pattern
high,high,low,low,high.\text{high},\quad\text{high},\quad\text{low},\quad\text{low},\quad\text{high}.
In particular, PC4 has substantially lower variance than PC2 and PC3
(λ4=3.74\lambda_{4}=3.74, compared with
λ2=9.41\lambda_{2}=9.41 and λ3=7.93\lambda_{3}=7.93), yet LPIPS assigns it a
larger gain. The spike at PC4 is therefore not explained by variance
ordering alone; it coincides with the return of a patch-global chromatic
mode after two spatial-gradient modes.
This effect is also more specific than a preference for color. Several
later PCA directions contain strong chromatic variation but are spatially
non-uniform, and they do not receive the same gain increase. The observed
pattern instead suggests that LPIPS distinguishes between patch-global
tone or color changes and spatially varying patterns.
One possible explanation follows from the conditional-mean behavior of
squared-error regression. In a finite-capacity model, a more
𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-like parameterization can shrink uncertain components toward
their conditional means. For a spatially varying component, such
shrinkage may primarily smooth a local gradient or texture. For a
patch-global component, however, it can coherently alter the brightness,
contrast, or color of the entire patch. Because LPIPS is applied to the
recovered clean image, it may make these perceptually coherent shifts
more costly. The learned parameterization responds by retaining a larger
velocity-like component for the patch-global modes, thereby reducing
their tendency toward mean reversion.
The controlled comparison establishes that adding LPIPS produces the
non-monotonic 𝑴{\bm{M}}-curve and that the unusually high gains align with
patch-global PCA modes. The mean-reversion explanation is a plausible
interpretation of this alignment rather than a causal mechanism directly
established by the ablation.
A.5 A Capacity-Constrained View of 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}- and 𝒖{\bm{u}}-Prediction
The choice between clean-data and velocity prediction involves two
competing considerations. Clean-data prediction avoids asking the model
to reproduce full-dimensional Gaussian noise, which can be advantageous
when a high-dimensional representation has much lower intrinsic
dimension. Velocity prediction, on the other hand, provides better
numerical conditioning near the clean endpoint. Our RAEv2 experiment
illustrates this trade-off particularly clearly.
RAEv2 predominantly favors clean-data prediction.
In our clean-latent RAEv2 setup, the pure 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction baseline
obtains an FID of 1.051.05, while AsymFlow with r=8r=8 and MFlow obtain
1.081.08 and 1.091.09, respectively, after 80 epochs
(Table 10). Consistently, the learned MFlow gains in
Fig. 12(c) move almost entirely toward zero,
recovering an approximately pure 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} parameterization. These results
indicate that, for this representation, model, and training budget, the
network prefers to avoid predicting noise in most latent directions.
At the same time, the AsymFlow result is only 0.030.03 FID behind the
pure-𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0} baseline, despite using full velocity prediction in its
leading eight PCA directions. This result does not show that velocity
prediction is superior in this setting; pure 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction remains
best in Table 10. It instead shows that a small
velocity-prediction subspace can be accommodated at little measured cost,
even in a representation that strongly favors 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction overall.
Numerical conditioning versus target complexity.
The benefit of retaining a velocity-like component can be understood from
the recovery map. Under our convention,
𝒙t=t​𝒙0+(1−t)​ϵ,t=1​ is the clean endpoint.{\bm{x}}_{t}=t{\bm{x}}_{0}+(1-t)\bm{\epsilon},\qquad t=1\text{ is the clean endpoint}.
For pure 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction, the velocity is recovered as

(25)
Consequently, an error

The factor 1/(1−t)1/(1-t) becomes ill-conditioned as t→1t\rightarrow 1.
For MFlow, the corresponding relation is

so the clean-endpoint singularity is removed in that direction. Thus,

component gives better-conditioned velocity recovery near clean samples.
This conditioning advantage comes with an opposing cost. Increasing
mim_{i} changes the target from clean-data prediction toward
u~G,i=x~0,i−mi​ϵ~i,\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{G},i}=\tilde{x}_{0,i}-m_{i}\tilde{\epsilon}_{i},
requiring the finite-capacity network to retain a larger Gaussian-noise
component. Full velocity prediction is therefore well conditioned during
recovery but can be difficult to approximate in high-dimensional spaces,
whereas pure 𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction removes the noise-prediction burden but
retains the clean-endpoint amplification. The optimal parameterization
must balance these two effects.
A soft velocity budget.
This trade-off suggests viewing MFlow as learning both how much
velocity prediction the model can support and where it should be
allocated. A useful summary of the former is the soft velocity rank
reff≔tr⁡(𝑴)=∑imi.r_{\mathrm{eff}}\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}({\bm{M}})=\sum_{i}m_{i}.

Our results suggest a capacity-constrained principle:
use velocity-like prediction in as many directions as the model can
accurately support, and retain clean-data-like prediction in the remaining
directions. The RAEv2 curve indicates a small velocity budget for this
high-dimensional latent representation, whereas the text-to-image curves
show that increasing model capacity shifts more directions toward
velocity prediction. We therefore interpret model capacity as influencing
the available velocity budget, while the data representation and training
objective determine how that budget should be distributed.
Why leading PCA directions are a natural default.
Suppose that only NN directions can be assigned substantial velocity
gains. In the absence of perceptual or other auxiliary losses, the leading
PCA directions provide a natural initial allocation. In the PCA basis,
Var⁡(x~0,i)=λi,Var⁡(ϵ~i)=1,\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{x}_{0,i})=\lambda_{i},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{\epsilon}_{i})=1,
(29)
where λi\lambda_{i} decreases with ii, while the isotropic noise variance
is identical in every direction. The velocity target therefore becomes
increasingly noise-dominated as λi\lambda_{i} decreases. Allocating a
limited velocity budget to high-variance directions concentrates the
noise-prediction burden in the dominant data subspace, while keeping the
many low-variance, predominantly off-manifold directions closer to
𝒙0{\bm{x}}_{0}-prediction. It also improves near-clean conditioning in the
directions carrying the largest fraction of the data variation.
This PCA ordering should nevertheless be viewed as an initialization
prior rather than a universal rule. Auxiliary objectives can favor
directions according to their visual function rather than variance, as
shown by the non-monotonic LPIPS 𝑴{\bm{M}}-curve in
Fig. 12(a). We also observe that a non-leading PCA
block can outperform the first eight directions in one ImageNet pilot.
MFlow therefore initializes gains using normalized variance but lets the
model modify both the effective velocity budget and its direction-wise
allocation through end-to-end training.
A.6 Radially-Averaged Power Spectrum
To quantify how faithfully a model reproduces the spatial-frequency content of natural images, we compare the azimuthally-averaged 2D power spectrum of generated and real images. Each image is converted to luma (Y=0.299​R+0.587​G+0.114​BY=0.299R+0.587G+0.114B), mean-subtracted to remove the DC component, and multiplied by a separable 2D Hann window to suppress boundary leakage. We compute the squared magnitude of its 2D DFT, P⁡(𝐟)=|ℱ​g​(𝐟)|2P(\mathbf{f})=|\mathcal{F}{g}(\mathbf{f})|^{2}, center it (fftshift), and azimuthally average it, i.e., average over all orientations at each fixed radius r=∥𝐟∥r=\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert, to obtain a 1D profile P⁡(f)P(f) as a function of radial frequency ff (cycles/pixel). Averaging P⁡(f)P(f) over N=4000N=4000 images gives the RAPS of a distribution.

We have two types of batches – all-tt batch and near-clean batch (t∼𝒰⁡(0.9,0.98)t\sim\mathcal{U}(0.9,0.98)). A.8 Limitations.
Although MFlow is a general formulation that transfers across
pixel-space image and video generation, high-dimensional representation
latents such as RAEv2 Singh et al. (2026), and text-to-image models
of different capacities, its improvements over strong fixed
parameterizations are sometimes modest. One possible reason is the joint
optimization of the gains and network weights. While the underlying
velocity target remains fixed, updating 𝑴{\bm{M}} continuously changes the
network’s direct output parameterization
𝒖G=𝒙0−𝑴​ϵ{\bm{u}}_{\mathrm{G}}={\bm{x}}_{0}-{\bm{M}}\bm{\epsilon}, creating an effectively moving
regression target and allowing the gains and network to co-adapt. Indeed,
under the same velocity-space objective, MFlow attains a slightly lower
training loss than JiT and AsymFlow on the ImageNet H-level experiment
(Fig. 21); we observe similar trends for
B-level models with and without LPIPS. However, the lower diffusion loss
does not consistently translate into better FID. This mismatch suggests
that improved regression conditioning need not directly improve
distribution-level sample quality, and its precise cause remains unclear.
Staged or decoupled optimization of the gains is an important direction
for future work.
ConvRefiner reveals a complementary evaluation limitation. It
substantially closes the high-frequency spectral gap and brings the
generated spectrum closer to that of real images, yet this improvement
produces little or no change in FID. This suggests that FID is relatively
insensitive to the local frequency statistics modified by the refiner.
Consequently, spectral fidelity and FID should be viewed as complementary
rather than interchangeable measures; texture-sensitive metrics and
human evaluation are needed to determine how strongly the recovered
high-frequency content translates to perceived image quality.
