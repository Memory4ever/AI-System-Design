Abstract.
Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) have become a common backbone for high-quality text-to-image and text-to-video generation, but repeated dense self-attention remains a major inference bottleneck as token sequences grow and the same operator is invoked across many denoising steps. Our study suggests that dense attention in pretrained DiTs contains reusable layer/head-specific high-mass structure that can be identified from a small offline calibration set and reused across future generations. This leads to Sink-Sparse Hybrid Attention, a train-free attention replacement that reuses static sink masks and supplements them with a lightweight pooled-KV branch, while leaving model weights and sampling schedules unchanged. On Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, the method attains 1.54×\times end-to-end speedup while maintaining generation quality. On Z-Image-Turbo, it attains 1.57×\times end-to-end speedup with competitive GenEval2 performance. These results indicate that reusable importance-aware sparse support can provide an effective train-free acceleration path for both T2V and T2I DiTs.
Keywords: diffusion transformers, sparse attention, train-free acceleration, text-to-image, text-to-video
1. Introduction
Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) have become an important backbone for high-quality visual generation in both text-to-image (T2I) and text-to-video (T2V) systems (Peebles and Xie, 2023; Bao et al., 2023; Polyak et al., 2024; Wan Team, 2025; Z-Image Team et al., 2025). As these systems move toward higher resolutions, longer outputs, and repeated sampling in practical workflows, inference efficiency becomes a first-order deployment concern. The relevant cost is not only the latency of a single sample: users often regenerate multiple candidates under different prompts, seeds, or motion descriptions, and online services must support many such requests concurrently. Cumulative latency and hardware budget therefore become dominant practical constraints. This pressure is especially pronounced in modern visual generation because quality improvements often come from exactly the factors that increase compute demand: more denoising steps, larger latent grids, longer videos, and richer global token interaction.
A major source of this cost is self-attention. In video DiTs, self-attention must model all interactions over long spatio-temporal token sequences, and this computation is repeated across layers, heads, and denoising steps (Vaswani et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2025b; Xia et al., 2025). In high-resolution image DiTs, the temporal axis disappears, but the large spatial token grid still makes repeated global interaction expensive. Although optimized dense kernels can substantially reduce implementation overhead, they do not change the underlying quadratic interaction pattern (Dao et al., 2022). Consequently, attention remains a major runtime bottleneck even when the rest of the inference stack is already carefully optimized. Because diffusion sampling evaluates nearby latent states over many denoising iterations, the same expensive operator is invoked again and again; any inefficiency is therefore multiplied along the full trajectory. As later shown in Sec. 3.1, self-attention alone accounts for a large fraction of end-to-end sampling time in the evaluated setting.
This bottleneck has motivated extensive research on efficient diffusion inference. Some methods reduce redundant computation across denoising steps through feature caching or cross-step reuse, such as DeepCache, TGATE, FasterCache, ToCa, and AB-Cache (Ma et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Another line targets the attention operator itself. For video DiTs, train-free methods such as SVG, SVG2, STA, and AdaSpa exploit spatial-temporal sparsity, local windows, or adaptive sparse search to accelerate pretrained backbones (Xi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Xia et al., 2025). In parallel, more generic sparse operators such as XAttention and SpargeAttn provide reusable block selection and sparse execution mechanisms that can also be instantiated in visual generation models (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025d). Training-based methods such as VSA and Video-BLADE instead learn sparse behavior through optimization or distillation (Zhang et al., 2025a; Gu et al., 2025). Together, these studies show that dense attention is not the only viable operating point. Yet for deployment, the most attractive solution is one whose sparse structure can be prepared once, reused across diverse generation requests, and integrated into existing inference stacks without retraining, fine-tuning, or architecture-specific redesign.
However, train-free sparse inference still faces a persistent difficulty. When the pretrained backbone is frozen, the outcome depends not only on how much computation is removed, but also on which interactions are preserved. Existing sparse rules often rely on locality priors, online search, or generic scoring functions. These choices can be effective, yet they do not directly answer a more reusable question: can a small subset of important interactions be identified once offline and then amortized across future generations? Local windows are simple and hardware-friendly, but long-range semantic or temporal dependencies do not always respect a fixed neighborhood. More adaptive selection rules can recover missing interactions, but they also introduce online overhead and repeated pattern discovery. In other words, train-free sparse inference often has to choose between cheap but rigid sparse layouts and flexible but repeatedly re-computed sparse layouts.
Prior work already suggests that attention in diffusion transformers is structured rather than arbitrary. Locality-based methods reveal strong spatial-temporal regularity, while adaptive methods exploit cross-step or input-dependent sparsity (Xi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Xia et al., 2025). Our question is more specific: can a small offline calibration set identify layer/head-specific block interactions that remain useful across prompts, noise seeds, and denoising steps, so that a static mask can be reused with low online overhead? If the answer is yes, train-free sparse attention need not choose only between rigid hand-designed layouts and repeated online pattern discovery.
This question also aligns with importance-aware findings in long-context transformers. Attention-sink, heavy-hitter, KV-selection, and structured sparse-prefill studies show that a relatively small subset of tokens or regions can repeatedly dominate attention and stabilize compressed inference (Xiao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Although diffusion attention differs substantially from causal LLM decoding, these results support the broader intuition that preserving recurrent high-importance structure can be more effective than treating all interactions as equally necessary. For diffusion transformers, the relevant reusable unit is not a causal token sink but a layer/head-specific block-level support pattern in bidirectional attention.
Our key observation is that a small calibration set can expose reusable block-level supports that repeatedly capture a large fraction of dense attention mass across prompts, noise seeds, and denoising steps. We refer to these retained regions as sink blocks. Unlike attention-sink tokens in causal LLMs, sink blocks here are layer/head-specific block supports in bidirectional attention. This observation makes static sparsification attractive, but it also exposes its limitation: the dominant reusable support captures most, not all, of the useful context. Discarding the remaining support entirely can remove weak but globally distributed interactions, as well as input-dependent context that is less stable than the main sink structure. This is why we use a hybrid attention design rather than a sink-only sparse operator.
We instantiate this idea in Sink-Sparse Hybrid Attention, a structure-aware, train-free replacement for dense self-attention in pretrained DiTs. For each layer and head, a static sink mask is calibrated once offline and then reused during inference. A sink-preserving sparse branch handles the recurrent high-mass interactions that can be amortized across runs, while a pooled-KV compensation branch provides a low-cost approximation to the residual global context using full-resolution queries and pooled keys/values. The two branches are fused with a normalization-aware rule so that the sparse sink branch remains dominant when its retained support is sufficient, while the compensation branch contributes when omitted context becomes important. The resulting workflow is simple and deployment-friendly, requiring no retraining, fine-tuning, or sampling-schedule changes.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We show that a small calibration set can identify reusable block-level static masks that remain well aligned with dense attention across prompts, noise seeds, and denoising steps.
•
We develop Sink-Sparse Hybrid Attention, a train-free attention replacement that combines per-layer, per-head static sink masks with a pooled-KV compensation branch and normalization-aware fusion, and we clarify why the hybrid branch is useful for recovering coarse omitted context at low cost.
•
We validate the framework on both T2V and T2I diffusion transformers, showing favorable quality–efficiency trade-offs across the two generation settings and demonstrating that hybrid compensation improves the practicality of reusable train-free sparsity.
2. Related Work
2.1. Efficient Inference for Diffusion Models
A broad literature studies how to reduce the cost of diffusion sampling. One important direction, especially in train-free settings, exploits redundancy across denoising steps through feature caching, attention reuse, or selective recomputation. Representative examples include DeepCache, TGATE, FasterCache, ToCa, and AB-Cache (Ma et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). These methods are effective because diffusion sampling repeatedly evaluates nearby latent states, so part of the computation can often be reused rather than recomputed from scratch.
Another direction improves diffusion efficiency through attention approximation, architectural redesign, or task-specific acceleration. DiTFastAttn targets faster attention in pretrained diffusion backbones, whereas DiG redesigns the generator itself, and recent editing-oriented methods accelerate related diffusion workflows (Yuan et al., 2024; Pu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Becker et al., 2025). Together, these studies show that attention is not merely a low-level kernel concern but a major algorithmic target for practical diffusion inference acceleration.
2.2. Sparse Attention in Diffusion Transformers
Sparse attention is especially attractive for DiTs because transformer-based generators inherit the high cost of dense token interaction while evaluating the same operator many times during sampling. In the T2V setting, train-free methods such as SVG, SVG2, STA, and AdaSpa exploit spatial-temporal locality, sparse support, or adaptive search to accelerate pretrained video DiTs (Xi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Xia et al., 2025). These methods demonstrate that video-DiT attention is far from unstructured and that substantial end-to-end savings are possible without changing model weights.
In parallel, more operator-oriented approaches such as SpargeAttn and XAttention emphasize reusable sparse selection rules and efficient sparse execution primitives that can transfer across workloads (Zhang et al., 2025d; Xu et al., 2025). A separate line of work, including VSA and Video-BLADE, incorporates sparsity through training, adaptation, or distillation (Zhang et al., 2025a; Gu et al., 2025). Compared with these methods, our goal is deliberately narrow and practical: we focus on the train-free setting and ask whether a small offline calibration stage can identify a reusable sparse support that is cheap to deploy later.
2.3. Importance-Aware and Structured Sparsity in Transformers
Our work is also related to a broader importance-aware view of transformer sparsity. In long-context language models, attention-sink, heavy-hitter, KV-selection, and structured sparse-prefill methods show that a relatively small subset of tokens or regions can repeatedly dominate attention and support efficient approximate inference (Xiao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c; Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Although these methods were developed primarily for causal language modeling or prefilling rather than bidirectional diffusion attention, they provide an important conceptual cue: preserving recurrently important structure can be more effective than treating all interactions as equally necessary.
Our method adopts this perspective in a different regime. Instead of causal token sinks or online heavy-hitter selection, we study layer/head-specific block-level support in pretrained DiTs and reuse it across denoising steps and future runs. In this sense, Sink-Sparse Hybrid Attention occupies a middle ground between fully hand-designed sparse layouts and fully online pattern discovery: the sparse structure is empirical and model-specific, but most of its cost is paid once during offline calibration.
3. Motivation
This section asks two questions: (1) whether self-attention is a major runtime bottleneck, and (2) whether dense attention exhibits reusable structure that can support a static sparse pattern.
Beyond raw speed, we are interested in whether attention structure is stable enough to justify paying the pattern-discovery cost only once. A useful train-free sparse operator should therefore satisfy two practical criteria: it should target a component that occupies a substantial portion of end-to-end latency, and the retained support should remain informative across prompts, seeds, and denoising phases. The following evidence is organized around exactly these two requirements and motivates why a reusable static mask is a reasonable design target.
3.1. Self-Attention Is a Major Runtime Bottleneck
We first verify that self-attention is a meaningful target for acceleration. Under the evaluated long-sequence setting, self-attention consistently accounts for about 44%–51% of sampling time across denoising schedules, as shown in Fig. 1. This confirms that reducing attention cost can translate into end-to-end savings rather than only micro-kernel improvements.
The profiling result also clarifies why a train-free attention replacement is worth studying. The same high-cost attention operator is invoked repeatedly along the denoising trajectory, so even a moderate per-step reduction can accumulate into substantial wall-clock savings. At the same time, the success of prior sparse-attention methods suggests that dense attention contains structured redundancy: some interactions appear much more important than others and therefore should be preserved more carefully.

3.2. Stable High-Mass Attention Regions Across Inputs and Steps
We next ask whether dense attention exhibits a reusable structure that could support a static sparse pattern. When the same layer and head are visualized under different prompt–seed pairs, prominent stripe-like bands and recurrent long-range links remain visible even though local intensities change, as shown in Fig. 2. This indicates that the attention pattern contains a persistent structural component in addition to instance-specific variation. Our goal is not to claim that the dense maps are identical across runs, but rather that a stable block-level scaffold persists strongly enough to guide a reusable sparse operator.
To quantify this behavior at the model level, we measure how much dense attention probability mass falls inside the token-level expansion M~(l,h)\widetilde{M}^{(l,h)} of a layer/head-specific static sink mask. Implication.
These observations make a static train-free design plausible. If recurrent high-mass regions persist across runs, they can be preserved by a reusable sink mask. At the same time, coverage is clearly below one, so a pure sink-only approximation would still discard some weaker but useful context. This motivates a hybrid design: the sink branch preserves the stable scaffold, while a lightweight complementary branch restores coarse global information omitted by the static mask.

4.3. Sink-Sparse Hybrid Inference
At inference time, the same static sink mask is reused at every denoising step.
The online module contains a static sparse sink branch and an optional pooled-KV compensation branch.
Using only the sink branch gives Ours-Sink; enabling both branches gives Ours-Hybrid.
Static sparse sink branch.
The sink branch applies block-sparse self-attention only on the block pairs retained by M(l,h)M^{(l,h)}.
This branch preserves the recurrent high-mass interactions identified offline and provides the main computational savings.
Pooled-KV compensation branch.
The hybrid variant adds a coarse complementary path.
The static sink mask is calibrated to preserve the recurrently dominant attention mass, but the omitted support is not completely negligible: some useful context is weaker, more distributed, or more input-dependent than the main sink structure.
Instead of recovering this missing support with another expensive dense branch, we use a low-cost approximation.
Queries remain at full resolution, while keys and values are compressed by non-overlapping mean pooling with gap gcompg_{\text{comp}}.
This branch therefore serves as a coarse completion path that restores global context beyond the retained sink support.
Its usefulness is practical: sink-only sparsification preserves the dominant attention skeleton but can miss weak, distributed context.
The pooled branch summarizes this omitted global context at low cost and therefore stabilizes aggressive sparse settings.
Adaptive fusion.
Because the two branches operate on different support sizes, their outputs should not be combined with a naive fixed average.
We therefore compute one row-wise fusion weight from the log-sum-exp normalizers of the two branches:

4.4. Complexity and Deployment
Let ρ(l,h)\rho^{(l,h)} denote the token-level density induced by the static sink mask. Relative to dense per-head attention with dominant cost 𝒪⁡(N2)\mathcal{O}(N^{2}), the hybrid operator can be summarized as
(7)
𝒞hyb(l,h)\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{hyb}}^{(l,h)}
=𝒪⁡(ρ(l,h)​N2+N2gcomp)\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\rho^{(l,h)}N^{2}+\frac{N^{2}}{g_{\text{comp}}}\right)
=𝒪⁡((ρ(l,h)+gcomp−1)​N2).\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(\rho^{(l,h)}+g_{\text{comp}}^{-1}\right)N^{2}\right).
where the first term comes from the static sparse sink branch and the second term comes from the pooled-KV compensation branch, which keeps the query length at NN while reducing the key/value length to about N/gcompN/g_{\text{comp}}. For reporting, we define the main-branch effective sparsity as 1−ρmain1-\rho_{\text{main}}, where ρmain\rho_{\text{main}} denotes the runtime block-interaction density of the main sparse branch, i.e., the fraction of block interactions retained by its runtime block mask. The resulting design remains fully train-free and deployment-friendly: static sink masks are calibrated once for each backbone from a small dense run and then reused across future generations without retraining, fine-tuning, or modifying the denoising schedule.

5. Experiments
We evaluate Sink-Sparse Hybrid Attention on both text-to-video and text-to-image generation. We use Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B (Wan Team, 2025) as the T2V backbone and Z-Image-Turbo (Z-Image Team et al., 2025) as the T2I backbone. Unless otherwise stated, each backbone uses its own model-specific static sink masks calibrated from a disjoint offline set.
5.1. Experimental Setup
T2V setup.
For text-to-video, we evaluate on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B under a unified generation setting with resolution 832×\times480, 81 frames, and 50 denoising steps. For quality, we evaluate eight VBench-1.0 dimensions (Huang et al., 2024) and report their mean as VBench Avg (8 dims)↑\uparrow.
T2I setup.
For text-to-image, we evaluate on Z-Image-Turbo (Z-Image Team et al., 2025) at 2048×\times2048 resolution with 8 denoising steps. We adopt the GenEval2 (Kamath et al., 2025) VQA evaluation protocol and report Soft-TIFA GM↑\uparrow as the primary metric together with Soft-TIFA AM↑\uparrow as an auxiliary metric.
Latency metric and hardware.
For efficiency, we report end-to-end Time(s)↓\downarrow and Speedup↑\uparrow. Unless otherwise stated, latency excludes model initialization and output writing. Time is measured on a single NVIDIA A800-SXM4-80GB GPU.
Calibration and implementation details.
Following Sec. 4, Ours-Sink denotes the sink-preserving sparse branch alone, while Ours-Hybrid denotes the full method with the pooled-KV compensation branch and adaptive fusion. Unless otherwise stated, both variants use static sink masks identified from calibration runs over 4 prompts and 2 noise seeds, with attention statistics aggregated across denoising steps. The calibration set is disjoint from the downstream evaluation set. We use a fixed consecutive block partition for mask acquisition, and set gcomp=30g_{\text{comp}}=30 for Ours-Hybrid. For Z-Image-Turbo, we keep the same overall pipeline and calibrate a separate mask set for the image backbone.

5.2. Main Results
Table 1 reports the main T2V comparison. Ours-Hybrid exposes two useful operating points. At τ=0.70\tau=0.70, it is the fastest setting in the table, reducing end-to-end time from 164.80s to 94.57s (1.74×\times) with 0.647 VBench Avg (8 dims). At τ=0.85\tau=0.85, it reaches 1.54×\times speedup while improving VBench Avg (8 dims) to 0.659. This quality-oriented setting is slightly better than SVG1 in overall average (0.659 vs. 0.656) while remaining faster (106.73s vs. 115.49s). Compared with SpargeAttn, it gives up a small amount of average quality (0.659 vs. 0.665) in exchange for a much larger runtime reduction (1.54×\times vs. 1.18×\times). This indicates that hybrid compensation is useful because it retains most of the speed benefit of static sparsity while making the quality-oriented point much more competitive.
Figure 5 provides representative qualitative examples on both backbones. Relative to the sparse baselines, Ours-Hybrid remains visually closer to the dense reference in overall structure and appearance, which is consistent with the quantitative trade-offs in Tables 1 and 2.
Table 2 reports the T2I comparison. Ours-Hybrid at τ=0.85\tau=0.85 reduces end-to-end runtime from 23.39s to 14.88s, yielding 1.57×\times speedup. Under the GenEval2 VQA protocol, it attains 15.21 Soft-TIFA GM and 66.48 Soft-TIFA AM. Compared with SpargeAttn, it is faster (1.57×\times vs. 1.47×\times) and slightly better on the primary Soft-TIFA GM metric (15.21 vs. 15.07), while remaining close on Soft-TIFA AM (66.48 vs. 66.88). The T2I result also suggests that the hybrid branch is a general global-context complement rather than a video-specific heuristic. These results indicate that the same train-free framework transfers to the two tested backbones.
5.3. Efficiency–Quality Trade-off
Figure 6 summarizes the efficiency trend and the corresponding quality–efficiency frontier in the T2V setting. The left panel shows that lower coverage thresholds produce sparser main branches and lower runtime for both Ours-Sink and Ours-Hybrid. For example, Ours-Sink at τ=0.60\tau=0.60 reaches 79.09% main-branch effective sparsity and reduces end-to-end time to 81.82s (2.01×\times), while the pooled-KV compensation branch adds a modest latency overhead and recovers a substantial part of the quality gap. At comparable sparsity budgets, Ours-Hybrid consistently shifts the operating frontier upward relative to Ours-Sink, which further confirms that the hybrid attention is useful rather than redundant: the extra branch converts a small amount of additional computation into a noticeably better quality–efficiency trade-off.
The right panel shows the corresponding quality–efficiency frontier for Ours-Hybrid. Increasing τ\tau improves quality up to a peak at τ=0.85\tau=0.85, whereas τ=0.70\tau=0.70 provides the stronger speed-oriented operating point. The frontier also shows a clear knee between τ=0.70\tau=0.70 and τ=0.85\tau=0.85: below this range, further sparsification yields additional speed but a more visible quality drop; above it, the masks become denser yet the incremental quality gain is smaller.

5.4. Calibration Size

A calibration trajectory denotes one prompt–seed pair whose attention statistics are aggregated over the full 50-step denoising process. We evaluate four calibration budgets, namely 2×\times2, 4×\times2, 4×\times4, and 8×\times4 prompt–seed configurations. All calibration prompts are disjoint from the downstream evaluation set.

Table 3 shows that enlarging the calibration budget brings modest but nonzero quality gains. The most visible gain appears when increasing the budget from 2×\times2 to the default 4×\times2 setting, where VBench Avg (8 dims) improves from 0.645 to 0.647 with only a small runtime difference (92.84s to 94.57s). Increasing the calibration size to 4×\times4 further improves VBench Avg (8 dims) to 0.650. The largest budget, 8×\times4, yields 0.649, which is effectively on par with 4×\times4 rather than clearly better. This indicates that the quality gains largely saturate once the calibration set becomes moderately large.
Two additional observations are worth noting. First, runtime stays nearly unchanged across calibration budgets, which means that calibration mainly affects which block interactions are retained rather than how many interactions are executed at test time. Second, the saturation around 4×\times4 suggests that the dominant layer/head-specific sink structure becomes stable after a moderate offline budget, making 4×\times2 or 4×\times4 a practical trade-off between one-time calibration cost and downstream quality.
5.5. Effect of Hybrid Compensation
Table 4 shows that, on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, the pooled-KV compensation branch consistently improves VBench Avg (8 dims) over the corresponding Ours-Sink setting across all tested thresholds. The gain is largest in the more aggressive regime, reaching +0.071 at τ=0.60\tau=0.60, +0.063 at τ=0.65\tau=0.65, and +0.066 at τ=0.70\tau=0.70. As τ\tau increases and the static sink mask becomes denser, the quality gap narrows, but the hybrid branch still remains beneficial.
The trend is informative. The compensation branch contributes most when the main sparse branch is aggressive and therefore more likely to miss weaker or more distributed context. Once τ\tau becomes large and the sink branch already preserves most dominant support, the omitted context shrinks and the additional gain becomes marginal. This behavior is desirable for deployment: the extra branch pays for itself primarily in the operating regime where static sparsification alone would otherwise lose the most quality.

6. Conclusion
We presented Sink-Sparse Hybrid Attention, a train-free framework that identifies reusable layer/head-specific sink structure from a small offline calibration set, preserves it with a static sparse branch, and complements it with a lightweight pooled-KV branch. Experiments on T2V and T2I backbones demonstrate favorable quality–efficiency trade-offs, achieving 1.54×\times end-to-end speedup on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B and 1.57×\times on Z-Image-Turbo. The results clarify why the hybrid approach is practically useful: it restores coarse omitted context at modest cost, making aggressive train-free sparsification more robust than a sink-only design. These results suggest that reusable importance-aware sparse support is a practical acceleration path for pretrained DiTs.
Limitations.
The current evaluation covers a limited set of backbones, and the method requires backbone-specific offline calibration. Additionally, fixed static masks may adapt less well to distribution shifts or unusual prompts whose salient interactions differ from the calibration set. The current study also does not examine whether masks transfer reliably across backbones. Under aggressive sparsity, rare or strongly input-dependent dependencies may still be lost.
