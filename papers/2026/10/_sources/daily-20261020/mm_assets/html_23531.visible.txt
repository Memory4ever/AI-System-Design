Abstract
We introduce PONDER, a method that learns in which order an image generator should fill in content and which of its earlier predictions to revise.
Masked generative models construct a sample by repeatedly filling in subsets of missing variables. The order of these partial predictions determines the context available in subsequent steps and therefore affects the quality of the final sample. Common strategies are to choose the order randomly or based on local confidence estimates.
Conditioned on the partially generated sample and the generator’s features, our policy chooses which positions to fill next and/or which previously filled positions to mask for later regeneration, a mechanism we call rethinking. In this way, the generation process is able to adapt to the evolving context and also to revise earlier predictions. We optimize the policy using group-relative policy gradients, with rewards that measure constraint satisfaction, human preference, or distribution quality. For the latter, we introduce FIDgrad, an image-level reward derived from the (first-order) change in Fréchet Inception Distance caused by adding a generated sample to the current feature distribution. With the generators frozen, learned ordering reduces MAR-B ImageNet FID from 4.09 to 2.78 and raises the accuracy on hard visual Sudokus from 3.9% to 56.6%, surpassing uncertainty-based greedy ordering (38.7%). Rethinking further improves these results to 2.70 FID and 96.7% accuracy, while increasing the rethinking budget of a fixed policy yields continued improvements at inference time. Our code is available here.

1 Introduction
Standard diffusion models generate images by iteratively denoising all spatial locations jointly (Ho et al., 2020; Lipman et al., 2023; Karras et al., 2022; Rombach et al., 2022). An alternative is to factorize generation over spatial locations, using a diffusion model to predict individual patches or subsets conditioned on content that has already been generated (Li et al., 2024).
In this setting, the generation order becomes consequential because each prediction determines the context available for subsequent ones.
It has been shown that such conditional generation is capable of high-quality image synthesis and spatial reasoning (Fan et al., 2025; Wewer et al., 2025), but also that its performance depends substantially on the chosen patch ordering (Fan et al., 2025; Wewer et al., 2025).
If all conditionals were known exactly, different orderings would yield equivalent factorizations. However, the conditionals that are actually learned do not preserve the equivalence. In practice, generation order substantially affects sample quality (Wewer et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025). This observation leads to a natural question: Can we learn to adapt the ordering in a sample-dependent way, such that the complete, final sample has the best possible quality?
There are three common strategies to define the order: Fixed schedules, most often in raster-scan order, are independent of the generated content (Esser et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2026; Z.ai, 2026). Random schedules sample a permutation per roll-out, as in masked autoregressive models (Li et al., 2024). These decorrelate training from any particular order but provide no mechanism for exploiting context during inference. Greedy schedules prioritize positions according to confidence or (predicted) difficulty: MaskGIT (Chang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2026) unmasks the tokens the generator is most confident about, SRM (Wewer et al., 2025) selects the ones with the lowest predicted uncertainty, and Patch Forcing (Schusterbauer et al., 2026) estimates a per-patch difficulty score and advances easy regions first, so as to obtain more context for harder ones.
All three families share the same limitation: either the order of generation is completely independent of the sample being generated, or it is determined by a greedy one-step proxy.
Among those strategies, only greedy confidence-based schedules adapt the ordering to the intermediate, partial state at all. However, the decision is based on an extremely local proxy: it prioritizes positions that are easy to predict given the current context. Such a strategy does not consider how complex future steps will become based on that decision. Thus, a local prediction that is made with high confidence does not, by itself, mean that it will greatly improve the global sample quality. A highly predictable position may provide little useful context, whereas a less certain one may establish useful structure that constrains further patches.
The delayed effect of intermediate decisions turns generative modeling into a sequential decision-making problem, driven by a policy that selects positions conditioned on the current partial sample. Taking that view further, earlier predictions made with limited context may become inconsistent at later stages of the generation process, calling for a mechanism to also revisit earlier choices.
We introduce Ponder (Policy for Ordering, Next-element DEcisions and Rethinking), a scheme for learning a policy that controls the sequence order during generation, including removal of earlier predictions for subsequent regeneration. To that end, we make the following contributions:
•
Learned ordering for continuous generators. A policy trained with final-sample rewards that decides where a frozen generator fills content next. It requires only the policy’s own action probabilities, not the generator’s likelihoods, which makes it the first such approach applicable to continuous-token generators with diffusion heads.
•
A rethinking strategy, where the policy
learns which earlier predictions to discard and regenerate as
new context becomes available. This enables adaptive revision
during sequential generation with a controllable rethinking budget that can be varied at inference time.
•
FIDgrad, a reward function that turns a distribution-level objective into an image-level reinforcement learning signal. By estimating each sample’s first-order effect on Fréchet Inception Distance, FIDgrad enables a generation policy that directly targets quality at the distribution level.
A suite of experiments demonstrates the generality of Ponder across class-conditional generation, text-to-image synthesis, and visual constraint solving. With learned ordering alone, it reduces MAR-B ImageNet FID from 4.09 to 2.78, improves MaskGen-KL-XL HPSv2 from 24.50 to 26.50, and raises hard-Sudoku accuracy from 3.9% to 56.6%. Rethinking further improves these results, to 2.70 FID, 27.80 HPSv2 at R=32R=32, and 96.7% accuracy. Moreover, performance continues to improve as the inference-time budget increases while the policy and generator remain fixed (Figure 1).
2 Related Work
Masked generation with continuous representations.
Masked generative models predict missing content from a partially observed sample, allowing generation to proceed in different orders autoregressively. MaskGIT (Chang et al., 2022) uses discrete image tokens and iterative confidence-based decoding. MAR (Li et al., 2024) instead models conditional distributions over continuous patches using a patch-wise diffusion head, removing the need for vector quantization. FLUID (Fan et al., 2025) studies the effects of token representation and generation order in text-to-image synthesis, finding advantages for continuous tokens and random-order generation. MaskGen (Kim et al., 2025) supports both discrete and continuous representations through compact one-dimensional image tokens. We build on continuous masked generators and learn how to use their conditional predictions while keeping their weights frozen.
Adaptive and learned generation orders.
SRM (Wewer et al., 2025) demonstrates the importance of sequentialization and generation order for reasoning over continuous spatial variables, using predicted uncertainty to select the next position. Patch Forcing (Schusterbauer et al., 2026) learns per-patch difficulty estimates and adapts denoising progress across an image, allowing easier regions to provide context for harder ones. These approaches motivate adapting generation to the current sample. Our policy is trained against rewards on the completed image. Learned ordering has also been studied in discrete diffusion language models. DCoLT (Huang et al., 2025) uses final-answer rewards to optimize the reverse generation trajectory and its LLaDA generator jointly trains a token-unmasking model and the language model, adapting both generation order and token predictions. Hong et al. (2026) isolate the ordering component by keeping the language model frozen and learning a position-selection policy from verifiable terminal rewards, with regularization relative to confidence-based reference policies. Fox et al. (2026) approach ordering through variational inference: a posterior conditioned on complete training examples provides a learning signal for an inference-time token selector through an evidence lower bound.
Concurrent work by Ouyang et al. (2026) learns an unmasking controller with group-relative policy optimization for multimodal diffusion language models. All of these methods operate on discrete tokens and only decide which masked position to reveal next; once revealed, a token stays fixed. Several also rely on quantities that continuous generators with a diffusion head do not provide, such as token confidences or likelihoods. Our work studies continuous-latent diffusion generators and additionally learns which previously generated positions to remove and later regenerate under a rethinking budget.
Revision during generation.
Allowing accepted predictions to change can correct errors that become apparent as additional context is generated. Path Planning (Peng et al., 2025) augments masked discrete diffusion sampling with planners that can select both masked and previously unmasked tokens for updating. It also derives an evidence lower bound for training planners with a frozen denoiser. Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Image (Li et al., 2026) enables revision of discrete image tokens through an augmented corruption objective and confidence-thresholded token replacement at inference. Ponder learns revision decisions from final-sample rewards without retraining the generator. Its position-selection policy allocates a prescribed removal budget: removed content is excluded from subsequent context and regenerated when its position is selected again. This separates the decision to discard a prediction from its later regeneration.
Reward optimization for generative models.
DDPO (Black et al., 2024) formulates diffusion denoising as a sequential decision process and optimizes generator parameters using downstream rewards. Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) extends group-relative policy optimization to flow matching models, while Mask-GRPO (Luo et al., 2025) develops reward-based post-training for masked text-to-image generators. Ponder applies reinforcement learning to the position-selection policy, leaving content generation to the frozen model. The optimized action probabilities are those of selecting positions to fill or remove, so policy training does not require evaluating the likelihood of generated continuous content.
Distributional objectives.
FD-loss (Yang et al., 2026) optimizes representation-space Fréchet distance by maintaining a large feature population through a queue or moving moments while backpropagating through the current generated batch. FIDgrad shares the use of evolving population statistics but derives a scalar reward reflecting the incfluence of a single sample on the FID. It measures the first-order effect of adding probability mass at a generated image’s feature. This reward trains the position-selection policy through policy gradients, without differentiating through the generator or feature extractor. It therefore connects a distributional quality objective to discrete generation and revision decisions over continuous content.
3 Method
Sequential image generation can be cast as masked image modeling. Starting from an empty canvas, the generator unmasks one or multiple regions (in practice: patches) and fills them by generating suitable pixel values or latents. That process is repeated in autoregressive fashion until the entire canvas has been filled.
The goal of Ponder is to devise a policy in which order to unmask the patches. Additionally, the policy can also choose to re-mask patches, such that they can be regenerated in subsequent steps. The policy is trained to maximize a suitable target metric for the task at hand, e.g., FID (Heusel et al., 2017a) or Human Preference Score (Wu et al., 2023b).

3.3 Pondering by Revisiting Patches
Figure 3: An example of sequential generation with rethinking on a visual Sudoku instance. We visualize next patch log probabilities as background color and policy decisions as border colors.
Restricting the generation order to strict permutations simplifies the process, but makes it impossible to correct early decisions after additional context becomes available.
To enable such revisions, we allow the policy to remove previously generated content. We note that the same patch selection policy can control both generation and revision. Only a minor change is needed, namely to allow the selection of already visible patches. Whenever a masked patch is selected in iteration tt, it is filled. When an unmasked patch is selected, it is returned to the masked state, thus excluding it from the backbone evaluation in the next iteration t+1t+1. The patch then remains masked until the policy selects it again, at which point it is regenerated using the new context.
Because backbone features are computed before position selection, removal does not change the features used within the current round and requires no additional backbone evaluation in that round. The next scheduled backbone evaluation uses the updated mask.
We prescribe a rethinking budget of RR removal actions. Starting from a fully masked canvas, completing the sample requires NN net fill actions. Each removal cancels one previous fill and therefore requires one additional fill later in the roll-out. A trajectory with RR removals consequently contains N+RN+R fill actions and RR removal actions, for a total of
T=N+2​RT=N+2R
(3)
patch decisions. A patch may be removed more than once, with each removal counting toward the budget.
Book-keeping of legal decisions ensures completion: removals are disabled once the budget is exhausted and in the final iteration L−1L-1. Conversely, they are forced when necessary to exhaust the budget before the final iteration.
Importantly, rethinking is distinct from repeatedly resampling a position under the same context, because regeneration can happen after other parts of the canvas have been filled, providing more context for the generator. The policy therefore learns not only where to generate next, but also when an earlier prediction is worth revisiting. This differs from fixed refinement schedules that allocate additional computation independently of the evolving sample.
Setting R=0R=0 recovers adaptive ordering without rethinking. Because RR changes the roll-out horizon but not the frozen generator or the policy parameterization, it can be varied at inference time within the schedules supported by the policy.

3.5 Distribution-Level Optimization with FIDgrad
Standard rewards, like constraint satisfaction or human preference, can be evaluated independently for each generated sample. In contrast, population-level quality metrics, like the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), measure the discrepancy between the distributions of generated and real images and cannot directly be broken down into per-sample rewards. Inspired by the FD-loss (Yang et al., 2026) for generative model training, we introduce FIDgrad: a reward that scores each generated image by its first-order effect on the FID of the current generative distribution. This makes it possible to optimize distribution quality through reinforcement learning, while keeping the generative model frozen.

During training, we track the generated mean and covariance using exponential moving first and second moments. Each batch is scored before these estimates are updated, while the real-data moments remain fixed. Initialization and numerical conventions are specified in Appendix A.1.2. The resulting scores serve as terminal rewards in the group-relative objective.
4 Experiments
We train and evaluate Ponder policies for three generative models: MAR-B (Li et al., 2024, ImageNet,), MAR (Wewer et al., 2025, Sudoku; similar to SRM,), and the continuous-token text-to-image model MaskGen-KL-XL (Kim et al., 2025). All generators are frozen throughout our experiments. To speed up policy training, we use DDIM sampling with 25 steps for MaskGen-KL-XL and MAR-B, and flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) with 12 steps for the Sudoku network. Policy training details are described in Appendix A.1, and generator architectures and inference settings in Appendix A.2. To our knowledge, PONDER is the first method to learn generation and revision order for continuous-token generators, so no prior method applies directly. We therefore construct baselines that isolate each component.
Visual Sudoku.
Following SRM (Wewer et al., 2025), we generate images of handwritten (MNIST) Sudokus. Such samples can look realistic while violating the Sudoku rules, making the task a test of global constraint satisfaction rather than local image quality. We classify every cell and verify the row, column, and box constraints, using SRM’s classifier and its easy, medium, and hard splits, which differ in the number of given cells. The frozen generator is an MAR (Li et al., 2024) with a flow-matching head operating on per-cell VAE latents (Appendix A.2, Table 7). A single policy is trained for all difficulty levels with a binary reward for a valid solution (Appendix A.1.4).
Learned ordering alone raises hard-split accuracy from 3.9% to 56.6%, well above SRM’s uncertainty-based greedy ordering (38.7%, Table 1); with a rethinking budget of R=9R=9, it reaches 96.7%. The resampling of patches itself only helps marginally: with random ordering, R=9R=9 leaves accuracy at 4.0%, and with the learned order but randomly chosen removal locations, it reaches only 62.7% compared to 56.6% with no rethinking and 96.7% with learned order and learned rethinking positions. The 34-percent gap to learned rethinking is therefore attributable to learning which predictions to revisit. The policy trained with R=9R=9 also generalizes to larger test-time budgets, reaching 98.6% at R=128R=128 (Figure 5).

5 Conclusion and Discussion
We present Ponder, a general framework for learning the generation order of frozen masked generative models. Rather than relying on fixed, random, or locally greedy schedules, Ponder learns a position-selection policy from rewards defined on the completed sample. The same policy can also revisit earlier predictions through rethinking, allowing decisions made with limited context to be removed and reconsidered once additional information becomes available. Across visual constraint solving, class-conditional image generation, and text-to-image synthesis, this improves the observed metrics over the generator’s default sampling schedule, while leaving the underlying generator untouched. Importantly, improvements are not limited to the reward we used. This suggests that the observed gains are not solely due to overoptimization of the training reward. Generation order thus becomes an optimizable component of the generative process rather than a predefined heuristic.
Limitations.
The policy adds a forward pass per generation iteration, which is small relative to the generative backbone and the scheduled denoising steps but adds a minor overhead. Its behavior is strongly dependent on the reward that is used for training. Reward design therefore remains a critical requirement for policy success. Finally, rethinking is governed by a prescribed budget and not allocated on demand. Learning when to stop, in addition to where to generate and revise, and extending the action space to inference parameters such as guidance strength, are natural next steps toward generative models that learn their own inference strategy.

MAE, SigLIP, and CLIP representations, excluding the Inception reward space,
using the FD-Loss evaluator (Yang et al., 2026).
A.5 MaskGen preference test-time scaling
At test time, the policy uses one position decision per round, giving
32+2​R32+2R rounds for R∈{0,1,2,4,8,16,32}R\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16,32\}.
Training uses the official HPSv2 training prompts for the reward; evaluation
uses all 3,200 HPSv2 benchmark prompts.
All learned-policy budgets use the same start noise. We report HPSv2, PickScore, and ImageReward.
