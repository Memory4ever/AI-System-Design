Abstract
Visual information worth extracting from an image and the token budget it warrants depend on the text query. Yet modern vision and vision-language models largely encode images independently of the query and allocate a fixed visual token budget to every input. We introduce the Query-Adaptive Recurrent Tokenizer (QART), a backbone-agnostic module that makes visual representations both query-steerable and budget-adaptive. QART steers the visual representation toward query-relevant evidence while adapting its token budget to the amount of information required. Its key principle is simple. Additional tokens are allocated until the visual features relevant to the query are represented with sufficient fidelity. Starting from frozen encoder features, QART recurrently constructs a compact representation and uses reconstruction quality over the query-relevant visual support to control token allocation. On Visual7W and VQAv2 , QART achieves 81.96% and 76.37% accuracy using only about 29 visual tokens, reducing token counts by 20–25×\times relative to dense VLM inputs. It further reaches 92.0% P@1 on CORE and 36.3% PR-AUC on PODS, outperforming SteerViT on PODS by 8.4%. Together, these results show that visual representations need not be fixed in either content or capacity and can instead adapt to what each query requires.
1 Introduction
“A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention and a need to allocate that attention efficiently among the overabundance of information sources that might consume it.”
— Herbert A. Simon (1971)
An image contains evidence for many possible questions, and the visual information that matters depends on what is being asked Anderson et al. (2018); Hudson and Manning (2019); Pantazopoulos and Özyiğit (2025). In the same street scene, identifying the color of a traffic light requires evidence from a small region, whereas counting vehicles turning left requires information distributed across objects and their spatial relationships (Acharya et al., 2018; Rajabi and Košecká, 2024; Ishihara et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Sadeghi et al., 2026). Yet SOTA vision encoders (Tschannen et al., 2025; Radford et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2023; He et al., 2022) produce the same representation of an image regardless of the query (Figure  1(a)). These representations capture broadly useful visual information but cannot explicitly prioritize the requested evidence (Ruthardt et al., 2026). More importantly, visual salience is not akin to query relevance: a prominent object may be irrelevant to the question, while a small detail may determine the answer. A useful visual representation should therefore be steerable: changing the query should change which visual information the representation emphasizes.
A separate limitation concerns how much representational capacity is allocated to that evidence. Visual tokenizers commonly produce a fixed-size sequence; ViT-based encoders (Oquab et al., 2023; Tschannen et al., 2025) provides a fixed set of patch tokens, while the Q-Former uses a fixed number of latent tokens regardless of the image or the evidence required by the downstream task (Li et al., 2023). A large token budget increases downstream computation for every example (Yin et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2022; Li et al., 2026a), while a small budget may discard necessary details (Kim et al., 2026). These considerations expose two distinct axes of visual tokenization: which evidence to preserve and how much capacity to allocate (adaptivity), as shown in Fig. 1.
These properties are increasingly relevant to embodied systems, where agents repeatedly interpret visual observations under changing goals, and efficient processing matters over long interaction horizons (Saxena et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2024).
We seek a tokenizer that connects both axes, allocating capacity according to how well the current representation preserves query-relevant visual evidence.
Figure 1: QART combines query steerability with budget adaptivity.
Fixed patch tokenizers use the same representation and token budget for every query. Adaptive tokenizers change how many tokens are allocated, while steerable methods change what visual information is emphasized. QART does both, steering the representation toward the query while adapting its capacity to the required visual evidence. The clear regions represent the tokenizers’ focus.
Prior work addresses parts of this problem. Learned resamplers create compact latent representations, steerable encoders condition visual features on language (Ruthardt et al., 2026), and token-pruning methods remove redundant or query-irrelevant patches (Li et al., 2026a; Takezoe et al., 2026). Adaptive image tokenizers additionally vary their sequence length according to image complexity (Duggal et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2024). What remains less explored is an explicit mechanism that couples these choices: rather than prescribing a token count or predicting one directly, can a tokenizer continue allocating capacity until the evidence relevant to the current query has been represented with sufficient fidelity?
We introduce the Query-Adaptive Recurrent Tokenizer (QART), a visual tokenizer that converts dense encoder features into a variable-length set of query-conditioned visual tokens. Given frozen image features and a text query, a cross-modal relevance module first identifies the visual evidence relevant to the query. QART begins with a small set of tokens and recurrently refines them using this evidence and the query. After each round, a shared decoder reconstructs the relevant visual features, and reconstruction quality determines whether more tokens are added or computation halts. The resulting tokens form a compact representation whose content and capacity are both shaped by the query, and can be used by a downstream task head or language model.
We evaluate QART on prompt-conditioned retrieval (CORE (Ruthardt et al., 2026), GeneCIS (Vaze et al., 2023), PODS (Sundaram et al., 2025)), compositional matching (Winoground (Thrush et al., 2022)), Visual Question Answering (VQAv2 (Goyal et al., 2017)), and grounding (Visual7W pointing (Zhu et al., 2016). QART learns non-uniform allocations and compresses dense patch tokens by  25×\times. On CORE and GeneCIS, it improves over the query-agnostic SigLIP2 representation by 23.5% and 2.7% respectively. On Visual7W pointing, it outperforms the strongest token-reduction baseline DART Wen et al. (2025) by 22.1% under a matched protocol; on VQAv2, it retains 93.8% of dense accuracy while reducing tokens by 25×\times and outperforms a matched 32-token Q-Former, while improving end-to-end throughput by 1.53×\times. Together, these results highlight the significance of query-adaptive visual representations.
2 Related Work
2.1 Compact and Variable-Length Visual Tokenization
Vision encoders commonly represent an image as a dense grid of patch embeddings, motivating learned bottlenecks that summarize this grid with a smaller set of visual tokens (Bachmann et al., 2025). TokenLearner learns a compact set of content-adaptive tokens (Ryoo et al., 2021), while BLIP-2 uses a Querying Transformer (Q-Former) to map frozen image features into a fixed-size latent sequence for a downstream language model (Li et al., 2023). InstructBLIP further conditions the Q-Former on the input instruction, but retains a fixed latent budget (Dai et al., 2023). Matryoshka Query Transformer (MQT) supports multiple output lengths within one model by training nested prefixes of latent queries; however, the desired length is selected externally at inference time rather than inferred from the visual evidence required by each query (Hu et al., 2024).
Adaptive-length image tokenizers vary representation capacity with image content (Yan et al., 2024; Duggal et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). ALIT, the closest architectural precedent to our work, recurrently updates a latent sequence, adds tokens over successive rounds, and uses reconstruction as a learning signal for variable-length image representations (Duggal et al., 2025b). QART changes the unit of adaptation from the image alone to the image–query pair. Its text-conditioned relevance model determines which patch features must be preserved, and the recurrent tokenizer halts when those query-relevant features have been reconstructed sufficiently well. Consequently, the same image can produce different token contents and lengths under different queries.
2.2 Query-Conditioned and Steerable Visual Representations
Other work studies how language can steer visual representation (Mao et al., 2025). InstructBLIP extracts instruction-specific features through an instruction-aware Q-Former (Dai et al., 2023); FLAIR applies text-conditioned attention pooling to local image features for fine-grained retrieval (Xiao et al., 2025); and TIE conditions the image encoder on the text query (Thirukovalluru et al., 2025). SteerViT injects language into intermediate vision-transformer layers to produce dense visual features that can be directed toward a requested concept (Ruthardt et al., 2026). QMoP uses the query to route visual features among pooling, resampling, and pruning branches (Li et al., 2026b).
These methods establish that useful visual evidence depends on the language context; QART is therefore not the first query-conditioned visual representation. Its distinction lies in coupling query conditioning to recurrent capacity allocation. Rather than only changing which information is emphasized within a dense or prescribed-size representation, QART jointly determines what information to encode and how many latent tokens are required. The token count follows from an explicit representation-adequacy criterion instead of a fixed budget or a separately predicted length.
2.3 Visual Token Pruning and Merging
Visual-token reduction is also widely studied through pruning and merging (Chen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Shang et al., 2025). ToMe reduces vision-transformer sequences by progressively merging similar tokens (Bolya et al., 2023). In vision-language systems, QG-VTC uses question–patch relevance to progressively compress visual tokens within the vision encoder (Li et al., 2025), while QuietPrune introduces query guidance early in the vision transformer so that pruning preserves prompt-relevant regions (Gao et al., 2026). ATP-LLaVA learns instance- and layer-dependent pruning ratios inside the language model (Ye et al., 2025); LearnPruner combines learned post-encoder reduction with query-guided pruning in intermediate language-model layers (Takezoe et al., 2026); and OccamToken derives image- and query-adaptive pruning thresholds without additional training (Li et al., 2026a).
These approaches primarily retain, merge, or discard existing tokens. QART instead constructs and recurrently expands a slot-based representation from frozen patch features until a query-conditioned reconstruction criterion is met, which supplies a shorter visual sequence to downstream models.

4 Experiments
Our experiments probe the central properties of QART rather than performance on individual benchmarks alone. We ask whether query conditioning can steer a frozen visual representation toward the requested content, whether the resulting compact representation preserves downstream reasoning, whether its behavior genuinely depends on the query, whether it retains information beyond what the query explicitly specifies, and whether its compactness translates into practical inference gains.
4.1 Experimental Setup
Unless stated otherwise, QART uses frozen SigLIP2 vision and text encoders and produces at most 32 visual tokens from 729 patch features. A single pretrained QART checkpoint is used across the representation benchmarks without task-specific finetuning. For VQA, QART replaces the visual tokens of LLaVA-1.5-7B while both QART and the language model remain frozen; only a lightweight visual projector is trained. Full architecture, training, and evaluation details are provided in the appendix A. We additionally evaluate backbone generalization by using DINOv2 in App. B.

4.2 Does QART Redirect a Pretrained Visual Representation?
We first ask whether QART changes what a frozen visual representation encodes. If query conditioning is effective, the resulting representation should emphasize the visual evidence requested by the query rather than simply inherit the dominant semantics of the pretrained backbone.
Controlled evaluation on CORE.
CORE Ruthardt et al. (2026) 11
1
We follow SteerViT’s protocol for CORE. directly tests this behavior by requiring retrieval according to a queried object that may occupy only a small part of the image. QART reaches 92.0%92.0\% P@1, compared with 68.5%68.5\% for the native SigLIP2 global representation (Fig. 3a).
Because both representations originate from the same frozen vision encoder, this improvement isolates the effect of query-conditioned representation construction. QART therefore does more than compress the underlying patch features; it changes which visual evidence dominates the representation. QART also outperforms open-vocabulary localization methods like Grounding-DINO (Liu et al., 2024) and SteerViT (Ruthardt et al., 2026) showcasing the benefit of query-relevant adaptive representations.
Does the same behavior transfer to natural images?
We next test whether this steering behavior transfers beyond the controlled CORE setting. On GeneCIS Focus Object, QART uses the same pretrained checkpoint without GeneCIS-specific tuning. It improves over native SigLIP2 representations by 2.72.7, 5.05.0, and 7.17.1% at R@1, R@2, and R@3, respectively (Fig. 3b).
The gain increases with retrieval depth, suggesting that query conditioning consistently promotes condition-compatible images throughout the ranked list rather than affecting only the top prediction. QART also improves over simple Image + Text composition at every rank and approaches Combiner, despite Combiner being designed specifically for composed image-text retrieval.
Insight
QART can steer a frozen visual representation toward query-relevant content, and this behavior transfers from controlled scenes to natural images without task-specific adaptation.

4.3 Can QART Preserve Reasoning and Grounding Under Extreme Visual Compression?
Steering a representation is useful only if the resulting compact tokens retain the information required downstream. We test this in two complementary settings. VQAv2 evaluates general visual reasoning, while Visual7W places greater emphasis on grounding and localized visual evidence.
General reasoning on VQAv2.
Replacing the dense visual tokens of LLaVA-1.5-7B+SigLIP2 with QART tokens, QART reaches 76.37% acc., retaining 93.8% of dense accuracy (81.4%) with 24.8× fewer tokens, and outperforms a matched 32-token Q-Former by 1.7%. Pruning baselines use a different base model, so we compare relative accuracies (Tab. 1a); strong pruners retain slightly more performance here, as VQAv2’s general questions limit the benefit of query-directed representation.
Grounded reasoning on Visual7W.
We next test whether this compact representation preserves the spatially localized evidence required for grounding in Tab. 1b. We specifically focus on the pointing task as it requires selecting the box that answers a question, so evidence at specific locations must be preserved. Under a matched pipeline (App. A.3), QART reaches 81.96% with 29 tokens, retaining 92.6% of dense model’s accuracy and outperforming the strongest baseline, DART, by 22.1%. This larger gap indicates that aggressive compression is more demanding when predictions depend on localized visual evidence. Nevertheless, QART retains substantially more grounding information than existing low-token pruning methods. This suggests that query-conditioned token construction preserves the spatial evidence needed to associate the query with relevant image regions.
Does recurrence refine the representation?
To isolate recurrent refinement from token growth, we evaluate the same QART checkpoint while fixing the representation to two slots throughout recurrence, such that no additional slots are added. VQAv2 accuracy increases from 46.27%46.27\% after one round to 50.93%50.93\% after three rounds and remains around that number thereafter (Fig. 4a). Since both the query-relevant support and token count remain fixed, this improvement shows that recurrent updates can refine the representation independently of increasing its capacity. Additional qualitative results are provided in App. C.2.
Insight
QART preserves general reasoning and excels at grounding under severe compression. With  29 tokens, it retains over 92% of dense tokens’ accuracy on both benchmarks, with the largest gains when answers depend on localized evidence.
4.4 Does the Conditioning Query Actually Control the Representation?
We use Winoground (Thrush et al., 2022) to test whether QART responds to the compositional meaning of the conditioning query. The benchmark pairs two images with two captions that contain the same words but differ in composition, and evaluates whether the model matches each caption to the correct image. We emphasize Image accuracy, where both candidate images are conditioned on the same caption, and Group accuracy, which additionally requires the Text judgment to be correct.

QART reaches 24.75%24.75\% Image and 19.25%19.25\% Group accuracy, compared with 19.75%19.75\% and 17.00%17.00\% for native SigLIP2. It also has 36.25% text accuracy. Further, it outperforms stronger models like FLAVA (Singh et al., 2022) and Vin-VL (Hu et al., 2022) comfortably. More importantly, replacing the sample-specific query with a fixed query reduces Image accuracy to 15.25%15.25\% and Group accuracy to 12.00%12.00\% (Fig. 3c).
This controlled intervention isolates the effect of conditioning from the tokenizer itself, showing that QART changes its visual representation according to the compositional meaning of the query rather than only the words it contains. Sec. 4.7. further probes the impact of query
conditioning in representation steering and adaptive token allocation.
4.5 Does Query Conditioning Preserve Information Beyond the Prompt?
Query-conditioned compression introduces a complementary risk. A representation may become highly focused on the prompt while discarding visual detail that the prompt does not explicitly mention. We test this on PODS, where QART receives only a coarse supercategory prompt, such as “mug” or “shoe”, but must distinguish a particular object from visually similar instances of the same category.
QART reaches 36.3%36.3\% PR-AUC and 61.3%61.3\% NDCG, improving PR-AUC by 8.48.4% over SteerViT under the same supercategory prompting setting and by 6.76.7% over unconditioned DINOv2 (Fig. 3d).
The query itself cannot identify the correct instance. QART must therefore use it to determine which region or semantic content is relevant while still preserving visual cues that are more specific than the language supervision. The improvement on PODS shows that steering does not collapse the representation to only what is explicitly stated in the prompt. Instead, the query determines where representational capacity is focused while fine-grained visual information within that region is retained.
4.6 Does Compactness Translate into Real Inference Gains?
A shorter visual sequence is useful only if the reduction offsets the cost of constructing it. We therefore measure end-to-end VLM inference as batch size increases.
At batch size one, QART’s recurrent tokenization introduces more overhead than is recovered by the shorter language-model sequence, resulting in a 0.87×0.87\times end-to-end speed ratio. The balance changes as batch size increases and processing the visual sequence inside the language model becomes more expensive. At batch size four, QART reaches 1.14×1.14\times end-to-end and 1.84×1.84\times prefill speed-up. At batch size eight, these gains increase to 1.53×1.53\times and 2.54×2.54\times, respectively, with throughput increasing from 21.021.0 to 32.232.2 samples/s (Fig. 4).
The different scaling of tokenization and language-model processing explains this trend. QART incurs a largely fixed cost to construct its representation, while reducing the sequence subsequently processed by the 7B language model. Its advantage therefore becomes larger when downstream sequence processing dominates the computation.
Insight
QART trades recurrent tokenization overhead for substantially cheaper downstream sequence processing.
The benefit grows under batched inference, reaching 1.53×1.53\times end-to-end and 2.54×2.54\times prefill speed-up at batch size eight.
4.7 What Does Query Conditioning Control?
Query conditioning enters QART in two places.
It determines the visual support available during recurrent refinement, and it
defines the evidence used by the reconstruction coverage c(r)c^{(r)} to control slot growth and
halting.
We ablate these two roles on CORE, and illustrate the results in Tab. 2.
Making the budget decisions query-agnostic has almost no effect on retrieval
accuracy, but removes the computational adaptivity of QART.
The model reaches 92.08%92.08\% P@1, yet activates all 3232 slots and runs all six
rounds.
Using query-relevant evidence for growth and halting preserves essentially the
same accuracy while reducing the average budget to 17.2517.25 slots and 4.254.25
rounds.
This corresponds to roughly 46%46\% fewer active tokens and 29%29\% fewer
recurrent rounds.
The query-relevant support itself plays a different role.
When the relevance threshold τ\tau is set to zero, all patches participate rather
than focusing recurrence on the queried evidence.
When there is no query-conditioning for the visual support, P@1 drops to 79.7%79.7\%, still outperforming the SigLIP2 number.
Thus, query conditioning of the visual support is important for steering the
representation, whereas query conditioning of the reconstruction coverage
enables efficient allocation of capacity.

Query conditioning serves two complementary roles in QART.
It enhances steering of the representation toward the requested visual evidence, while
query-aware growth and halting avoid spending capacity on information that the
query does not require.

5 Conclusion
We introduced QART, a recurrent visual tokenizer that combines query steerability with budget adaptivity. QART steers its representation toward query-relevant evidence and allocates additional visual tokens only when that evidence is not yet represented with sufficient fidelity. Across retrieval, grounding, compositional matching, and visual question answering, QART produces compact representations with substantially fewer tokens while preserving strong downstream performance. Its gains are strongest when predictions depend on localized visual evidence, while on general VQA it remains competitive with existing token-reduction methods. Overall, these results show that visual representations need not be fixed in either content or capacity, and can instead be shaped by what each query requires.

Query-relevance supervision.
During tokenizer pretraining, we use dense query-region targets to supervise the relevance
predictor, following Ruthardt et al. (Ruthardt et al., 2026).
For the COCO-family referring-expression datasets (Yu et al., 2016),
relevance targets are derived from segmentation annotations.
For Mapillary Vistas (Neuhold et al., 2017) and Visual Genome (Krishna et al., 2017),
we use cached dense query-region alignment maps.
The resulting heatmaps are retained as soft targets in [0,1][0,1] rather than being
converted to binary bounding boxes. These targets provide training supervision
for the relevance predictor and the query-weighted reconstruction objective, but
are not used to define the visual support or the recurrent growth and halting
decisions. The recurrent tokenizer instead operates on the support predicted by
the relevance module,
𝒜={i∣pi>τ}\mathcal{A}=\{i\mid p_{i}>\tau\},
both during training and inference.
Vision–language alignment data.
For integration with LLaVA-1.5-7B Liu et al. (2023b), we train the visual projector in two stages.
The first stage performs one epoch of Visual Genome-based alignment using
native-image VG region descriptions.
The region phrase conditions QART, while the language model receives a generic
region-description instruction and predicts the corresponding phrase.
The second stage trains the projector for one epoch on the full
LLaVA-Instruct-665K mixture Liu et al. (2023b).
QART and the language model remain frozen throughout both stages, and only the visual projector is optimized.
A.2 Setup
Backbone and input representations.
Our main configuration uses the frozen vision and text encoders from
google/siglip2-so400m-patch14-384.
Images are processed at 384×384384\times 384 resolution.
The visual encoder produces a 27×2727\times 27 grid of N=729N=729 patch features,
each with dimension 11521152.
We precompute and cache these frozen visual features in FP16.
A bias-free linear layer projects the visual features to the tokenizer
dimension D=768D=768.
The frozen text encoder provides token-level query representations, which are
projected to the same 768768-dimensional space when necessary.
Padding tokens are masked, and a masked mean of the query features is used to
initialize the slot bank.
Tokenizer pretraining uses a maximum text length of 32 tokens.
The reconstruction target is the projected visual feature map used by the tokenizer. Specifically, the frozen backbone features are first projected to the tokenizer dimension, and the decoder reconstructs these projected visual features rather than RGB pixels.
Relevance predictor.
The relevance predictor consists of five visual-to-text cross-attention layers
with eight attention heads. Visual patches act as queries and text tokens as
keys and values. Each layer uses pre-layer normalization (Nguyen and Salazar, 2019)
and a residual connection without an intermediate feed-forward block.
A final linear projection followed by a sigmoid predicts one relevance score
pi∈[0,1]p_{i}\in[0,1] for each visual patch.
Queries and keys are normalized independently within each attention head after
their learned projections. At inference, the query-relevant visual support is
defined as
𝒜={i∣pi>τ}.\mathcal{A}=\{i\mid p_{i}>\tau\}.
(8)
The relevance set is computed once before recurrence and remains fixed across
recurrent rounds. For tasks in which a visual target is guaranteed to exist,
an empty selection falls back to the 100 patches with the highest relevance
scores.

Tokenizer optimization.
We optimize the trainable QART components using AdamW with learning rate
10−410^{-4}, (β1,β2)=(0.9,0.999)(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999), and weight decay 0.010.01.
The learning rate increases linearly for 4,000 optimizer steps and then follows
a cosine schedule over 25 epochs.
Training uses a global batch size of 256, BF16 mixed precision, and
global-norm gradient clipping at 0.50.5.
The training objective combines query-weighted feature reconstruction and
relevance prediction as described in Sec. 3.4.

The implementation additionally includes a slot-count term with weight 0.010.01.

The global batch size is 32.
The first Visual Genome alignment stage is run for one epoch, followed by one

Only the projector parameters are updated in both stages.
A.3 Evaluation Protocols
Shared representation evaluation.
CORE, GeneCIS, PODS, and Winoground use the frozen pretrained QART tokenizer
without benchmark-specific fitting.
For retrieval-style evaluations, we mean-pool the active slots, compute the
representation in FP32, apply ℓ2\ell_{2} normalization, and use cosine
similarity.
Unless stated otherwise, transfer experiments use the same SigLIP2 QART
checkpoint and the relevance threshold
τ=0.25\tau=0.25.
GeneCIS, PODS, and Winoground execute each image–query pair independently so
that recurrent halting is determined per example.
CORE.
We evaluate on the JonaRuthardt/CORE test split using the provided
scene-specific galleries and object prompts.
For each target object, all images in its gallery are encoded under the same
query.
Each positive image is used as a retrieval query, while itself and variants of
the same source image are excluded from the gallery.
We report precision at rank one (P@1) over the eligible retrieval queries.
GeneCIS.
We evaluate the GeneCIS Vaze et al. (2023) Focus Object split.
For every query, the reference image and its 15 candidate images are encoded
using the same object condition.
Candidate membership from the benchmark is preserved without introducing
additional negatives.
Candidates are ranked using cosine similarity between normalized mean-slot
representations, and we report R@1, R@2, and R@3.
VQAv2.
For VQAv2 Goyal et al. (2017), we use the QART–LLaVA-1.5-7B model obtained after the two-stage
projector training described above.
QART and the 7B language model remain frozen, and the relevance threshold is set to τ=0.05\tau=0.05.
Only active QART slots are projected into the language-model embedding space
and inserted before the textual question.
Answers are generated autoregressively using greedy decoding.
We use at most 16 generated tokens and evaluate predictions using the standard
VQAv2 evaluation protocol.
For VQAv2, we reproduce LLaVA+SigLIP2 where the visual representations are replaced with siglip tokens, and Q-Former Li et al. (2023) by following the same protocol as QART. The other numbers are reported from their corresponding papers.
Visual7W.
For Visual7W, we evaluate it on the pointing task. For the pointing task, the 4 candidate bounding boxes are given in [x1, y1, x2, y2] format. Also, the 4 candidates are given as A, B, C and D to the LLM, and the answer is always one of the 4 alphabets. Evaluation is normal accuracy checking for a perfect match.
We reproduce all compared methods under a common evaluation
pipeline.
We use the LLaVA+SigLIP2 configuration as the base model for all the pruning baselines which is finetuned with the same protocol as qart, which is one epoch of vg-alignment and 6 epochs of Visual7W projector finetuning. And for all learnable baselines, we additionally finetune for 4 epochs on Visual7W data to adapt to the compressed representations.
Winoground.
We evaluate all 400 official Winoground Thrush et al. (2022) examples and score all four
image–caption pairings.
For an image IiI_{i} conditioned on caption cjc_{j}, QART reconstructs the visual
feature grid from its active slots.
We then apply the frozen SigLIP2 attention pooler and compute cosine similarity
with the native SigLIP2 text representation,
Si​j=cos⁡(Hv​(V^​(Ii,cj)),Ht​(cj)).S_{ij}=\cos\left(H_{v}\bigl(\hat{V}(I_{i},c_{j})\bigr),H_{t}(c_{j})\right).
(18)
We compute the official Text, Image, and Group accuracies from the resulting
2×22\times 2 similarity matrix.
For the fixed-query control, the same generic text query is used to condition
QART for every image while the official Winoground scoring protocol remains
unchanged.
PODS.
We follow the PODS Sundaram et al. (2025) evaluation protocol over 100 target objects, 300 reference
images, and 10,888 test images.
For the main experiment, the dataset’s coarse supercategory label is used as
the conditioning query for all reference and test images associated with a
target object.
For classification, each test image is scored by its maximum cosine similarity
to the three positive reference representations.
We compute average precision independently for each target object and report
the macro average.
For retrieval, each positive test image ranks all 300 references, with the
three references belonging to the same target object treated as relevant.
We compute binary NDCG over the complete ranking and average first within each
target object and then across objects.

B.3 Discussion
The DINOv2 results show that QART is not tied to a particular visual backbone.
The variant remains close to SigLIP2 on CORE and retains competitive VQAv2
performance at a compact visual-token budget, despite using independently
pretrained vision and text encoders.
The stronger SigLIP2 results are consistent with the benefit of native
cross-modal alignment.
SigLIP2 jointly learns visual and textual representations, whereas DINOv2 and
RoBERTa are pretrained independently and therefore provide a weaker starting
point for query-conditioned representation learning.
We therefore view the DINOv2 experiments as evidence that QART generalizes
across backbone families, while also highlighting the importance of aligned
vision–language features for its strongest performance.

C.2 Does Recurrence Refine the Representation?
QART does not construct its representation in a single step.
The active slots are repeatedly refined using the same query and visual
evidence, allowing their spatial support to change across recurrent rounds.
Figure 5 shows representative examples of this
behavior.
The progression is particularly clear when the initial representation attends
to a nearby distractor or only coarsely identifies the relevant region.
For the query “person in foreground with backpack,” the early support is
spread over nearby foreground content before progressively concentrating on
the queried person and backpack.
A similar shift occurs for “guy in plaid,” where the support moves toward
the queried individual over successive rounds.
For “number 13,” the representation initially responds to nearby players
before becoming localized around the jersey number of the target player.
These examples suggest that recurrence provides more than additional
representational capacity.
Later rounds can revise where the active slots attend, progressively moving
their support toward evidence that is better aligned with the query.
This is useful when the relevant region is visually ambiguous or surrounded
by similar content, where a single tokenization step may commit too early to
an incorrect or overly broad region.
Insight
Recurrence allows QART to revise, rather than simply expand, its
representation.
Across rounds, slot support can move from coarse or distracting regions toward
the queried visual evidence, providing a mechanism for iterative
query-conditioned refinement.

Each row shows the spatial support of an active QART slot over successive
rounds for a different query.
The support progressively shifts toward the query-relevant region, including
the foreground backpack, the person in plaid, and jersey number 13.

C.4 Limitations
While QART is evaluated across retrieval, compositional matching, visual question answering,
and grounding, our experiments focus on single-image settings. Extending query-adaptive
token allocation to video, multi-turn interaction, and temporally evolving visual observations
remains an interesting direction for future work.
The current formulation also uses a small number of thresholds to control relevance selection,
slot growth, and halting. As shown in our sensitivity analysis, the preferred operating point can
vary across tasks. Exploring fully learned or calibration-free allocation criteria could further
simplify deployment across heterogeneous downstream settings.
Finally, QART uses dense query-region supervision during tokenizer pretraining to train the
relevance predictor and reconstruction objective. This supervision is not required at inference,
but future work could investigate weaker or self-supervised relevance signals to reduce the
annotation requirements of tokenizer pretraining.
