SCALE: Improving Guidance-Free Visual Generation via Self-Contrastive Alignment for
Likelihood Estimation
Abstract
Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is essential for improving sample quality in visual generation, but it incurs substantial sampling overhead. Existing guidance-free approaches halve this cost by approximating the CFG with a single model inference, but their performance is bounded by the CFG and inherits its limitations.
In this paper, we propose self-contrastive guidance (SCG), which contrasts the model’s prediction on real and self-generated samples to obtain a direct, model-based signal that pushes generation toward the true data manifold.
Integrating SCG into the standard likelihood objective via reparameterization yields Self-Contrastive Alignment for Likelihood Estimation (SCALE), a guidance-free training framework that produces SCG-enhanced predictions in a single forward pass.
Across class-conditional and text-to-image generation on diffusion and autoregressive models, SCALE consistently outperforms CFG-mimicking guidance-free baselines and improves further when combined with DPO, demonstrating that the self-contrastive signal yields gains complementary to both CFG and preference-based fine-tuning.
1 Introduction
Likelihood-based generative models, including diffusion [18, 53] and autoregressive (AR) models [48, 54], have demonstrated remarkable scalability and performance.
Yet, for complex visual datasets, trained models often produce samples whose clarity falls short of the training images. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) [20] has thus become the de facto remedy.
By contrasting unconditional and conditional predictions, CFG effectively enhances fidelity, but at the cost of doubled sampling cost.
To eliminate this overhead, recent guidance-free approaches approximate the CFG distribution with a single inference via teacher distillation [41], contrastive objectives [7], or direct reparametrization of the CFG objective [6].
While effective, these methods are fundamentally constrained to CFG and are bounded by its performance.
Furthermore, recent analyses have clarified that CFG is not flawless. A primary challenge is that condition alignment and sample fidelity are entangled along a single guidance.
Therefore, amplifying the CFG for higher fidelity over-amplifies the conditional signal. This often leads to distorted sampling trajectories [8], greatly exaggerated truncation [26], and mode dropping that collapses samples onto a narrow set of per-class templates [30] (Fig. 1a). Whether a single-model approach can move beyond these limitations, therefore, remains an open question.
A complementary line of work, information-based sampling guidance [21, 24] generalizes CFG by amplifying any salient information while avoiding a degraded reference that lacks it. While effective at targeting specific quality dimensions, these methods still incur additional sampling-time forward passes (if combined with CFG, the total cost steeply rises to three model evaluations per step) and rely on heuristic, domain-specific definitions of the salient information and the degraded reference.
In this paper, we propose self-contrastive guidance (SCG), which contrasts the model’s prediction on real and self-generated samples (Fig. 1b).
This contrast yields a direct, model-based estimate of the model’s own approximation error, and extrapolating along it pushes the generation process toward the true data manifold.
Unlike prior information-based guidance, SCG is domain-agnostic and is directly applicable to both diffusion and AR models as it relies only on model predictions.
Figure 1: Comparison between CFG and our self-contrastive guidance (SCG). (a) CFG amplifies the conditional signal to improve sample quality, often reducing sample diversity by overemphasizing the condition. (b) SCG derives a model-based signal that measures the discrepancy between real and self-generated samples, pushing generation toward the true data manifold.
(c) SCALE integrates CFG and SCG into a single model, achieving superior guidance-free generation performance.
By reparametrizing the SCG signal as a learnable term within the standard likelihood objective, we obtain Self-Contrastive Alignment for Likelihood Estimation (SCALE), a guidance-free training framework in which the model learns to directly produce SCG-enhanced predictions in a single forward pass. For conditional generation, we further integrate CFG into SCALE so that the trained model directly refines the CFG distribution rather than simply mimicking it.
Experimental results show that SCALE consistently surpasses existing guidance-free methods that only approximate CFG across diffusion and AR architectures, on both class-conditional ImageNet (DiT [44], LlamaGen [54]) and text-to-image generation (Stable Diffusion v1.5 [49]). It matches DPO-finetuned guidance-free models alone, and applying DPO on top of SCALE brings further gains (Fig. 1c).
SCALE introduces minimal additional training overhead and complexity without compromising the base model’s capabilities.
As a result, SCALE provides an orthogonal direction for improving guidance-free visual generation, widely applicable to both diffusion and AR models.

2.2 Guidance-Free Visual Generation via Mimicking CFG
Several approaches approximate the CFG distribution ptcfgp_{t}^{\text{cfg}} with a single inference, including guidance distillation [41] for diffusion models, Condition Contrastive Alignment (CCA) [7] for AR models, and Guidance-Free Training (GFT) [6] for both. GFT in particular integrates the CFG output directly into standard likelihood training by introducing a pseudo-temperature β≔1/(1+scfg)\beta\coloneqq 1/(1+s_{\text{cfg}}) and reformulating the conditional output fθcf_{\theta}^{c} in Eq. (3) as an interpolation of CFG and unconditional predictions:
fθc​(𝒙t,c,β)=β​fθcfg​(𝒙t,c,β)+(1−β)​sg​[fθu​(𝒙t|∅,1)],\begin{split}f^{c}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},c,\beta)=\beta f^{\text{cfg}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},c,\beta)+(1-\beta)\text{sg}\left[f^{u}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}|\varnothing,1)\right],\end{split}
(4)
where a stop-gradient 𝐬𝐠⁡[⋅]\mathbf{sg}[\cdot] is applied to keep the training cost minimal without affecting convergence.
Despite halving the inference cost of CFG, these methods remain constrained to the CFG-induced distribution ptcfgp_{t}^{\text{cfg}} as their training target, which alone is insufficient for high-quality generation due to its inherent limitations.
In the following section, we examine these limitations and motivate a complementary guidance signal that directly improves the guidance-free visual generation.

CFG is not flawless.
CFG enhances conditional generation by implicitly amplifying the conditional density to a power greater than one, sharpening the distribution toward its modes [2]. While effective, recent analyses have clarified that CFG does not merely trade compute for quality: its unconditional-to-conditional extrapolation fundamentally entangles prompt alignment and perceptual quality, making them difficult to control independently [26].
Consequently, scaling up CFG for higher fidelity disproportionately amplifies the conditional signal, driving samples off the data manifold and truncating the distribution toward a narrow set of per-class “templates” [30, 8].
Existing guidance-free generation methods halve the inference cost of CFG, but they do not address this underlying distortion.
We illustrate this in Fig. 2. CFG-mimicking guidance-free methods over-amplify the conditioning signal, pulling samples away from class boundaries and off the data manifold (Fig. 2c and 2d).

(5)
and extends to discrete generative models from the optimization perspective22

While these methods can target specific quality dimensions, they require additional sampling-time forward passes, and the total cost rises to three model evaluations per step if combined with CFG. Furthermore, they rely on heuristic and domain-specific choices for defining 𝒙¯t\bar{{\bm{x}}}_{t} and hth_{t}, such as applying a Gaussian blur with attention mask [21] or utilizing models trained on auxiliary tasks [24].
Our approach.
Our key idea is to contrast the model’s prediction on a real sample with its prediction on a self-generated sample.
This contrast provides a direct model-based signal of the discrepancy between real and self-generated samples, and pushing generation along this direction encourages samples to move toward the data manifold.
Because this signal depends on real samples, it is not available at sampling time, but it arises naturally during training. We therefore incorporate it into the guidance-free training objective so that the model learns to approximate the same guidance effect with a single forward pass at inference time (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, since this formulation relies only on comparing model outputs, it applies broadly across likelihood-based generative models. In this paper, we specifically focus on two representative model types: diffusion and AR architecture.
4 Self-Contrastive Alignment for Likelihood Estimation
In this section, we formalize self-contrastive guidance (SCG) and integrate it into a guidance-free training objective, named Self-Contrastive Alignment for Likelihood Estimation (SCALE).
4.1 Self-Contrastive Guidance
We formulate SCG by contrasting outputs from real and self-generated samples.
Formally, we define this by substituting a pair of real and generated samples (𝒙t,𝒙~t)({\bm{x}}_{t},\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t}) into Eqs. (5) and (6), by setting 𝒙¯t=𝒙~t\bar{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t} and ht=𝒙t−𝒙~th_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t}-\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t} and assuming fθ​(𝒙~t,𝒙t−𝒙~t)=fθ​(𝒙t)f_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t}-\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})=f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}):
fθscg(𝒙t)=fθ(𝒙t)+sscg(fθ(𝒙t)−fθ(𝒙~t)),f^{\text{scg}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})=f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})+s_{\text{scg}}\bigr(f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})-f_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})\bigr),
(7)
where sscgs_{\text{scg}} controls the strength of the SCG.
This formulation applies to a broad class of generative models, including both continuous and discrete modalities.
By the definition of information-based guidance above, Eq. (7) steers the sampling trajectory toward enhancing ht=𝒙t−𝒙~th_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t}-\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t}, locally amplifying the model’s residual contrast between real and self-generated supports. This residual encodes how the model’s prediction on a real sample diverges from its prediction on the self-generated sample, and extrapolating along it pushes the generation toward the high-density region of the data.

We integrate SCG into maximum likelihood training, so that the trained model directly approximates the self-contrastive signal and performs guidance-free sampling at sampling time.

4.3 Practical Implementation
Regularizing SCALE via interpolation.
The analysis above assumes that 𝒙t{\bm{x}}_{t} and 𝒙~t\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} lie on nearby supports so that the prediction fθ​(𝒙~t)f_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}) is well defined and the local density-ratio is informative. In practice, a self-generated sample can be highly degraded under limited model capacity or the stochasticity of early generation steps (t≪Tt\ll T), making direct contrast unstable [26, 25].
To address this, we regularize SCALE training by interpolating the real and self-generated samples into an intermediate support, thereby providing a stable anchor for the guidance.
Concretely, we replace a fraction of patches in 𝒙t{\bm{x}}_{t} with those from 𝒙~t\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}. Given an interpolation ratio λ∈[0,1]\lambda\in[0,1] and a per-patch Bernoulli mask Mλ∼Bernoulli⁡(λ)M_{\lambda}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\lambda), the mixed sample 𝒙~t,λ\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t,\lambda} is defined as

(11)
where ⊙\odot denotes patch-wise multiplication. This preserves local semantic structures of dense visual data and applies uniformly across both continuous and discrete data representations, unlike linear interpolation, which is ill-defined for vector-quantized tokens (see Appendix G.2). Reference choice and training stability.
In Eq. (13), we utilize the unguided reference from the α=1\alpha=1 slice with stop-gradient and PatchMix regularization.
This stabilizes the SCALE training by keeping target dependencies acyclic and reducing the effects of reference displacement. We analyze these in the diffusion setting.
(i) Unguided reference. Stop-gradient applies only to the reference branch; the same network is trained through the prediction branch with α\alpha sampled uniformly from [αmin,1][\alpha_{\min},1]. Assuming the predictor is smooth in α\alpha near 11, training at nearby values also constrains its α=1\alpha=1 slice, whose regression target is fixed by the data. The resulting chain of target definitions is acyclic within each update (data →α=1→α<1\to\alpha=1\to\alpha<1). As the α=1\alpha=1 slice converges, the SCG target stabilizes, and the α<1\alpha<1 slices can converge toward it.
(ii) PatchMix. Interpolation brings the reference closer to the real input on average. For finite-capacity models, PatchMix reduces the contributions from reference displacement to the bounds on SCG target variation and prediction error by a factor of λ\sqrt{\lambda}. These bounds support using mixed samples as more reliable guidance anchors when self-generated samples are degraded. Detailed proofs are provided in Appendix C.

SCALE for conditional generation.
The basic SCALE objective sharpens sample distribution but does not enforce condition alignment, which becomes critical in large-scale visual generation. We therefore integrate CFG into SCALE training, while ensuring the inference fully guidance-free.
Formally, we apply regularized SCG (Eq. 12) on top of the CFG prediction fθcfgf^{\text{cfg}}_{\theta}, which means it directly refines CFG-generated outputs using the self-contrastive signal:
fθscg-cfg​(𝒙t∣c)=fθcfg​(𝒙t∣c)+sscg​(fθcfg​(𝒙t∣c)−fθcfg​(𝒙~t,λ∣c)).\begin{split}f^{\text{{scg-cfg}}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid c)=f_{\theta}^{\text{cfg}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid c)+s_{\text{scg}}(f^{\text{cfg}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid c)-f^{\text{cfg}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t,\lambda}\mid c)).\end{split}
(14)
Following the derivation in Section 4.2, we reformulate this unified target into a learnable form:
fθcfg​(𝒙t|c,α)=α​fθscg-cfg​(𝒙t|c,α)+(1−α)​sg​[fθcfg​(𝒙~t,λ∣c,1)].\begin{split}f_{\theta}^{\text{cfg}}({\bm{x}}_{t}|c,\alpha)={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha f_{\theta}^{\text{{scg-cfg}}}({\bm{x}}_{t}|c,\alpha)+(1-\alpha)\ \text{sg}\left[f_{\theta}^{\text{cfg}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t,\lambda}\mid c,1)\right]}.\end{split}
(15)
Since fθcfgf_{\theta}^{\text{cfg}} on the left-hand side follows ptcfgp_{t}^{\text{cfg}}, it is not directly learnable. We therefore integrate it into the guidance-free training framework for CFG (Eq. (4)), yielding a fully tractable training target:

&\beta\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha f_{\theta}^{\text{{scg-cfg}}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid c,\alpha,\beta)+(1-\alpha)\text{sg}\left[f_{\theta}^{\text{cfg}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t,\lambda}\mid c,1,\beta)\right]}\right\}+(1-\beta)\ \text{sg}[f^{u}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}|\varnothing,1,1)],\end{split}
(16)
where β\beta is a hyperparameter controlling the CFG scale (β=1/(1+scfg)\beta=1/(1+s_{\text{cfg}})).
This formulation seamlessly integrates SCG with CFG, allowing the target trained model fθscg-cfgf_{\theta}^{\text{scg-cfg}} to be directly optimized by replacing the model prediction in Eqs. 1 and 2 with fθc​(𝒙t|c,α,β)f^{c}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}|c,\alpha,\beta).
Setting β=1\beta=1 recovers basic SCALE, while setting α=1\alpha=1 reduces to the GFT [6].
Consequently, the trained model generates samples that incorporate both SCG and CFG in a single forward pass.
In practice, we sample 𝒙~t\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} from the model’s unconditional prediction during training. This adds no extra cost and yields a stable reference, free from both CFG and SCG amplification.

4.4 Comparison with Other Methods
We compare SCALE against related methods; see Appendix F for more related works.
Computational overhead.
As summarized in Section 4.3, the training-time overhead is minimal and VRAM is unchanged relative to CFG training as the extra forward passes introduced by SCALE are computed without gradients.
Since SCALE introduces minimal additional parameters for embedding α\alpha and β\beta, the parameter ratio is unchanged.
We defer the detailed setup and analysis to Appendix G.1.
Versus CFG-mimicking methods.
Prior guidance-free methods train the model to reproduce the CFG distribution ptcfgp_{t}^{\text{cfg}}, so their quality is bounded by CFG itself. SCALE instead amplifies a model-based density ratio, pt​(𝒙t)/pt​(𝒙~t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})/p_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}) for basic SCALE or ptcfg​(𝒙t|c)/ptcfg​(𝒙~t|c)p^{\text{cfg}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}|c)/p^{\text{cfg}}_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}|c) for CFG-augmented SCALE, providing a stronger alignment with the data distribution and superior sample quality.
Versus RL post-training.
RL post-training methods such as DPO [46] and GRPO [52] also use a contrastive signal but pursue a different goal. They align the model to human preferences via preference data or reward models and typically still rely on CFG in the visual domain [60, 38], whereas SCALE aligns to the ground-truth data distribution from the model’s own predictions and is fully guidance-free. The two are therefore complementary, and Section 5.2 shows that DPO [60] on top of SCALE further improves quality while remaining guidance-free.
5 Results
5.1 Experimental Setups
Models, datasets, and metrics.
We evaluate SCALE on class-conditional (C2I) ImageNet [9] at 256×256256\times 256 by fine-tuning DiT-XL/2 [44] and LlamaGen-L [54], and additionally train DiT-B/2 for the analysis. We report FID-50k, IS, Precision, and Recall following Dhariwal and Nichol [11].
For text-to-image generation (T2I) at 512×512512\times 512, we fine-tune Stable Diffusion v1.5 (SD1.5) [49] on LAION-Aesthetics 5+ [51] and report FID-30k, CLIP Score [45], PickScore [29], HPSv2.1 [62], Aesthetic Score [51], and Image Reward [63], complemented by a human preference study. Computational cost is measured by the number of function evaluations (NFE). See Appendix E for more details.
Baseline methods.
We compare SCALE against three groups of baselines, which enhance the base model’s performance in different ways.
Guidance-free methods: We include guidance distillation [41] for diffusion models, CCA [7] for AR models, and GFT [6] for both.
Guided sampling methods: We compare against CFG [20], SAG [21], and discriminator guidance (G++) [27].
For text-to-image, we further compare against RL post-training with DiffusionDPO [60].
We additionally report with representative diffusion models [11, 49, 19], and AR models [14, 66, 32, 57].
Implementation details.
We train all models with the SCALE objective in Eq. (16), introducing only a few additional hyperparameters. For fine-tuning, we limit training to less than 5%5\% of the pre-training budget and inherit the base model’s hyperparameters. We fix the PatchMix ratio at λ=0.2\lambda=0.2 across all experiments. The patch size is matched to each model’s self-attention granularity: 22 for DiT, 11 for LlamaGen, and 22 for SD1.5 (the finest self-attention resolution in its UNet). For diffusion sampling, we use 50-step DPM-Solver++ [40].
See Appendix E for the full setup.

5.3 Analysis on SCALE
Effect of varying α\alpha and β\beta.
As noted in Section 4.3, SCALE provides inference-time flexibility for controlling the sample quality by varying α\alpha and β\beta.
Fig. 6(a) visualizes their effects.
Decreasing β\beta improves fidelity by amplifying the conditional signal, but stronger CFG pushes samples toward canonical templates and can change their semantic structure. Decreasing α\alpha instead targets fidelity directly while preserving semantic structure, though it may fall short when samples lie far from the data manifold (first row of Fig. 6(a)). More detailed visual analysis is provided in Appendix H.
Combination of SCG and CFG.
Aforementioned analysis suggests that SCG and CFG are complementary to each other. Therefore, we combine SCG on top of the CFG prediction, which directly rectifies the CFG-generated samples toward the data manifold. This compensates for the limitation of each individual guidance: moderate CFG enhances prompt alignment and rectifies the underlying semantic structure, while SCG improves the sample quality on top of the CFG prediction. Utilizing CFG alone with a higher guidance scale may push samples away from the data manifold or incur reduced diversity as discussed in Section 3.
Notably, SCALE reduces to GFT [6] at α=1\alpha=1, which only approximates the CFG distribution. Fig. 6(b) shows that lowering α\alpha consistently improves FID and IS over this baseline.
SCALE thus serves as a plug-and-play framework for improving guidance-free generation that maintains baseline performance rather than just adding complexity.
Training SCALE from scratch.
Unlike most guidance-free methods that require a CFG-trained model [41, 7], SCALE can be trained from scratch as it does not require a pretrained teacher model. Due to resource constraints, we verify this on DiT-B/2 [44], where SCALE significantly improves guidance-free generation (Table 5). Scaling to larger models is left as future work.

6 Conclusion
We introduce SCALE, a guidance-free training framework built on self-contrastive guidance (SCG), a signal derived from the model’s own predictions on real and self-generated samples.
Grounded in the information-based guidance theorem, SCG directly pushes the generation process toward the true data manifold.
Extensive experiments confirm that SCALE outperforms existing guidance-free baselines on both diffusion and AR models, and show complementary performance gain when combined with human preference-based fine-tuning.
Limitations.
SCALE’s nested guidance formulation introduces additional training overhead. Extensions to broader visual alignment tasks, post-training beyond DPO, and larger, more recent generative architectures remain open directions. See Appendix I for further discussion.

Appendix A Derivation of Generalized Information-Based Guidance for Discrete Generative Models
In this section, we derive the optimization-perspective formulation of information-based guidance for discrete generative models in Eq. (6), following the framework of Tang et al. [15] and Hur et al. [24].
Let 𝒙¯t\bar{\bm{x}}_{t} denote a degraded sample that lacks some salient information hth_{t} present in the true sample 𝒙t{\bm{x}}_{t}, such that the data distribution decomposes as p⁡(𝒙t)=p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)p({\bm{x}}_{t})=p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t}).
Generalized information-based guidance recovers 𝒙t{\bm{x}}_{t} from 𝒙¯t\bar{\bm{x}}_{t} by jointly maximizing the joint likelihood and amplifying the correlation between 𝒙¯t\bar{\bm{x}}_{t} and hth_{t}, captured by p⁡(ht∣𝒙¯t)p(h_{t}\mid\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}):
arg​max𝐱¯t⁡[log⁡p⁡(𝐱¯t,ht)+s​log⁡p⁡(ht∣𝐱¯t)],\argmax_{\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}}\bigl[\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})+s\,\log p(h_{t}\mid\bar{\bm{x}}_{t})\bigr],
(17)
where s≥0s\geq 0 is the guidance scale that controls the strength of information amplification.
Applying the chain rule of probability p⁡(ht∣𝒙¯t)=p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)/p⁡(𝒙¯t)p(h_{t}\mid\bar{\bm{x}}_{t})=p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})/p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}), the conditional log-density expands as log⁡p⁡(ht∣𝒙¯t)=log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)−log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t)\log p(h_{t}\mid\bar{\bm{x}}_{t})=\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})-\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}). Substituting into Eq. (17) yields
arg​max𝐱¯t⁡[log⁡p⁡(𝐱¯t,ht)+s⁡(log⁡p⁡(𝐱¯t,ht)−log⁡p⁡(𝐱¯t))],\argmax_{\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}}\bigl[\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})+s\bigl(\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})-\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t})\bigr)\bigr],
(18)
which recovers Eq. (6) in the main text. This formulation explicitly amplifies the pointwise mutual information log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)−log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t)=log⁡p⁡(ht∣𝒙¯t)\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})-\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t})=\log p(h_{t}\mid\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}) by a factor of ss on top of the base log-density log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t|ht)=log⁡p⁡(𝒙t)\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}|h_{t})=\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}).
Connection to Hur et al. [24].
Hur et al. [24] formulate the same objective with the conditional p⁡(𝒙¯t∣ht)p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}\mid h_{t}) in place of our joint p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t}). Since log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t∣ht)=log⁡p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)−log⁡p⁡(ht)\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}\mid h_{t})=\log p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})-\log p(h_{t}) and log⁡p⁡(ht)\log p(h_{t}) does not depend on 𝒙¯t\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}, the two formulations differ only by an additive constant inside the arg​max\argmax when hth_{t} is held fixed, and therefore lead to identical optima. We adopt the joint form because it avoids treating hth_{t} as a freely defined random variable, which the conditional p⁡(𝒙¯t∣ht)p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}\mid h_{t}) requires. Setting p⁡(𝒙¯t,ht)=p⁡(𝒙t)p(\bar{\bm{x}}_{t},h_{t})=p({\bm{x}}_{t}) instead lets us work directly with the data distribution at hand without specifying a separate distribution over hth_{t}.

Here 𝒙~t=gθ∗​(𝒙t,ϵ)\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=g_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}), and the expectation is over ϵ|𝒙t\bm{\epsilon}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}. By the assumed score accuracy at the reference inputs, ϵθ∗​(𝒙~t)=−σt​∇𝒙~t​log⁡pt​(𝒙~t)\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta^{*}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})=-\sigma_{t}\nabla_{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}). Substituting this identity inside the expectation and solving for the SCG branch fθ∗scg​(𝒙t∣α)f^{\text{scg}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha) gives
fθ∗scg​(𝒙t∣α)\displaystyle f^{\text{scg}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)
=1α​[−σt​∇𝒙t​log⁡pt​(𝒙t)−(1−α)​𝔼​[ϵθ∗​(𝒙~t)∣𝒙t]]\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigl[-\sigma_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-(1-\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta^{*}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}\bigr]\Bigr]
(26)

(27)
This form coincides with Eq. (10), recovering the conditional mean SCG prediction. ∎
Proof for autoregressive models.
We now derive the autoregressive (AR) counterpart, where the optimization directly works in the probability domain.
For AR models, fθf_{\theta} is a next-token predictor that outputs a categorical distribution over the token vocabulary at each generation step.
To match the diffusion notation, we write 𝒙t{\bm{x}}_{t} for the next-token target at position tt given 𝒙<t{\bm{x}}_{<t}.
Concretely, the next-token logit is ℓθ​(𝒙t∣α)\ell_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha) over the token vocabulary, and the predicted probability is fθ​(𝒙t∣α)=softmax⁡(ℓθ​(𝒙t∣α))f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(\ell_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)\bigr).
Under the cross-entropy objective in Eq. (2), the optimal next-token predictor converges to the true next-token conditional:
fθ∗​(𝒙t∣α)=pt​(𝒙t),i.e., ​ℓθ∗​(𝒙t∣α)=log⁡pt​(𝒙t)+Z⁡(α),f_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)=p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),\qquad\text{i.e., }\ell_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)=\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+Z(\alpha),
(28)
for all α\alpha in the training distribution, where pt​(𝒙t)p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}) refers to the true next-token distribution at position tt given 𝒙<t{\bm{x}}_{<t} and Z⁡(α)Z(\alpha) is a vocabulary-wide additive constant absorbed by the softmax normalization.
By the SCALE training objective, the trained logit is parameterized as the interpolation between the SCG branch and the self-generated branch:
ℓθ​(𝒙t∣α)=α​ℓθscg​(𝒙t∣α)+(1−α)​sg​[ℓθ​(𝒙~t∣1)],\ell_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)=\alpha\,\ell^{\text{scg}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)+(1-\alpha)\,\text{sg}\bigl[\ell_{\theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}\mid 1)\bigr],
(29)
where the self-generated token 𝒙~t\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} is drawn from the unconditional next-token distribution fθ(⋅∣1)f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid 1) via standard token-wise sampling.
At α=1\alpha=1, the SCG branch reduces to the unconditional next-token prediction, so ℓθ∗​(𝒙~t∣1)=log⁡pt​(𝒙~t)+Z⁡(1)\ell_{\theta^{*}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t}\mid 1)=\log p_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})+Z(1) at convergence.
Substituting the convergence condition into the SCALE parameterization and solving for the SCG branch:
α​ℓθ∗scg​(𝒙t∣α)+(1−α)​[log⁡pt​(𝒙~t)+Z⁡(1)]=log⁡pt​(𝒙t)+Z⁡(α),\displaystyle\alpha\,\ell^{\text{scg}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)+(1-\alpha)\bigl[\log p_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})+Z(1)\bigr]=\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+Z(\alpha),
(30)
ℓθ∗scg​(𝒙t∣α)=1α​log⁡pt​(𝒙t)−(1α−1)​log⁡pt​(𝒙~t)+Z′,\displaystyle\ell^{\text{scg}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\Bigl(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\Bigr)\log p_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})+Z^{\prime},
(31)
where Z′Z^{\prime} denotes the sum of additive constants.
With α=1/(1+sscg)\alpha=1/(1+s_{\text{scg}}), applying softmax to Eq. (31) yields
fθ∗scg​(𝒙t∣α)∝pt​(𝒙t)​[pt​(𝒙t)pt​(𝒙~t)]sscg,f^{\text{scg}}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\alpha)\;\propto\;p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\left[\frac{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{p_{t}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t})}\right]^{s_{\text{scg}}},
(32)
which exactly matches the SCG-induced next-token distribution obtained by substituting 𝒙¯t=𝒙~t\bar{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} and ht=𝒙t−𝒙~th_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t}-\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} into the discrete information-based guidance objective in Eq. (6). The SCALE-trained AR model therefore recovers the SCG distribution at convergence in the probability domain, mirroring the score-domain result for diffusion. ∎
Appendix C Analysis of the SCALE Training
The use of a self-generated reference in Eq. (9) makes the SCALE training target dependent on the model’s current predictions. We examine how this target evolves under stop-gradient updates and how prediction errors propagate through the PatchMix reference. The analysis is presented for diffusion models, where fθf_{\theta} denotes the noise predictor and ϵ\epsilon the ground-truth noise. We fix tt and α∈[αmin,1]\alpha\in[\alpha_{\min},1] with αmin>0\alpha_{\min}>0; conditional expectations average over ϵ|xt\epsilon\mid x_{t} and an independent PatchMix mask.
C.1 Training with Stop-Gradient and PatchMix
Across training iterations.
Within each update, the SCALE training target uses a frozen reference. Under stop-gradient in Eq. (9), each training step follows the target-network iteration
θk+1←θk−μ​∇θ∥ϵ−α​fθscg​(xt∣α)⏟optimization target−(1−α)​fθk​(x~t,λ∣1)⏟frozen reference(stop-gradient)∥2.\theta_{k+1}\leftarrow\theta_{k}-\mu\nabla_{\theta}\lVert\epsilon-\alpha\underbrace{f^{\text{scg}}_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid\alpha)}_{\text{optimization target}}-(1{-}\alpha)\underbrace{f_{\theta_{k}}(\tilde{x}_{t,\lambda}\mid 1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{frozen reference}\\
\text{(stop-gradient)}\end{subarray}}\rVert^{2}.
(33)
Each iteration applies a gradient step to a regression loss with a frozen reference, evaluated at θ=θk\theta=\theta_{k}. Both x~t,λ\tilde{x}_{t,\lambda} and the prediction at that input are obtained from the α=1\alpha{=}1 slice. At α=1\alpha=1, the objective reduces to standard noise regression toward the fixed, model-independent target fθ∗(xt∣1)=−σt∇logpt(xt)f_{\theta^{\ast}}(x_{t}\mid 1)=-\sigma_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t}). Within each update, the resulting reference dependency chain is acyclic (data →α=1→α<1\to\alpha{=}1\to\alpha{<}1); the target depends on the model but is anchored to the data distribution through the base slice’s fixed noise-regression target. Since the slices share parameters, guided-slice updates can still change the base slice across iterations.
Each gradient step.
The reference evolves across successive model snapshots (θk→θk+1\theta_{k}\to\theta_{k+1}), inducing a moving target for the α<1\alpha{<}1 slices. The target variation is bounded by the base-slice change and vanishes as this change tends to zero. Rearranging the loss in Eq. (33) gives a sample target for the SCG branch. We denote its conditional mean given xtx_{t} by T⁡[θk]T[\theta_{k}], where θk\theta_{k} specifies the fixed model snapshot used to construct and evaluate the reference:
T[θk](xt,α)=1α(−σt∇logpt(xt))−(1α−1)𝔼[fθk(x~t,λ∣1)∣xt],T[\theta_{k}](x_{t},\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\bigl(-\sigma_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\bigr)-\bigl(\frac{1}{\alpha}{-}1\bigr)\mathbb{E}\bigl[f_{\theta_{k}}(\tilde{x}_{t,\lambda}\mid 1)\mid x_{t}\bigr],
(34)
so a base-slice change Δf:=fθk+1(⋅∣1)−fθk(⋅∣1)\Delta f:=f_{\theta_{k+1}}(\cdot\mid 1)-f_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot\mid 1) enters only through the last term. With a uniform change bound ∥Δ​f∥\lVert\Delta f\rVert and a common Lipschitz constant LL, we bound its effect through two contributions. First, the prediction at the reference input x~t,λ\tilde{x}_{t,\lambda} itself changes, contributing 1−αα​∥Δ​f∥\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\lVert\Delta f\rVert. Second, the base prediction also changes the generated sample x~t=xt+σt​(ϵ−fθk​(xt∣1))\tilde{x}_{t}=x_{t}+\sigma_{t}(\epsilon-f_{\theta_{k}}(x_{t}\mid 1)), shifting the reference input x~t,λ\tilde{x}_{t,\lambda}. PatchMix reduces the displacement bound from σt​∥Δ​f∥\sigma_{t}\lVert\Delta f\rVert to λ​σt​∥Δ​f∥\sqrt{\lambda}\,\sigma_{t}\lVert\Delta f\rVert in RMS. Feeding the shifted reference into the LL-Lipschitz base predictor changes its output, giving a second contribution of at most 1−αα​λ​σt​L​∥Δ​f∥\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\sqrt{\lambda}\,\sigma_{t}L\lVert\Delta f\rVert. Together,
∥Δ​T∥≤κ⁡∥Δ​f∥,κ=1−αα​(1+λ​σt​L).\lVert\Delta T\rVert\leq\kappa\,\lVert\Delta f\rVert,\qquad\kappa=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}(1+\sqrt{\lambda}\,\sigma_{t}L).
(35)
If the base slice converges uniformly to fθ∗(⋅∣1)f_{\theta^{\ast}}(\cdot\mid 1), then ∥Δ​f∥→0\lVert\Delta f\rVert\to 0 and hence ∥Δ​T∥→0\lVert\Delta T\rVert\to 0. The bound also gives T⁡[θk]→T⁡[θ∗]T[\theta_{k}]\to T[\theta^{\ast}], so the moving target settles to a fixed limit. The α<1\alpha{<}1 slices converge to it if their regression error relative to T⁡[θk]T[\theta_{k}] also vanishes.

Appendix E Further Implementation Details
In this section, we provide more detailed implementation details for clarity. As denoted in the main manuscript, we explicitly follow the training recipe from the base generative models and hyperparameters [6]. For the fine-tuning, the training epoch is set to 1 5% of the pre-training.
We elaborate on the detailed training configurations used for SCALE training.
Input of α,β\alpha,\beta.
Following implementation in GFT [6], we use Fourier embedding with MLP layers [49] to add conditioning for the models. For the fine-tuning, we adopt zero initialization for the conditioning network [69]. With this formulation, the additional embeddings for the α\alpha and β\beta provide a lightweight (0.0012%) parameter overhead, and negligible impact on inference latency.
PatchMix patch size.
We set the PatchMix patch size in Eq. (11) to align with the model’s self-attention granularity, so that each mixed patch respects the unit at which the model processes spatial information. For Transformer-based models with an explicit patch tokenizer, this is the token/patch size of the input: 2 for DiT-XL/2 and DiT-B/2 [44] (latent patches of size 2), and 1 for LlamaGen-L [54] (one VQ token per spatial location). For UNet-based models such as SD 1.5 [49], which contain multiple self-attention resolutions, we use the finest self-attention resolution in the latent space, corresponding to a patch size of 2.
Training and sampling.
For fine-tuning, we limit training to less than 5%5\% of the pre-training budget and inherit all hyperparameters from the base model. For training from scratch, we follow the original training recipe of the base model. For diffusion sampling, we use 50-step DPM-Solver++ [40]. For text-to-image, we additionally use LoRA [50] due to compute constraints (full setup in the SD 1.5 paragraph below).
In our experiments, we found that combining CFG with SCG yields the best performance, as it provides an orthogonal guidance direction (see 5.3). To ensure that the model focuses on the effective range of guidance strengths, we set a minimum α\alpha of 0.5 for class-conditional models and 0.25 for text-to-image models during training, which corresponds to setting the maximum SCG strength as 1.0 and 3.0, respectively.
E.1 Fine-tuning
LlamaGen
We utilize LlamaGen-L for fine-tuning AR models with SCALE. We fine-tune these models with 15 epochs with a batch size of 256. The learning rate is set to 2e-4 with cosine decay. All pre-trained models are obtained from the official repository [54]. We obtain 𝒙~t\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} by categorical sampling from the unconditional logits, without temperature scaling or top-kk truncation, so that 𝒙~t\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} is drawn directly from the model’s distribution.
DiT
For the main experiments and ablation studies, we fine-tune DiT-B/2 and DiT-XL/2 with SCALE using a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of 1e-4 with cosine decay. The EMA decay rate is set to 0.9999.
We observed that initializing from a pretrained from GFT objective model yields slightly better performance. We posit that incorporating self-contrastive guidance subsequent to CFG training is advantageous.
For sampling, we adopt 50-step DPM-Solver++ [40].
Stable Diffusion v1.5 (Text-to-Image)
For text-to-image generation at 512×512512\times 512 resolution, we fine-tune Stable Diffusion v1.5 (SD 1.5) [49] with SCALE on LAION-Aesthetics 5+ [51], which contains 18M text-image pairs. Due to compute constraints, we use LoRA [23] with rank r=128r=128 and scaling factor 128128, trained for 50k iterations using a batch size of 256, a learning rate of 1​e−51\text{e}{-5}, and 1000 warmup steps. We initialize from a GFT fine-tuned SD 1.5 checkpoint and apply SCALE on top, which compensates for the limited capacity of LoRA. For evaluation, zero-shot FID-30k, CLIP Score [45], and Aesthetic Score [51] are measured on 30k images generated from COCO 2014 [36] validation prompts, while the human-preference metrics including PickScore [29], HPSv2.1 [62], and Image Reward [63] are measured on the Pick-a-Pic test prompts [29]. For all quantitative and qualitative results, we utilize 50-step DPM-Solver++ [40] for sampling.

This mirrors the standard practice in language-model alignment, where DPO training is performed with sampling temperature 1.01.0 and the temperature is lowered only at inference [46, 60]. Training GFT with DPO follows the same practice, setting β=1\beta=1 for the DPO training and a lower β\beta at inference, keeping other training hyperparameters unchanged, such as LoRA rank, learning rate, and effective batch size.
E.2 Training From Scratch
Due to the computational resource limitations within an academic research setting, pre-training with large-scale visual generative models was not feasible. In this paper, to demonstrate SCALE’s applicability to pre-training, we primarily conduct experiments using DiT-B/2 models [44]. This model was selected as it requires moderate training resources, requiring 80 epochs (400k iterations with a batch size of 256) for training and taking ∼3\sim 3 days with 8 RTX4090 GPUs. For the pre-training, we drop conditions for the generated sample branch of SCALE.
In this case, 𝒙¯t=𝒙~t\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}=\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} and the hth_{t} is a combination of 𝒙t−𝒙~t{\bm{x}}_{t}-\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t} and the condition cc for the information-based guidance formulation in Eqs. (5) and (6).
By omitting the conditional signal, the objective initially emulates a CFG, which effectively stabilizes the model before the self-contrastive signal becomes reliable. This approach facilitates a natural curriculum, where the model first learns CFG signals and seamlessly transitions to incorporating self-contrastive signals as generation quality matures.
E.3 Human Preference Study
We complement the automated human preference metrics on SD 1.5 with a user study that directly compares SCALE against the strongest guidance-free baseline (GFT) in a side-by-side setting.
The study is conducted as a blind A/B test with 20 participants, each responding to 15 image pairs for a total of 300 votes. For each trial, the participant is shown one text prompt and two generated images (A and B), one from GFT and one from SCALE, with model identities hidden and the left/right order randomized per trial. The participant is asked to select the image they prefer overall, or to choose “Tie” if the two are of comparable quality. Prompts are sampled from the Pick-a-Pic test set so that the comparison covers diverse content. An example trial is shown in Fig. 7.

Appendix F Related Works
F.1 Visual Generative Models
Likelihood-based visual generative models have been actively researched along several architectural lines. Diffusion models [18, 53, 43, 49, 44, 42] learn the data score and generate samples through iterative denoising. Autoregressive models [14, 54, 57, 47, 55, 34, 16, 68] factorize the joint distribution into a sequence of next-token predictions over discrete or continuous tokens. Masked generative models [4, 33, 3, 61] predict masked tokens in parallel and trade autoregressive ordering for faster sampling. Flow matching models [39, 37, 13, 31] learn a velocity field between noise and data and have recently scaled to large text-to-image systems such as Stable Diffusion 3 [13] and FLUX [31]. Despite their architectural differences, all of these families produce samples whose unconditional fidelity falls short of the training distribution and rely on additional guidance signals to close the gap.
F.2 Contrastive Signals for High-Fidelity Visual Generation
A broad range of methods enhances sample fidelity by leveraging a contrastive signal that distinguishes desired samples from undesired ones.
Information-based guidance.
Classifier-free guidance (CFG) [20] contrasts a conditional and an unconditional prediction at sampling time, amplifying the conditional signal.
Information-based guidance [21, 24] generalizes CFG in terms of model-based interpretation, constructing the undesired sample from a degraded version of the input itself. From this perspective, CFG is a special case where the degradation is dropping the condition. Formally, these methods amplify any salient information hth_{t} relative to a degraded reference 𝒙¯t\bar{\bm{x}}_{t}.
Self-Attention Guidance (SAG) [21] utilized a Gaussian blur to amplify high-frequency details of the input, with a self-attention mask to stabilize the guidance. Self-Guidance [24] utilizes an auxiliary model to produce a degraded reference input for the semantic vector-quantized tokens [48, 14]. While effective, these methods require additional forward passes and rely on heuristics for constructing degraded inputs, which are often domain-specific and hard to generalize to other domains.
Weak-model guidance.
These methods leverage a weaker version of the model itself to produce undesired samples.
These methods generalize CFG in terms of model-based interpretation by replacing the unconditional reference with a weaker version of the model itself: autoguidance [26] contrasts the main model with a smaller or earlier checkpoint, Perturbed Attention Guidance [1] and Smoothed Energy Guidance [22] construct weak references through attention perturbation, and S2S^{2}-guidance [5] contrasts with random subnetworks of the main model, constructed by stochastic block-dropping.
Guidance-free generation.
Guidance-free methods incorporate CFG during training to enable sampling with a single model evaluation per step.
Guidance distillation [41] trains a student to match the guided predictions of a pretrained diffusion teacher.
Condition Contrastive Alignment (CCA) [7] fine-tunes AR models using matched and mismatched image–condition pairs and a frozen pretrained reference model, with a contrastive alignment objective whose optimum recovers the CFG sampling distribution.
Guidance-Free Training (GFT) [6] retains the standard likelihood objective and reparameterizes the conditional model output as an interpolation between the sampling and unconditional predictions. This enables guidance-free training from scratch across diffusion, AR, and masked generative models.
Model-guidance (MG) [56] similarly learns CFG during training, but adds a stop-gradient CFG residual directly to the regression target rather than reparameterizing the model output.
These approaches derive their guidance signal from the conditional–unconditional contrast of CFG. SCALE introduces a complementary contrast between real and self-generated samples and learns it alongside CFG, aiming to improve generation beyond reproducing CFG behavior.
Reinforcement learning-based methods.
Developed from the language model alignment literature, reinforcement learning (RL) post-training methods often construct a desired and undesired contrast through reward or preference signals.
Direct preference optimization (DPO) [46, 60, 71, 65, 35, 10] optimizes the policy directly on paired win–lose paired preference data. In this case, the model is to maximize the win probability over the lose probability, which implicitly encourages the model to generate samples that are preferred by the human.
Recently, group relative policy optimization (GRPO) [52, 38, 64, 17] samples a group of responses for each prompt and uses their reward statistics within the group as a critic-free baseline, optimizing the model with the resulting relative advantages.
While effective, these methods require an external network or curated preference data and typically still rely on CFG at inference for generating high-quality samples in the visual domain.

We unpack each row of Section 4.3 below.
Train time / step.
The relative cost is determined by the number of model evaluations per gradient step.
Guidance distillation [41] evaluates the teacher’s conditional and unconditional predictions in addition to the student’s forward pass, yielding a ×1.12\times 1.12 overhead.

Effect of varying β\beta (implicit CFG).
By decreasing β\beta, the sample quality is improved by amplifying the conditional signal. However, it often pushes samples toward canonical templates and the semantic structure of samples can be steeply changed as CFG becomes stronger, as shown in each column of the image grid in Fig. 9 and Fig. 10. Consequently, strong CFG (i.e., lower β\beta) produces samples with reduced diversity as shown in the bottom row of each image grid.
Effect of varying α\alpha (implicit SCG).
By decreasing α\alpha, the sample quality is improved by amplifying the self-contrastive signal. It also improves the sample quality, but preserves the semantic structure as shown in each row of the image grid in Fig. 9 and Fig. 10.
As a result, strong SCG (i.e., lower α\alpha) produces more diverse high-quality samples as shown in the rightmost column of each image grid. However, limitations exist. Since SCG improves the sample quality while preserving the semantic structure, it may not be able to produce high-quality samples if the unguided sample is far from the data manifold, as shown in the top row of each image grid.
Combination of SCG and CFG.
Aforementioned analysis suggests that SCG and CFG are complementary to each other. Therefore, we combine SCG on top of the CFG prediction, which directly rectifies the CFG-generated samples to the data manifold. This compensates for the limitation of each individual guidance: moderate CFG enhances prompt alignment and rectifies the underlying semantic structure, while SCG improves the sample quality on top of the CFG prediction. Utilizing CFG alone with a higher guidance scale may push samples away from the data manifold or incur reduced diversity as discussed in Section 3.

Appendix I Limitations and Future Work
While SCALE provides consistent gains across both class-conditional and text-to-image generation on diffusion and AR architectures, several questions remain open.
Unified guidance formulation.
The current SCALE framework is built upon the hierarchical guidance formulation, which combines the CFG and the SCG.
This two-step formulation is effective by combining the benefits of each individual guidance as discussed in Appendix H, and provides an interpretable guidance signal.
However, this approach inevitably introduces additional training overhead (+6%​p+6\%\text{p} per step compared to CFG). A unified, single-step guidance formulation to further improve the guidance-free training efficiency could be a promising future direction.
Extension to visual alignment tasks.
The SCALE framework currently focuses on improving the sample quality of visual generative models without guidance. However, self-contrastive signals are applicable to broader domains, such as visual alignment tasks. For instance, large visual generative models can generate unsafe, NSFW samples, which can be harmful to society. Self-contrastive signals can be used to pull the models toward the distribution of safe data while pushing it away from unsafe, NSFW distributions, without any guidance at sampling time and without loss of sample quality.
Beyond DPO post-training.
We empirically verify that SCALE is complementary to DiffusionDPO [60] by post-training a SCALE-trained model with DPO. However, our investigation is currently limited to a preliminary combination with DPO, and a systematic study of how SCALE interacts with RL-based post-training remains an open question. In particular, validating the compatibility of SCALE with more recent RL-based post-training methods, such as DSPO [71], D3PO [65], SPO [35], FlowGRPO [38], or DanceGRPO [64], would be a valuable direction for future work.
Scaling to larger and more recent generative architectures.
Our experiments cover diffusion models (DiT, SD 1.5) and AR models (LlamaGen) on academic-scale compute. Recent text-to-image models adopt rectified-flow transformers and a billion-scale parameter regime, including Stable Diffusion 3 [13] and FLUX [31]. Validating that the self-contrastive signal scales to these flow-based, large-scale models would be a promising direction.
Appendix J Ethics Statement
SCALE is a technical framework for improving the visual fidelity of guidance-free generative models.
While SCALE can enhance sample quality and align outputs more closely with learned data distributions without relying on external classifier guidance, the same capabilities could be misused by unauthorized actors. Potential risks include the generation of more convincing, deceptive, or misleading visual media, with downstream harms to privacy, reputation, and public trust. Because SCALE often operates as a post-training method on pretrained backbones while sharing the training datasets, it inherits both the capabilities and the limitations of the underlying models, including any biases, memorization effects, or harmful content patterns present in their training data.
Our experiments are conducted exclusively on publicly available, well-established generative models that ship with safety filters and community-vetted usage policies. SCALE itself is not a safety mechanism; it modifies the sampling trajectory to improve fidelity but does not detect, suppress, or mitigate unsafe content. It should therefore be deployed only in conjunction with robust prompt- and output-level moderation, provenance signals (e.g., watermarking or content credentials) where appropriate, and human oversight for sensitive applications.
This work is intended for academic research and constructive applications such as creative tools, scientific visualization, and the study of guidance mechanisms in diffusion and autoregressive generative models. We explicitly prohibit malicious or unethical use, including the generation of non-consensual imagery, impersonation, disinformation, or any content intended to cause harm to individuals or groups. We encourage practitioners adopting SCALE to carefully document their assumptions and design choices, adhere to the licenses and safety settings of the underlying models, and contribute to the development of clear ethical guidelines, so as to support the responsible advancement of guidance-free generation methods and the broader generative modeling community.

Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
Answer: [No]
Justification: We do not include the error bars for the experiments. However, most of the reported metrics are computed over a large number of samples (50,000 for class-conditional
generation and 30,000 for text-to-image generation), following the standard evaluation protocol, which is known to be statistically stable accross different runs.
Guidelines:
•
The answer [N/A]  means that the paper does not include experiments.
•
The authors should answer [Yes]  if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.
•
The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given experimental conditions).
•
The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula, call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
•
The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
•
It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of the mean.

It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis of Normality of errors is not verified.
•

•
If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.

Broader impacts
Question: Does the paper discuss both potential positive societal impacts and negative societal impacts of the work performed?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We discuss the societal impacts of the proposed method in the appendix.
Guidelines:
•
The answer [N/A]  means that there is no societal impact of the work performed.
•
If the authors answer [N/A]  or [No] , they should explain why their work has no societal impact or why the paper does not address societal impact.
•
Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.
•
The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.
•
The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.
•
If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).

Safeguards
Question: Does the paper describe safeguards that have been put in place for responsible release of data or models that have a high risk for misuse (e.g., pre-trained language models, image generators, or scraped datasets)?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Our work utilizes publicly released large pretrained visual generative model, Stable Diffusion 1.5 [49]. We use the model through the official diffusers library, which provides a built-in safety checker that filters NSFW and otherwise unsafe outputs by default. We do not release any large pretrained model that could be potentially misused. We only release the fine-tuning code and model weights that build upon existing checkpoints and publicly available datasets, and inherit the license and safety settings of the underlying models. While a pretrained model is involved (DiT-B/2 [44]), it poses low risk of misuse as it is relatively small and trained on publicly available, non-sensitive data (ImageNet [9]).
Guidelines:
•
The answer [N/A]  means that the paper poses no such risks.
•
Released models that have a high risk for misuse or dual-use should be released with necessary safeguards to allow for controlled use of the model, for example by requiring that users adhere to usage guidelines or restrictions to access the model or implementing safety filters.
•
Datasets that have been scraped from the Internet could pose safety risks. The authors should describe how they avoided releasing unsafe images.
•
We recognize that providing effective safeguards is challenging, and many papers do not require this, but we encourage authors to take this into account and make a best faith effort.
