Abstract
Flow-based policies such as π0\pi_{0} are trained on a fixed set of embodiments, and transferring them to new ones is an open problem.
To adapt a frozen π0\pi_{0} to a new embodiment without additional demonstrations or parameter updates, we train a lightweight embodiment classifier to distinguish successful action chunks on the source body from those on the target body, using a few calibration tasks.
On transfer tasks that π0\pi_{0} saw on its source body but the classifier was never trained on, we nudge each denoising step of π0\pi_{0} with the classifier’s gradient, which estimates the direction from the distribution of successful actions on the source body toward that on the target body.
On the LIBERO simulation benchmark with four modified controllers, embodiment-classifier guidance raises π0\pi_{0}’s average success on the target bodies from 72% to 80% across 12 suite–controller pairs, with a 31% relative reduction in failures (11 of 12 gain; 5 significant after Holm correction).
In simulation, guidance is data-efficient, needing few target-body rollouts per task, which makes it promising for sim-to-real transfer, where rollouts are costly.
Guidance is mixed on weak wrists and harmful on a locked wrist, when the classifier lacks data or favors infeasible motions.
Collecting more target-body data with guided π0\pi_{0} can fill such gaps: on one weak-wrist arm, guided π0\pi_{0} solved a task on which unguided π0\pi_{0} had zero successes in the original data, and retraining the classifier on the guided successes raised success from 52.5% to 70.7%.
Keywords: VLA, flow matching, classifier guidance, embodiment transfer

1 Introduction
Flow-based VLA policies such as π0\pi_{0} [2] are trained on a fixed set of robot bodies (an arm together with its low-level controller), and deploying them on new ones remains an open challenge.
On a body with different dynamics (a softer controller, command latency, a weaker wrist motor, another arm), the same policy can fail even when the scene and the task are unchanged (Fig. 1).
Such gaps separate simulation from the lab and the lab from the field; closing them usually takes target-body demonstrations and fine-tuning [11] or a per-task steering policy learned from reward [24].
Background. π0\pi_{0} maps an observation (camera images, robot state and a language instruction) to an action chunk: the next 50 actions of 7 numbers each, which together form a single point in a 350-dimensional space.
Like an image diffusion model, it generates this point by sampling: starting from Gaussian noise, a learned velocity field moves it toward a plausible chunk over 10 denoising steps (Fig. 1, middle).
The robot executes only the first 5 actions of each chunk, discards the rest, and samples a new chunk from the next observation.
Approach. We instead change only how a frozen flow policy samples.
π0\pi_{0} has no input that says which body it controls, so it samples chunks that suit its source body.
We train a small embodiment classifier to tell π0\pi_{0}’s successful action chunks on the source body from those on the target body, and add its gradient to π0\pi_{0}’s velocity at every denoising step.
Intuitively, the classifier scores how much a noisy chunk looks like a target-body success, so its gradient points toward chunks that work on the target body, while π0\pi_{0}’s velocity field still provides the task-level plan, such as which object to reach and where to place it.
As we derive in Eq. (2), with an exact classifier this gradient, suitably scaled, is exactly the velocity difference between the two bodies’ distributions of successful chunks; under the assumptions in App. B, guided sampling would then draw from the target body’s successes, and we hypothesize that these assumptions hold approximately in practice.
Relation to prior work. Similar classifier guidance has been used to refine image diffusion models [14] and to adapt pretrained diffusion models to data-scarce target domains [21]; we apply it to robot embodiment transfer, with the body as the classifier’s label. Unlike embodiment-aware guidance from a known controller’s tracking cost [9], our guidance is learned from successful rollouts.
Because the classifier sees no images, instruction or task label, only noisy action chunks (which implicitly carry the end-effector pose; Sec. 2), guidance with a classifier trained on a few calibration tasks can improve π0\pi_{0}’s target-body success on other transfer tasks, which the classifier was never trained on. π0\pi_{0} itself was trained on these tasks with its source body, so our tests measure transfer across robot dynamics, not generalization to new tasks.
LIBERO [18] is a simulated benchmark of tabletop manipulation tasks in four suites. Our contributions, evaluated on it with one π0\pi_{0} checkpoint and paired, pre-registered tests, are:
•
Embodiment-classifier guidance: a way to steer a frozen flow VLA to a new body with a classifier learned from successful rollouts, with no demonstrations or fine-tuning (Sec. 2).
•
Guidance improves frozen π0\pi_{0} on most controller changes: with four modified controllers, it raises average success from 72% to 80% over 12 suite–body pairs, with a 31% relative reduction in failures (gains in 11 of 12; Sec. 4.1).
•
Embodiment-classifier guidance is data-efficient in simulation: the classifier trains on the successful episodes among about 23 target-body rollouts per calibration task on LIBERO-Object (pick-and-place), with no demonstrations, reward model or updates to π0\pi_{0}. In a preliminary check on one body, an untuned LoRA fine-tune with the same collection budget lowered success (Sec. 4.3).
•
It is not a universal fix: it is mixed on weak-wrist arms and hurts where the body cannot execute π0\pi_{0}’s plans (Sec. 4.2).
•
Guided rollouts fill data gaps: collecting more target-body data with guided π0\pi_{0} finds successes on a task where unguided π0\pi_{0} had none, and retraining the classifier on the guided successes lifts a weak-wrist KUKA arm from 52.5% to 70.7%, while the same amount of extra data from unguided π0\pi_{0} reaches only 57.8% (Sec. 4.3).

after which the robot executes 5 actions and π0\pi_{0} plans again.

On each body, we may run π0\pi_{0} on a few calibration tasks and observe which episodes succeed (2,500 π0\pi_{0} queries per task, about 23 episodes on LIBERO-Object); transfer tasks get no demonstrations, reward or target-body data.

The clamp keeps the factor finite at t=1t=1, the cap limits each nudge to 20% of π0\pi_{0}’s step, and w∈{1,2,4}w\in\{1,2,4\} is picked on the calibration tasks; these, and a classifier that does not see oo directly, are heuristics (App. B).
What the classifier learns.
π0\pi_{0} nearly always succeeds on its source body, so DAD_{A} is roughly π0\pi_{0}’s own chunks and DBD_{B} is those chunks filtered by success on the target body: the aim is π0\pi_{0} reweighted by the chance that a chunk succeeds there (App. B).
The classifier sees no images, instruction or task label, but π0\pi_{0}’s action space subtracts the current end-effector pose (App. A), so the pose is implicitly visible.
Guided self-collection.
If guided π0\pi_{0} solves calibration tasks where DBD_{B} has few successes, we add the successes of one round of guided rollouts to DBD_{B} and retrain the classifier.
3 Experimental Setup
Policy and tasks.
We use the frozen public π0\pi_{0} LIBERO checkpoint [2] in LIBERO [18] (robosuite [28]), a simulated benchmark of tabletop manipulation tasks in four suites that vary the objects (Object), spatial layouts (Spatial) and goals (Goal), plus long-horizon tasks (LIBERO-10).
Each suite has four calibration and four transfer tasks (App. A), all of which π0\pi_{0} saw on its source body.
Episode limits are about twice LIBERO’s (capped at 980 steps for LIBERO-10), so slow bodies are not failed for speed alone, and every condition sees the same 200 transfer-task episodes.
Target bodies (Table 2) form three families.
Controller changes Ctrl(kk, dd s) are the Panda with its controller stiffness scaled by kk and its commands delayed by dd seconds: Ctrl(1, 0.2 s), Ctrl(0.5, 0.2 s), Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s), Ctrl(0.33, 0.3 s) and six sweep bodies.
Weak wrists are the KUKA, Kinova and Sawyer arms (with the Panda gripper) and the Panda, with wrist motors at 25%, 10%, 25% and 10% strength.
Other faults: a UR5e capped at 0.3 rad/s, a 5×\times slower gripper, a worn Panda, a locked wrist.
Statistics.
We report success rates over 200 transfer-task episodes and paired differences in percentage points (points) with 95% paired-bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs). Conditions, seeds and criteria (a gain of at least +5 or +10 points with a CI above zero) were committed before data collection (exceptions: App. A.1).

4.1 Controller changes: guidance helps
Across the four suites, guidance improves 11 of the 12 suite–body pairs with a changed controller, 7 with a CI above zero (Fig. 2A, B); 5 remain significant after a Holm correction over the 12 pairs. Pooled over the 12 pairs, success rises from 72% to 80% (1,718 to 1,931 of 2,400 episodes), a 31% relative reduction in failures.
On LIBERO-Object, Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) rises from 73% to 93% (+20 points [+13.5, +27]); two more classifier seeds give 93.5% and 91%.
The exception is LIBERO-10, where Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) drops by 5.5 points [−13-13, +2]. All five second runs (full repeats with new target-body data, a new classifier and a new ww; three controller bodies and two KUKA arm runs) also gain; two of them were registered after inconclusive first runs.
Pre-registered sweep. Of six more controller bodies registered before data collection (squares in Fig. 2A), 3 met our criterion, so our registered family claim (at least 4 of 6) fails (App. C.1). Among controller changes, guidance helps most when unguided π0\pi_{0} succeeds on a third to two thirds of episodes (Fig. 4).
4.2 Weak wrists and other faults: mixed
Guidance lifts the Kinova arm (10% wrist) from 70% to 85% (+15 points [+7.5, +22.5]) and the KUKA arm (25% wrist) from 42% to 52.5% (3-seed mean), but lowers the Sawyer arm (41% to 25%) and the Panda with a 10% wrist (47% to 37.5%); over all 12 original LIBERO-Object bodies (controller changes included), the net gain is only +0.3 points.
The worst case is a locked wrist (68.5% to 36%): guided plans ask for wrist roll the joint cannot make, and switching guidance on only after the first grasp restores 67%.
Where the fault is known, simple rules can win: smoothing on the capped UR5e (85.5% vs. 77.5%; unguided 84%), and sending actions planned 4 steps ahead on Ctrl(1, 0.2 s) (97.5% at 280 steps).
4.3 Ablations and guided collection
The gradient is what helps.
Reranking and LoRA both ran on the KUKA arm, with reranking also on Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s).
Reranking NN unguided samples with the guidance classifier (N=8N=8 on the KUKA arm, 4 on Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s)) shows no gain (KUKA arm: 35.5% vs. 42% unguided; Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s): 76% vs. 73%), while gradient guidance with the same classifiers helps (73.5% and 93%; on the KUKA arm, both use the seed-0 guided-collection classifier); π0\pi_{0}’s samples at one state differ by only 0.1–0.5 logits.
With the same number of target-body π0\pi_{0} queries as the classifier (56 successful episodes), an untuned LoRA fine-tune drops to 29.5% (−12.5-12.5 points [−21-21, −4-4]); on a 26% larger re-collection made with the guided-collection protocol it reaches 41.5%, against 73.5% for guidance with the seed-0 guided-collection classifier (App. C.6).
Guided self-collection.
On the KUKA arm, the original unguided data had no successes on calibration task 2, but guided π0\pi_{0} solved it.
Retraining the classifier with the guided successes from all four calibration tasks raises transfer-task success from 52.5% to 70.7% (3-seed means; Fig. 2C), against 57.8% when the same number of extra queries came from unguided π0\pi_{0}.
The round was designed to fix a loss on transfer task 3, so this transfer-task set is not fully clean (App. C.3). A second round added nothing beyond the same number of extra episodes (75.2% vs. 74.5%), and guided collection did not help Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) on LIBERO-Goal (67% both).
5 Related Work
Our operator is the density-ratio correction of discriminator guidance [14] and TGDP [21], applied to a frozen model as in classifier guidance [6].
Other steering signals for frozen robot policies encode the task or its success, such as a value function [19] or a noise-space actor [24], or a known controller’s cost [9]; embodiment-transfer methods fine-tune on target demonstrations [27], bridge appearance [4] or need paired rollouts [12].
We learn the correction from success-labeled rollouts alone (App. E).
6 Limitations and Conclusion
Embodiment-classifier guidance can steer a frozen π0\pi_{0} toward some target bodies, mostly controller changes, but we cannot yet tell which in advance: a pre-registered rule for when to guide failed (App. A.1).
Limitations.
(i) All results are in simulation, with one policy and one benchmark.
(ii) The method needs success-labeled rollouts on both bodies, and target-body data for each suite.
(iii) The classifier’s input carries the end-effector pose, so we have not separated where the bodies go from how they move.
(iv) π0\pi_{0} saw the transfer tasks on its source body, and transfer task 3 informed guided collection.
(v) Most results use one classifier and evaluation seed; after a Holm correction, 5 of the 12 controller pairs remain significant; guidance can hurt, and simple rules fix known faults such as a fixed delay better.
(vi) We test one single-body fine-tuned checkpoint; steering a multi-body base policy is untested.
(vii) Guidance adds a classifier gradient at each of the 10 denoising steps, which slows every query; since the classifier is small (0.8M weights versus π0\pi_{0}’s 3B), we expect the overhead to be modest, but we did not measure it under controlled conditions.
(viii) Our evidence for data efficiency rests on one fine-tuning baseline: a single untuned LoRA run on one body, trained on task-imbalanced data, whereas ww was selected for guidance; a tuned comparison across data budgets and bodies is needed to establish it.

Appendix A Setup and Protocol
Reporting convention. Throughout the appendix, success is given as a percentage with the underlying count, e.g. 73% (146/200), and differences are in percentage points (points) with 95% paired-bootstrap CIs, as in the main text.
Simulator and policy.
All runs use LIBERO [18] on robosuite [28] (MuJoCo physics): one robot arm at a table with household objects.
The policy is π0\pi_{0} [2], a 3-billion-parameter VLA, from Physical Intelligence’s public LIBERO checkpoint (JAX), frozen.
Its input is two camera images (third-person and wrist), the robot state and the instruction; its action is 7-D (end-effector translation, rotation and gripper), sent at 20 Hz.
Each π0\pi_{0} query returns a 50-step chunk, of which the robot executes 5 (0.25 s).
On the source body, LIBERO’s operational-space controller (OSC) turns each end-effector command into joint torques.
Each task has 50 fixed starting states (ids 0–49), and success is LIBERO’s task predicate on the final scene. Table 1 lists the tasks and Table 2 the bodies.

Is the KUKA arm’s loss the arm or the wrist?
A KUKA iiwa with a full-strength wrist succeeds on 87.5% (70/80) of transfer-task pilot episodes, against 36.3% (29/80) with the 25% wrist on the same starting states, so the arm swap alone does not explain the loss. That the weak wrist causes most of it is an inference: restoring the wrist within the same run rescued about 40% of the failures.
Classifier data.
The embodiment classifier’s target-body data come from unguided π0\pi_{0} on the calibration tasks, 2,500 π0\pi_{0} queries per task, keeping successful episodes; source-body data come from successful source-body rollouts on the same tasks. Each suite uses only its own calibration tasks.
On LIBERO-Object, a 560-step episode uses at most 112 queries (5 executed actions each), so the budget gives at least 22 episodes per task, and more when episodes end early; this is the “about 23 rollouts per calibration task” of the main text.
Chunks are built as π0\pi_{0}’s input transform builds them: the next 50 commanded actions, with the current state subtracted from dimensions 0–5 (end-effector position and axis-angle rotation), then z-scored with the checkpoint’s statistics.
The subtraction means that every chunk carries the current end-effector pose as a constant offset.
A classifier for Ctrl(1, 0.2 s) trained on one task reached validation AUC 0.998 on that task but 0.65 on transfer tasks; trained on four tasks, its transfer-task AUC was 0.919, 0.907 and 0.913 over three seeds with fresh data.
Choosing ww.
We try w∈{1,2,4}w\in\{1,2,4\} on the calibration tasks (starting states 0–19, 80 episodes, evaluation seed 0) and keep the one with the most successes, breaking ties toward the smaller ww. For Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s), Ctrl(1, 0.2 s) and the UR5e, ww was chosen at 280 steps and evaluated at 560 steps, as stated in their registrations.
The choice is mostly within noise: in 31 of 38 runs with saved selection scores, the chosen ww won by at most 5 points (4/80), about one standard deviation of such a difference.
Episode limits.
If the target body does the same motions ss times slower, success within a fixed limit HH drops by FA​(H)−FA​(H/s)F_{A}(H)-F_{A}(H/s), where FA​(h)F_{A}(h) is the source body’s chance of success within hh steps; slowness alone would then count as failure. We therefore use about twice LIBERO’s native limits (Table 1). On Ctrl(1, 0.2 s), the gain read at 280 steps was 22.5 points, against 11.75 points on the same episodes at 560 steps (App. C.2).
A.1 Statistics and pre-registration
•
Pairing. Every condition uses the same 50 starting states per task and the same environment seed. Episodes are not bit-reproducible across runs, so “paired” means the same starting state, not the same random stream.
•
Intervals and tests. Per condition: success and a 95% Wilson interval. Differences: paired bootstrap over (task, starting state) pairs, 10,000 resamples, percentile interval, and an exact two-sided McNemar test on discordant pairs. The bootstrap was run separately for the text and for the per-task tables, so a CI endpoint can differ by 0.5 points between them. All numbers were recomputed from per-episode records by a separate script.
•
Multiple comparisons. Over the 12 controller-change suite–body pairs, a Holm correction at α=0.05\alpha=0.05 leaves 5 significant: Object Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) and Ctrl(0.33, 0.3 s), Spatial Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) and Ctrl(0.33, 0.3 s), and Goal Ctrl(0.33, 0.3 s). It uses exact McNemar pp-values where reported and otherwise pp-values approximated from the paired-bootstrap CIs (normal approximation). Other rows are uncorrected; App. C.5 applies a Bonferroni correction over the four reuse tests.
•
Registration. Each run’s conditions, horizon, seeds and criteria were committed to version control before its data existed. The usual criterion is a gain of at least 5 points (some runs: 10 points) with a paired CI above zero; a gain below 1.5 points counts as no effect, and anything in between, or a CI touching zero, is inconclusive.
•
Body screen. A body entered a full run only if unguided π0\pi_{0} succeeded on 30–75% of an 80-episode transfer-task pilot. Ctrl(0.5, 0.2 s) entered after the band was widened from 30–70% to 30–75%, after its pilot was seen. The sweep bodies had no pilot; registered no-room and floor rules took its place.
•
Guide-or-not rule (failed). We registered a rule to guide only if the best guided calibration-task success is at least 10 points (8/80) above unguided. On 10 bodies with known outcomes it had to be right on at least 7 of the 8 clear-cut ones, beat always-guiding by at least 67 successes in total, and block at most one body that gains. It was right on 5 of 8: it blocked all three harmful bodies (Panda (10% wrist), Sawyer arm, locked wrist) but also three that gain (Kinova arm, KUKA arm, Ctrl(1, 0.2 s)), and beat always-guiding by 50.5 successes. A 5-point margin would have been right on 7 of 8, but it was chosen after seeing the results, so we do not use it. Calibration-task success on 80 episodes is too noisy to separate a real gain from none, so the method always guides.
•
Deviations. Two stopping rules for the sweep were added after its first five bodies had run, and they stopped two bodies before evaluation (App. C.1). The second LIBERO-Goal runs of the KUKA arm and Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) were registered after their first runs came out inconclusive. Analyses labeled descriptive had no registered criterion.
Appendix B Method Details and Theory
Intuition.
The embodiment classifier learns which chunk shapes separate the target body’s successful chunks from the source body’s chunks, and its gradient points toward chunks that look more like the former.
Each nudge therefore bends the plan toward motions that worked on the target body, while π0\pi_{0}’s velocity field still provides the task-level plan (which object to reach and where to place it) (we did not measure which features the classifier uses).
This picture is consistent with the failures in App. D: if no plan of a useful shape exists (a locked joint), the nudge asks for motion the robot cannot make; if the fault is timing (a slow gripper), plan shape is not the cause; and where the classifier saw no target-body data, its gradient is a guess.

When guided self-collection runs.
The round runs only if guided π0\pi_{0} solves a calibration task on which DBD_{B} has few or no successes.
The KUKA arm (25% wrist) met this condition; the Sawyer arm did not (0 guided task-2 successes); Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) on LIBERO-Goal ran the round, which did not help (App. C.3).

B.1 Flow path, velocity and score
π0\pi_{0} uses xt=t​ε+(1−t)​ax_{t}=t\varepsilon+(1-t)a and target u=ε−au=\varepsilon-a, trained with t∼0.999​Beta​(1.5,1)+0.001t\sim 0.999\,\mathrm{Beta}(1.5,1)+0.001.
The best network is the conditional mean v(x,t,o)=𝔼[ε−a∣xt=x,o]v(x,t,o)=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon-a\mid x_{t}=x,o].
Let a^=𝔼⁡[a∣xt=x]\hat{a}=\mathbb{E}[a\mid x_{t}=x] and ε^=𝔼⁡[ε∣xt=x]\hat{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid x_{t}=x], so x=t​ε^+(1−t)​a^x=t\hat{\varepsilon}+(1-t)\hat{a}.
1.

B.2 The intended distribution and what an action-only classifier estimates
DXD_{X} holds chunks from successful episodes of unguided π0\pi_{0} on body XX, on the calibration tasks.
Let 𝒮\mathcal{S} be success and ρX​(o)\rho_{X}(o) the observations visited on body XX.
Treating episode success as a property of each chunk [heuristic], the chunk density in DXD_{X} is πX​(a∣o)=pA​(a∣o)​PX​(𝒮∣o,a)/PX​(𝒮∣o)\pi_{X}(a\mid o)=p_{A}(a\mid o)P_{X}(\mathcal{S}\mid o,a)/P_{X}(\mathcal{S}\mid o).
The intended distribution is πB​(a∣o)=pA​(a∣o)​r​(a,o)/Z⁡(o)\pi_{B}(a\mid o)=p_{A}(a\mid o)\,r(a,o)/Z(o) with density ratio r=πB/pAr=\pi_{B}/p_{A}, that is, π0\pi_{0} reweighted by the probability of success on the target body.
The classifier never sees oo, so it can only compare qX​(a)=∫πX​(a∣o)​ρX​(o)​𝑑oq_{X}(a)=\int\pi_{X}(a\mid o)\rho_{X}(o)\,do.
Its optimum equals log⁡r⁡(a,o)\log r(a,o), up to a term in oo only, under these assumptions:
A0
πA≈pA\pi_{A}\approx p_{A}: π0\pi_{0} nearly always succeeds on the source body.
A1
ρB=ρA\rho_{B}=\rho_{A}: both bodies visit the same observations. False in general; a slow body sees different states.
A2
The noised ratio Rt​(x,o)=ptB​(x∣o)/ptA​(x∣o)R_{t}(x,o)=p^{B}_{t}(x\mid o)/p^{A}_{t}(x\mid o) is free of oo at every tt.
A3
Each chunk of a successful episode counts as a success chunk.
A4
Planned chunks (what guidance acts on) and executed windows (what the classifier trains on) have the same distribution.
A5
The ratio learned on calibration tasks also holds on transfer tasks.
Without A1 and A2, qtB​(x)/qtA​(x)=𝔼o∼ρA​(o∣xt=x)​[ρB​(o)ρA​(o)​Rt​(x,o)]q^{B}_{t}(x)/q^{A}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\rho_{A}(o\mid x_{t}=x)}\big[\tfrac{\rho_{B}(o)}{\rho_{A}(o)}R_{t}(x,o)\big], so every state-dependent part of the correction is lost and only the average change in chunk shape remains.
One exception: chunks in π0\pi_{0}’s action space have the current end-effector pose subtracted (App. A), so the pose part of oo reaches the classifier, and a violation of A1 in where the bodies go can be read off the chunks.
B.3 The noise-conditioned classifier
The embodiment classifier’s training loss, with the target body as the positive class and 128 chunks per class in each batch, is
L⁡(ϕ)=𝔼a∼DA,t,ε​sp​(ℓϕ​(xt,t))+𝔼a∼DB,t,ε​sp​(−ℓϕ​(xt,t)),sp⁡(z)=log⁡(1+ez),L(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim D_{A},t,\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{sp}\big(\ell_{\phi}(x_{t},t)\big)+\mathbb{E}_{a\sim D_{B},t,\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{sp}\big(-\ell_{\phi}(x_{t},t)\big),\quad\mathrm{sp}(z)=\log(1+e^{z}),
(5)
with t∼U⁡(0,1)t\sim U(0,1).
For fixed (x,t)(x,t), the pointwise loss qtA​sp​(ℓ)+qtB​sp​(−ℓ)q^{A}_{t}\,\mathrm{sp}(\ell)+q^{B}_{t}\,\mathrm{sp}(-\ell) is minimized at ℓ⋆​(x,t)=log⁡qtB​(x)/qtA​(x)\ell^{\star}(x,t)=\log q^{B}_{t}(x)/q^{A}_{t}(x); the distribution of tt only weights noise levels.
Noise conditioning is needed because the noised ratio is an average of the clean ratio r0r_{0}, rt​(x)=𝔼a∼qA​(a∣xt=x)​[r0​(a)]r_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim q_{A}(a\mid x_{t}=x)}[r_{0}(a)], and the log of an average is not the average of the log.
Guidance acts on the 7 real action dimensions of π0\pi_{0}’s 32 padded dimensions, at all 10 flow steps.
The network has 4 transformer blocks of width 128 (about 0.8M weights) and mixes information across the 50 chunk steps.
B.4 Gaps between the implementation and the exact correction
•
The clamp at t=1t=1. The implementation uses the factor 1/0.05=201/0.05=20 at the first step. A perfect classifier has zero gradient there, because both noised marginals are 𝒩⁡(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0,I), while the exact correction at t=1t=1 is a mean shift −(μB−μA)-(\mu_{B}-\mu_{A}). We did not measure the size of this gap.
•
The weight w≠1w\neq 1 [heuristic]. The field then targets intermediate densities ∝ptA​rtw\propto p^{A}_{t}r_{t}^{\,w}, which are not the noised versions of pA​rwp_{A}r^{w}; this is the known gap of guidance with a scale.
•
The cap [heuristic]. It breaks exactness and is often active (71% of guidance calls on task 0 for the KUKA arm).
•
Discretization and network error. Ten Euler steps and the network’s own error are not bounded here.
With w>1w>1 and the cap, we therefore sample from no known distribution; we call the result “guided”, not “samples of πB\pi_{B}”.

C.2 Robustness: seeds, evaluation seeds and episode limits
KUKA arm (25% wrist). The main number is the mean over three classifier seeds: 57% (114/200), 52% (104/200) and 48.5% (97/200), against 42% (84/200) unguided, all at w=4w=4 (chosen for seed 0). Seeds 1 and 2 alone are not significant (+10 [0, +20], p=0.064p=0.064; +6.5 [−3-3, +16], p=0.21p=0.21).
Seed 0 raised success in three paired evaluations: 57% vs. 42%, 60% (120/200) vs. 44.5% (89/200) on a second evaluation seed, and 67.5% (135/200) vs. 50.5% (101/200) on 50 new object placements per task (+17 [+7, +26.5], p=0.001p=0.001); pooled, 61.5% (369/600) vs. 45.7% (274/600).
The gain comes from more grasps, not from speed: failures that reach the object without grasping it fall from 57 to 35 episodes. Tasks 0, 6 and 7 gain in every evaluation, and task 3 loses in every one (pooled −18.7-18.7 [−30.7-30.7, −7.3-7.3] points; App. D).
A classifier that also saw the gripper-to-object offset and the object lift gave no gain (44.5%, 89/200).
Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s), three classifier seeds (registered). Two more seeds on the same data, with ww re-selected by the original rule, give 93% (186/200), 93.5% (187/200) and 91% (182/200) against 73% (146/200) unguided (pooled +19.5 [+13.5, +26]); the criterion (mean gain of at least 7.5 points, pooled CI and every seed’s CI above zero) holds. The seeds share one data set, and seed 2 at w=4w=4 hit the cap on 81% of denoising steps (seed 1 at w=1w=1: 22%), so this does not test robustness to ww.
Episode limits. On Ctrl(1, 0.2 s) at 560 steps, guidance raises success from 74.8% (299/400) to 86.5% (346/400) over two evaluation seeds (+11.75 [+6.75, +16.75]); read off the same episodes at 280 steps, the gain is 22.5 points, and guided episodes succeed sooner (mean time to success 256 vs. 324 steps). A speed-only re-timing rule (gain 1.25) scores 47.3% (189/400), while sending the action planned 4 steps ahead scores 97.5% (195/200) at 280 steps, equal to the source body’s own unguided score. On Ctrl(0.5, 0.2 s), guidance gives 95% (190/200) vs. 73.5% (147/200) at 280 steps, where every failure is a time-out, but only +3 [−1-1, +7] points at 560 steps: on this body, guidance mainly makes the robot finish sooner.
Provenance. Runs used two machines with the same code and seeds; for the second sibling-reuse pair and the cross-arm tests, the reference runs ran on the other machine one day earlier. Unfinished replicates are not reported.
C.3 KUKA arm (25% wrist): one round of guided collection
Guided collection ran guided π0\pi_{0} with the seed-0 classifier (w=4w=4) on calibration tasks 1, 2, 4 and 5 (starting states 20–41, 22 episodes per task, 6,709 queries).
Guided π0\pi_{0} solved task 2 in 14 episodes, where the original target-body data had none.
We added the guided successes to the original target-body successes (10,772 chunks), retrained the classifier with the same recipe (seeds 0, 1 and 2) and chose ww again (w=4w=4, 4 and 2).
Success is 73.5% (147/200), 70% (140/200) and 68.5% (137/200), mean 70.7%, against 57%, 52% and 48.5% for the original classifier and 42% unguided; per seed, the paired gain over the original classifier is +16.5 [+9.5, +24], +18 [+10.5, +25.5] and +20 [+12, +27.5] points.
Task 3 rises to 71.3% on average (original classifier 41.3%; unguided 54%). The target-body cost is about 1.7×\times the original collection.
Equal-budget control (registered). With about the same number of target-body π0\pi_{0} queries (6,520 vs. 6,709), a classifier retrained on unguided data reaches 51.5% (103/200), 59% (118/200) and 63% (126/200), mean 57.8%. Per-seed gaps to guided collection are +22 [+14.5, +29.5], +11 [+3.5, +18.5] and +5.5 [−1.5-1.5, +12.5] points, meeting the registered criterion (CI above zero in 2 of 3 seeds and a mean gap of at least 5 points). Extra unguided data add 5.3 points over the original classifier, and guidance adds 12.8 more; at the same budget, guided collection gathered 2.8×\times more task-2 success data (14 vs. 5 episodes).
Without the task-2 data (registered). Using guided successes from tasks 1, 4 and 5 only gives 66.5% (3-seed mean), meeting its criterion (≥65.5%\geq 65.5\%) by 1 point. The task-2 data add about 4 points (pooled CI touches zero) and about half of the task-3 gain (task-3 means 41.3%, 56% and 71.3% for the original classifier, this ablation and the full round).
A second round (registered). The round-1 classifier collected 88 more guided episodes; an equal-episode control collected the same episodes with the original classifier. The second round gives 76% (152/200), 78.5% (157/200) and 71% (142/200), mean 75.2%, and the control 79% (158/200), 70% (140/200) and 74.5% (149/200), mean 74.5%; per seed, −3-3 [−9.5-9.5, +3.5], +8.5 [+1.5, +15.5] and −3.5-3.5 [−10.5-10.5, +3.5] points. A second round adds no detectable gain, although this test cannot rule out an effect of about 7.5 points either way.
Fresh scenes. On 200 unused scenes, the seed-0 guided-collection classifier (the best of the three) scores 92% (184/200) vs. 52% (104/200) unguided (+40 [+32, +47.5]). No original-classifier or control condition ran on those scenes.
Caveats. All three seeds share one guided data set; one evaluation seed; task 3 shaped the design; calibration task 1 does not gain (64%, 66% and 74% vs. 68% unguided, of 50). Guided successes are partly separable from unguided ones (probe AUC 0.72), and a registered drift flag was raised (source-body task-2 logit +2.15 at t=0.5t=0.5, limit +2.0), so we do not claim that coverage alone explains the gain. Weaker steering alone does not: the original seed-0 classifier at w=2w=2, which steers about as weakly, scores 54.5% (109/200).
Other bodies. On LIBERO-Goal, guided collection did not help Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) (67% vs. 67%, 134/200 each). On the Sawyer arm, guided π0\pi_{0} solved no task-2 episodes, so the round did not run.

Classifier and ww changed together (at w=4w=4 the cap binds on 64–74% of denoising steps on the Kinova arm and 49–54% on the Panda, against 9–23% for their own classifiers at w=1w=1), so these runs show no cause.
Shared shift (registered prediction). On saved successful calibration-task chunks, a linear probe (5 folds by episode) cannot tell the siblings Ctrl(0.5, 0.2 s) and Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) apart (AUC 0.51 at t=0t=0) but separates the KUKA and Sawyer arms, both with 25% wrists, almost perfectly (0.99). The siblings’ guidance gradients align (cosine +0.71 at t=0.3t=0.3; +0.57 to +0.60 across other seeds) while the arms’ do not (+0.07). Both registered gaps hold (AUC gap 0.481, bar 0.10; cosine gap 0.632, bar 0.20).
Caveats: the Sawyer arm stands in for the Kinova arm (no Kinova chunks were saved); both sibling shifts are small (probe AUC against the source body 0.62 and 0.66, against 0.99 and 0.98 for the arms); shared seed-0 initialization inflates the sibling cosine. Because chunks carry the end-effector pose (App. A), part of the arms’ separability may reflect different poses rather than different motions; we did not test this.

Appendix D Failure Analysis
Analyses without a registered criterion are labeled descriptive.
Panda (locked wrist). Guidance (w=2w=2) gives 36% (72/200) vs. 68.5% (137/200) unguided, and every transfer task drops. Many guided plans ask for wrist roll that the locked joint cannot make. Switching guidance on only at the first executed gripper close restores the unguided level (67%, 134/200; −1.5-1.5 [−9.5-9.5, +7] points), so the harm comes mostly from guidance during the approach.
Sawyer arm (25% wrist). 25% (50/200) vs. 41% (82/200) unguided (−16-16 [−23-23, −9-9] points). Its embodiment classifier saw target-body data from calibration tasks 1, 4 and 5 only (task 2: no successes). Calibration task 1 rises (60% to 72%, of 50) while every transfer task falls, which points to a coverage gap.
Panda (10% wrist). 37.5% (75/200) vs. 47% (94/200) (−9.5-9.5 [−17-17, −2-2] points). Transfer task 6 is at 0% (0/50) unguided, and calibration task 1 also drops (60% to 42%). On the two calibration tasks without target-body successes (2 and 4), guided π0\pi_{0} succeeded on at most 10% (2/20) of episodes per task and ww, so guided collection would add almost no data.
KUKA arm, task 3 (descriptive). Task 3 is a tall bottle in the center slot; the other transfer tasks use side slots, and the target body had no successes on task 2, the calibration task with a center-slot tall object. Under guidance, failures in which the hand never reaches the object rise (13 to 18 and 12 to 17 episodes on two evaluation seeds; 2 to 14 on new scenes), and task-3 approach chunks get the lowest classifier logit and the largest gradient. The grasp gate moves the harm from task 3 to task 6, and guided collection, which adds task-2 data, lifts task 3 (App. C.3).
UR5e (speed cap). 77.5% (155/200) vs. 84% (168/200) at 560 steps (−6.5-6.5 [−13-13, 0] points; McNemar p=0.08p=0.08). A hand-made rule (rotation gain 0.3, smoothing 0.6) beats guidance by 8 [+1, +15] points, and a state-aware oracle speed limiter is the worst condition (52.5%, 105/200). Descriptively, the UR5e’s successful calibration-task chunks command about 17% less rotation than the source body’s and the same translation, yet 69–82% of the classifier gradient’s energy lies on the translation dimensions, so guidance probably redirects translation rather than trimming rotation (guided actions were not logged).
Panda (slow gripper) and other near misses. With a slow gripper, guidance gives 73.5% (147/200) vs. 76% (152/200); a slow gripper changes only the timing of the close, which chunk-shape guidance hardly addresses. The worn Panda shows no significant change (48% vs. 43%). Ctrl(0.33, 0.3 s) on LIBERO-Object gains 9 [+4, +14] points (p=0.0009p=0.0009) but misses its registered criterion of 10 points, with unguided π0\pi_{0} near the ceiling (88.5%).
Panda (locked upper arm). Its unguided pilot (14%) was outside the 30–75% band, so it is not a body-level result. Tasks 0, 6 and 7 fail in both conditions; on task 3, guidance gives 84% (42/50) vs. 44% (22/50) (paired p=9×10−5p=9\times 10^{-5}).
Predicting where guidance helps. None of our registered predictors worked: body separability vs. gain (Spearman −0.14-0.14 on 6 bodies; bar 0.70), a failure-predicted gate (loses 4–7.5 points to always guiding on four KUKA-arm conditions), a confidence gate (0 of 4 conditions), and an open-loop replay forecast of unguided failure (within-body Spearman −0.18-0.18; bar 0.4). Descriptively, on the KUKA arm, guidance helps where early chunks already look target-body-like (logit AUC 0.65–0.71) and hurts where the classifier gradient is largest (AUC 0.66–0.73).
Cross-suite transfer (registered, failed). A LIBERO-Object embodiment classifier for Ctrl(0.5, 0.3 s) does not transfer to LIBERO-Goal: AUC 0.55 at t=0.1t=0.1 and 0.61 at t=0.5t=0.5 (bars 0.85 and 0.80), and its mean guidance direction has cosine −0.22-0.22 and −0.36-0.36 with the Object-suite direction (bar 0.7). Goal-suite chunks sit far from Object-suite chunks in π0\pi_{0}’s action space (per-dimension means differ by up to about one standard deviation, because that space subtracts the end-effector pose), so part of the failure may be a layout shift rather than a body difference. Each suite therefore needs its own target-body data.
Appendix E Extended Related Work
Guidance with a domain classifier.
Classifier guidance adds a noised-sample classifier’s gradient to a frozen model’s score [6]; classifier-free guidance [10] would need π0\pi_{0} retrained with a body label.
Our operator is the density-ratio correction of discriminator guidance [14] and TGDP [21], which add a classifier-derived density-ratio gradient (real vs. generated, or target vs. source; TGDP through a learned guidance network) to a frozen score model. X-Diffusion [22] uses a noise-level human-vs-robot classifier to decide from which noise level each human action enters training, and DARC [8] corrects RL rewards for dynamics gaps with domain classifiers.
Steering frozen robot policies.
Most steering signals encode the task or its success: a success predictor [26], a value function [19], human input [25], a dynamics model [7], a latent barrier [23], proxy policies [20], guided-rollout retraining [16] or a noise-space actor [24].
Real-time chunking guides a flow VLA’s sampler under inference delay [3], and UMI-on-Air [9] steers a diffusion policy with a known controller’s tracking cost; we learn the correction from success-labeled rollouts.
Embodiment transfer.
Prior methods fine-tune on target demonstrations [27], bridge appearance rather than dynamics [4, 15, 5], edit weights [13], condition on joint health [1], or train a residual calibrator for joint faults from paired rollouts [12]. We need no joint data or paired rollouts, but do not compare to them.
