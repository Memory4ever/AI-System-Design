Abstract
Humanoid control requires coordinating whole-body posture with position and heading in the world frame.
Mainstream controllers imitate trajectories in the root frame, but struggle to regain the target world pose after displacement.
We introduce  BeJEPA , a goal-conditioned Behavior World Model learned through unsupervised
reinforcement learning from human motion priors and robot interaction.
BeJEPA learns a shared representation of body configuration and world placement
through a joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA).
A Dynamic Predictor predicts the next state embedding under an action, while a Successor Predictor
summarizes a goal-conditioned policy’s expected discounted future state embeddings in a single vector.
During joint training, Dynamic grounds the initial representation in observed physical transitions,
while Successor increasingly organizes it around long-term behavior.
Their composition guides policy improvement through one physical prediction and one behavioral summary,
without long imagined rollouts.
In 20-second motion-tracking experiments, BeJEPA with online action refinement using Dynamic outperforms
all evaluated baselines in global body-position error, reducing it on AMASS
by 61.7% relative to BFM-Zero with world-pose inputs and 58.7% relative to SONIC.
Physical deployment on a Unitree G1 demonstrates joint attainment of posture, world position,
and heading goals, recovery from the ground, and closed-loop return after disturbances and goal changes.
Project page:  https://ylyem9x.github.io/BeJEPA-project/ .

1  Introduction
Whole-body control (WBC) is the physical foundation of humanoid applications, enabling manipulation, expressive motion, and the execution of higher-level commands  ( Ben et al., 2025 ;  Wei et al., 2026 ) .
It translates desired robot states into coordinated actuator commands.
For world-coordinate tasks, the desired body posture must be reached together with the requested base position and heading.
Existing motion-centered controllers can reproduce a posture while remaining displaced from its intended location.
Correcting this displacement requires the robot to step, temporarily departing from the requested posture before reaching it at the right place.
Whole-body control must therefore judge a movement by the goal it helps achieve, even when that movement initially worsens posture matching.
Human motion references show coordinated movement, making imitation a practical starting point for learning WBC  ( Peng et al., 2018 ;  He et al., 2025 ;  Liao et al., 2026 ) .
However, tracking a given trajectory does not explain how to reach a new goal when no trajectory is provided.
This motivates reinforcement learning (RL), through which the robot can discover intermediate movements and evaluate how they help it reach the goal.
Meta Motivo and BFM-Zero use unsupervised RL to learn reusable behaviors  ( Tirinzoni et al., 2025 ;  Li et al., 2026 ) .
Given a reward that is linear in the learned state features, these methods can select behaviors to maximize return without specifying intermediate states.
BFM-Zero uses body-relative features, so its learned objective focuses on posture and excludes absolute position and heading.
To control posture and world placement together, the robot must learn how actions change its body and how the resulting behavior brings it closer to the goal.
We introduce  BeJEPA , a  goal-conditioned Behavior World Model  for humanoid control, learned through unsupervised RL from human motion priors and robot interaction.
Its shared representation encodes body configuration, world position, and heading.
A goal-independent  Dynamic Predictor  models one-step action consequences, while a goal-conditioned  Successor Predictor  summarizes the policy’s expected future state features.
Early in joint training, Dynamic provides physical supervision that helps preserve useful features despite the unskilled policy’s noisy long-term targets.
As the policy improves, Successor organizes these features around long-term behavior.
Their composition trains the actor to choose movements by their future goal alignment, including steps that temporarily worsen posture matching, without long imagined rollouts.
Dynamic also supports online action refinement for time-indexed tracking.
Our contributions are threefold:
•
A goal-conditioned Behavior World Model for humanoid control.
BeJEPA learns a shared representation of posture and world placement through unsupervised RL, supporting goal reaching and motion tracking.
•
A Dynamic–Successor predictor decomposition.
We compose immediate dynamics with a goal-conditioned prediction of future state features, jointly learning the representation and guiding long-term policy improvement without long imagined rollouts.
•
World-anchored control in simulation and on hardware.
The learned actor improves global body alignment on AMASS and LAFAN. G1 demonstrates joint posture and world-pose control, ground recovery, and closed-loop return after disturbances and goal changes.
2  Related Work
Humanoid behavioral foundation models.
Motion imitation is a dominant route to humanoid behavioral foundation models  ( Peng et al., 2018 ;  He et al., 2025 ;  Liao et al., 2026 ) .
SONIC and Humanoid-GPT scale tracking accuracy and generalization across motions while retaining a reference-sequence interface  ( Luo et al., 2026 ;  Qi et al., 2026 ) .
Isolated or abruptly changed goals thus need an additional mechanism for intermediate motion.
Hierarchical systems supply this motion with a separate planner  ( Tevet et al., 2025 ) .
Generated motions must remain within the tracker’s capabilities, while replanning frequency trades motion consistency against responsiveness  ( Huang et al., 2025 ) .
Meta Motivo and BFM-Zero instead infer behaviors directly through unsupervised RL  ( Tirinzoni et al., 2025 ;  Li et al., 2026 ) .
However, BFM-Zero’s body-relative formulation leaves world position and heading outside its learned goals.
BeJEPA learns world placement through both physical and behavioral prediction, outperforming BFM-Zero retrained with world-pose inputs (Section   4.2  ).
World models and latent-space control.
Reconstruction-based models such as PlaNet and Dreamer allocate representation capacity to observation detail, including variation irrelevant to control  ( Hafner et al., 2019b ;  Hafner et al., 2019a ;  Hafner et al., 2025 ) .
Dreamer and the decoder-free TD-MPC family limit rollout horizons and estimate the remaining return with learned values  ( Hafner et al., 2019a ;  Hansen et al., 2022 ;  Hansen et al., 2024 ) .
These values depend on training rewards, so their long-term predictions do not automatically transfer to newly specified objectives.
Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn by predicting state embeddings, allowing abstraction from unpredictable observation detail  ( Assran et al., 2023 ;  Assran et al., 2025 ) .
Yet planning with latent dynamics, as in DINO-WM and LeWorldModel, composes transitions and risks accumulating prediction error  ( Zhou et al., 2024 ;  Maes et al., 2026 ) .
BeJEPA combines feature prediction with a directly learned, goal-conditioned summary of future state features, enabling long-term policy improvement without extended dynamics rollouts or downstream reward supervision.
Successor representations and reward-free reinforcement learning.
Successor features summarize a policy’s expected discounted future state features, separating behavioral prediction from reward readout  ( Barreto et al., 2017 ) .
Forward–Backward representations learn this structure to infer policies for new rewards and goals  ( Touati and Ollivier, 2021 ;  Touati et al., 2022 ) .
TD-JEPA introduces joint-embedding learning of these long-term predictions from offline transitions  ( Bagatella et al., 2026 ) .
Acquiring informative transitions online remains a separate problem, motivating dedicated FB exploration strategies  ( Urpí et al., 2025 ) .
In online humanoid learning, the policy and its long-term prediction targets evolve together.
Early movements may reveal little about how to reach a distant goal, although each executed action already provides an observed physical transition.
BeJEPA learns these two timescales jointly: observed one-step transitions directly supervise the encoder while goal-conditioned continuation organizes the same representation over longer horizons.
3  BeJEPA: A Behavior World Model
BeJEPA learns a controller for goal reaching and motion tracking without task-specific retraining.
It composes Dynamic’s one-step physical prediction with Successor’s goal-conditioned future-state feature summary in a shared representation of body configuration and world placement to guide policy improvement (Figure   2  ).
Dynamic also refines actions online for spatial and temporal alignment.
3.1  Problem formulation
We formulate humanoid control as a partially observable Markov decision process
ℳ  =   (  𝒮  ,  𝒪  ,  𝒜  ,  P  ,  Ω  ,   p  0   ,  γ  )    \mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{A},P,\Omega,p_{0},\gamma)   .
Here      s  t   ∈  𝒮   s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}    is the full state,      o  t   ∈  𝒪   o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}    is the controller’s observation, and      a  t   ∈  𝒜  ⊂   ℝ  29    a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{29}    specifies joint targets for a low-level proportional–derivative controller.
Transitions follow      s   t  +  1    ∼  P   (  ⋅  ∣   s  t   ,   a  t   )    s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_{t},a_{t})   , observations follow     Ω   (  ⋅  ∣   s  t   )    \Omega(\cdot\mid s_{t})   , initial states follow     p  0   p_{0}   , and     γ  ∈   (  0  ,  1  )    \gamma\in(0,1)    discounts future outcomes.
The observation contains proprioception, estimated root pose, and recent sensor and action history; the full state additionally exposes simulated body kinematics.
Root pose is represented as      ρ  t   =   (   x  t   ,   y  t   ,   sin  ⁡   ψ  t    ,   cos  ⁡   ψ  t    )    \rho_{t}=(x_{t},y_{t},\sin\psi_{t},\cos\psi_{t})    relative to a fixed reference anchor shared by current and target states.
A fixed target state     s  g   s_{g}    specifies goal reaching; a time-indexed reference     s  t  ref   s_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}}    specifies motion tracking.
We jointly train the world model and a goal-conditioned, off-policy actor–critic from a replay buffer    ℬ  \mathcal{B}    of online robot transitions.
A retargeted human motion dataset     𝒟  ref   \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}    supplies goals and a conditional motion prior  ( Peng et al., 2021 ) ; this prior and task-independent physical penalties guide executable control without downstream task rewards.

3.2  Goal-conditioned control with successor features
Goal reaching and motion tracking both require matching a target body configuration and its world placement.
Let      E  ϕ   :   𝒮  →   ℝ  d     E_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}    encode a state as      z  t   =    E  ϕ   ​   (   s  t   )     z_{t}=E_{\phi}(s_{t})    and a target as      z  g   =    E  ϕ   ​   (   s  g   )     z_{g}=E_{\phi}(s_{g})   .
We normalize both embeddings to radius     d   \sqrt{d}    and define the goal reward by their similarity:
r  g   ​   (  s  )    =   ⟨     E  ϕ   ​   (  s  )    ,   z  g    ⟩   =   d  −     1  2    ​    ‖     E  ϕ   ​   (  s  )    −   z  g    ‖   2  2      .   r_{g}(s)=\langle E_{\phi}(s),z_{g}\rangle=d-\tfrac{1}{2}\|E_{\phi}(s)-z_{g}\|_{2}^{2}.
(1)
Thus maximizing reward is exactly minimizing distance to the target in the learned representation.
The goal reward is linear in state features:     z  g   z_{g}    selects the target, and     E  ϕ   E_{\phi}    learns the similarity geometry.
A fixed     z  g   z_{g}    specifies goal reaching; successive reference embeddings specify tracking goals.
Successor features turn instantaneous similarity into a future-behavior objective  ( Barreto et al., 2017 ) , providing a basis for zero-shot reward transfer  ( Touati et al., 2022 ) .
For policy     π  g   \pi_{g}    conditioned on     z  g   z_{g}    and a state region     X  ⊆  𝒮   X\subseteq\mathcal{S}   , the normalized  successor measure   ( Touati and Ollivier, 2021 )  is
M   π  g     (  X  ∣  s  )   =   (  1  −  γ  )    𝔼   π  g     [   ∑   k  =  0   ∞    γ  k   𝟏   {   s   t  +  k    ∈  X  }   |   s  t   =  s  ]   .   M^{\pi_{g}}(X\mid s)=(1-\gamma)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{g}}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\mathbf{1}\{s_{t+k}\in X\}\,\middle|\,s_{t}=s\right].
(2)
This quantity counts discounted visits to    X  X   , including the current state; the factor     1  −  γ   1-\gamma    makes its total mass one.
Generative temporal-difference models also learn discounted future-state distributions directly, avoiding recursive one-step prediction over the full horizon  ( Janner et al., 2020 ) .
Instead of representing a distribution over all future states, we retain its expected embedding:
S   π  g    ​   (  s  )    :=    ∫  𝒮     E  ϕ   ​   (   s  ′   )   ​   M   π  g    ​   (   d  ​   s  ′    ∣  s  )     =    (   1  −  γ   )   ​   𝔼   π  g    ​   [    ∑   k  =  0   ∞     γ  k   ​   z   t  +  k      |    s  t   =  s   ]     .   S^{\pi_{g}}(s):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}E_{\phi}(s^{\prime})\,M^{\pi_{g}}(\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mid s)=(1-\gamma)\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{g}}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}z_{t+k}\,\middle|\,s_{t}=s\right].
(3)
This  successor feature  summarizes the states the policy is expected to occupy.
Because the goal reward is linear in this embedding, its normalized return follows directly:
V  g   ​   (  s  )    :=    ∫  𝒮     r  g   ​   (   s  ′   )   ​   M   π  g    ​   (   d  ​   s  ′    ∣  s  )     =   ⟨     S   π  g    ​   (  s  )    ,   z  g    ⟩    .   V_{g}(s):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}r_{g}(s^{\prime})\,M^{\pi_{g}}(\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mid s)=\left\langle S^{\pi_{g}}(s),z_{g}\right\rangle.
(4)
The successor predicts the future states visited while pursuing the goal; its inner product with the goal evaluates their alignment.
An action can therefore be useful even if the next posture is less similar to the target, if subsequent behavior brings the robot closer to it.
Contrastive goal-conditioned RL shows a related connection between representation inner products and goal values  ( Eysenbach et al., 2022 ) .
To learn this long-term prediction from individual transitions, separate the current feature:
S   π  g    ​   (   s  t   )    =     (   1  −  γ   )   ​   z  t    +   γ  ​   𝔼     a  t   ∼   π  g    ,    s   t  +  1    ∼  P     ​   [    S   π  g    ​   (   s   t  +  1    )    ]      .   S^{\pi_{g}}(s_{t})=(1-\gamma)z_{t}+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi_{g},\,s_{t+1}\sim P}\left[S^{\pi_{g}}(s_{t+1})\right].
(5)
This vector Bellman equation replaces an entire future trajectory with one observed transition and a prediction of its continuation.
We use this identity to learn a goal-conditioned summary of future state features alongside one-step physical prediction.
3.3  Learning with Dynamics and Successor Prediction
BeJEPA separates learning what an action changes from learning what a goal-conditioned policy subsequently achieves (Figure   2  ).
An immediate physical consequence can be learned from every attempted movement, even before the robot can reliably reach the intended goal.
We therefore introduce a goal-independent  Dynamic Predictor        D  ν   ​   (   z  t   ,   a  t   )    ≈   z   t  +  1     D_{\nu}(z_{t},a_{t})\approx z_{t+1}    and a goal-conditioned  Successor Predictor        S  ξ   ​   (   z  t   ,   z  g   )    ≈    S   π  g    ​   (   s  t   )     S_{\xi}(z_{t},z_{g})\approx S^{\pi_{g}}(s_{t})   , both operating in the shared representation.
Dynamic reuses physical transitions across goals; Successor shares a state-based continuation across actions with the same latent consequence, reducing the action dependence that must be learned through bootstrapping.
These two predictions connect through the Bellman equation.

Substituting Equation   4   gives

Here, action conditioning makes a control input’s repeatable consequences the prediction target.

3.4  Aligning Behavior in Space and Time
Motion tracking requires the right body configuration  at the right place and time .
Successive goal embeddings guide the actor, but its objective evaluates discounted future similarity to each goal, leaving room for spatial offset and temporal lag.
Finite-dimensional task features can also blur spatial precision, as analyzed in FB-AWARE  ( Cetin et al., 2024 ) .
We use Dynamic to compare a short predicted trajectory with time-indexed references and refine the actor’s actions online, using gradient-based latent MPC similar to Dream-MPC  ( Spieler and Behnke, 2026 ) .

4  Experiments
Our experiments examine whether BeJEPA can coordinate body posture with world placement, and how physical and behavioral prediction support this ability.
We ask four questions:
Q1.
How well does BeJEPA perform on standard tracking metrics?
Q2.
Does the Dynamic–Successor split improve world-goal control?
Q3.
How are position, motion, and physical state organized in the latent space?
Q4.
How does BeJEPA perform on the physical robot?

Unlike prior protocols that exclude trajectories with large spatial deviations and mark them as failures, we retain these trajectories to fairly evaluate the error in world coordinates.
We measure pelvis-centered and global MPJPE, root translation and heading errors, and local wrist position and orientation errors.

4.4  Q4: How does BeJEPA perform on the physical robot?
We deploy the pretrained BeJEPA policy on a physical Unitree G1.
The onboard LiDAR, coupled with FAST-LIO  ( Xu and Zhang, 2021 ;  Xu et al., 2022 ) , provides the position and heading estimates used by the world-anchored observation interface.
Together with proprioception, these estimates allow the policy to relate the robot’s current body configuration to a goal placed in the world.
Recovering balance and posture.
A world-anchored goal remains meaningful when an external disturbance moves the robot away from it.
Following a kick, the G1 first steps toward its original standing location, then makes a smaller second step to complete the return (Figure   6  a). Starting from the ground presents a larger change in body configuration: the robot pushes up, rises, reorients, and reaches the commanded standing pose (Figure   6  b). The two sequences demonstrate complementary aspects of recovery: correcting displacement and restoring an upright body configuration.
Figure 6:   World-anchored control on the physical G1.
(a) Recovery after a kick. (b) Standing up from the ground. (c) Sequential goal switching.
Gold rings identify the floor reference marks in (a,c).
Changing behavior preserves the spatial reference.
Sequential goals take the robot from a T-pose through kneeling, advancing, and turning before returning to its starting location (Figure   6  c).
Returning to the same floor mark despite these posture and heading changes demonstrates that the learned behaviors retain a persistent spatial reference.

A.4  Why separating Dynamic and Successor improves learning
We show how separating action consequences from behavioral continuation improves replay reuse while preserving the goal-conditioned control objective.
Following JEPA’s emphasis on predictable features  [ Assran et al., 2023 ,  Assran et al., 2025 ] , assume that     z  =    E  ϕ   ​   (  s  )     z=E_{\phi}(s)    removes unpredictable nuisance variation while retaining all information needed for goal rewards and policy decisions.
The resulting latent is Markov, and its next state is determined by the current latent and action:      z  +   =    D  ⋆   ​   (  z  ,  a  )     z^{+}=D^{\star}(z,a)   .
Fix the encoder and a goal-conditioned policy     π  g   \pi_{g}    for the following argument.
Proof.
Let      S  g   ​   (  z  )    S_{g}(z)    denote the policy’s successor feature in this representation.
By Equation   21  , the action-conditioned future feature satisfies
F  g  +    (  z  ,  a  )   =  𝔼   [   S  g    (   z  +   )   ∣  z  ,  a  ]   =   S  g    (   D  ⋆    (  z  ,  a  )   )   .   F_{g}^{+}(z,a)=\mathbb{E}[S_{g}(z^{+})\mid z,a]=S_{g}(D^{\star}(z,a)).
(25)
Substituting this identity into Equation   6   recovers the composed value in Equation   7   with exact predictors.
The decomposition therefore preserves action values and their maximizing actions.
The same identity exposes the sharing that makes learning more efficient.
A merged predictor learns continuation as a function of     (  z  ,  a  ,   z  g   )   (z,a,z_{g})   .
The split assigns immediate action dependence to Dynamic, whose observed next-latent targets can be learned from transitions collected under any goal.
Each improvement in this physical prediction is consequently reused across goals.
Successor learns continuation as a function of     (  z  ,   z  g   )   (z,z_{g})   : all predecessor state–action pairs reaching the same latent share its future-feature prediction.
An update to that continuation therefore benefits every action leading to it, reducing duplicated long-term prediction across actions.
Moreover, Equation   9   learns this continuation directly from observed state transitions, without querying a next action in the bootstrap.
Thus action effects receive direct one-step supervision, while TD learning estimates a shared state-based continuation; the long-term predictor no longer needs to separately learn the initiating action’s effect on future value.

Appendix   D   supports this efficiency gain empirically: even with Dynamic’s encoder gradient stopped, the split model achieves lower early global-tracking and fixed-goal heading errors than the merged predictor.
Full BeJEPA improves further when Dynamic also shapes the encoder, linking the predictable-representation premise to the observed learning advantage.

C.1  Motion data and goal relabeling
Our motion prior contains    2,000  2{,}000    G1 motion clips:    862  862    ten-second LAFAN clips  [ Harvey et al., 2020 ]  and    1,138  1{,}138    motions from the SONIC/Bones collection  [ Luo et al., 2026 ] .

Retargeted body states provide reference goals and expert state–goal pairs for the conditional prior; robot interaction supplies the state–action transitions used to learn Dynamic and Successor.
Half of the rollout environments follow moving reference goals in 250-step windows, with randomized initial position and heading.
The remaining environments collect fixed-goal behavior, including translations of up to 3 m and heading changes of up to    π  \pi   .

For actor and CPR/AUX critic updates, we replace this goal with probability    0.8  0.8   .

C.3  CPR and AUX guidance
Conditional policy regularization (CPR) follows the motion-prior approach of BFM-Zero  [ Li et al., 2026 ] .
A discriminator learns to distinguish reference state–goal pairs from collected state–goal pairs using logistic classification with a gradient penalty.
Its reward trains a goal-conditioned critic    C  C    that favors behavior compatible with the motion prior.
An auxiliary critic    A  A    learns a physical-execution reward combining torque, action smoothness, joint-limit, contact, foot-orientation, and slippage penalties.
Both critics use the Bellman target
y  t  j   =   r  t  j   +   γ  t     Q  ¯   j    (   s   t  +  1    ,   z  g   ,   a   t  +  1    )   ,  j  ∈   {  CPR  ,  AUX  }   ,   a   t  +  1    ∼   π  θ    (  ⋅  ∣   o   t  +  1    ,   z  g   )   ,   y_{t}^{j}=r_{t}^{j}+\gamma_{t}\overline{Q}^{\,j}(s_{t+1},z_{g},a_{t+1}),\qquad j\in\{\mathrm{CPR},\mathrm{AUX}\},\quad a_{t+1}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid o_{t+1},z_{g}),
(31)
where      Q  CPR   =  C   Q^{\mathrm{CPR}}=C    and      Q  AUX   =  A   Q^{\mathrm{AUX}}=A   ; the physical reward is scale-normalized.
For scalar ensemble predictions      q  1   ,  …  ,   q  m    q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}   , define
Pess  κ   ⁡   (  q  )    =     1  m   ​    ∑  i    q  i     −    κ   m  ⁡   (   m  −  1   )     ​    ∑   i  ≠  j     |    q  i   −   q  j    |       .   \operatorname{Pess}_{\kappa}(q)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}q_{i}-\frac{\kappa}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}|q_{i}-q_{j}|.

Using     κ  =  0.25   \kappa=0.25    for this ensemble and for the two-member critics, the complete actor objective is
ℒ  π   =   −    1  d   ​   𝔼   ​   [    q  −   +   w  ⁡   (    0.005  ​   C  −    +   0.005  ​   A  −     )     ]      ,   w  =    sg   ⁡   [   𝔼  ​   |   q  −   |    ]      ,   \mathcal{L}_{\pi}=-\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\!\left[q^{-}+w\left(0.005C^{-}+0.005A^{-}\right)\right],\qquad w=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\mathbb{E}|q^{-}|\right],

Appendix D  Learning Dynamics
D.1  Convergence of posture and world alignment
Q2 (Section   4.2  ) compares the final learned behaviors; Figure   7   follows their acquisition throughout training.
We compare BeJEPA with the merged predictor and two split models that stop either Dynamic’s or Successor’s gradient to the encoder, evaluating their actors directly under matched tracking and fixed-goal protocols.
Local posture tracking improves quickly across models, while global tracking and fixed-goal errors expose differences in learning world alignment.
The merged predictor must learn immediate action effects and goal-conditioned continuation together, without a separately supervised physical transition model.
The split model with Dynamic’s encoder gradient stopped benefits from separating these predictions, but its representation receives no direct physical-prediction gradient; world alignment remains slower and less accurate than in full BeJEPA.
When only Dynamic among the two predictors updates the encoder, convergence is generally better than with the merged predictor or stopped Dynamic gradient.
This ablation retains a trained Successor for policy improvement, but removes its influence on the representation.
Its remaining fixed-goal position and heading errors indicate that physical predictability alone is insufficient for accurate longer-term goal reaching.
Full BeJEPA combines physical supervision with goal-conditioned continuation learning in the shared encoder, yielding faster world alignment and lower residual goal errors.

Early learning: physical prediction establishes the latent coordinates.
During the first 5 million transitions, the median weighted gradient ratio      G  D   /   G  S    G_{D}/G_{S}    is    2.63  2.63   ; during 5–10 million it is    1.02  1.02   , as Successor catches up (Figure   8  a–c).

Can the policy jointly reach a world position and a requested body posture?
We command standing, arms-out, and shallow-squat goals at displaced positions and headings, and track root-position, yaw, and joint-angle errors throughout the approach and subsequent holding period.
Figure   9   resolves the initial adjustment densely and follows the same executions after arrival.

The illustrated postures receive the same position and heading commands.
The initial joint-error response differs across postures: it rises from zero for standing, but falls for arms out and shallow squat.

E.3  Separating destination from arrival heading
Can the robot reach destinations in different directions while independently controlling its arrival heading?
We place stationary posture goals on a circle around the initial base position.
In one condition, target headings point radially outward; in the other, every destination shares the same world heading.
The resulting paths and heading vectors distinguish control of world position from simply walking forward along the body’s facing direction.
