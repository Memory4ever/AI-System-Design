Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models are rapidly expanding what individual
robots can accomplish, yet many real-world manipulation settings require
multiple arms to operate as a coordinated system. Existing approaches organize
multi-arm coordination in different ways, including centralized joint policies,
high-level task or role assignment, and locally executed policies that react
to teammates. However, how complete action-producing VLAs should explicitly
interact with one another during action generation remains largely unexplored.
We introduce VLA-Fabric, which organizes one complete VLA per arm while
preserving local perception and action ownership, and enables coordination
through learned interaction across policy boundaries. We first systematically
study where such interaction should occur, how peer information should be
aggregated, and how the interaction should be learned, revealing complementary
coordination functions spanning shared context, peer-specific latent
information, and action-stage interaction. We then re-instantiate these
functions in a structurally different VLA architecture, showing that the
coordination principle is not tied to a particular tensor layout or backbone
implementation. Finally, we compose teams of three and four arms
and evaluate them in simulation and physical experiments.
On all four physical tasks, Full interaction improves success over
independently trained local policies by 16–40 percentage points.
Together, these results suggest that explicit interaction among complete VLA
policies offers a scalable path toward networked embodied intelligence.
1  Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models are advancing the capabilities of individual
robots by connecting visual and language understanding with learned action
generation  ( Brohan et al., 2023 ;  Kim et al., 2024 ;  Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ) . Many important robotic applications,
however, require several manipulators to contribute to a common physical
objective. Cooperative manufacturing, multi-part assembly, shared-load handling,
and flexible robotic workcells motivate systems in which multiple arms work
together  ( Dio et al., 2024 ;  Huang et al., 2025 ;  Tian et al., 2025 ) . As pretrained VLAs become more
capable, their progress creates an opportunity beyond improving a single robot:
organizing multiple action-producing policies into a collaborative system.
The ability of each constituent model to perceive and act does not, by itself,
specify how those models should work together.
Recent multi-arm VLAs pursue this goal through different organizations.
RDT-1B, ET-VLA, TwinVLA, and Dexora place cross-arm dependencies within a joint
policy  ( Liu et al., 2025 ;  Li and Peng, 2025 ;  Im et al., 2026 ;  Zhang et al., 2026b ) .
Other approaches coordinate at different levels: MA-VLA assigns
atomic behaviors to individual arms  ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) , while CHORUS demonstrates
implicit decentralized coordination through locally executed policies that
observe and react to teammates without runtime messages  ( Doshi et al., 2026 ) .
These approaches establish effective collaboration, with coordination inside a
joint policy, above the action policy through behavior assignment, or indirectly
through observations of teammates. This raises an organizational challenge as
the number and roles of manipulators grow. How can a multi-arm system remain
modular at the level of individual manipulators while still supporting tightly
coupled collaboration?
A natural way to obtain such modularity is to organize the system around
complete arm-local policies rather than a growing joint policy. We therefore
treat each manipulator and its complete VLA as a self-contained execution unit
that perceives its local context and produces its own actions.
Local information can remain with the policy that uses it,
and larger teams can be formed by composing additional execution units.
Crucially,  local does not mean isolated . Policies participating in the
same physical task still need to adapt their actions to one another. The key
challenge is to connect these local policies through explicit interaction
without giving up their local action ownership.
To realize this local-but-connected organization, we introduce

First, we establish an effective VLA-Fabric interaction architecture through a
broad controlled study. Starting from a decomposable bimanual VLA with
pretrained single-arm streams  ( Im et al., 2026 ) , we separate its original cross-arm
dependencies. We systematically explore a combinatorial design space covering
interaction entry points, aggregation operators, and fine-tuning strategies.
The study shows that exposing more peer information does not by itself ensure
coordination; interaction structure and adaptation determine how effectively
local policies use one another’s information. This exploration identifies
complementary interaction at shared-context, peer-specific intermediate, and
action-generation stages.
Second, we demonstrate that the same VLA-Fabric interaction paradigm can be
re-instantiated in substantially different VLA architectures. We use     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}
( Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 )  as a second realization, whose multimodal processing and action
generation differ markedly from the Eagle2 VLM-based SingleVLA used in our
initial study. Accordingly, the same coordination functions are realized at
architecture-appropriate computational boundaries while preserving complete
local policies with explicit policy interaction. The resulting
π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}   -Fabric system enables multiple complete     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    policies to
interact and achieves strong performance across several
bimanual tasks. Its stronger results suggest that advances in the constituent
VLA may translate into stronger collaborative systems. This observation is
consistent with the value of pretrained single-arm capabilities demonstrated
by TwinVLA  ( Im et al., 2026 ) .
Third, we establish the extensibility of VLA-Fabric from bimanual coordination
to larger teams of complete VLA policies. Additional manipulators are
incorporated by composing additional local policies and extending their
interaction. We instantiate three- and four-arm VLA-Fabric teams and evaluate
them on simulation tasks with different coordination demands.
We further validate the same organization on four physical
three- and four-arm tasks, where Full interaction consistently improves over
independently trained local policies.
Our contributions are:
VLA-Fabric interaction architecture.
We introduce VLA-Fabric, which organizes complete arm-local VLAs as the execution
units of multi-arm collaboration and connects them through learned
representation interaction. A broad controlled architecture study establishes
effective interaction structure, aggregation, and adaptation for coordinated
action generation.
Cross-architecture transfer of VLA-Fabric.
We re-instantiate the same interaction paradigm in Eagle2 VLM-based SingleVLA
and     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    despite their substantially different policy computations.
The resulting systems demonstrate a common coordination organization across
distinct VLA architectures.
Composable multi-arm scalability.
We scale VLA-Fabric from two complete VLA policies to teams of three and four
while preserving local action generation and explicit policy interaction.
We validate the resulting organization across simulation and physical
multi-arm tasks with differing coordination demands.
2  Related Work
Cooperative physical manipulation.
Multi-arm robotics has studied coordinated transport, cooperative assembly,
and learned dual-arm manipulation under shared physical constraints
( Dio et al., 2024 ;  Huang et al., 2025 ;  Tian et al., 2025 ) . These settings require compatible
arm motions under a shared load or coordinated holding, alignment, and
placement during assembly. Planning and control address the resulting task
and motion dependencies. Our work studies the complementary policy-level
problem of organizing complete learned VLAs into a collaborative system.
Multi-arm VLA coordination.
Multi-arm VLAs organize coordination through several mechanisms.
RDT-1B, ET-VLA, TwinVLA, and Dexora resolve cross-arm dependencies within
joint bimanual policies  ( Liu et al., 2025 ;  Li and Peng, 2025 ;  Im et al., 2026 ;  Zhang et al., 2026b ) .
MA-VLA uses high-level behavior assignment  ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) , while CHORUS
coordinates locally executed policies through teammate observations
without inference-time communication  ( Doshi et al., 2026 ) .
VLA-Fabric studies explicit learned interaction between complete arm-local
VLA policies during action generation.
Learned interaction among embodied policies.
LatentToM combines ego and consensus representations in decentralized
diffusion policies and supports explicit consensus exchange for cooperative
manipulation  ( He et al., 2025 ) . Its no-message variant instead infers teammate
behavior from local observations.
CommNet, DIAL, and TarMAC establish broader foundations for learning
communication jointly with agent policies  ( Sukhbaatar et al., 2016 ;  Foerster et al., 2016 ;  Das et al., 2019 ) .
Here, each agent is a complete pretrained VLA containing perception,
multimodal reasoning, and action generation. We study which internal
coordination functions should connect these policies and whether the resulting
interaction organization transfers across substantially different VLA
architectures.
3  VLA-Fabric: Learning Interaction among Local VLA Agents
3.1  Local VLA Agents and the Interaction Problem
VLA-Fabric organizes multi-arm manipulation around complete local execution
units, termed  VLA agents . Each agent runs a complete VLA policy and
owns one arm’s perception-action loop, including its policy parameters,
inference state, private observations, proprioception, and action output.
Selected peer representations condition local action generation through
learned interaction. We use  policy  for the learned mapping

To study interaction between complete VLA agents, we expose the cross-arm
dependencies of a centralized policy while preserving its single-arm priors.
TwinVLA provides two identifiable pretrained SingleVLA branches
( Im et al., 2026 ) , which we separate into local policies and reconnect through
explicit representation interfaces.
Figure   2  (a) organizes the design space around three
decisions: where policies interact, how peer information is combined,
and how the resulting interaction is adapted.
Interaction entry.  Candidates range from Independent policies and
Private K/V exchange to additional Common and action-side interaction.
Interaction operator.  Common exchange uses one-way transfer,
symmetric averaging (AVG), or operator-level synchronization (RAW);
action-side interaction uses Linear or Transformer fusion.
Interaction adaptation.  Single-stage enables the candidate
architecture from the start of fine-tuning; Common-first initializes it
from a Common-only policy before introducing the remaining interactions.
Nine Single-stage designs and seven matched Common-first variants per task
yield 32 runs on Handover Box and Shoes Table.
Checkpoints are selected on 200 development conditions and evaluated on
200 disjoint paired conditions, as summarized in
Figure   2  (b).
Where to interact.
Private K/V alone improves over Independent policies on both tasks, and
richer configurations obtain further gains from Common and action-side
interaction. Although additional inputs do not always help, these comparisons
support complementary interaction across policy stages rather than one
insertion point for every coordination requirement.
How to interact.
The tasks impose different coordination demands, against which the operator
choices expose different advantages. Symmetric Common exchange improves over
one-way transfer in the Common-first comparisons, while action-side fusion
provides further gains in several configurations. Neither AVG versus RAW
nor Linear versus Transformer yields a consistent winner across both tasks.
These observations motivate retaining complementary interaction functions
that policies can exploit as coordination demands change, rather than
defining VLA-Fabric around one task’s peak configuration.
How to adapt.
Common-first consistently improves the matched configurations, showing that
interaction structure and its adaptation should be considered jointly when
learning richer cross-policy coordination.
The interaction functions and their adaptation recipe thus play distinct
roles. The former specify which peer representations enter local computation,
how they are combined, and where they condition action generation.
The latter determines how policies learn to exploit this information during
task adaptation.
Selecting a reusable realization.
The targeted longer-run comparison finds no consistent advantage for
Transformer action fusion over the simpler Linear alternative.
We therefore carry forward RAW Common, Private K/V, and Linear action
interaction as the canonical SingleVLA-Fabric realization.
This choice yields a compact realization that retains the three complementary
coordination functions used in the subsequent transfer and scaling studies.
3.3  From Architecture Search to Functional Interaction
The architecture study motivates three complementary interaction roles.
Shared semantic context  combines Common representations into
team-level context.
Peer latent context  exposes peer K/V to local queries, allowing each
receiver to select information relevant to its own computation.
Action intent  conditions local action generation on the peer’s
action-side state.
In the selected SingleVLA realization, these roles correspond to RAW Common,
Private K/V, and Linear fusion of final  readouts .
They define coordination functions rather than fixed tensor locations;
their formal operators are given in Appendix   A.2  .
Together, these three roles establish the core VLA-Fabric interaction
paradigm in the SingleVLA realization.

Relay3, Relay4, and Frame4 impose tighter dependencies through handover timing

execution, beyond assigning each arm a role or target. The physical study
therefore examines whether complete local policies can accommodate these
shared constraints under perception and control uncertainty.
The results demonstrate that the multi-VLA organization remains effective
under these conditions, while reliable execution of the more coupled behaviors
remains an important direction for improvement.
Across simulation and hardware, the recurring pattern suggests that
interaction benefits reflect physical coupling among agents, beyond arm
count alone.
7  Discussion and Limitations
VLA-Fabric makes a complete local VLA an explicit organizational unit of
multi-arm coordination.
Local perception and action ownership are preserved, while collaboration is
learned through representation exchange across policy boundaries.
The same coordination roles can be re-instantiated across VLA architectures,
and additional policies can form larger teams without redefining a global
joint action space.
This separates progress in constituent models from progress in team
organization, allowing the two to advance together.
Stronger VLAs can improve the execution units, while interaction mechanisms
determine which information is shared, when it enters policy computation,
and with which peers.
Across the evaluated architectures, tasks, and team sizes, this common
organization supports reuse of the coordination principle while allowing
the underlying policy computation to change.
Our experiments use homogeneous teams with fixed roles and up to
four arms, leaving changing team compositions and heterogeneous VLAs as
directions for broader composability.
Broader composition will require interfaces that accommodate differences in
representation and action generation while maintaining clear local policy
boundaries.
Such interfaces must align the meaning and timing of exchanged information
across policies with different internal computations.
The current interaction structure is static; adapting content, topology, and
frequency to task phase and agent participation could make coordination more
selective.
Long-horizon, high-precision manipulation remains difficult, as illustrated
by Arch Building, motivating complementary improvements in planning and
control of the constituent VLAs.
Larger teams also motivate more selective representation exchange and
interaction mechanisms that accommodate changing role assignments and
available communication resources.
8  Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced VLA-Fabric to address how multiple complete VLA
policies can collaborate as a coordinated system for increasingly demanding
multi-arm manipulation.
Each arm-local VLA remains a complete execution unit, preserving its own
perception and action generation while acquiring collaborative capability
through learned representation interaction.
We established this organization through systematic exploration of interaction
entry, aggregation, and adaptation, then re-instantiated its coordination
functions in a structurally different VLA.
Extending the same principle to three- and four-agent teams in simulation and
physical systems completes a framework for discovering, transferring, and
scaling inter-VLA coordination.
This organization allows individual VLA capability and multi-VLA coordination
to advance together, connecting improvements in local models with the
mechanisms through which they collaborate.
Beyond the operators studied here, it opens directions in coordination-aware
model design, adaptive representation exchange, communication-efficient
deployment, and heterogeneous VLA teams.
VLA-Fabric thereby offers a concrete step from increasingly capable individual
embodied models toward networked embodied intelligence.

Appendix A  Interaction Architecture Discovery
A.1  SingleVLA Decomposition and Design Space
TwinVLA’s two pretrained SingleVLA branches expose identifiable cross-arm
dependencies while preserving each arm’s pretrained prior  ( Im et al., 2026 ) .
We separate these branches into complete local policies and vary the
representations through which they interact. All candidates originate from
the same pretrained SingleVLA policies, using an Eagle2 VLM-based backbone.
Table   A1   defines the nine interaction designs.
One-way Common shares the Common keys and values from one designated arm.
AVG Common averages the two agents’ Common keys and values before they
condition private attention. RAW Common synchronizes Common computation at operator boundaries,
before the agents proceed with their local computation.
Both symmetric variants retain Private K/V interaction.
The Linear and Transformer variants additionally combine role-ordered
action-side readouts before each arm’s local action head.
Thus aggregation, receiver-specific latent conditioning, and late-action
capacity can be varied without changing local observation or action ownership.

A.3  Experimental Setup
The controlled study uses ALOHA Handover Box and Shoes Table.
Each task includes nine Single-stage designs and seven Common-first variants,
giving 32 model–task runs. Independent and Private K/V lack Common
interaction and serve as shared controls across the two adaptation settings.
Single-stage enables the target interaction architecture directly during
fine-tuning. Common-first initializes the policies with Common interaction
before introducing the remaining interaction functions.
The total adaptation budgets are 40k updates for Single-stage and 50k for
Common-first, including initialization. With these unequal budgets, the
comparison measures the sensitivity of each design to the complete adaptation recipe.
All variants freeze the vision encoder and adapt the language backbone,
action heads, and enabled interaction modules. Interaction operators are
evaluated in one differentiable training graph, with the native action
objective applied to every arm. A normalized description is
ℒ  act   =   1  N    ∑   i  =  1   N    ℒ  VLA    (    a  ^   i   t  :    t  +  H   −  1     ,   a  i   t  :    t  +  H   −  1     )   .   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VLA}}\!\left(\widehat{a}_{i}^{t:t+H-1},a_{i}^{t:t+H-1}\right).
(A.4)
Here    H  H    denotes the predicted action horizon. The implementation sums the
arm-local losses, differing by a constant scale at fixed team size.
The prediction target is unchanged across interaction designs.
Models are selected using 200 development conditions and evaluated on
200 disjoint paired conditions per task. The paired conditions are shared
across designs, allowing comparison under the same task initializations.
Success is the proportion of episodes satisfying the task’s completion
criterion; episodes reaching the horizon without completion remain failures.

Appendix B  Cross-Architecture Transfer of VLA-Fabric
B.1  From SingleVLA to     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}
Action-generation boundary.
Both VLA families generate continuous actions with flow matching
( Lipman et al., 2022 ) . SingleVLA uses an Eagle2-based VLM and a separate
DiT action head  ( Li et al., 2025 ;  Im et al., 2026 ) . Its backbone readout is fused once
before the two local action heads run their iterative samplers.
In     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}   , peer-action interaction instead operates on the evolving
Action Expert suffix within each flow step.
Common and Private Prefix interaction.
Each arm retains a complete     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    policy with its own parameters,
proprioception, action-generation state, and output head.
Common interaction synchronizes the Common-owned multimodal PaliGemma
prefix at seven operator boundaries in each of 18 layers.
Private Prefix K/V remain associated with their sending role and augment
the context available to the receiver’s Action Expert.
The receiver keeps its query local, so peer context is selected according
to its own evolving action computation. Raw private images are not exchanged.
Receiver-local action interaction.
A separate peer-action attention branch adds a gated residual before the
Action Expert feed-forward block. For receiver    i  i    and peer    j  j   , let    k  k
index the expert layer and    s  s    the flow step. Peer action-side keys and
values condition the local action-side query:
r  i   k  ,  s    =    α  i  k   ​   W  i  k   ​    Attn   ⁡   (   Q   i  ,  act    k  ,  s    ,   K   j  ,  act    k  ,  s    ,   V   j  ,  act    k  ,  s    )      ,    h  i   k  ,  s    =    h   i  ,  core    k  ,  s    +   r  i   k  ,  s       .   r_{i}^{k,s}=\alpha_{i}^{k}W_{i}^{k}\operatorname{Attn}\!\left(Q_{i,\mathrm{act}}^{k,s},K_{j,\mathrm{act}}^{k,s},V_{j,\mathrm{act}}^{k,s}\right),\qquad h_{i}^{k,s}=h_{i,\mathrm{core}}^{k,s}+r_{i}^{k,s}.
(B.1)
Here     h   i  ,  core    k  ,  s    h_{i,\mathrm{core}}^{k,s}    is the receiver’s core attention state;
W  i  k   W_{i}^{k}    and     α  i  k   \alpha_{i}^{k}    are its output projection and scalar gate.
The gate is zero-initialized and learned without a hard bound, initially
preserving the core computation while allowing the interaction to develop.
Peer attention has a separate softmax from core attention and is refreshed
at every Action Expert layer and flow step.
The update conditions the receiver’s evolving action representations throughout
the iterative generation process.
The correspondence with Appendix   A.2   is functional:
Common forms shared context, Private Prefix K/V provide receiver-specific
context, and the residual branch supplies action-stage interaction.
Each homogeneous team uses interaction parameters fine-tuned within its own
VLA architecture.

Appendix C  Scaling VLA-Fabric to Larger Teams
C.1  From Two to    N  N    Local VLA Agents
The interaction functions in Appendix   A.2   extend
to a team by expanding each receiver’s peer set.
For the evaluated fully connected teams,
𝒩  ⁡   (  i  )    =    {  1  ,  …  ,  N  }   ∖   {  i  }     \mathcal{N}(i)=\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{i\}   .
Each added agent retains its complete policy, local proprioception,
action-generation state, and local action head.
Common aggregation includes the additional agent, and Private Prefix K/V
are concatenated in the team’s fixed role order.
The action branch attends to each peer separately, then concatenates the
resulting contexts before the receiver-local projection:
u   i  ​  j    k  ,  s    =    Attn   ⁡   (   Q   i  ,  act    k  ,  s    ,   K   j  ,  act    k  ,  s    ,   V   j  ,  act    k  ,  s    )     ,    r  i   k  ,  s    =    α  i  k   ​   W  i  k   ​   Concat   j  ∈   𝒩  ⁡   (  i  )     role   ​   u   i  ​  j    k  ,  s       .   u_{ij}^{k,s}=\operatorname{Attn}\!\left(Q_{i,\mathrm{act}}^{k,s},K_{j,\mathrm{act}}^{k,s},V_{j,\mathrm{act}}^{k,s}\right),\quad r_{i}^{k,s}=\alpha_{i}^{k}W_{i}^{k}\operatorname{Concat}^{\mathrm{role}}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}u_{ij}^{k,s}.
(C.1)
This extends Equation   B.1   while keeping both
the receiver’s query and the attention normalization for each peer local.
The local observation and action dimensions remain unchanged as arms are
added, although peer context and role-dependent modules can grow.
Each task and team configuration is fine-tuned separately.

Beyond the final comparisons in Table   2  , a direct-Full

Additional SingleVLA-Fabric teams, based on the Eagle2 VLM, perform

C.4  Physical-Robot Setup and Evaluation
Hardware and observations.
The platform uses AgileX PiPER robot arms, each with six actuated joints
and a gripper, and Intel RealSense D455 cameras.
Each policy receives its local wrist RGB image, joint positions, and gripper
state. One global RGB view provides shared scene context in both Full and
Independent settings.
Demonstrations and action interface.
Each VLA generates local chunks of six
joint-position targets and one gripper command per arm.
Each task uses 50 physical demonstrations for task-specific     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}
fine-tuning.
