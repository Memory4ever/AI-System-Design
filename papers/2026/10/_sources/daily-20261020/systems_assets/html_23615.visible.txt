Shan Zhang  4   ,   Na Zhao  3   ,   Zechao Li  1

Abstract
Latent world models learn action-conditioned state transitions in a compact representation space, providing a predictive model for visual planning and control. However, planning with these models typically requires costly test-time optimization over action sequences. We propose to replace this online search with a goal-conditioned inverse model that directly predicts actions from current and goal observations. A key challenge is that action prediction alone neither ensures that goal representations encode action-relevant information nor that predicted actions are consistent with the learned latent prediction model. We introduce Hindsight Inverse JEPA Planning (HIJP), a search-free framework that addresses both aspects during training. HIJP relabels future observations along offline trajectories as goals, providing action supervision across multiple temporal horizons. By retaining gradients through the goal representations, this supervision directly shapes the visual representation. HIJP further feeds predicted actions into the latent predictor model and aligns the resulting next-state representations with encoded successor observations, enforcing action-transition consistency. At inference time, the inverse dynamics model and latent predictor models are applied alternately to generate multi-step action sequences without online search. Across four visual control tasks, HIJP achieves 86.8% overall success and a 1.9–16.5× planning speedup over search-based planning.
1  Introduction
Latent world models encode high-dimensional visual observations into compact latent representations and learn an action-conditioned latent predictor from offline trajectories, providing a foundation for visual planning and control  ( Zhou et al., 2025 ;  Maes et al., 2026 ;  Zhang et al., 2025 ) .
However, planning with these models typically requires optimizers such as CEM  ( Rubinstein, 1999 )  or MPPI  ( Williams et al., 2017 )  to repeatedly generate and evaluate candidate action sequences at each replanning step, which requires substantial test-time computation  ( Pinneri et al., 2021 ) .
Learning a direct mapping from the current state and goal to actions therefore provides a promising alternative for improving the planning efficiency of latent world models  ( Sun et al., 2026 ;  Nguyen et al., 2026 ) .
Recent work has introduced action supervision to improve latent world models’ control capabilities.
SMWM learns action-relevant representations through inverse dynamics supervision  ( Ivashkov et al., 2026 ) , while Delta-JEPA  ( Zhang et al., 2026 )  recovers actions from latent state differences, encouraging representations to retain action-related state changes.
GC-IDM  ( Nguyen et al., 2026 )  further learns a goal-conditioned action mapping on frozen representations, while INTACT  ( Sun et al., 2026 ) incorporates local and future-goal action supervision into end-to-end training, supporting search-free recursive control.
These approaches demonstrate the value of action supervision for goal-conditioned control. However, accurate action prediction alone does not necessarily provide all the supervision needed for search-free planning.
We identify two aspects that remain underconstrained.
First, goal-conditioned action supervision should not only train an inverse model to predict actions, but also help shape visual representations to capture how actions relate current states to future goals.
Offline trajectories naturally contain such relations at multiple temporal scales: for the same current state and demonstrated action, different future observations define goals at different temporal horizons.
However, existing training schemes  ( Sun et al., 2026 ;  Wang et al., 2026a )  exploit only a subset of these relations, for example by conditioning action prediction on a fixed future endpoint.
More generally, treating the goal representation only as a conditioning target limits the extent to which action supervision can shape goal-side visual features.
Second, accurate action prediction alone does not ensure that the predicted action induces the correct transition under the learned latent predictor.
The inverse dynamics model and the latent predictor model are typically supervised separately, with the latter conditioned on demonstrated actions  ( Ivashkov et al., 2026 ;  Sun et al., 2026 ) .
During search-free rollout planning, however, these two mappings are applied sequentially: the inverse dynamics model predicts an action toward the goal, and the latent predictor model uses this predicted action to advance the latent state for subsequent action generation.
The resulting inverse-to-predictor composition is therefore directly involved in planning, yet is not explicitly constrained by separate action and latent prediction objectives.
This motivates extending action supervision beyond action prediction itself, using it both to shape goal-conditioned representations and to couple predicted actions with their induced latent transitions.
Based on this principle, we propose Hindsight Inverse JEPA Planning (HIJP), which jointly trains a visual encoder, a goal-conditioned inverse dynamics model, and a latent predictor model end-to-end. HIJP relabels future observations within each trajectory window as goals, exposing the inverse objective to diverse state–goal relations across temporal horizons. Gradients are retained through the goal representations, allowing inverse-action supervision to directly shape the visual features used for goal-conditioned action prediction. We further feed the predicted actions into the latent predictor and align the resulting successor latents with the encodings of the next observations. This action-transition consistency objective jointly updates the inverse and predictor models, directly coupling action generation with its induced latent transition. In this way, HIJP trains the same inverse-to-predictor mapping that is repeatedly applied during planning. At test time, the inverse dynamics module and latent predictor model alternate to generate multi-step action sequences, which are executed through closed-loop replanning without online action search.
We evaluate HIJP on four visual control tasks under four configurations of goal temporal offsets and execution budgets. HIJP achieves an overall success rate of     86.8  %   86.8\%   , outperforming the strongest baseline by    4.9  4.9    percentage points. Ablations on hindsight goal sampling, training objectives, and gradient routing examine how goal-side representation learning and inverse - predictor coupling contribute to control performance. Beyond success rates, we also evaluate efficiency and the temporal smoothness of executed actions. With matched numbers of replanning calls, HIJP achieves a    1.9  1.9   –    16.5  ×   16.5\times    planning speedup over LeWM. Under the most challenging setting, its executed actions exhibit lower jerk than those produced by CEM using the same representations, indicating smoother executed controls.
2  Related work
Latent World Models
Latent world models learn action-conditioned changes in representation space to support goal-directed planning from offline visual trajectories. Existing approaches span pretrained visual features, end-to-end representation learning, and large-scale video pretraining  ( Zhou et al., 2025 ;  Maes et al., 2026 ;  Assran et al., 2025 ;  Bo et al., 2026 ) .
Systematic studies further investigate how architectures, training objectives, and planning algorithms affect control performance  ( Terver et al., 2025 ) .   Gao and Xu (2026)   reduce forward-rollout costs through parallel prediction conditioned on action prefixes.   Wang et al. (2026a)   use inverse dynamics and goal-conditioned action supervision to improve planning representations, while retaining CEM for control.
HIJP builds on latent forward dynamics and focuses on learning a goal-conditioned action mapping that can be directly deployed for control.
Inverse Dynamics and Goal-Conditioned Action Learning
Inverse dynamics and action-supervised objectives encourage representations to retain action-relevant state changes.
These objectives can also be combined with physical-state alignment to improve representations for control  ( Ivashkov et al., 2026 ;  Zhang et al., 2026 ;  Liu et al., 2026 ) .
Hindsight learning relabels achieved states along trajectories as goals, providing supervision for goal-conditioned policies and inverse dynamics learning  ( Andrychowicz et al., 2018 ;  Ghosh et al., 2019 ;  Sun et al., 2019 ) .
Goal-conditioned action-sequence prediction has also been used for visual planning  ( Paster et al., 2020 ) . Related theoretical studies analyze the benefits of inverse dynamics through bias–variance trade-offs and learning-problem complexity  ( Schäfer et al., 2026 ;  Morin et al., 2026 ) .
Within latent world models, GC-IDM ( Nguyen et al., 2026 )  learns a goal- and horizon-conditioned action mapping on frozen representations, while INTACT  ( Sun et al., 2026 )  jointly learns local and goal-conditioned action prediction to support search-free recursive control. HIJP follows this direction by enriching supervision through future-goal relabeling within trajectory windows.
Learned and Amortized Planning
Planning computation can be shifted to training by learning action priors, latent subgoals, or plan-update rules  ( Wang et al., 2026b ;  Cheng et al., 2026 ;  Masip et al., 2026 ;  Sommer and Schilling, 2026 ;  Tang et al., 2025 ) .
Another line of work first predicts future states or generates state trajectories and then recovers actions through inverse dynamics  ( Tian et al., 2025 ;  Xie et al., 2025 ;  Zeng et al., 2026 ;  Ajay et al., 2022 ;  Huang et al., 2026 ) .   Rakhimov et al. (2026)   jointly train state and action flows, allowing a single model to support multiple control inference modes.
HIJP alternates goal-conditioned action prediction with forward latent-state prediction to construct multi-step plans without online action search.
Consistency and Goal-Aware Planning Objectives
Consistency objectives also connect model prediction with planning.
Seo et al. (2026)   incorporate action-recovery errors from predicted transitions into the online planning cost, while   Bai and Xiong (2026)   combine temporal-progress learning with multi-step rollout consistency supervision.
HIJP focuses on the composed output of the goal-conditioned action module and the forward model. During training, it aligns the successor latent state predicted under the generated action with the encoding of the next observation in the recorded trajectory, allowing state-prediction errors to supervise action learning directly.
3  Methodology
Our goal is to extend action supervision beyond action prediction itself, such that it also shapes goal-conditioned visual representations and constrains the latent transitions induced by predicted actions.
HIJP jointly learns a visual encoder     f  θ   f_{\theta}   , a latent predictor model     g  ϕ   g_{\phi}   , and a goal-conditioned inverse dynamics model     h  ξ   h_{\xi}   .
Given offline visual trajectories, the inverse dynamics model learns to predict actions toward future goals, while the predictor predicts action-conditioned latent transitions.
We couple these components during training to supervise the local inverse-to-predictor composition repeatedly used in search-free planning.

(B) The hindsight-conditioned inverse dynamics model infers the action from current and future latent states. (C) The inferred action is reused to enforce action–transition consistency. All modules are jointly optimized using the prediction, regularization, inverse-dynamics, and action transition consistency losses.
3.1  Problem Formulation
Let     𝒟  =    {   τ   (  i  )    }    i  =  1   N    \mathcal{D}=\{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}    be a dataset of trajectories, where
τ  =   (   o  0   ,   𝐚  0   ,   o  1   ,  …  ,   𝐚   T  −  1    ,   o  T   )    .   \tau=(o_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0},o_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{T-1},o_{T}).
(1)
Here     o  t   o_{t}    is a visual observation and     𝐚  t   \mathbf{a}_{t}    is the action taken at time    t  t   .
Given a current observation     o  t   o_{t}    and a goal observation     o  g   o_{g}   , the task is to produce actions that reach the goal within a fixed interaction budget. Training uses recorded observations and actions, with future observations in each trajectory serving as hindsight goals, where reward annotations are not required.
Our planning procedure repeatedly composes two mappings. An inverse model predicts an action from the current latent state and goal latent state, and a latent predictor model predicts the latent state induced by that action. This composition motivates training the models together: action prediction must identify a useful action, while the resulting transition must agree with the observed dynamics.
3.2  Latent World Model
Following the prior work  ( Maes et al., 2026 ) , a visual encoder      f  θ   :   𝒪  →   ℝ  d     f_{\theta}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}    maps observations to latent states      z  t   =    f  θ   ​   (   o  t   )     z_{t}=f_{\theta}(o_{t})   , and an action-conditioned latent predictor model     g  ϕ   g_{\phi}    predicts the next latent state from a causal context of latent states and actions:
z  ^    t  +  1    =   g  ϕ    (   z     t  −  m   +  1   :  t    ,   a     t  −  m   +  1   :  t    )   ,   z   t  +  1    =   f  θ    (   o   t  +  1    )   .   \hat{z}_{t+1}=g_{\phi}\!\left(z_{t-m+1:t},a_{t-m+1:t}\right),\qquad z_{t+1}=f_{\theta}(o_{t+1}).
(2)
We train the latent prediction model on demonstrated transitions using
ℒ  pred   =   MSE  ⁡   (    z  ^    t  +  1    ,   z   t  +  1    )     .   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}=\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{z}_{t+1},z_{t+1}).
(3)
We also apply     ℒ  sigreg   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sigreg}}    to the encoded observations in each training window to discourage representational collapse.
We use a ViT-Tiny encoder trained from scratch and a causal Transformer model with context length     m  =  3   m=3   . An action encoder supplies the latent prediction model with action embeddings. Architecture and optimization details are given in Appendix    B  . The same model also accepts the shorter context used for the one-step consistency objective below.
The latent prediction model learns how demonstrated actions transform latent states. Our next objective is complementary: to learn how actions can be inferred from current and goal representations, while allowing this action supervision to shape the representations themselves.
3.3  Hindsight-conditioned Inverse Dynamics
A trajectory contains multiple valid future goals for a given observation. To expose these relations during training, we sample a window of    W  W    observations, an anchor    i  i    and a future offset    κ  \kappa   :
i  ∼  Unif   {  0  ,  …  ,  W  −  2  }   ,  κ  ∣  i  ∼  Unif   {  1  ,  …  ,  W  −  1  −  i  }   .   i\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{0,\ldots,W-2\},\qquad\kappa\mid i\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,W-1-i\}.
(4)
Each sampled pair therefore defines a goal-conditioned action-learning problem at a potentially different temporal horizon. Unlike using only the endpoint of a trajectory window, this construction exposes the inverse objective to diverse relations between current observations and future goals using the same offline data.
The observation at     i  +  κ   i+\kappa    becomes the hindsight goal. The goal-conditioned inverse model predicts the demonstrated action at the anchor:
a  ^   i   =    h  ξ   ​   (   z  i   ,   z   i  +  κ    )     ,    ℒ  inv   =   MAE  ⁡   (    a  ^   i   ,   a  i   )      .   \hat{a}_{i}=h_{\xi}(z_{i},z_{i+\kappa}),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}=\operatorname{MAE}(\hat{a}_{i},a_{i}).
(5)
Although the sampled goal may be several steps ahead, the target is the immediate action     a  i   a_{i}   , not the intervening action sequence. Each training window therefore supplies action supervision under goals at different temporal offsets.
Gradients from     ℒ  inv   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}    flow through both     z  i   z_{i}    and     z   i  +  κ    z_{i+\kappa}   . Consequently, the inverse objective trains not only     h  ξ   h_{\xi}   , but also the visual features used to represent the current observation and the future goal. We implement     h  ξ   h_{\xi}    as an MLP operating on the concatenated latent pair.
3.4  Action Transition Consistency
The inverse model can predict an action close to the demonstrated action without ensuring that this prediction produces the observed transition under the learned predictor model. This distinction matters at planning time, where the model receives predicted rather than demonstrated actions. We therefore pass the predicted action through     g  ϕ   g_{\phi}   :
z  ~    i  +  1    =    g  ϕ   ​   (   z  i   ,    a  ^   i   )     ,     a  ^   i   =    h  ξ   ​   (   z  i   ,   z   i  +  κ    )      ,   \tilde{z}_{i+1}=g_{\phi}\!\left(z_{i},\hat{a}_{i}\right),\qquad\hat{a}_{i}=h_{\xi}(z_{i},z_{i+\kappa}),
(6)
and align the result with the encoded one-step successor:
ℒ  cyc   =    MSE   ⁡   (    z  ~    i  +  1    ,   sg  ⁡   [   z   i  +  1    ]    )     ,   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\operatorname{MSE}\!\left(\tilde{z}_{i+1},\operatorname{sg}[z_{i+1}]\right),
(7)
where    sg  \operatorname{sg}    denotes stop-gradient.
This objective uses the one-step successor     z   i  +  1    z_{i+1}    even when the hindsight goal     z   i  +  κ    z_{i+\kappa}    is farther away.
It allows     L  cyc   L_{\mathrm{cyc}}    to update the inverse head, action encoder, latent prediction model, and the anchor-side visual representation. Thus, the consistency objective does not merely use the prediction model to provide an error signal to the inverse dynamics model; it jointly adapts action generation and the latent predictor around their composed prediction.
This directly supervises the inverse-to-latent predictor transition that is repeatedly composed during search-free planning.
3.5  Joint Objective and Search-free Planning
Joint training.
The full training objective combines latent prediction, representation regularization, hindsight-conditioned action prediction, and action-transition consistency:
ℒ  =    ℒ  pred   +    λ  reg   ​   ℒ  sigreg    +    λ  inv   ​   ℒ  inv    +    λ  cyc   ​   ℒ  cyc      .   \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}+\lambda_{\text{reg}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{sigreg}}+\lambda_{\text{inv}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}+\lambda_{\text{cyc}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cyc}}.
(8)
All components are optimized jointly, and all terms are averaged over their corresponding batch, temporal, sampled-pair, and feature dimensions before weighting.
For all tasks, the loss weights are set to      λ  reg   =  0.09   \lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}=0.09   ,      λ  inv   =  1.0   \lambda_{\mathrm{inv}}=1.0   , and
λ  cyc   =  0.05   \lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}=0.05    for TwoRoom and    0.10  0.10    for the remaining tasks.
Search-free planning.
At planning time, we use the inverse dynamics model and latent prediction models in the same order in which they are coupled during training. At each replanning point, the latest observation and fixed goal observation are encoded as      z  t   =    f  θ   ​   (   o  t   )     z_{t}=f_{\theta}(o_{t})    and      z  g   =    f  θ   ​   (   o  g   )     z_{g}=f_{\theta}(o_{g})   . Starting from       z  ^   0   =   z  t    \hat{z}_{0}=z_{t}   , we construct an    H  H   -step plan by alternating goal-conditioned action prediction and latent prediction:
a  j   =   h  ξ    (    z  ^   j   ,   z  g   )   ,    z  ^    j  +  1    =   g  ϕ    (    z  ^     ℓ  j   :  j    ,   a    ℓ  j   :  j    )   ,   ℓ  j   =  max   (  0  ,  j  −  m  +  1  )   ,  j  =  0  ,  …  ,  H  −  1  .   a_{j}=h_{\xi}(\hat{z}_{j},z_{g}),\qquad\hat{z}_{j+1}=g_{\phi}\!\left(\hat{z}_{\ell_{j}:j},a_{\ell_{j}:j}\right),\quad\ell_{j}=\max(0,j-m+1),\quad j=0,\ldots,H-1.
(9)
The inverse model therefore predicts each action from the current imagined state and fixed goal, while the latent prediction model advances the imagined state under that predicted action. We use     H  =  5   H=5   , execute the resulting action sequence, observe the environment again, and repeat the procedure in a receding-horizon manner. Each replanning call requires only one inverse-to-latent prediction rollout, without sampling or iteratively refining candidate action sequences.
4  Experiments
We conduct experiments on four tasks to answer the following key questions: (i) whether our method achieves strong goal-reaching performance, particularly for long-horizon goals; (ii) how much inference speed is gained by replacing the CEM search at test time with a single amortized rollout; (iii) how hindsight goal relabeling, inverse-action supervision, gradient routing, and predicted-action transition consistency contribute to control performance; and (iv) how HIJP and CEM differ in
latent-space goal progress and executed-action smoothness.
4.1  Experiment setup
Benchmarks and Tasks
We evaluate on four goal-conditioned control benchmarks with distinct dynamics:  TwoRoom  (   2  2   D navigation),  Reacher  (DMControl reacher),  PushT  (planar pushing), and  OGCube  (OGBench cube manipulation).
All tasks use     224  ×  224   224{\times}224    RGB observations, continuous actions, and observation-specified goals. The dataset information is provided in Appendix   A  .
We use the offline datasets released by LeWM  ( Maes et al., 2026 )  without modification.
To vary difficulty, we set each goal to the environment state     K  ∈   {   10  ,  25  ,  50  ,  75   }    K\in\{10,25,50,75\}    steps ahead of the start and assign a corresponding interaction budget     B  ∈   {   25  ,  50  ,  100  ,  100   }    B\in\{25,50,100,100\}   , defining the  easy ,  default  (LeWM’s ( Maes et al., 2026 )  original goal-setting horizon and interaction budget),  hard , and  extreme  tiers, respectively.
All methods are evaluated in the original simulator on the same start–goal instances, with    50  50    episodes per task–tier combination. An episode counts as successful only when the simulator’s native success condition is met; we use no latent-space distance threshold. We report success rate as the primary metric and planning time for the efficiency study, with additional details in Appendix   A.2  .
Baselines
We compare against eight baselines: Random, GCBC  ( Ghosh et al., 2021 ) , DINO-WM  ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) , PLDM  ( Sobal et al., 2025 ) , LeWM  ( Maes et al., 2026 ) , SMWM  ( Ivashkov et al., 2026 ) , delta-JEPA  ( Zhang et al., 2026 )  and INTACT ( Sun et al., 2026 ) .
Random provides a chance-level reference; GCBC and INTACT provide direct goal-conditioned controllers, with INTACT representing the closest end-to-end search-free baseline. The remaining methods are latent world-model planners that rely on CEM- or MPPI-based search at test time.
All learned methods are trained on the same offline datasets and evaluated under the same protocol.
All CEM-based comparisons use the shared search and execution
protocol specified in Appendix   A.3  .
Implementation Details
HIJP combines a latent world model with an amortized inverse head trained end-to-end.
At planning time, it produces an     H  =  5   H=5    step latent plan with a single amortized rollout, executes the corresponding actions in a receding-horizon manner, and then replans from the latest observation. Unlike the CEM-based world-model baselines, HIJP uses no test-time search. Architecture and optimization details are provided in Appendix   B  .

5  Conclusion
We introduced Hindsight Inverse JEPA Planning (HIJP), a search-free framework for goal-conditioned visual control. HIJP samples future observations as hindsight goals to enrich inverse-dynamics supervision and allows training gradients to update goal representations. It further feeds predicted actions into a latent predictor model and constrains the resulting latent transitions through a consistency objective. At planning time, the inverse and latent predictor models alternate to construct multi-step action plans without online search. Across four visual control tasks and multiple evaluation configurations, HIJP achieves the highest aggregate goal-reaching performance among the evaluated methods while reducing planning time compared to a search-based world-model planner. Under the most challenging setting, its executed actions are also smoother than those produced by CEM using the same representations.
Together, these results suggest that aligning goal-conditioned action learning with the latent transitions used during planning provides an effective route toward efficient, search-free visual control.
6  Limitations
Our evaluation focuses on tasks with a limited temporal span, using fixed planning horizons and replanning schedules.
Extending HIJP to substantially longer-horizon tasks remains an important direction.
Future work will explore hierarchical subgoal generation, adaptive planning horizons, and dynamic replanning schedules.
These extensions aim to decompose longer tasks into shorter goal-conditioned segments and adapt planning and execution intervals to the requirements of each segment.

Appendix A  Dataset Details and Protocol
We evaluate on four continuous-control benchmarks:  Tworoom ,  Reacher ,  PushT , and  OGBench-Cube  (hereafter OGCube), which together span    2  2   D navigation, low-dimensional continuous control, planar object pushing, and robotic cube manipulation; Figure   3   shows a representative trajectory from each. We do not collect any trajectories ourselves: we reuse the full-scale offline datasets released by LeWM  ( Maes et al., 2026 )  without modification.
A.1  Datasets
The four datasets are gathered under different behavior policies: a goal-directed navigation policy for Tworoom, a near-uniform random policy for Reacher, and expert policies for PushT and OGCube. A.3  CEM Planning Protocol

test-time optimizer for the CEM-based world-model baselines, as the computational reference in the planning-efficiency experiment, and as a controlled planner ablation in which HIJP’s amortized action

At every replanning point, CEM maintains a factorized Gaussian distribution over action sequences of length     H  =  5   H=5    model steps.
Each model-level action contains a chunk of     k  =  5   k=5    environment actions, so every candidate sequence covers      H  ​  k   =  25   Hk=25    environment steps. The sampling distribution is initialized with zero mean and sampling scale    1.0  1.0   .

Appendix B  Implementation Details
This appendix provides a concrete instantiation of the method.
We first specify the temporal discretization and the architecture of each component, then describe the training procedure and test-time planning protocol.
Unless stated otherwise, the architecture and temporal settings are shared across all four tasks.
B.1  Temporal Discretization and Data Flow
The environments expose atomic actions      u  s   ∈   ℝ   d  a     u_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}    at raw environment step    s  s   . The world model operates at a coarser temporal resolution by grouping    k  k    consecutive atomic actions into one model-level action chunk,
𝐚  t   =   (   u   t  ​  k    ,   u    t  ​  k   +  1    ,  …  ,   u     t  ​  k   +  k   −  1    )   ∈   ℝ   k  ​   d  a      .   \mathbf{a}_{t}=\left(u_{tk},u_{tk+1},\ldots,u_{tk+k-1}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{kd_{a}}.
We use     k  =  5   k=5    for every task.
Consequently, one transition in the learned latent dynamics corresponds to five raw environment steps. The raw action dimension is      d  a   =  2   d_{a}=2    for Tworoom, Reacher, and PushT, and      d  a   =  5   d_{a}=5    for OGCube.
The effective model-level action dimension is therefore      d  𝐚   =   k  ​   d  a    ∈   {  10  ,  25  }    d_{\mathbf{a}}=kd_{a}\in\{10,25\}   .
A training sample contains    W  W    temporally subsampled observations, together with their aligned model-level action chunks.     k  =  5   k=5    raw environment steps separate consecutive observations. Thus, the first and final observations in a window are separated by      (   W  −  1   )   ​  k   (W-1)k    raw environment steps.
Before being passed to the model, images are converted to floating-point, normalized using ImageNet statistics, and resized to     224  ×  224   224\times 224   .
Following the LeWM preprocessing procedure, continuous actions are standardized per dimension using statistics computed from the training dataset.
At execution time, predicted action chunks are mapped back to
the environment action space using the inverse transformation.

B.3  Training
All four modules are optimized jointly.
Training combines next-latent prediction, anti-collapse representation regularization, hindsight-conditioned inverse dynamics, and action transition consistency.
latent prediction loss.
Although every sampled window contains     W  =  16   W=16    model steps, the prediction term uses a causal context of length     m  =  3   m=3   .
Specifically, the predictor consumes     z      0  :   m  −  1      z_{0:m-1}    and the corresponding action embeddings and predicts the shifted latent sequence     z      1  :  m     z_{1:m}   .
Because causal attention is used, the output at position    t  t    only depends on positions up to    t  t   .
Denoting the three predicted latents by      z  ^       1  :  m     \hat{z}_{1:m}   , we compute
ℒ  pred   =    1   B  ​  m  ​  d    ​    ∑   b  =  1   B     ∑   t  =  0    m  −  1      ‖     z  ^    t  +  1    (  b  )    −   z   t  +  1    (  b  )     ‖   2  2       .   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}=\frac{1}{Bmd}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}\left\|\hat{z}_{t+1}^{(b)}-z_{t+1}^{(b)}\right\|_{2}^{2}.
(14)
The target latent in this term remains attached to the computational graph, for which we do not employ a momentum target encoder.
Anti-collapse regularization.
We apply the Sketch Isotropic Gaussian Regularizer (SIGReg;   Balestriero and LeCun, 2025  ) to the latents from all    W  W    frames, including frames that are not used by Equation   14  .
SIGReg measures the discrepancy between random one-dimensional projections of the latent distribution and a standard Gaussian reference distribution.
We use    17  17    integration knots and    1024  1024    random projections.
Applying this objective over the complete window increases the number and temporal diversity of latent samples contributing to the regularizer.
Hindsight-conditioned inverse dynamics.
For each training window, we independently sample     N  a   N_{a}    anchor–goal pairs.
For the    n  n   -th pair, the anchor is sampled uniformly from all positions with an available successor,
i  n   ∼   Unif  ⁡   {   0  ,  …  ,   W  −  2    }     .   i_{n}\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{0,\ldots,W-2\}.
(15)
Conditioned on     i  n   i_{n}   , we sample a future offset uniformly from the remaining
part of the window,
κ  n   ∼   Unif  ⁡   {   1  ,  …  ,   W  −  1  −   i  n     }     .   \kappa_{n}\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{1,\ldots,W-1-i_{n}\}.
(16)
The resulting goal index is      i  n   +   κ  n    i_{n}+\kappa_{n}   .
Sampling is performed independently for each pair and each element of the minibatch, so anchors may repeat.

For every sampled pair, the inverse head predicts the demonstrated action chunk executed at the anchor,
𝐚  ^    i  n    =    h  ξ   ​   (   z   i  n    ,   z    i  n   +   κ  n     )     .   \hat{\mathbf{a}}_{i_{n}}=h_{\xi}(z_{i_{n}},z_{i_{n}+\kappa_{n}}).

𝐚  j   =    h  ξ   ​   (   z  j   ,   z  g   )     .   \mathbf{a}_{j}=h_{\xi}(z_{j},z_{g}).

z   j  +  1    =   g  ϕ    (   z    max  ⁡   (  0  ,    j  −  m   +  1   )    :  j    ,   ψ  ω    (   𝐚    max  ⁡   (  0  ,    j  −  m   +  1   )    :  j    )   )   .   z_{j+1}=g_{\phi}\!\left(z_{\max(0,j-m+1):j},\psi_{\omega}(\mathbf{a}_{\max(0,j-m+1):j})\right).

No population of candidate action sequences or iterative test-time action optimization is required.
We use     H  =  5   H=5    model steps for every task.
Since each model-level action contains     k  =  5   k=5    atomic actions, one predicted plan covers      H  ​  k   =  25   Hk=25    raw environment steps.
The controller operates in a receding-horizon loop with execution stride     R  =  5   R=5    model steps.
It therefore executes the complete predicted action sequence, re-encodes the resulting observation, and constructs a new plan.
The target observation remains unchanged within an episode but is encoded together with the current observation at each replanning point.

Appendix C  Loss Weight Ablation
To examine the sensitivity of HIJP to the weighting coefficients of its training objectives, we conduct ablations on the inverse dynamics loss weight     λ  inv   \lambda_{\mathrm{inv}}    and the cycle-consistency loss weight     λ  cyc   \lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}   .
Specifically, we vary      λ  inv   ∈   {   0  ,  0.1  ,  1.0  ,  2.0   }    \lambda_{\mathrm{inv}}\in\{0,0.1,1.0,2.0\}    while fixing      λ  cyc   =  0.1   \lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}=0.1   , and vary      λ  cyc   ∈   {   0  ,  0.01  ,  0.1  ,  0.2   }    \lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}\in\{0,0.01,0.1,0.2\}    while fixing      λ  inv   =  1.0   \lambda_{\mathrm{inv}}=1.0   .
We train a separate model for each weight setting and evaluate the resulting checkpoint using the same evaluation protocol as in the main experiments. All training and evaluation settings follow Section   4.1  .
We report the results on Reacher, PushT, and OGCube in Table   12  .
We omit TwoRoom because its performance is already saturated under our evaluation protocol, with near 100% success rates, making it uninformative for distinguishing different loss-weight choices.

Appendix D  Action Smoothness Experiment
This appendix provides the complete evaluation protocol, metric definition, and per-tier results for the action-smoothness study in Section   4.6  . We evaluate HIJP, its CEM ablation, and LeWM on the four domains described in Appendix   A  .
For each domain and difficulty tier, all methods are evaluated closed-loop on the same set of start–goal instances. Instance sampling depends only on the dataset, random seed, goal offset, and number of evaluation episodes, and is therefore independent of the evaluated method.
We use 50 episodes per task with random seed 42. The four difficulty tiers are defined by the goal-offset and step-budget pairs     (  K  ,  B  )   (K,B)    listed in Table   7  .
Each rollout is allowed to run for the corresponding step budget, and we record the action executed at every environment step.
HIJP directly executes the amortized plan produced by its inverse-guided controller and performs no test-time search.
The CEM ablation uses the same learned representation and predictive model as HIJP, but replaces inverse-guided action generation with CEM planning.
LeWM, which performs CEM planning over its own forward model, is included as an additional reference.
All methods use the same control frequency and normalized action space.

If an episode reaches its goal before exhausting the step budget, we truncate its action sequence at the first success step and compute smoothness only over the executed prefix.
This avoids either rewarding or penalizing post-success idling and measures the controls actually required to complete the task.
Unsuccessful episodes contribute their full budget-length action sequences.
Let      a   1  :  T    =   (   a  1   ,  …  ,   a  T   )    a_{1:T}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{T})    denote the executed action sequence of an episode.
We define  action jerk  as the mean     ℓ  2   \ell_{2}    norm of the discrete second difference:
Jerk   (   a   1  :  T    )   =   1   T  −  2     ∑   t  =  3   T     ‖   a  t   −  2   a   t  −  1    +   a   t  −  2    ‖   2   .   \operatorname{Jerk}(a_{1:T})=\frac{1}{T-2}\sum_{t=3}^{T}\left\lVert a_{t}-2a_{t-1}+a_{t-2}\right\rVert_{2}.
(25)
Lower values indicate smoother controls.
A temporally constant or linearly varying action sequence has zero second difference, whereas oscillatory or abruptly changing commands produce a large value.
For each method, environment, and difficulty tier, we compute
Eq.    25   independently for every episode and report the mean across episodes. Let      J  ¯   m   \overline{J}_{m}    denote this episode-averaged jerk for method    m  m   .
We additionally report
R  CEM   =     J  ¯   CEM     J  ¯   HIJP     ,    R  LeWM   =     J  ¯   LeWM     J  ¯   HIJP      .   R_{\mathrm{CEM}}=\frac{\overline{J}_{\mathrm{CEM}}}{\overline{J}_{\mathrm{HIJP}}},\qquad R_{\mathrm{LeWM}}=\frac{\overline{J}_{\mathrm{LeWM}}}{\overline{J}_{\mathrm{HIJP}}}.
(26)
A ratio greater than one indicates that HIJP produces smoother actions than the corresponding CEM-based planner. Because all methods are evaluated in the same normalized action space, their jerk values are directly comparable.
Table   13   reports the results across all four difficulty tiers. HIJP consistently achieves lower action jerk than both its CEM ablation and LeWM.
On the extreme tier (    K  =  75   K=75   ), the CEM ablation produces     14.7  ×   14.7\times   –    26.4  ×   26.4\times    higher jerk than HIJP, despite sharing the same learned representation and predictive model.
This controlled comparison shows that the smoothness advantage primarily arises from HIJP’s amortized inverse-guided planning rather than from a difference in learned representations.
LeWM exhibits a similar level of jerk to the CEM ablation, suggesting that independently optimizing successive plans with CEM can introduce substantial temporal discontinuities into the executed controls.
