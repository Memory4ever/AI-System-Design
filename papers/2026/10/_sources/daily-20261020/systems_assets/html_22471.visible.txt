Has GPT-6-Astra Achieved Embodied Intelligence?
Joint Research Team
Abstract
GPT-6-Astra demonstrates a level of competence in robotic manipulation and navigation that challenges the conventional paradigm of building specialized models for individual tasks and embodiments. In this report, we ask not only how capable GPT-6-Astra is, but what its capabilities imply for the field.
First, we probe the limits of standalone GPT-6-Astra.  We conduct a large-scale evaluation across 12 representative benchmarks spanning tabletop manipulation, humanoids, mobile platforms, and dexterous hands. Under a unified protocol that allows in-context learning (ICL) from training-set demonstrations and substantially increases the number of execution steps, GPT-6-Astra achieves unprecedented performance (  Fig.   1  ). Our analysis suggests that future research should increasingly focus on improving
1) the generality of in-context learning
2) cost and execution time,
3) physical-state verification,
4) recovery from irreversible failures, and
5) long-horizon preservation of progress.
Second, we study the agentic harness for embodied control.  Besides exploring the ideal action representation and chucking parameters, we identify that close-loop coordination between a high-level "System 2" reasoner ( i.e. , GPT-6-Astra) and a low-level "System 1" controller ( e.g. ,     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}   ) is costly but effective.
Third, we examine GPT-6-Astra on real-world deployment.  Our experiments on 8 robot configurations reveal substantial gaps: 1) robot demonstrations required for in-context learning are expensive to collect, and 2) it is implausible to trade longer execution for higher success rates with physical constraints.
Fourth, we envision the next generation of embodied benchmarks.  We develop a prototype that 1) derives tasks from real-world needs rather than current robot capabilities, 2) provides no in-domain demonstrations, and 3) judges agents on a world clock that keeps running while they deliberate.
Taken together, our results suggest that GPT-6-Astra has largely crossed the threshold of embodied competence, but not yet that of practical embodied intelligence. The key remaining challenge is to translate benchmark success into reliable, efficient, and scalable real-world deployment.

1  Introduction
The advent of GPT-6-Astra marks a major shift in embodied intelligence: a general-purpose model can now tackle robotic manipulation and navigation tasks that have traditionally been dominated by specialized models, such as VLAs and WAMs, while exhibiting capabilities including zero-shot generalization, in-context learning, and failure recovery.
In this technical report, we use GPT-6-Astra as a lens to examine a broader question: what does this new level of embodied capability mean for the research community, and where should the field go next? We first conduct a large-scale evaluation across diverse embodied tasks to characterize the extent of its capabilities and identify which problems are already approaching saturation. We then study harness design to understand the performance–cost trade-offs involved in translating these capabilities into effective control. Next, we validate our findings on real robots, asking how far benchmark success carries over to physical deployment and what gaps remain.Finally, we revisit benchmark design and envision what the next generation of embodied benchmarks should look like.
First, we probe the upper bound of GPT-6-Astra’s embodied capabilities under a unified protocol.  We conduct a large-scale study across 12 representative benchmarks, covering tabletop robotic arms, humanoids, mobile platforms, and dexterous hands. Under this protocol, GPT-6-Astra outperforms existing methods by substantial margins, including recent agentic approaches that also build on GPT-6-Astra. Notably, as shown in   Fig.   1  , it approaches saturation on the LIBERO series and SIMPLE, while achieving 81.9% on RoboDojo and 87.3% on DexJoCo. The protocol follows two simple and well-motivated principles:
1) We allow in-context learning (ICL) from the training set  whenever successful demonstrations are available, which we consider reasonable since such demonstrations are conventionally accessible when training smaller task-specific policies. GPT-6-Astra studies the available training data and constructs a frozen knowledge package containing task guidance, motion and visual references, executable helpers, recovery procedures, and metadata. At test time, it retrieves and adapts these resources to the current scene. This substantially improves performance when the demonstrations closely cover the test distribution, but the benefit weakens as tasks move away from the data used to build the package.
2) We extensively increase the execution budget , allowing up to 10   ×  \times    more interaction steps than the official setting. We find that standard step limits can significantly underestimate GPT-6-Astra’s capabilities. Additional interaction yields large gains on tasks that benefit from repeated correction, such as RoboDojo and Bench2Dex, because GPT-6-Astra can detect failed actions, revise its strategy, and recover through further interaction. This emerging failure-recovery capability is the key mechanism that allows a larger execution budget to translate into higher success rates.
Our strong empirical results put us in a position to identify the  remaining limitations of GPT-6-Astra  more clearly. Specifically, we find that:
i) in-context learning transfer poorly beyond the tasks from which the demonstrations are drawn;
ii) even simple tasks incur inference cost and execution time far beyond what would be practical for commercial deployment;
iii) GPT-6-Astra often struggles to verify physical success and identify which task condition remains unmet;
iv) GPT-6-Astra often struggles to identify the cause of task failure;
v) it cannot reliably recover from consequential or irreversible failures; and
vi) long-horizon execution remains difficult, as the model struggles to preserve and track progress across many subtasks.
Second, we ablate the harness design  and find that effectively unleashing GPT-6-Astra’s embodied performance depends critically on both the collaboration protocol and the action interface. We first study the interaction between “System 2” ( i.e. , GPT-6-Astra) and “System 1” ( e.g. ,     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}     [ 13 ] ). Closed-loop collaboration, where GPT-6-Astra reviews and refines VLA-proposed actions before execution, substantially improves performance over standalone or open-loop execution, but frequent System-2 supervision incurs significant token cost. Reviewing longer action sequences improves this trade-off, while combining closed-loop collaboration with ICL yields the strongest performance at the highest cost.
We further study action representation and chunking, and identify a representation gap between multimodal reasoning and low-level robot control. Direct joint-angle control underperforms, while more semantically aligned end-effector commands work better; adaptive action chunking further improves performance while reducing unnecessary model calls. Together, these findings suggest that harness design should expose action spaces aligned with the model’s reasoning capabilities while delegating precise execution to lower-level controllers, with adaptive interaction to balance performance and inference cost.
Third, we validate GPT-6-Astra on real-world robots  spanning single and dual robotic arms, grippers, dexterous hands, a wheeled mobile manipulator, and a humanoid. Our study reveals three key findings.
1) GPT-6-Astra operates effectively across a wide range of embodiments, but its success rate remains highly task-dependent. It performs particularly well on tasks such as tabletop grasping, while deformable-object manipulation, precise contact, and long-horizon tasks remain challenging.
2) GPT-6-Astra demonstrates surprising active-vision capabilities, intentionally repositioning the camera to obtain observations needed for subsequent actions.
3) GPT-6-Astra shows that a slow “System 2” model can directly collaborate with a high-frequency “System 0” locomotion controller to perform humanoid manipulation that requires high-frequency local control.
At the same time, real-world deployment introduces challenges that are largely absent from simulation.
GPT-6-Astra can no longer easily trade additional execution steps for higher success rates, due to safety constraints, mechanical limits, and real-time requirements.
Moreover, successful trajectory demonstrations, which are central to our ICL setup in simulation, are often expensive or unavailable in real-world applications.
Fourth, we showcase a prototype of what we envision as the next generation of benchmarks  for embodied intelligence.
We propose three principles.
1) Tasks should be derived from human needs and expressed as people would naturally state them, with evaluation based on the utility of the outcome rather than a fixed goal state.
2) No in-domain training episodes or scene-specific demonstrations should be provided; instead, agents should acquire experience during deployment.
3) The world should continue to evolve while the agent deliberates, making reasoning time and its physical consequences part of the evaluation.
The prototype makes several remaining limitations more apparent in a deployment-oriented setting:
i) GPT-6-Astra focuses on the current step but can lose track of other changes it has caused in the environment, such as leaving a refrigerator open;
ii) without demonstrations, it struggles to figure out how unfamiliar objects and mechanisms work, or how to position its own body to interact with them; and
iii) because the world continues to change while it reasons, its observations can become outdated before the resulting action is executed.
Building such benchmarks also raises practical challenges in reliable judging, simulation fidelity, real-time evaluation, and evaluation cost.
In summary, by pushing GPT-6-Astra toward its capability limits, dissecting the harness that enables this performance, validating its behavior on real robots, and rethinking how embodied intelligence should be evaluated, we aim to clarify both what has changed and what remains fundamentally unsolved.
2  Large-Scale Study under A Unified Protocol
We study GPT-6-Astra across twelve embodied settings, spanning manipulation, humanoid loco-manipulation, household activities, and navigation, under a single protocol in which its model weights remain fixed. Two conditions shape the study: in-context learning (ICL), which gives GPT-6-Astra access to the same training demonstrations from which conventional policies are learned, and an extended interaction budget, which gives it more opportunity to correct mistakes within an episode.
2.1  Protocol
Our study covers twelve evaluation settings, grouped into five categories: tabletop manipulation (RoboDojo  [ 26 ] , RoboLab  [ 161 ] , LIBERO-Long++ and LIBERO-Ultra-Long  [ 162 ] , and LIBERO-PRO  [ 183 ] ), dexterous manipulation (Bench2Dex  [ 163 ]  and DexJoCo  [ 144 ] ), humanoid loco-manipulation (SIMPLE  [ 152 ] ), long-horizon household activities (BEHAVIOR  [ 8 ] ), and vision-language navigation (VLN-CE R2R and RxR  [ 87 ] , and LHPR-VLN  [ 134 ] ).
We evaluate GPT-6-Astra under a demonstration-informed protocol with two interaction budgets (1   ×  \times    and 10   ×  \times   ), as illustrated in Fig.   2  . The protocol separates preparation using training demonstrations from test-time interaction. We report performance under the standard budget and an extended budget that allows more interaction steps for execution and recovery.
Motivation.
Learned robotic policies are commonly trained or fine-tuned on demonstrations provided by the benchmark. Evaluating GPT-6-Astra without access to these demonstrations would therefore measure its performance under a different information regime. We use in-context learning (ICL) to investigate how effectively GPT-6-Astra can exploit the available training demonstrations without updating its model weights. Our objective is to characterize its capabilities when the task-relevant information used to train conventional policies is also available to it through contextual preparation.
We additionally investigate the effect of the interaction budget. In our evaluations, GPT-6-Astra often identifies unsuccessful actions, revises its strategy, and recovers through further interaction. A strict step limit can terminate this process before recovery succeeds. We therefore evaluate under two budgets. The standard budget (    1  ×   1\times   ) is the interaction budget prescribed by each benchmark and measures performance under its intended conditions; the extended budget (    10  ×   10\times   ) allows up to ten times as many action steps and measures how task completion improves when the policy has more opportunities to correct mistakes.
In-context learning preparation.
Before testing, GPT-6-Astra undergoes a separate ICL preparation phase for each task, using the available training demonstrations represented through benchmark-appropriate observations and demonstrated actions. During preparation, GPT-6-Astra may analyze the demonstrations and develop task-specific notes and reusable code. These materials form a knowledge package, which is frozen before evaluation and provided to every test episode of that task. GPT-6-Astra’s model weights remain unchanged. Preparation excludes held-out test episodes and evaluator-private information.
Episode evaluation.
Each test episode begins in a fresh, isolated session with the task instruction, the frozen knowledge package, and the permitted observation and action interfaces. At each decision point, GPT-6-Astra examines the current observations and specifies the next action or action sequence. The environment executes these actions and returns new observations, which GPT-6-Astra uses to assess the outcome and determine its next action. The interaction history is retained within the episode, allowing GPT-6-Astra to diagnose mistakes and attempt recovery. Neither the knowledge package nor the model weights are updated during testing, and information from one test episode is not transferred to another. Observation access, robot-control action semantics, control frequency, and success criteria remain fixed across budget conditions.
Extended interaction budget.
The extended budget allows ten times as many action steps as the standard budget. We report outcomes at     1  ×   1\times    and at cumulative cutoffs of     2  ×  ,  …  ,  10  ×   2\times,\ldots,10\times    the standard budget, using the same initialization, policy configuration, and success criterion. An extension continues the same episode, preserving its interaction history and accumulated environmental consequences; additional attempts occur within that episode rather than through resets. Episodes terminate upon verified success, an applicable terminal failure, or exhaustion of the allotted budget. Reporting success across these cutoffs quantifies the contribution of additional interaction while retaining the standard-budget result as a separate comparison point.

2.2  In-context Learning
With in-context learning, GPT-6-Astra achieves strong performance across embodied benchmarks without any update to its model weights. Figure   1   compares GPT-6-Astra with the best published result (SOTA) and the best published agentic method (Agentic SOTA) on all 12 settings, both reported under the standard budget. Already at the standard (    1  ×   1\times   ) budget, GPT-6-Astra matches or exceeds the strongest published result on most settings, with the largest margins on LIBERO-Ultra-Long and DexJoCo, where it nearly doubles the best published success rate. To isolate the contribution of ICL, we compare zero-shot and ICL conditions on SIMPLE: task-level ICL raises standard-budget success on the four common tasks from 70.8% to 90.8% and shortens successful episodes (Table   12  ). The benefit of ICL, however, depends on how closely the training data covers the test tasks. Where test tasks closely follow the training distribution, as in the LIBERO family, SIMPLE and VLN-CE, the package can retrieve and adapt demonstrated motions or routes almost directly, and success approaches saturation. DexJoCo’s contact-rich dexterous tasks are demanding, but phase-indexed motion references and controller helpers cover them well. RoboLab, in contrast, provides no training set from which a task-level package can be built, and its success remains moderate; Sec.   2.4   discusses this limitation further.
Package contents.
Each knowledge package is organized around six recurring components (Fig.   3  ): task guidance, motion references, visual references, executable helpers, checks and recovery, and metadata and provenance. This structure is not prescribed. During ICL preparation, GPT-6-Astra independently chooses what to inspect and retain within each benchmark’s permitted training data and tool interfaces, and we do not specify which demonstrations, frames, or state/action records it should examine or save; the six components nonetheless recur across benchmarks.
•
Task guidance.
Written instructions describe task goals, execution stages, and strategies. They specify subtask ordering, object roles, and constraints that guide the selection and use of stored references.
•
Motion references.
Demonstration-derived trajectories, grasp configurations, and phase anchors provide numerical examples of how actions unfold. The agent retrieves relevant segments or poses and adapts them to the current scene and robot state.
•
Visual references.
Selected demonstration frames and contact crops illustrate object appearance, grasp regions, and intermediate task states. These examples support interpretation of the stored motions and comparison with current observations.
•
Executable helpers.
Reusable Python programs support forward and inverse kinematics, reference retrieval, and action generation. They convert stored references and current geometric estimates into candidate actions.
•
Checks & recovery.
Completion tests describe observable evidence that a grasp, subtask, or overall task has succeeded. Recovery procedures associate failure symptoms with corrective actions, such as adjusting an approach or selecting an alternative grasp.
•
Metadata & provenance.
Robot specifications and interface conventions define how stored states and actions should be interpreted. Source records and validation notes document the origin of the retained material and the scope of its offline checks.

Within this shared structure, the retained information reflects each benchmark’s demands. Bench2Dex  [ 163 ]  and DexJoCo  [ 144 ]  emphasize hand configurations, phase-indexed motion examples, and helpers for dexterous control. LIBERO-Long++ and LIBERO-Ultra-Long  [ 162 ]  organize selected demonstrations around composable atomic skills, while LIBERO-PRO  [ 183 ]  primarily retains textual strategies and recovery knowledge from exploration. SIMPLE  [ 152 ]  preserves complete numerical training trajectories and helpers for extracting and modifying candidate action chunks under current feedback. LHPR-VLN  [ 134 ]  and VLN-CE  [ 87 ]  retain navigation strategies, landmark references, stopping guidance, and recovery notes from training-scene practice. BEHAVIOR  [ 8 ]  emphasizes object-specific grasping affordances, bimanual and tool-contact geometry, and conditional verification and recovery procedures.
Package usage.
Retrospective usage analyses reveal a common workflow on every benchmark we audited: GPT-6-Astra reads the task guidance, retrieves a candidate motion through a prepared helper, and checks its execution against the visual references. Numerical demonstrations are frequently loaded internally by these helpers, linking stored examples directly to action generation; on DexJoCo, controllers and visual references are used repeatedly during both initial execution and continuation. Rather than replaying demonstrations, GPT-6-Astra adapts them to the current scene. In Bench2Dex Ball Box Loading, it remaps reference joints and compensates for a 4–5 cm difference in workspace height while preserving the demonstrated bimanual support geometry; in DexJoCo Photograph, it selects an alternative reference pickup after a slip. The packages thus serve as reusable resources for proposing, adapting, and revising actions under feedback, although these analyses do not isolate the causal contribution of individual components.
2.3  Extending Interaction Budget
Beyond the coverage of training data (Sec.   2.2  ), the demands of the tasks themselves limit performance at     1  ×   1\times   , leaving four benchmarks far from solved even with ICL: RoboDojo, Bench2Dex, BEHAVIOR, and LHPR-VLN. RoboDojo and Bench2Dex require precise bimanual or dexterous manipulation, often involving repeated corrections. BEHAVIOR and LHPR-VLN require long sequences of household manipulation or navigation, with subgoals tracked across many stages, and have the lowest success rates of all settings.
To assess how much the standard step limit contributes to these shortfalls, we extend the budget to     10  ×   10\times    while keeping model weights and ICL packages fixed (Figs.   1   and   4  ). Additional interaction yields large gains on RoboDojo and Bench2Dex. On RoboDojo, success roughly triples, and about three quarters of the episodes unfinished at     1  ×   1\times    are completed, with the Precision group gaining most. Success on Bench2Dex more than doubles. These gains indicate that the standard budget can interrupt productive execution and recovery, while additional interaction allows GPT-6-Astra to complete further corrective attempts.
On LHPR-VLN, however, the extended budget yields only a modest gain, and success remains the lowest of all settings. This suggests that the demands of long-horizon navigation, including tracking subgoals across many stages, are not resolved by additional interaction alone (Sec.   2.4  ). We do not extend the budget on BEHAVIOR, whose standard budget is already long.
Benchmarks that already perform well at     1  ×   1\times    also benefit from additional interaction. DexJoCo and VLN-CE reach success rates of around 90%, while the LIBERO family and SIMPLE exceed 95% and approach saturation (Fig.   1  ).

2.4  Limitations and Failure Modes
Despite the substantial gains from ICL and the extended budget, several limitations and recurring failure modes remain. We first discuss two limitations of the approach, transfer and cost, and then three behavioral failure modes identified from episode videos and agent logs (Fig.   6  ). Infrastructure interruptions remain scored as failures but are considered separately from the behavioral analysis.
Figure 6 :   Residual failures under ICL with the extended budget.  Each pair shows the state before a key event and its outcome; purple arrows mark the relevant objects.  (a) Verifying physical success:  apparent or self-reported completion diverges from the actual task state.  (b) Recovering from consequential actions:  an early mishap, such as a spill or a dropped part, cannot be undone and derails later subtasks.  (c) Preserving progress over long horizons:  progress that later steps depend on, such as an open lid or a placed object, is lost and blocks the task.
ICL packages are largely task-specific, which limits transfer.
Although ICL packages substantially improve performance across benchmarks, they are built from each benchmark’s training data and mostly encode task-level guidance, motion references, and helpers, so their benefit weakens when test tasks move away from the data they were built from. In the RoboDojo Open group, which has no task-specific demonstrations and relies on a package studied from the other four groups, GPT-6-Astra + ICL still benefits from additional interaction but attains the lowest success among all groups (Appendix   9.3  , Table   5  ). Similarly, RoboLab provides no training set from which a package can be built; GPT-6-Astra leads the listed baselines there, yet its absolute success remains moderate (Table   6  ). Building packages that generalize beyond their source tasks remains an open direction.
Time and cost remain high.
Strong performance comes at a substantial cost in tokens and wall-clock time, even on benchmarks that GPT-6-Astra nearly solves (Fig.   5  ). On LIBERO-Long++, where success at the standard budget already exceeds 97%, each episode still consumes about a million tokens and nearly an hour of inference and tool use; on LIBERO-Ultra-Long, these costs rise to roughly nine million tokens and nearly two hours (Table   4  ). Extending the budget increases the cost further, and much of the additional cost is concentrated in difficult episodes that often still fail: on RoboDojo, a single episode under the extended budget takes more than three hours on average. Since the     10  ×   10\times    setting only caps the number of action steps and episodes may end earlier upon success or terminal failure, these costs reflect the steps actually executed. Because inference latency is not counted in the action-step budget, this cost also limits deployment in settings that require real-time control.
Verifying physical success.
GPT-6-Astra can detect that a task is not yet complete without identifying which physical condition remains unmet; consequently, its correction process fails to converge (Fig.   6  (a)). In Bench2Dex Jigsaw Puzzle Assembly, it reports that all four pieces form the required rectangle while noting that the episode has not ended, and continues to adjust the residual offsets, but its corrections pull the pieces apart again instead of closing the remaining gaps. In RoboDojo Hang mugs, GPT-6-Astra takes an overlap between a mug’s handle and the peg in its head camera as evidence that the mug is hung, without confirming that the peg passes through the handle; the mug ends up on the table, and in its later regrasps the closing of its fingers is likewise taken as contact that has not occurred. In RoboDojo Fill egg holder, GPT-6-Astra moves all four eggs from the basket into the holder and reports that they remain seated after it withdraws, yet the episode ends as a failure with only partial credit. Visual plausibility and self-reported progress can therefore diverge from the task’s actual completion conditions, sustaining repeated correction or premature confidence. Reliable execution requires checks that resolve the task’s actual contact, support, and alignment constraints.
Recovering from consequential actions.
Each attempt changes the state from which recovery must proceed, and some changes cannot be undone (Fig.   6  (b)). In RoboDojo Pour liquid into cup, the cup tips over early in the episode and the liquid spills onto the table; GPT-6-Astra rights the cup and resumes pouring, but the spill cannot be reversed, and the episode ends with most of its budget unused. In DexJoCo Assembly, a peg slips from the grasp and falls to the floor; GPT-6-Astra judges it unreachable and parks the arms with most of the budget remaining. Recovery is also costly when the failed step is a prerequisite for later ones, such as opening a container or securing an object: until it succeeds, downstream subtasks cannot proceed, and repeated retries consume the budget without advancing the task. Such dependencies are especially prevalent in long-horizon benchmarks like BEHAVIOR and LHPR-VLN, where a single unresolved subtask can stall progress toward the overall goal. In LHPR-VLN, for example, an agent may become stuck trying to reach an intermediate destination, lose track of its navigation plan, and resort to aimless exploration without making meaningful progress. Additional budget therefore helps only when a recovery path still exists, and successful recovery usually involves a meaningful change of strategy rather than repetition. In DexJoCo Fold Glasses, for example, GPT-6-Astra switches to a side approach, verifies the grasp with a test lift, and completes the task after exceeding the standard budget.
Preserving progress over long horizons.
Long-horizon tasks require earlier progress to remain intact while later actions are executed, and small deviations compound across stages (Fig.   6  (c)). In RoboDojo Store laptop and headphones, GPT-6-Astra has already stored the laptop and hung the headphones on their stand, then returns to adjust one earcup; the correction pulls the headband off the stand, and the headphones are not re-hung before the budget runs out. In BEHAVIOR can_meat, a jar that is already held beside the counter tilts during the next motion and falls to the floor, so progress has to be rebuilt before the task can continue. Beyond such direct disruptions, small errors in grasping, placement, or navigation accumulate over successive stages and alter the conditions for later actions, forcing GPT-6-Astra to re-localize and correct earlier mistakes. Reliable long-horizon execution therefore requires tracking and rechecking conditions established earlier, rather than treating completed subgoals as permanently resolved.
These observations motivate task-relevant verification, recovery strategies that account for irreversible consequences and prerequisite dependencies, and persistent tracking of earlier progress. They suggest that further progress depends on how effectively the agent uses additional interaction to revise its strategy and verify the resulting state.

We therefore conduct controlled ablations to investigate which harness designs improve task performance and at what inference cost. We study two aspects: System 1–System 2 collaboration and action representations and chunking.
3.1  System 1–System 2 Collaboration
We use GPT-6-Astra as System 2 and     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}     [ 13 ]  fine-tuned on RoboDojo as System 1 (VLA). We compare three interaction settings: (i) standalone VLA, which executes actions without GPT supervision; (ii) open-loop collaboration, where GPT specifies subgoals and the VLA directly executes joint-action sequences; and (iii) closed-loop collaboration, where the VLA proposes actions that are converted into candidate EEF motions through forward kinematics, allowing GPT to accept, modify, or reject them before execution. For closed-loop collaboration, we vary the review horizon     L  ∈   {   1  ,  4   }    L\in\{1,4\}   , where    L  L    denotes the number of VLA-proposed EEF actions reviewed per GPT call. Finally, we evaluate in-context learning (ICL) following the setup described in Section   2.2  , both for the direct-control agent and in combination with closed-loop VLA collaboration (    L  =  2   L=2   ).
As shown in Figure   7  , the standalone VLA achieves an average success rate of 6.91%, indicating limited performance on this benchmark. Open-loop collaboration similarly achieves 5.83% while using 0.387 million GPT-6-Astra tokens. In contrast, closed-loop collaboration, where System 2 reviews and refines VLA-proposed EEF actions, improves the success rate by up to 20.00 percentage points over open-loop execution. However, per-action review requires 7.664 million tokens. Increasing the review horizon to     L  =  4   L=4    reduces token consumption by 63.5% to 2.796 million tokens, while improving the success rate from 23.61% to 25.83%. Combining ICL with closed-loop VLA achieves the highest success rate of 54.17%, exceeding the best non-ICL closed-loop configuration by 28.34 percentage points, but consumes 19.335 million tokens, approximately 6.9 times as many as that configuration.
These findings suggest that delegating execution to System 1 alone is insufficient for reliable task completion, while frequent System 2 supervision incurs substantial token consumption. Reviewing longer action sequences improves both performance and token efficiency relative to per-action review, highlighting the importance of the collaboration protocol. The configuration combining ICL with closed-loop VLA achieves substantially higher performance, although at a much larger token cost.  A key direction is therefore to make verification and recovery more selective and to incorporate task-relevant experience more efficiently, balancing reliable execution with token consumption.
3.2  Action Representation and Chunking
We study action representations and execution horizons under the same direct-control framework. Our baseline follows the RoboRSI  [ 118 ]  agent framework, where GPT-6-Astra observes RGB images and robot states and generates one Cartesian end-effector (EEF) action per decision. For action representations, we compare absolute Cartesian EEF targets (baseline), directional motion commands that specify relative movement directions and distances, and direct joint-angle control. For execution horizons, we compare single-action execution (    L  =  1   L=1   ), fixed action chunks (    L  =  4   L=4   ), and adaptive chunking, where GPT-6-Astra selects     L  ∈   {   1  ,  2  ,  3  ,  4   }    L\in\{1,2,3,4\}   . Here,    L  L    denotes the number of EEF actions generated per GPT decision, and token consumption includes both input and output tokens.
As shown in Figure   7  , directional motion commands improve the average success rate by 2.50 percentage points, whereas direct joint-angle control leads to a 5.84-percentage-point drop. This suggests a potential representation gap between the embodied intelligence of multimodal models and low-level robot control spaces. Action chunking further reveals a trade-off between success rate and token consumption. Fixed action chunking (    L  =  4   L=4   ) uses 0.435 million tokens but decreases the average success rate by 4.17 percentage points relative to the baseline. In contrast, adaptive action chunking improves the average success rate by 4.44 percentage points while using 1.146 million tokens. Thus, adaptive chunking achieves a higher success rate than fixed chunking at a higher token cost. These results indicate that both action representation and execution horizon influence the success rate and token consumption of embodied control.
These findings suggest that effectively translating embodied intelligence into physical actions requires interfaces aligned with the model’s reasoning capabilities  [ 28 ] .  A promising direction is to expose semantically meaningful action commands while delegating precise motion execution to robot controllers, with adaptive execution horizons to further balance task performance and token consumption.

Since the simulation protocol of Sec.   2.1   rests on two assumptions that do not hold on physical robots, namely that successful demonstrations of the test task are available and that additional execution steps are free, we further study a  real-world variant  of the protocol that revises both. First, the context changes: since new tasks often come without demonstrations in real-world deployment, GPT-6-Astra operates in a zero-shot setting and receives only structured information provided by a human as context. Second, the budget changes: the step limit is replaced by wall-clock time, because on real hardware the time a task takes determines not only token cost but also safety, hardware health and human labor.
4.1  Setting: The Real-World Variant
We deploy GPT-6-Astra on eight platforms spanning five embodiment classes, ordered from simple to complex in Table   1  . The real-world variant departs from the simulation protocol in three respects; per-platform settings, context and results are summarized in Table   14   and detailed in App.   11  .
(1) Zero-shot.
GPT-6-Astra receives no demonstration and no knowledge package distilled from data, and performs as a generalist, without task- or robot-specific training.
(2) Structured human priors as context.
In place of the knowledge package, GPT-6-Astra receives context written by a human. This context may encode information that is routinely available when a robot is deployed, such as tool schemas, joint limits, camera placement, safety constraints and open/close presets for dexterous hands, and, on some platforms, task-level guidance such as admissible grasp points. No platform exposes high-level semantic skills such as pick, place or insert.
(3) Wall-clock budget and success criteria.
Trials are budgeted in wall-clock time rather than in steps, normally 60 minutes; per-platform exceptions are listed in App.   11  . An operator holds an emergency stop throughout, and a person judges success from the physical outcome rather than from the model’s own completion statement. For deformable tasks such as cloth folding, a single success criterion is hard to define across platforms, so their success rates indicate that the task was carried through rather than a common level of quality.
4.2  Findings
Finding 1: one model drives every embodiment; success depends on the task.
Without platform-specific training, the same model controls all eight platforms, from single arms to a humanoid (Fig.   8  ). What limits success is the nature of the task: rigid pick-and-place is reliable (17/18 trials) and matches simulation, whereas deformable objects (e.g., cloth folding: 2/4 on the Franka 3), precise contact (e.g., plug insertion: 0/11 across three platforms) and long-horizon mobile tasks (e.g., navigation and search: 9/23 on the AgileX Tracer) remain markedly harder. A second arm extends the range of zero-shot tasks GPT-6-Astra completes (Fig.   13  ), and GPT-6-Astra re-plans from execution feedback when the scene changes (Fig.   8  c). Finding 2: what GPT-6-Astra can do depends on what it can see.
Beyond the prompt and the available primitives, real-world success depends critically on the view configuration, that is, on whether the cameras available to the model can observe the region in which the task is decided. The flower insertion task illustrates this dependence. The gripper holds the stem vertically from above, so the wrist camera, which moves with the gripper, cannot see the mouth of the vase below the flower. On a single arm (Franka 3), the wrist camera is the only close-up view: GPT-6-Astra never sees the vase mouth during the insertion, and the attempt fails, with the flower dropped beside the vase (Fig.   9  a). On the dual-arm Piper, the wrist view of the arm holding the flower is limited in the same way, but GPT-6-Astra moves the free arm until its wrist camera looks at the vase mouth, and then completes the insertion (Fig.   9  b). The difference is  active vision : the viewpoint becomes part of the action space. This behavior is largely absent from teleoperated demonstrations: a human operator typically watches the scene from their own vantage point rather than through the robot’s cameras, so demonstrations rarely move a camera to resolve an occlusion, and policies learned from them have little to imitate. A zero-shot agent that perceives only through the robot’s cameras has to discover it.

Finding 3: GPT-6-Astra as System 2 over existing System 0 motor control.
On a humanoid, GPT-6-Astra deliberates far more slowly than local motor control runs, so we test whether it can act directly through existing System 0 controllers, without a task-trained System 1 policy. In SIMPLE, GPT-6-Astra issues joint, body and locomotion targets that decoupled WBC or AMO  [ 91 ]  controllers execute. On a real Unitree G1 with Wuji hands, it commands arm-joint targets and binary hand open/close states for manipulation (2/3), and turns and walks through the vendor velocity interface (3/3); the two tasks are evaluated separately (Fig.   8  h and Fig.   13  , B3). This extends the System 2/System 1 coordination of Sec.   3   down to existing motor-control interfaces, but it establishes feasibility under slow, feedback-driven supervision rather than reliable real-time visuomotor control.
4.3  What Changes in the Real World
The two ingredients behind the simulation results in Sec.   2   are each constrained on real-world setting.
Physical constraints limit extended execution.
Extending the step limit by up to 10   ×  \times    works in simulation because additional steps and failed attempts carry no consequence; the physical world imposes three constraints that simulation does not model.
(i)  Safety.  Contacts beyond the controller’s limits trigger protective stops that usually end the trial, for instance when the gripper touches the table while folding thin cloth on the Franka 3, or when a plug-insertion attempt on the PiPER arm ends in a joint collision fault (2 of its 3 trials), and GPT-6-Astra cannot recover from such hardware-level faults. Some outcomes, such as a dropped object, are irreversible.
(ii)  Mechanical limits.  GPT-6-Astra has no direct feedback on the load its commands impose: on the Tianji M6S, finger joints of the Wuji hand stalled under load during repeated grasp attempts, and its only guard was a temperature threshold it had set for itself, which the hand reached (62   ∘  C), interrupting the attempt. Actuators overheating can terminate prolonged trials: a torso over-temperature fault ended both flower-collection runs on the Galaxea R1 Pro.
(iii)  Real time.  In simulation the environment pauses while the model deliberates. On a physical robot, elapsed time is the quantity through which every other cost accumulates: tokens, exposure to safety risks, hardware stress and human supervision. Episodes are long: on the G1, GPT-6-Astra spends about 90% of each run deliberating, and a single manipulation trial takes over an hour.
The trade-off between longer execution and higher success that GPT-6-Astra exploits in simulation is therefore not freely available on real hardware (App.   11  ).
Missing demonstrations reshape in-context learning.
The ICL protocol of Sec.   2.1   presumes that successful demonstrations of the test task, on the test robot, already exist. In real-world deployment new tasks often come without them. Human-written context suffices for a meaningful range of tasks, but it is still prompt engineering by an expert and its quality directly affects the outcome: on the Tianji hands, without predefined grasp primitives GPT-6-Astra did not complete a single grasp (0/3), whereas with human-written grasp primitives it succeeded in 3/3. This context also need not come from a person: on the Galaxea R1 Pro, GPT-6-Astra wrote its own robot profile in a motion-free familiarization session, from vendor documentation and live probes of sensors and controllers, and every task session starts from it. Human-written context represents only one point in a broader design space. Between a complete set of in-domain demonstrations and none at all, a range of alternative context sources may be available at deployment time, which we have not yet evaluated: cross-embodiment demonstrations, self-generated demonstrations in simulation, human demonstrations, the robot’s own deployment history, and knowledge packages shared across robots. The principled construction of context for embodied tasks from such heterogeneous sources remains an open problem, and constitutes a substantial research opportunity for in-context learning in real-world deployment.
5  Towards a Next-Generation Embodied Benchmark
The preceding sections show that GPT-6-Astra’s results on established embodied benchmarks depend heavily on conditions that the benchmarks grant. Its success rises with in-context access to the benchmark’s demonstrations and with an extended step budget in which failures cost nothing (Sec.   2  ), and depends on how a general agent is adapted to an action interface built for a learned policy (Sec.   3  ). These conditions follow from how most embodied benchmarks were designed, for policies trained by imitation: demonstrations of a task are collected, a policy is fitted to them, and it is evaluated on held-out episodes of the same task  [ 99 ,  112 ,  26 ,  8 ] . On real robots, by contrast, neither demonstrations of a new task nor extra time comes for free (Sec.   4  ). For a general-purpose agent, such benchmarks therefore measure in part how well it exploits what they provide, and raising their difficulty, the usual response once benchmarks saturate  [ 80 ,  20 ] , would not change this. We instead propose three principles that change how embodied benchmarks are designed and judged, and illustrate them with a household proof of concept.
5.1  Principles for Next-Generation Benchmarks
Following the protocol now common for language agents, in which an agent acts in an environment by any means and is scored on the outcome  [ 74 ,  155 ,  110 ,  130 ] , we target a setting in which the agent perceives the world only through its robot’s sensors, acts through the commands its hardware exposes, and is evaluated on a single deployment in a world that keeps evolving; everything it builds above that interface is part of the system under test. Current embodied benchmarks judge success as a planning domain language would: BDDL  [ 135 ] , inspired by PDDL  [ 50 ] , states a task as a goal condition over the scene’s objects that must hold in a single state (  Fig.   10  , left). Such goals inherit the assumptions of classical planning  [ 51 ,  126 ] : a fully specified goal with binary success, a world that changes only through the agent’s actions, time counted in steps, and a given world model. Extensions of PDDL relax some of these  [ 45 ,  49 ,  59 ] , but embodied benchmarks have rarely adopted them, and each principle below breaks at least one.

Tasks derived from human needs.
Benchmark tasks are usually ones on which a robot can be shown to succeed, because demonstrations must be collected for them; even generalist policies that operate zero-shot in unseen homes  [ 13 ]  are evaluated on tasks chosen from the robot’s perspective. A deployed robot must instead do what the people it serves need done, whether or not any robot has done it before. Following BEHAVIOR-1K  [ 90 ] , we propose that tasks be derived from such needs and stated as the person would state them. Such tasks also test whether the agent knows the limits of its embodiment: a humanoid may fetch a step stool to reach a ceiling bulb, whereas a wheeled base should ask for help. Real needs also carry obligations that no instruction mentions, such as an unattended stove, a running tap or a cabinet the agent itself left open. These have been studied in narrower forms, as tidying without instructions  [ 76 ,  127 ] , mitigating risks the agent itself creates  [ 106 ]  and rescuing objects from a spreading hazard  [ 182 ] ; in deployment they also arise while the robot is busy with something else, and the agent must notice them unprompted and decide whether to interrupt its work. We therefore place these obligations (Duty) in the same deployment as the explicit request (Intent), with deadlines on the world clock.
A request stated as a need cannot be judged as a goal. “Make breakfast” resembles a high-level action in hierarchical planning  [ 40 ,  126 ] : it admits many refinements, from a slice of toast to a cooked meal, of which a goal formula keeps only the one its author chose (  Fig.   10  ). Hierarchical languages accept any decomposition of the task into an executable plan  [ 59 ] , yet the refinements serve the person to different degrees, which calls for a utility over outcomes  [ 77 ]  rather than the fraction of satisfied goal predicates reported as partial credit  [ 135 ,  8 ] . A fixed check can even contradict its own instruction: Bench2Dex’s condiment-box-loading task  [ 163 ]  asks the agent to place three condiments into a box, but its success check also requires the soy-sauce and vinegar bottles to stand upright, which the instruction never states. Because a capable agent may also satisfy a request in ways a fixed check did not anticipate, or exploit a check weaker than the request  [ 86 ] , the judge should itself be validated against how people rank alternative outcomes  [ 32 ,  179 ] .
Experience acquired at test time.
Demonstrations are often unavailable for new tasks in deployment, and they also bound what an agent learns: recorded from the operator’s viewpoint, they rarely move a camera to resolve an occlusion, whereas an agent that understands its embodiment can make its viewpoint part of the action (Sec.   4  ). We therefore propose that the benchmark provide no in-domain training episodes or scene-specific demonstrations, so that the agent must ground its general priors in an unseen home and embodiment through its own exploration, mapping the layout, testing affordances and figuring out idiosyncratic appliances, and may find strategies that no demonstrator used. Planning languages presume the opposite, a given world model  [ 126 ] , and benchmark goals retain a trace of it: predicates such as  cooked  or  toggled_on  tell the agent which variables matter, whereas a real air fryer has only a winding timer and a toaster’s dial yields a continuum of results. The judge should therefore define its predicates on physical quantities kept private from the agent (  Fig.   10  , right), and charge exploration the same world time and side effects as any other action. Since experience carries over between requests within a deployment and is reset between deployments, the unit of assessment is the deployment, and how quickly the agent becomes competent in it is a quantity of interest  [ 31 ,  7 ] .
A world that keeps running.
An extended step budget raises GPT-6-Astra’s success at no cost in simulation, because the simulator pauses while it deliberates: on RoboDojo, the standard budgets correspond to 8–76 s of commanded motion against an average of 55 minutes of computation (Sec.   2.3  ). On a physical robot that time passes in the world, and on the G1 GPT-6-Astra spends about 90% of each run deliberating (Sec.   4.3  ). Following recent evaluations in which the environment keeps evolving while the agent reasons  [ 180 ,  153 ] , we propose that the world not wait for the agent: the time between an observation and the next command, whether spent on inference, tool use or communication, is simulated under the previous command, as on a physical robot  [ 15 ] , while the host’s simulation and rendering time is not charged. The judge must then observe the whole trajectory on the world clock, including some time after the agent declares that it is done, since a final-state goal cannot see an egg that was ready and then burned. Obligations become constraints on the trajectory, conditions to be avoided throughout or restored within a deadline once triggered  [ 38 ,  84 ] ; PDDL3 states such constraints for plans  [ 49 ] , and PARTNR and IS-Bench check ordering and triggered safety conditions in embodied tasks  [ 21 ,  106 ] , but without deadlines. Time also sets the value of an outcome: a resident leaving in fifteen minutes is better served by eggs and toast in ten than by a full breakfast in an hour. The judge should therefore assign a utility that depends jointly on what was achieved and when  [ 56 ]  (  Fig.   10  , right), which makes the agent’s thinking time part of its performance.
5.2  A Household Proof of Concept
We ground these principles in the household. Requests there are open-ended, chores chain many steps across rooms and appliances, much of the work is contact-rich, and the home keeps changing while the robot works, which are the conditions under which GPT-6-Astra still falls short (Secs.   2   and   4  ). We built a fully interactive one-bedroom apartment in NVIDIA Isaac Sim  [ 115 ]  through a reproducible pipeline (  Fig.   11  ): publicly available assets  [ 167 ,  75 ,  90 ,  130 ]  and a floor plan form the home, coding agents furnish it at real-world scale under human review, and every part is bound to physics, including articulation, contact, position-based fluids and cloth  [ 111 ,  107 ] , and the heating and cooking processes of everyday appliances. Because judged outcomes are only as meaningful as the physics behind them, the environment is built for causal fidelity: whatever an agent’s decisions depend on, from an object’s mass and friction to heat flowing from burner to pan to food, should be simulated with the right cause and effect and grounded in real-world references.

Finding 1: GPT-6-Astra attends to the object in hand and loses track of the rest of the home.
Tasks derived from human needs ask the agent to serve the whole request, including obligations that no one states; GPT-6-Astra serves only the step in front of it. Tidying a desk, it moves the book in its gripper while its arm knocks other items to the floor and pushes the monitor out of place (  Fig.   12  a). Putting groceries away, it opens the refrigerator to store the milk, then turns to carrying fruit to the table and leaves the door open from 300 s to 765 s of world time (  Fig.   12  b). In both episodes, the rest of the home, including the robot’s own arm and the states it has changed, seems to enter the model’s decisions only when it blocks the next step, much as on the real robots of Sec.   4.3  . A check on the final state would miss both: the refrigerator is closed by the end of the episode, and where the book ends up says nothing about the rest of the desk. The judge in   Fig.   10   therefore tracks obligations over the whole trajectory rather than only at the end.
Finding 2: Without demonstrations, GPT-6-Astra does not work out how unfamiliar fixtures move or where its own body has to be.
With no demonstration to follow, the agent has to discover both through its own interaction. Making breakfast, GPT-6-Astra has to take bacon from the refrigerator (  Fig.   12  c). It pulls the door open and holds it with one arm, but from where its base stands in the narrow aisle, the open door lies between its free arm and the shelf, and the other arm can only reach around it. Getting to the bacon would require placing the base so that the swinging door and the robot’s own body both fit between the refrigerator and the counter behind it. GPT-6-Astra plans for the gripper and the handle but not for the rest of its body: it lets go of the handle to reach in, the door swings shut, and it is back where it started. Faced with an unfamiliar tap, it likewise keeps pushing the handle instead of turning it while the sink fills and overflows (  Fig.   12  d). In both cases it repeats and adjusts the same motion. On the benchmarks of Sec.   2  , the demonstrations it distills in context show both, so this weakness does not appear there.
Finding 3: GPT-6-Astra bases decisions on observations that become outdated while it thinks.
In a world that keeps running, an action must suit the state of the world when it takes effect. Watching an egg through the head camera, GPT-6-Astra correctly decides to turn the burner off once the egg has set, but each decision takes seconds of world time with the burner still on, and by the time the burner is off the egg has charred (  Fig.   12  e). The model reasons as though the world waits for it, which in the simulators of Sec.   2   it does.
These episodes are few and qualitative, but they point the same way. On the benchmarks of Sec.   2  , GPT-6-Astra’s ability to decide what to do is enough. In a home that states only needs, offers no demonstrations and does not pause, it must also act on that decision, and this is where it currently falls short.
5.3  Open Challenges
Realizing these principles in a full benchmark raises several open problems, which building our prototype made concrete. The first challenge is evidence for the judge. To score what happened rather than check a list of predicates, the judge needs physical facts, such as whether an egg was flipped or burned, which simulators still model with heuristic state changes  [ 90 ] . Learned judges do not yet fill this gap: vision-language success detectors reach at most 60% balanced accuracy on contact-rich assembly  [ 113 ] , and vision-language judges of agent trajectories can be misled by the agent’s own reasoning  [ 78 ] . The second is simulation fidelity. Because the judge reads the simulator’s state, a modeling error looks the same as agent behavior: a wrongly modeled joint can make an agent appear to succeed, and a contact artifact can make it appear to fail, and simulation has so far been shown to predict real-robot results for short tabletop tasks, including a few with deformable objects  [ 95 ,  70 ,  173 ] , but not for long-horizon household activities. The third is the cost of a world that does not pause. If the world keeps running while the agent thinks, every physical process must be simulated during inference, and scores then depend on latency, hardware and service load; existing benchmarks avoid this by pausing the environment  [ 171 ] , which removes inference speed from the score, or by advancing time per generated token  [ 153 ] , which is reproducible but does not reflect deployed latency. Finally, a deployment lasting an hour or more is expensive to repeat, while even short robot trials are typically run too few times to separate policies reliably  [ 133 ] .
6  Conclusion
In conclusion, GPT-6-Astra represents a clear leap in embodied intelligence, but not its endpoint. Our study shows that strong harness design with in-context learning and sufficient execution budget can push many isolated benchmarks close to saturation, while the remaining challenges shift toward transfer across tasks, long-horizon reasoning, failure recovery, and real-world constraints on time, cost, and safety. These findings also suggest that benchmark success should be interpreted with increasing care: the next frontier is not simply to solve more isolated tasks, but to build embodied systems that can operate reliably and efficiently under realistic deployment conditions.

8  Related Work
8.1  Foundation Models as Embodied Agents
Large language and vision-language models have long been used to bring semantic knowledge into robot control, but mostly at the level of high-level reasoning. SayCan  [ 68 ]  grounds an LLM’s proposals in the affordances of a pretrained skill library, and Inner Monologue  [ 66 ]  closes the loop by feeding success detection and scene descriptions back to the planner. Code as Policies  [ 98 ]  prompts the model to write programs that call perception and control APIs, while VoxPoser  [ 65 ]  and ReKep  [ 64 ]  have it compose 3D value maps or relational keypoint constraints that a motion planner then optimizes. Gemini Robotics-ER  [ 48 ]  extends this line with a model trained specifically for embodied reasoning. In all of these systems the foundation model specifies  what  to do, while a predefined skill set, planner or separately trained controller decides  how . We instead ask whether a frontier model can act as the controller itself, emitting end-effector actions in closed loop from its own observations.
A second line of work, which has grown rapidly in 2026, borrows the harness paradigm of coding agents  [ 74 ,  110 ]  and holds that embodied performance depends as much on the infrastructure around the model as on the model itself. Some harnesses redesign the action interface: RoboDawn  [ 54 ]  and Show-Harness  [ 28 ]  expose discrete, semantically meaningful motion commands that a frozen VLM can issue directly, and Robo-Harness K1  [ 97 ]  equips the model with calibrated perception tools such as depth, object tracking and grasp hypotheses. Others focus on context and verification: Thea  [ 145 ]  maintains a scene graph as persistent context and treats evaluation outcomes as exit codes, and Zetta  [ 36 ]  evolves code-based runtime critics and recovery skills around a frozen base policy. Guava  [ 101 ]  distills a frontier-model harness into a compact open model, while RHO  [ 39 ] , HARBOR  [ 96 ]  and EmbodiedSWE  [ 130 ]  use coding agents offline to build policy repositories, automate reinforcement-learning pipelines, or write programs against the full simulator state. Related studies examine how agents adapt from in-context experience  [ 7 ,  142 ]  and how reasoning latency interacts with a changing environment  [ 153 ,  180 ] . Our ablation in Sec.   3   complements these system-level proposals by isolating the contribution of individual harness components, namely the action interface, subagent roles and memory, under a common protocol.
8.2  Robot Policy Learning
The dominant paradigm for robot control learns a policy from demonstrations. Early large-scale efforts such as BC-Z  [ 71 ]  and RT-1  [ 17 ]  trained language-conditioned policies on large collections of teleoperated episodes, while Implicit BC  [ 44 ] , BeT  [ 128 ] , ACT  [ 178 ]  and Diffusion Policy  [ 30 ]  introduced action representations better suited to multimodal, high-precision behavior, and 3D Diffusion Policy  [ 170 ]  further conditions on point clouds. These policies are typically trained and evaluated per task, and most established manipulation benchmarks, including several used in this report, were designed around this setting (Sec.   5  ).
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) inherit semantic knowledge from web-scale pretraining by fine-tuning a vision-language model to output actions. RT-2  [ 185 ]  first demonstrated this transfer, OpenVLA  [ 83 ]  provided an open-source counterpart, and Octo  [ 52 ]  and RDT  [ 103 ]  trained generalist policies on cross-embodiment data such as Open X-Embodiment  [ 120 ]  and DROID  [ 79 ] . The    π  \pi    series  [ 14 ,  122 ,  13 ]  pairs a VLM backbone with flow-matching or tokenized action experts and reaches open-world generalization in unseen homes, while GR00T N1  [ 114 ]  and Gemini Robotics  [ 48 ]  extend the recipe to humanoids and to frontier-scale backbones. Recent systems such as Xiaomi-Robotics-0  [ 18 ]  and MolmoAct2  [ 41 ]  target real-time execution and deployable action reasoning. Grounding language in physical action nonetheless remains data-hungry, which has motivated the alternatives below.
World action models (WAMs) start instead from video or world-model backbones and predict how the scene will evolve together with the actions that produce it  [ 148 ,  129 ] . UniPi  [ 37 ]  generates videos and recovers actions with an inverse-dynamics model, GR-1 and GR-2  [ 154 ,  22 ]  pretrain on large-scale video, and Seer  [ 139 ] , VPP  [ 60 ] , UVA  [ 94 ]  and UWM  [ 184 ]  couple future prediction and action generation more tightly through predictive representations or joint diffusion. In 2026 this family has scaled rapidly. Cosmos Policy  [ 82 ]  and LingBot-VA  [ 93 ]  turn large video foundation models into policies, Fast-WAM  [ 169 ]  finds that most of the benefit comes from video modeling during training rather than from imagining the future at test time, and DreamZero  [ 166 ]  reports that WAMs generalize zero-shot to new tasks and environments substantially better than VLAs. A robustness study on LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0-Plus likewise finds WAMs more robust to visual and language perturbations  [ 176 ] .
The most recent systems blur the boundary between the two families. Motus  [ 10 ]  and BagelVLA  [ 61 ]  train unified models in which understanding, video-generation and action experts share attention, VLA-JEPA  [ 137 ]  augments a VLA with a latent world model, and     π  0.7   \pi_{0.7}     [ 123 ]  conditions its action expert on subgoal images produced by a world model. Despite their differences, all of these approaches learn a policy from robot data. We ask the complementary question of how far a general-purpose frontier model can go as a policy without benchmark-specific policy training, and our harness ablation (Sec.   3  ) also invokes a VLA (    π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}   ) as a callable tool.
8.3  Embodied Benchmarks
LIBERO  [ 99 ]  remains the most widely used simulation suite for language-conditioned manipulation. LIBERO-Plus  [ 42 ]  and LIBERO-PRO  [ 183 ]  perturb its scenes and instructions to expose brittleness and memorization, and LIBERO-Long++ and LIBERO-Ultra-Long  [ 162 ]  stress visual clutter and chains of 9 to 16 skills. SIMPLER  [ 95 ]  evaluates real-world policies in matched simulation, RoboCasa365  [ 112 ]  scales simulated household tasks for training and benchmarking generalist robots, RoboDojo  [ 26 ]  unifies simulated and real-robot evaluation of generalist policies, and RoboLab  [ 161 ]  adds high-fidelity scenes with controlled perturbations. For dexterous control, Bench2Dex  [ 163 ]  benchmarks visuo-tactile bimanual manipulation across hand designs, and DexJoCo  [ 144 ]  provides task-oriented dexterous tasks covering tool use, bimanual coordination and long-horizon execution in MuJoCo.
For navigation, Room-to-Room  [ 3 ]  established instruction following on a discrete navigation graph, VLN-CE  [ 87 ]  moved the task to continuous environments with low-level actions, and LHPR-VLN  [ 134 ]  extends it to long-horizon, multi-stage instructions.
Household benchmarks broaden the scope further. BEHAVIOR and BEHAVIOR-1K  [ 135 ,  90 ,  8 ]  derive everyday activities from human needs and evaluate full-length household tasks. Housekeep  [ 76 ]  and TIDEE  [ 127 ]  test commonsense tidying without explicit instructions, HAZARD  [ 182 ]  places agents in dynamically changing environments, PARTNR  [ 21 ]  evaluates multi-agent planning, and EmbodiedBench  [ 159 ]  and IS-Bench  [ 106 ]  evaluate multimodal LLM agents on vision-driven tasks and on interactive safety, respectively. Simulation platforms such as Genie Sim  [ 167 ]  continue to raise the fidelity of these evaluations.
Benchmarks lose discriminative power as leading systems saturate them  [ 80 ,  119 ] . Evaluation of language agents has responded with harder tasks  [ 20 ,  138 ,  4 ]  and with outcome-based protocols in which an agent acts in an environment by any means and is scored on the result  [ 74 ,  155 ,  110 ] , a protocol that EmbodiedSWE  [ 130 ]  brings to robotics. As discussed in Sec.   5  , most of the embodied benchmarks above were designed for policies trained by imitation, and we argue that general-purpose agents call for a different design.
8.4  Empirical Studies of Frontier Models in Embodied Settings
Each generation of frontier models has prompted empirical studies of its embodied abilities. Shortly after the release of ChatGPT and GPT-4V,   Vemprala et al. [141]   and   Wake et al. [143]   examined how these models could be prompted to plan and program robot tasks, and NavGPT  [ 181 ] , MapGPT  [ 24 ]  and Open-Nav  [ 124 ]  studied zero-shot vision-and-language navigation with LLMs reasoning over textual or map-based descriptions of the scene. GPT-4 also generated dense end-effector trajectories and replanned with external visual feedback  [ 89 ] . CaP-X  [ 46 ]  compared action abstractions, interaction modes, and perceptual grounding, exposing the dependence of model performance on supplied tools and interfaces. Our previous study  [ 19 ]  evaluated GPT-5 on eight spatial-intelligence benchmarks under a unified taxonomy and found unprecedented strength that nonetheless fell short of human performance across a broad range of tasks. The present report extends that effort from spatial to embodied intelligence, and from perception and reasoning to closed-loop control.
The release of GPT-6-Astra  [ 117 ]  set off a wave of such studies within weeks. In navigation, two evaluations on R2R-CE  [ 34 ,  33 ]  find that GPT-6-Astra rivals or surpasses prior zero-shot and supervised methods from monocular RGB input, yet still struggles to turn correct local judgments into reliable route execution and stopping decisions. In manipulation,   Zhang et al. [174]   report that GPT-6-Astra ranks first among public entries on the RoboDojo leaderboard while other frontier LLMs succeed in fewer than 2% of trials, but also that one-shot demonstrations lower rather than raise its success rate and that real-robot trials had to be halted because of unsafe actions. Astranomy  [ 158 ]  compares direct, agentic, and policy-assisted control, while Ditto-Bench  [ 104 ]  examines action interfaces, memory, and reasoning effort in contact-rich manipulation. Other studies examine how demonstration formats and accumulated interaction memory affect in-context adaptation  [ 100 ,  29 ,  165 ,  168 ] , the reuse of body knowledge and prior experience together with sim-to-real transfer  [ 57 ] , direct manipulation on LIBERO  [ 35 ] , token-efficient agents that compose classical primitives with VLA calls  [ 132 ] , and GPT-6-Astra’s capabilities as an embodied policy more broadly  [ 47 ,  164 ,  67 ] . RoboMME studies examine full-history direct control  [ 72 ]  and hierarchies combining Astra planning, learned execution, and lightweight monitoring  [ 23 ] . Real-robot studies demonstrate coding-agent control and correction  [ 73 ] , while broader evaluations expose reliability gaps and substantial execution time and inference costs  [ 5 ,  121 ,  177 ,  109 ] . Vals Robo-bench  [ 140 ]  incorporates inference delays through continuously running physics and wall-clock budgets. The Embodied Agent Arena  [ 62 ]  compares seven VLMs on 1,000 cases drawn from 32 benchmarks and finds that GPT-6-Astra excels at precise estimation and contact localization but not at coordinating goal-directed sequences,   Rasheed et al. [125]   observe a similar split between semantic and metric-geometric tasks across computer vision, and RoboQuest  [ 102 ]  examines evidence acquisition and exploration stopping decisions. RoboHarm  [ 136 ]  reports that GPT-6-Astra completed 60 of 100 deliberately harmful robot-arm trials and refused only two.
These studies typically examine one benchmark family, one capability or one harness at a time, and their conclusions do not always agree: demonstrations lower GPT-6-Astra’s success on RoboDojo in   Zhang et al. [174]  , yet RoboICL  [ 100 ]  reports gains when demonstrations are combined with interaction memory, and RoboDawn  [ 54 ]  reports similar gains from one-shot demonstrations with a frozen VLM. Our study differs in evaluating manipulation and navigation benchmarks under a single protocol, ablating harness design, validating on real robots, and drawing on these results to propose principles for the next generation of embodied benchmarks.

Observation and control settings.
GPT-6-Astra observes calibrated RGB images from a fixed third-person  agentview  camera and the Panda wrist camera (native       ×  256   256\!\times\!256    observations, with       ×  1024   1024\!\times\!1024    inspection renders), together with the measured end-effector pose and gripper state. The agent interface also provides calibration-based pixel back-projection into the current scene; LIBERO-PRO does not expose object poses or evaluator diagnostics to the planner. The underlying action is the benchmark’s seven-dimensional Panda OSC-pose command at 20 Hz: three translational components, three axis–angle rotational components, and one gripper command. The interface compiles model-selected Cartesian motion, wrist-rotation, and gripper primitives into these actions. A unit translational or rotational component corresponds to 5 cm or 0.5 rad, respectively.
Budgets and continuation.
The standard     1  ×   1\times    boundary is the first of 10,000 environment steps or 100 planner turns. An unsuccessful episode then continues from the same simulator state and planner conversation to a cumulative     10  ×   10\times    environment-step budget; the continuation is not terminated by the 100-turn     1  ×   1\times    cap. The frozen     1  ×   1\times    outcome is never replaced by the continuation. Table   3   reports success rate (SR) for every paired Task/Swap base task, the four family aggregates, and all 800 episodes, while integrating prior agentic and policy-based methods at their reported suite-level granularity.
Results and comparison.
At     1  ×   1\times   ,  GPT-6-Astra + ICL  succeeds in 786 of 800 episodes (98.25%), exceeding the published RPent/GPT-6-Astra baseline with low reasoning  [ 175 ] , the strongest prior result, by 5.62 percentage points. It obtains 99–100% SR on six suites; the remaining errors concentrate in Long Task (97%) and Long Swap (90%). Continuing to the cumulative     10  ×   10\times    boundary raises the result to 794/800 (99.25%), including 99% on Long Task and 95% on Long Swap. Observation and control settings.
GPT-6-Astra observes calibrated RGB images from a fixed third-person  agentview  camera and the Panda wrist camera (native       ×  256   256\!\times\!256    observations, with       ×  1024   1024\!\times\!1024    inspection renders), the measured end-effector pose and gripper state, and the released ordered-skill and instance-pose feedback used by our LiLo adapter. Calibration-based pixel back-projection supplies current-scene 3D geometry. The underlying action is the benchmark’s seven-dimensional Panda OSC-pose command at 20 Hz: three translational components, three axis–angle rotational components, and one gripper command. The agent interface compiles model-selected Cartesian motion, wrist-rotation, and gripper primitives into these actions. A unit translational or rotational component corresponds to 5 cm or 0.5 rad, respectively.
Budgets and continuation.
The standard     1  ×   1\times    step budgets are 600 or 800 steps on Long++ and 1,800, 2,000, or 3,200 steps on Ultra-Long. Success rate (SR) requires completion of the entire ordered chain within this boundary. Average progress (AP) is the fraction of the uninterrupted correct skill prefix completed at the same boundary. An unsuccessful episode continues from the same simulator state and planner conversation to a cumulative     10  ×   10\times    step budget; the     1  ×   1\times    outcome and prefix snapshot remain frozen. There is no separate planner-turn cap; a 12-hour planner wall-clock guard handles infrastructure censoring but does not change the environment-step budget. Prior work does not report an agentic method under this protocol. Table   4   reports every task and compares  GPT-6-Astra + ICL  with LiLo-VLA and two learned-policy baselines in the corresponding ordering-level aggregate rows.
Results and comparison.
At     1  ×   1\times   ,  GPT-6-Astra + ICL  achieves 97.5% SR and 99.2% AP on LIBERO-Long++, and 82.2% SR and 95.7% AP on LIBERO-Ultra-Long, exceeding LiLo-VLA by 15.5 and 38.2 percentage points in SR, respectively. The cumulative     10  ×   10\times    results reach 100.0% SR/AP on Long++ and 98.9% SR with 99.4% AP on Ultra-Long. Macro-averaged over the nine scenarios, the corresponding results are 92.4%/98.1% at     1  ×   1\times    and 99.6%/99.8% at     10  ×   10\times   . Observation and control settings.
Our RoboDojo experiments use the dual-arm ARX X5 robot, and GPT-6-Astra receives     640  ×  480   640\times 480    RGB images from head, left-wrist and right-wrist cameras, measured joint positions, gripper states and end-effector poses. Privileged information is withheld: object poses, depth images, segmentation masks, contact information, hidden simulator state, task source code and evaluator diagnostics. For each arm, GPT-6-Astra outputs a target end-effector position     (  x  ,  y  ,  z  )   (x,y,z)    in meters in the environment coordinate frame, a target orientation as a unit quaternion     (  w  ,  x  ,  y  ,  z  )   (w,x,y,z)   , and an open or close gripper command. Each target differs from the current measured pose by at most 5 cm in position and 0.35 rad in orientation. Inverse kinematics converts these end-effector targets into joint targets, with each control step limiting end-effector translation to 2 cm, rotation to 0.1 rad and each joint’s rotation to 0.05 rad. Control runs at 25 Hz; an action request can repeat a target for 1–10 control steps. After each action request, GPT-6-Astra uses the returned images and measured robot state to choose the next action.
Budgets and continuation.
We evaluate GPT-6-Astra + ICL with the     1  ×   1\times    and     10  ×   10\times    step limits. Each task has a standard control-step limit    B  B    ranging from 200 to 1,900 steps, with evaluation limits of    B  B    and     10  ​  B   10B   , respectively.
We first run each episode for up to    B  B    control steps, or until earlier termination, and measure the     1  ×   1\times    result from this initial execution. If the episode has not terminated, we continue the same episode up to a total of     10  ​  B   10B    steps and record the     10  ×   10\times    result. Execution ends when the simulator terminates the episode or GPT-6-Astra stops.
9.4  RoboLab
Benchmark and tasks.
RoboLab  [ 161 ]  evaluates task-generalist manipulation policies in high-fidelity simulation. Tasks span three difficulty levels and include object selection, spatial rearrangement, stacking, and reorientation. The benchmark supports evaluation of policies trained on real-world data without requiring co-training on simulation data.

9.7  LHPR-VLN
Benchmarks and tasks.
LHPR-VLN  [ 134 ]  evaluates long-horizon vision-language navigation. Given a global natural-language instruction, the policy must reach multiple navigation goals in a strictly specified order. Following these instructions requires visually grounding successive goals, tracking completed stages, and using earlier observations to guide subsequent movements.
Evaluation subset.
The official unseen test split contains 205 episodes across 27 scenes. Many episodes contain subtask target objects that are not visible along the reference trajectories. We manually checked target visibility along each episode’s reference trajectory and retained an episode only when every subtask target object was visible. The resulting subset contains 114 episodes across 25 scenes, with two to four goals per episode. The complete retained episode list appears below.
Protocol and metrics.
Each episode starts in a fresh session with the global instruction and a frozen ICL workspace containing exploration notes, code, and observations from training scenes that do not overlap with the test scenes. Test episodes do not share interaction history. During navigation, the policy receives three RGB views at a resolution of     366  ×  366   366\times 366   , facing left, forward, and right at     +   60  ∘    +60^{\circ}   ,     0  ∘   0^{\circ}   , and     −   60  ∘    -60^{\circ}   . Available actions are 0.25 m forward motion,     30  ∘   30^{\circ}    left or right turns, and  STOP . Three consecutive blocked forward actions trigger a random turn and a forward action, both counted toward the action budget. Ground-truth trajectories, target coordinates, and scores are withheld. To submit a goal, the policy issues  STOP , which advances the stage regardless of success. A submission succeeds within 1 m geodesic distance of the original target or a matching target object in the specified room. Environment feedback identifies the current stage without revealing submission success. The     1  ×   1\times    and     10  ×   10\times    settings allow 500 and 5,000 actions per sequence. Extended evaluation retains successful     1  ×   1\times    outcomes and evaluates only initially unsuccessful episodes with the larger budget, preserving earlier scores when they are higher. Metrics are averaged over all 114 episodes. SR measures full-sequence success. ISR, CSR, and CGT describe individual-goal and sequential success, with definitions in the table note.

9.8  VLN-CE
Benchmarks and tasks.
VLN-CE  [ 87 ]  evaluates instruction-guided navigation in continuous indoor environments. Given a natural-language route instruction, the policy must navigate from a specified starting pose to a single goal and stop. This requires grounding landmarks, tracking progress along the route, and choosing movements from visual observations without a predefined navigation graph.
Evaluation subsets.
R2R  [ 3 ]  uses the 100-episode val-unseen subset from Open-Nav  [ 124 ] , which covers 10 scenes and is also adopted by SmartWay  [ 131 ]  and AgentCanvas  [ 1 ] . For RxR  [ 88 ] , we select 100 English guide episodes from the val-unseen split of 3,669 English episodes across 11 scenes, matching the R2R subset’s episode counts in each of the same 10 scenes. With seed 0, we draw 20,000 stratified 100-episode subsets and retain the candidate with the smallest sum of two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances to the full English split for instruction word count and reference-path length. We then attempt 60,000 random within-scene episode replacements, accepting a change only if it reduces this sum. Instruction length is the whitespace-separated word count, and reference-path length is the sum of Euclidean distances between consecutive reference waypoints. The complete episode lists for both subsets appear below.
Protocol and metrics.
Each episode starts in a fresh session with a frozen ICL workspace prepared using training scenes that do not overlap with the test scenes. Test episodes do not share interaction history. Observations contain a single forward-facing RGB image at     512  ×  512   512\times 512    resolution. Forward actions move 0.25 m. R2R uses     15  ∘   15^{\circ}    left or right turns, while RxR uses     30  ∘   30^{\circ}    turns and permits     30  ∘   30^{\circ}    upward or downward camera tilts. Ground-truth routes, goal coordinates, and evaluation scores are withheld.  STOP  terminates the episode and succeeds within 3 m geodesic distance of the goal. The     1  ×   1\times    and     10  ×   10\times    limits are 500 and 5,000 actions. Extended evaluation is applied to initially unsuccessful episodes. Reported extended results retain prior successes and the better per-episode value of each metric. Metrics are averaged over each 100-episode subset. NE is the final geodesic distance to the goal. OS is the oracle success rate, measuring whether the trajectory ever enters the 3 m success region. SR measures successful completion, and SPL  [ 2 ]  weights success by path efficiency. nDTW  [ 69 ]  measures alignment with the reference trajectory using normalized dynamic time warping.

Demonstration preparation.
For task-level in-context learning (ICL), GPT-6-Astra explores the released training demonstrations through an information-bounded reader and compiles task notes, reference numerical data, image indices, and runnable helpers into a single hash-frozen package; the same package is shared across L0/L1/L2, every test episode starts from a fresh session, and no model weights are updated. Preparation accesses 99–100 released demonstrations per task; XMoveBendPick has no ICL package and is reported zero-shot only.
Observation and control settings.
At test time GPT-6-Astra receives a head-mounted left RGB image (typically     640  ×  360   640\times 360   ), a 32-column robot-interface state, the task text, and the current episode’s history. 9.10  BEHAVIOR
Benchmarks and tasks.  BEHAVIOR-1K  [ 90 ]  is a household embodied-AI benchmark covering 1,000 everyday activities grounded in human needs. Built on the OmniGibson simulator, it supports realistic interactions with rigid objects, deformable objects, and liquids. Its long-horizon activities require navigation and manipulation to achieve task-specific goal conditions.
We evaluate on all 100 tasks from the BEHAVIOR-1K 2026 Challenge, using public-test episode 0 with rollout seed 0 for each task and performing a single evaluation rollout per task instance. We retain the benchmark’s default R1Pro dual-arm mobile manipulator, with the arm-controller modification described below. Each rollout starts from the official task-instance initialization and proceeds through closed-loop interaction conditioned on head-mounted and bilateral wrist RGB-D observations, robot proprioception, and the task instruction. The simulator operates at 30 native steps per second, and each model-issued action can be repeated for 1–100 simulation steps. A rollout terminates upon environment termination, exhaustion of the official task-specific step budget, or an explicit agent finish call. Following the official evaluator, the step budget is set to 1.5 times the task’s mean human-demonstration length.
Protocol and Metrics.  We use GPT-6 Astra with maximum reasoning effort for both demonstration-based in-context learning and evaluation. During the ICL stage, all official training data are provided including RGB videos, actions, proprioceptive states, and annotations. We adopt a 21-dimensional action interface, consisting of three base-control values, four torso joint targets, two six-dimensional absolute end-effector pose targets, and two gripper commands. Each end-effector target specifies a position and axis-angle orientation in the robot’s current base frame, which the official inverse-kinematics controller translates into arm motion. For consistency with this interface, demonstration arm commands are converted from joint-space targets to end-effector poses using the official forward-kinematics implementation.
We assess performance using the official evaluator, reporting binary task success, final predicate-based Q-score, simulator step count and simulated time, and base and end-effector travel distances together with their official human-normalized counterparts. Model token usage and wall-clock durations for individual stages are recorded separately to characterize computational cost.
Results.  Across the 100 evaluation rollouts on the BEHAVIOR-1K 2026 Challenge, our method successfully completes 37 tasks, achieving an overall task success rate of 37%.
Other Discussion. The aggregate task success rate increases from 1% with the 23-dimensional joint-space interface to 37% with the 21-dimensional end-effector-pose interface. This substantial improvement is consistent with the hypothesis that task-space action representations better align language-model planning with robotic execution. By specifying the desired position and orientation of each end effector, the model can express manipulation intent directly while delegating joint-level kinematic computation to the official controller.
Qualitative inspection of the trajectories suggests that the model can extract procedural knowledge from demonstrations, identify task stages, and adapt its plans based on visual feedback. However, failures due to imprecise grasp alignment, arm collisions, navigation obstructions, and ineffective recovery strategies highlight persistent challenges in contact-rich manipulation. Taken together, these findings suggest that action representation plays a critical role in embodied task performance, but do not establish that high-level reasoning alone is sufficient for reliable robotic manipulation.

10.1  Agent Components
We evaluate four components individually: (i) a planning subagent that decomposes tasks into subgoals and completion criteria, with its outputs incorporated into the main agent’s context; (ii) an execution subagent that generates EEF actions from the main agent’s subgoals; (iii) a feedback subagent that evaluates execution outcomes and suggests continuation, adjustment, or retry; and (iv) history compression that retains the latest four turns and consolidates earlier interactions into a rolling summary capped at 2,000 tokens.
This study investigates how different agent components affect the embodied performance of GPT-6-Astra and whether increasing agent complexity consistently improves task execution. As shown in Table   13  , introducing a feedback subagent yields the largest improvement, increasing the average score by 7.22 points and the success rate by 9.16 percentage points, with only a 7.03% increase in token consumption. History compression improves the score by 5.25 points and the success rate by 5.55 percentage points while reducing token consumption by 66.41%, demonstrating benefits in both performance and efficiency. The planning subagent provides a smaller overall score improvement of 3.25 points at a 15.63% increase in token consumption, although its substantial gain in the Memory category indicates that its benefits vary across task categories. In contrast, the execution subagent reduces the score by 1.22 points at approximately the same token cost as the baseline. These results suggest that effective agent design depends on how additional reasoning supports task execution, rather than on the number of components alone.
Feedback and history compression offer complementary benefits: explicit outcome evaluation improves task performance with a modest increase in token consumption, while compressing earlier interactions improves performance and substantially reduces context overhead. These findings motivate further investigation of structured execution memory and targeted verification mechanisms for tracking task progress and failures. Future harness design should assess additional components by their contributions to both task performance and total token consumption.

Findings
•
Rigid pick-and-place transfers from simulation.  Block stacking succeeds in all 6 trials across different initial configurations, matching simulation.
•
Folding is judged without a shared criterion. 11.4  Single Arm with Gripper — PiPER with Pika Gripper: Container Nesting, Cloth Folding and Plug Insertion
Setting
Robot
One AgileX PiPER arm (6 joints) with a Pika parallel gripper
Cameras given to GPT-6-Astra
Wrist RealSense D405 (640   ×  \times   480 RGB) and a third-person Azure Kinect DK from the front right (1280   ×  \times   720 RGB). Depth is not given as an image, but a tool returns the depth and 3D point of a queried wrist-image pixel
Action interface
GPT-6-Astra outputs target end-effector poses, motion paths and gripper commands; local tools solve IK, check and execute the trajectory, and return the measured state with new images of both views
Harness
gpt-6-astra , with reasoning effort  high  for container nesting and plug insertion, and  max  for folding; every trial starts from a fresh context, without the trajectories or interaction records of other trials
Budget and success rule
60 minutes from the first model request (plus 60 s grace); a person judges success from the final state: containers must be stably nested in the intended order, the plug must be correctly seated in the socket, and folding is assessed qualitatively from the overall folded result
Context provided
•
Task prompt:  The task goal, step guidance and manipulation advice (e.g. cup wall or rim grasps).
•
System prompt:  Tool and coordinate conventions, joint-coordination principles, and instructions to verify action outcomes and replan when needed.
•
Observations and feedback:  Current images, measured robot state, calibration, joint limits and within-trial tool feedback. The model chooses grasp points, poses and routes; no demonstrations or reference trajectories are provided.

Findings
•
Re-planning from feedback.  When the receiving bowl is displaced by contact, GPT-6-Astra detects the change, raises the container and realigns before placing it (run 3, 07:06–10:40); after an empty grasp it re-grasps the cloth (folding run 2, 12:46–17:21).
•

•
Insertion is limited by observation and reachability.  The pins and the socket are poorly visible, and poses that would reveal them are limited by joint reachability. Reorienting the initially upward-facing plug for insertion can also bring the arm close to its joint limits.
•
Folding is judged without a shared criterion. All three reported trials met this practical criterion, although wrinkles and uneven edges remain (Fig.   13  , B1). One run reached the 60-minute limit and was subsequently accepted based on its final state. Since the success criterion was not standardized across platforms (App.   11.2  ), the reported folding success rates are not directly comparable.

11.9  Humanoid — Unitree G1: Tabletop Manipulation and Locomotion
Setting
Robot
Unitree G1 humanoid (29 body DoF) with two Wuji Gen-1 dexterous hands; the right thumb is mechanically frozen, and waist, legs and body height are locked during manipulation
Cameras given to GPT-6-Astra
Head-mounted Azure Kinect, RGB only; depth is recorded but neither given to the model nor used as a collision guard. Measured robot state is additionally available through tools
Action interface
Manipulation:  move_arms  (14 absolute arm-joint targets in radians, clamped to joint limits and interpolated by the offboard arm controller) and  set_hands  (binary open/close profile per hand, no individual-finger, grip-width or force control; hands driven by the onboard Jetson). Locomotion:  turn_to_heading  (IMU yaw feedback),  walk_forward  (fixed 0.15 m/s for a commanded duration, at most 1.5 s per step) and  stop , executed by the vendor high-level controller; GPT-6-Astra never commands leg joints, and no translational odometry is available to the policy
Harness
Tool calls through the OpenAI Responses API ( gpt-6-astra , reasoning effort  max , mode  pro ,  store=false ); model calls, tool calls and camera frames are archived
Budget and success rule
Six retained runs, three per task; manipulation and locomotion use different tool sets and are evaluated separately. No common 60-minute cutoff applies: both successful manipulation runs exceeded one hour. A person judges the binary outcome from third-person video after the run, separately from the model’s completion statement; the requested    ∼  \sim   10 cm stopping distance is approximate and the final gap was not independently measured. Operator emergency stop throughout; the locomotion runs additionally use a slack safety-gantry strap
Context provided
•
Task instruction, embodiment description, tool schemas, joint limits, camera placement, binary hand open/close profiles, locomotion and safety constraints, RGB observations, robot-state feedback, and the current run’s interaction history.
•
No demonstrations, no demonstration-derived ICL package, and no semantic pick, place or navigation primitives; every motion is chosen by the model, one tool call at a time.

Manipulation succeeds in 2/3 trials and locomotion in 3/3, giving 5/6 overall. The failed manipulation run drops the toy off the table, and recovery is not completed within the fixed-body workspace. With three trials per task, these results establish feasibility rather than reliability.
Findings
•
Observation-conditioned approach under a safety constraint.  In walk run 1, GPT-6-Astra shortens successive forward commands from 0.8 s to 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and 0.15 s after fresh images, and all three locomotion runs stop at a self-estimated 0.15–0.20 m rather than 0.10 m, citing the no-contact requirement. This shows conservative progress assessment, not metric stopping accuracy.
•
Valid joint targets do not guarantee faithful execution.  In pick run 3, all 26 arm motions return  stalled_short_of_target  (18) or  timeout  (8), with median final tracking error 0.07065 rad and maximum 0.1904 rad, yet the human-labelled outcome is a success: useful motion occurs without meeting the joint-target criterion. GPT-6-Astra notices the discrepancies and adjusts, but reliable error compensation is not established.
•
Slow supervision remains costly on hardware.  Across 108 archived API calls, median request latency is 24.84 s, and API request wall time accounts for 88.8–91.7% of elapsed run time; image capture takes 4.5–9.0%. The remainder includes tool execution and harness overhead and is not a direct measure of physical motion time. Both successful manipulation runs last over an hour.
