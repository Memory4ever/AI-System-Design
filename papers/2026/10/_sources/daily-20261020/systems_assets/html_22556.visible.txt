Abstract
Long-horizon, multi-stage Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies often require
visual information that is no longer present in the current observation.
Retaining the complete observation history is computationally expensive and
highly redundant, motivating selective visual memory. We introduce
RePlanMem-VLA  ( Replanning-Guided Keyframe Memory for VLA ), which turns a policy’s own plan revisions into an online memory-write signal. Under receding-horizon execution, consecutive action chunks predict some of the same future actions. RePlanMem-VLA treats unusually large disagreement between these temporally aligned predictions as evidence of a task-relevant event. We first compare 35 disagreement measures across single-task autoregressive decoder-only and Flow-Matching Transformers trained from scratch, then validate the selected signals on pretrained OpenVLA-OFT and     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    policies over MIKASA-Robo-VLA tasks. The resulting detectors achieve ROC-AUC of
0.981–0.997. We then use the selected signals in RePlanMem-VLA to trigger updates to a bounded visual memory, with a robot-masked write filter suppressing redundant frames. In the evaluated memory-retention experiments, RePlanMem-VLA preserves task-relevant observations until the later decision point in     100  %   100\%    of episodes. Real-robot replay data further show that event selection and redundancy suppression extend beyond simulation. These results establish plan revision as a policy-internal signal for controlling what a VLA remembers.

1  Introduction
Long-horizon manipulation often requires Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies to use visual information
that has left the current observation, including object properties  ( Chen et al., 2026a ;  Shi et al., 2026 ) ,
earlier environment states  ( Chen et al., 2026a ;  Shi et al., 2026 ) , and task
progress  ( Buamanee et al., 2026 ;  Feng et al., 2026 ) .
Retaining the full history preserves these cues but increases context length and
computation while adding visual redundancy  ( Jang et al., 2025 ;  Qiu et al., 2026 ;  Wang et al., 2026 ) .
We therefore seek a small set of informative observations.
We call these observations  keyframes : they preserve cues needed
later  ( Shi et al., 2026 ;  Lei et al., 2026 )  or mark task-phase
transitions  ( Buamanee et al., 2026 ;  Yi et al., 2026 ) . Prior work
selects them with vision-language models  ( Lei et al., 2026 ) , waypoint
heuristics  ( Shi et al., 2023 ) ,
learned retrieval  ( Sridhar et al., 2026 ) , progress estimates  ( Liu et al., 2026 ) ,
or event detectors  ( Zeng et al., 2026b ) . We investigate whether the policy’s own plan revisions can provide an online signal for selecting these observations.
Under receding-horizon execution, consecutive action chunks predict some of the same future actions, while the new plan incorporates
the latest observation (Fig.   1  ). Comparing these temporally aligned predictions reveals how the policy revises its intended behavior after receiving new visual information.
Our central hypothesis is that task-relevant observations induce unusually large revisions in predictions for the same future
actions.  This motivates using plan disagreement as a policy-internal signal to identify candidate keyframes for visual memory.
We introduce  RePlanMem-VLA
( Replanning-Guided Keyframe Memory for VLA ), which derives its selection
signal from revisions between overlapping action predictions already produced
during normal replanning. Selected keyframes become part of the policy’s visual context,
keeping past observations available for subsequent action prediction, while a robot-masked write filter suppresses redundant frames, reducing repeated overwrites. At deployment, the trigger requires neither keyframe annotations nor privileged simulator state.
Our experiments are conducted on MIKASA-Robo-VLA  ( Cherepanov et al., 2026b ) , an extension of MIKASA-Robo  ( Cherepanov et al., 2026a )
for evaluating memory-intensive manipulation with VLA policies. We first screen 35 disagreement metrics using autoregressive decoder-only and
Flow-Matching Transformers  ( Lipman et al., 2023 )
trained from scratch. We then evaluate detectors based on the selected signals with
pretrained OpenVLA-OFT  ( Kim et al., 2025 )  and
π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}     ( Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 )  policies, obtaining ROC-AUC of
0.981–0.997.
In the evaluated memory-retention experiments, RePlanMem-VLA
preserves task-relevant observations until the later decision
point in     100  %   100\%    of episodes, while robot-masked filtering
reduces redundant overwrites.
Real-robot replay data further support its event-selection and
redundancy-suppression behavior beyond simulation
(Fig.   2  ).
Our main contributions are as follows:
1.
We introduce  RePlanMem-VLA , which identifies keyframes from
policy-internal revisions of predictions for the same future actions across
consecutive replanning steps (Section   3.3  ).
2.
We  identify disagreement measures that best align with keyframe events  through systematic analysis across decoder-only and Flow-Matching Transformer policies, and verify their transfer to pretrained OpenVLA-OFT and     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    policies
(Sections   4.1   and   4.2  ).
3.
We develop a  bounded visual-memory mechanism  where plan disagreement determines when to write and redundancy-aware filtering, including robot-masked clustering, determines what to retain, substantially reducing redundant updates while preserving task-relevant observations (Sections   3   and   4.3  ).

2  Related Work
Memory for long-horizon, partially observable policies.
Long-horizon manipulation often requires access to information from earlier in
the episode. Recent VLA work addresses this through
recurrent or belief-state
latents  ( Cherepanov et al., 2026c ;  Li et al., 2026a ) ,
explicit memory representations
( Shi et al., 2026 ;  Guo et al., 2026 ) , long-context or
compressed multi-frame conditioning
( Jang et al., 2025 ) , and retrieval- or structure-based memory
( Sridhar et al., 2026 ;  Haresh et al., 2026 ;  Chung et al., 2026 ;  Li et al., 2026b ) .
Memory-dependent manipulation is also studied in dedicated benchmarks
( Cherepanov et al., 2026b ;  Chen et al., 2026a ;  Lei et al., 2026 ) . These works study how historical information is represented or retrieved, whereas our focus is on when an observation should be admitted to a bounded memory.
Keyframe and event selection for visual memory.
Existing approaches identify important observations using motion or
bottleneck-pose heuristics  ( James et al., 2022 ) ,
learned frame scoring  ( Mark et al., 2026 ;  Chen et al., 2026c ) ,
progress, value, or critic signals
( Liu et al., 2026 ;  Yi et al., 2026 ;  Buamanee et al., 2026 ) ,
trajectory annotation  ( Feng et al., 2026 ) , or dedicated event detectors
( Zeng et al., 2026b )  — each
relying on a separate selector or external signal. We instead derive the selection signal directly from changes in the acting policy’s own predicted action plans.
Action chunking, replanning, and plan consistency.
Action-chunking policies predict short action sequences and under receding-horizon
execution successive chunks overlap on predictions for the same future actions
( Zhao et al., 2023 ;  Chi et al., 2023 ;  Kim et al., 2025 ;  Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ) . Prior work mainly uses this overlap to improve consistency
( Zhao et al., 2023 ;  Liu et al., 2025 )
or studies the trade-off between open-loop execution and replanning
( Lazzati et al., 2026 ;  Zeng et al., 2026a ;  Chen et al., 2026b ;  Jing et al., 2026 ) .
We instead use disagreement between overlapping predictions as a signal that the
new observation has changed the policy’s intended behavior.
Memory representation, keyframe selection, and plan consistency are typically
studied separately, whereas we connect them by using plan disagreement as a
policy-internal signal for memory updates.
3  RePlanMem-VLA
3.1  Overview
In long-horizon manipulation, task-relevant visual information often leaves the cameras before it is needed. A policy that sees only the current frame cannot recover that cue, so we concatenate the live views with a bounded visual memory of    B  B    slots.
Slots are empty at the start of an episode and are never carried across episode boundaries.
The policy predicts a chunk of    K  K    actions from one observation and executes only the first     H  <  K   H<K    before replanning, so consecutive chunks overlap on     K  −  H   K{-}H    future steps: the same intended actions, predicted before and after the new frame.
Prior work treats disagreement on that overlap as noise to suppress.
We read a large revision as evidence that the new observation was task-relevant and use it to trigger a memory-update proposal. The direct update rule stores the immediately preceding frame, whereas the redundancy-aware variant retains only visual states that are not already represented in memory.
Figures   1   and   4   and Algorithm   1   summarize the procedure.
At every query the policy first predicts a pre-write chunk from the memory available before a potential write, then overlap disagreement is converted, without gradients, into a task-calibrated surprise score and an event triggers frame selection and a memory update only when a visually novel state is found. The final action prediction is conditioned on the resulting memory state, and only this prediction receives the original supervised fine-tuning (SFT) objective. Event detection never sees expert actions, so memory writes are driven only by the policy’s own plan revisions.
3.2  Memory-augmented action policy
In partially observable manipulation, identical current observations
can require different actions depending on earlier cues or task
progress  ( Cherepanov et al., 2026a ;  Chen et al., 2026a ) , as illustrated by the delayed-cue setup in Fig.   2  .
A policy conditioned only on the current observation cannot distinguish such histories.
Historical context can resolve this ambiguity, motivating multi-frame
conditioning and memory-augmented
policies  ( Jang et al., 2025 ;  Shi et al., 2026 ) . To bound the amount of historical visual input, we retain at most    B  B    observations. The resulting online selection problem is which observations
to preserve for future control. We call these observations keyframes, focusing on transient cues needed later and observations marking changes in task phase.
Let    q  q    index policy queries,     o  q   o_{q}    denote the current multi-view observation, and    ℓ  \ell    denote the language instruction. Action chunking predicts multiple future controls from a single
observation, supporting temporally coordinated behavior and efficient
execution  ( Zhao et al., 2023 ;  Chi et al., 2023 ;  Kim et al., 2025 ) .

at query    q  q    for the    j  j   -th step of the chunk.

3.5  Redundancy-aware frame selection
An event determines  when  memory should be reconsidered, but not
necessarily which frame should be stored. At an event, we jointly consider
the current FIFO contents and a short causal window     W  q   W_{q}    of observations
preceding the current query. We use the three observations preceding the
event query (    W  =  3   W=3   ) and apply deterministic complete-link clustering with
threshold     δ  =  0.01   \delta=0.01   . From each novel cluster, the most recent candidate
is selected as its representative. The current observation is excluded so that
selection cannot use information obtained after the detected plan revision.
We group visually similar frames using complete-link clustering and treat a
candidate cluster as novel only if it contains no frame already represented
in memory. From each novel cluster, we retain its most recent candidate and
append the selected representatives to the bounded FIFO in chronological
order. Thus, one detector event may result in zero, one, or multiple writes.
Visual masks, when available, are used only to suppress irrelevant appearance
changes during comparison, while the original RGB frames are stored and supplied
to the policy. The direct update rule is the special case in which the
selector returns only the immediately preceding frame without redundancy
filtering.

3.6  Event-gated memory update and second forward pass
When an event is detected at query    q  q   , the frame-selection rule returns a
possibly empty sequence     R  q   R_{q}    of candidate keyframes. The direct rule returns

Let     A  q  GT   A_{q}^{\mathrm{GT}}    be the expert action chunk associated with query    q  q   . We retain the backbone’s standard action objective and apply it only to

Expert actions supervise control only: they are not used to identify events or select keyframes.
The same causal detector and memory-update procedure can therefore be used at inference time without keyframe annotations or privileged simulator state.
4  Experimental evaluation
Our experiments address three Research Questions (RQs):
RQ1.
Does revision of overlapping action chunks contain
a task-relevant signal, and which disagreement measures extract it most
reliably?
RQ2.
Does this signal transfer to pretrained VLA policies
across different backbones and replanning frequencies, and can it support
accurate online event detection?
RQ3.
Can the resulting surprise signal be used to drive
selective visual-memory updates during VLA fine-tuning?
4.1  RQ1: Disagreement signals in single-task policies
We first examine whether disagreement between overlapping action predictions contains task-relevant information using single-task autoregressive and Flow-Matching Transformers trained from scratch. This analysis allows us to efficiently screen a
large set of disagreement metrics at substantially lower computational cost before transferring the most informative signals to pretrained VLA
policies. We evaluate 35 disagreement metrics
(Appendix   C  ) across eight MIKASA-Robo-VLA tasks (Appendix   A.1  ) and examine
whether their behavior remains consistent across different task structures and amounts of available historical information. For each architecture, we compare
two regimes: full-context, where the policy has
access to the full observation history up to the current query, and current-only, where the prediction is conditioned only on the current observation.
Visual analysis of disagreement profiles shows that scores often
remain near a relatively stable baseline, with peaks occurring near
keyframes. This pattern motivates a simple threshold-based rule:
a frame is predicted as a keyframe when its disagreement score
exceeds a threshold.
We use Frame ROC-AUC as the primary screening criterion,
evaluating how well these predictions match reference keyframe labels across detection thresholds. A more detailed analysis, including correlation analysis, and task-specific results, is provided in
Appendix   A  .
Experiments show that disagreement measures remain informative both with and without observation history, although their effectiveness varies across tasks and settings.
On tasks with clear phase transitions, such as
RememberColor3-VLA-v0  and  ShellGameColorLampTouch-VLA-v0 , for autoregressive policies, Causal Z-score reaches Frame ROC-AUC
of     0.798  /  0.746   0.798/0.746    under full-context/current-only conditioning, with EMA deviation showing a similar pattern (    0.790  /  0.712   0.790/0.712   ).
For Flow-Matching policies, distributional measures achieve higher scores: Wasserstein-1 reaches    0.9388  0.9388    and Energy distance
0.9427  0.9427   . Translation, rotation, and gripper disagreement also perform well,
with scores ranging from    0.851  0.851    to    0.911  0.911   . Causal Z-score remains stable
(    0.696  /  0.690   0.696/0.690   ), showing that the causal temporal signal persists across both action-generation paradigms.
The broader analysis establishes disagreement between consecutive action chunks as a useful signal of keyframes, with several types of plan revision measures contributing useful information
across tasks. These signals remain informative across action-generation architectures and
context regimes and remain visible on failed trajectories (Appendix   A.3  ). Correlation analysis further reveals substantial redundancy within
metric families (Appendix   A.4  ), indicating
that closely related measures often provide similar information about plan-revision structure. These findings motivate selecting a compact set of complementary
disagreement features for the subsequent VLA experiments.

4.3  RQ3: Surprise-gated visual-memory updates
We finally investigate whether plan disagreement can guide visual-memory updates in tasks that require information from earlier observations.
Specifically, taking the  RememberColor  task as an example, we examine whether the selected observations are written and remain
available in memory until they are needed for action selection.
4.3.1  Direct surprise-gated writing
We first evaluate the simplest memory-update rule, in which each
detected event directly writes the immediately preceding top-view
observation to the FIFO.
We examine the resulting memory-write behavior using
π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    and OpenVLA-OFT on  RememberColor3-VLA-v0 , testing different detector thresholds for writing keyframes to memory.
At the 95th-percentile threshold, the cue observation is written
in fewer than     20  %   20\%    of the evaluated     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    episodes
and in none of the evaluated OpenVLA-OFT episodes, whereas
the runs with an 80th-percentile threshold achieve cue-writing
rates of     48  %   48\%    and     85  %   85\%   , respectively.
Disagreement peaks also often span several replanning steps,
leading to repeated writes of observations associated with the
same keyframe event (Fig.   3  ).
These observations motivate using a lower detection threshold
and selecting visually distinct frames rather than writing
an observation at every query above the threshold.
4.3.2  Redundancy-aware memory selection
We next replace direct writes with the redundancy-aware selector from
Section   3.5  . At each detected event, we examine a short window of preceding
observations and group visually similar frames together with the observations already stored in memory.
We then select the most recent frame from each group not represented in memory. This allows the selector to identify candidate keyframes associated with the detected plan revision while avoiding repeated writes of visually similar observations.
With the addition of clustering, cue-writing and retention rates for OpenVLA-OFT improve to     100  %   100\%    in the evaluated 50 episodes. However, observations of the same environment state can still be assigned to different clusters because of changes in robot pose,
leading to frequent memory overwrites. With a bounded FIFO memory, these redundant writes can evict task-relevant information observed during the early stages
of the episode. This motivates masking the robot during visual comparison to suppress redundant updates caused by manipulator motion.
4.3.3  Robot-masked redundancy filtering
We next add robot masking to the frame-selection procedure and evaluate its effect on memory updates.
The masks shown in Figure   5   are applied only during visual comparison, while the original RGB observations are stored in memory and supplied to the VLA.
For OpenVLA-OFT, across 50 evaluated episodes, robot masking
nearly halves the number of memory writes from 336 to 169
and reduces FIFO evictions by almost a factor of ten,
from 186 to 19.
At the same time, the task-relevant keyframe is written and retained until the action phase in all     50  /  50   50/50    episodes.
We thus obtain a mechanism that uses plan disagreement to successfully detect keyframes and retain them in visual memory.

pipeline through offline replay

5  Limitations and Future Work
Our experiments show that disagreement between overlapping action
chunks can accurately identify task-relevant keyframes. However, we do
not claim that such disagreement is specific to semantic events.
Substantial plan revisions may also arise for other reasons, including
policy prediction errors. The proposed signal should therefore be
interpreted as an indicator of potentially important change rather than
as an unambiguous semantic event detector.
Another limitation concerns the temporal structure of the evaluated
tasks. The task phases in our experiments have relatively clear
boundaries. With an execution stride of     H  =  4   H=4   , short
phases may occur entirely between two replanning queries and therefore
be missed, whereas gradual transitions may distribute the corresponding
plan revision over several queries and produce no pronounced peak.
How disagreement behaves under less distinct phase transitions remains
to be investigated.
Our current implementation stores selected observations as full images
in a small visual memory and uses visual clustering to suppress
redundant entries. A promising direction is therefore to develop more compact
representations of stored observations, together with comparison and
aggregation mechanisms that preserve task-relevant information while
making historical context easier for the policy to use.
Finally, the current surprise estimate is defined relative to
task-conditioned statistics accumulated from previous replanning
queries, and the write threshold is selected empirically. Consequently,
the present formulation requires calibration data for each task and has
not yet been shown to transfer directly to previously unseen tasks
without accumulating new reference statistics and selecting an
appropriate threshold.
6  Conclusion
We investigated whether revisions of overlapping action chunks can
serve as an internal signal of observation importance in long-horizon
robot control. Across single-task autoregressive and Flow-Matching
policies, we find that disagreement between consecutive plans contains
a consistent signal of semantic task transitions, with complementary
distributional, cumulative, and causal temporal measures capturing
different aspects of plan revision. We further show that this signal
transfers to pretrained OpenVLA-OFT and     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    policies across
different replanning frequencies and remains predictive even when
control performance varies substantially.
Building on these observations, we use disagreement-derived surprise to
drive selective visual-memory updates in a memory-augmented VLA policy.
The resulting framework connects three stages that are usually treated
separately: replanning, event detection, and memory formation. Rather
than introducing an external keyframe selector, the policy reuses a signal that naturally arises from its own action predictions to decide
when the observation history should be updated.
Overall, our results suggest that changes in a policy’s intended future behavior provide a useful source of temporal proposals for sparse memory updates in partially observable, multi-stage manipulation.

We examine whether disagreement remains informative when the policy fails to complete the task.
Frame ROC-AUC is computed separately for successful and

by the median across tasks.
For autoregressive policies, median Causal Z-score

with Flow-Matching EMA deviation performing better
on successful trajectories.
Overall, task failure does not lead to a systematic
decrease in disagreement-based keyframe discrimination.
A.4  Correlation structure and metric redundancy
We assess whether different disagreement measures provide
similar information by computing pairwise Spearman correlations
between their within-episode score sequences.
Correlations are summarized within each task and aggregated
with equal weight across the eight tasks.
Figure   7   shows the resulting matrices
for both architectures and context regimes.
Figure 7 :
Correlation structure of the disagreement measures.
Pairwise within-episode Spearman correlations are aggregated
across eight tasks with equal task weights.
The top row shows autoregressive policies and the bottom row
shows Flow-Matching policies.
Columns correspond to full-context and current-only conditioning.
The matrices reveal substantial redundancy among magnitude-based
measures, including several point-wise, cumulative, and
distributional metrics.
Causal Z-score and EMA deviation are less strongly correlated
with many of these measures, suggesting that their temporal
normalization captures a different aspect of plan revision.
The overall correlation structure remains similar across
context regimes.
Spearman correlations between the corresponding off-diagonal
entries of the full-context and current-only matrices are
0.9647  0.9647    for autoregressive policies and    0.9742  0.9742    for
Flow-Matching policies.
These analyses motivate using a compact feature set
rather than retaining many closely correlated variants.

ρ  n   =     |    (   vec  ⁡   U  ~    )   n   |   +   ϵ  t      ∑  m    (    |    (   vec  ⁡   U  ~    )   m   |   +   ϵ  t    )      ,    η  n   =     |    (   vec  ⁡   V  ~    )   n   |   +   ϵ  t      ∑  m    (    |    (   vec  ⁡   V  ~    )   m   |   +   ϵ  t    )       .   \rho_{n}=\frac{|(\operatorname{vec}\widetilde{U})_{n}|+\epsilon_{t}}{\sum_{m}\bigl(|(\operatorname{vec}\widetilde{U})_{m}|+\epsilon_{t}\bigr)},\qquad\eta_{n}=\frac{|(\operatorname{vec}\widetilde{V})_{n}|+\epsilon_{t}}{\sum_{m}\bigl(|(\operatorname{vec}\widetilde{V})_{m}|+\epsilon_{t}\bigr)}.

Action-component measures.
The vectors      u  i   ,   v  i    u_{i},v_{i}    are the translation components of
U  i   ,   V  i    U_{i},V_{i}   . Their cosine distance is
c  ⁡   (  u  ,  v  )    =   1  −     u  ⊤   ​  v     (     ‖  u  ‖   2   +  ϵ   )   ​   (     ‖  v  ‖   2   +  ϵ   )       .   c(u,v)=1-\frac{u^{\top}v}{(\|u\|_{2}+\epsilon)(\|v\|_{2}+\epsilon)}.
Pairs with both vectors below the translation dead-zone
threshold have distance zero.
Pairs with only one vector below the threshold are excluded,
and    ℐ  \mathcal{I}    denotes the remaining positions.
The quantities      t  i  U   ,   t  i  V    t_{i}^{U},t_{i}^{V}    and
ℛ  i  U   ,   ℛ  i  V    \mathcal{R}_{i}^{U},\mathcal{R}_{i}^{V}    are
controller-preprocessed translation increments and rotation
matrices. The quantities      g  i  U   ,   g  i  V    g_{i}^{U},g_{i}^{V}    are gripper commands.
Their calibration scales are      σ  p   ,   σ  R   ,   s  g    \sigma_{p},\sigma_{R},s_{g}   .
