Abstract
Planning while moving faces an historical trade-off between deliberation and reactivity.
A planner running concurrently with execution needs time to compute, so the robot must commit to an open-loop action chunk for that duration: a longer commitment buys a better plan at the cost of feedback frequency. Existing controllers fix this action commitment, and consequently the computation budget, before deployment. However, the optimal computation time is highly state-dependent (due to factors such as unmodeled dynamics, safety constraints, and marginal optimisation gains), i.e. dynamic, therefore offline static tuning risks leaving systems blind during critical maneuvers or unable to find global effective solutions.
We cast the identification of the optimal computation budget as a semi-Markov decision process (SMDP) in which a single decision defines both the robot action commitments and the time allocated to the solver. To solve this, we train a reinforcement-learning policy conditioned on the current state, the executing plan, and inexpensive planner statistics. Across ground-robot navigation, agile quadrotor flight, and contact-rich manipulation, the learned policy changes its budget with the demands of the scenario, discovering efficient reasoning patterns that outperform fixed-budget baselines.

I   Introduction
Deciding  how much  to think is as hard as the thinking itself (e.g., deciding
what to do)  [ 1 ] . Biological agents dynamically arbitrate their cognitive resources, continuously balancing rapid, reactive responses against slower, deliberate reasoning based on environmental uncertainty and temporal constraints  [ 1 ,  2 ] . This adaptive allocation represents a fundamental speed-optimality trade-off where extended deliberation is penalized by elapsed time  [ 3 ,  4 ] .
The  value of computation  (VOC) formally characterizes this trade-off between the marginal gain in decision optimality and the cost of deliberation  [ 5 ,  6 ] . Traditional metareasoning frameworks assume a frozen world state during planning, requiring an explicitly defined cost function to penalize extended computation, a paradigm we refer to as the  economic  value of computation. Embodied autonomous agents, conversely, operate in continuous time where this assumption fails. Because the robot and its environment evolve concurrently with the planner’s execution, heuristic timing penalties are unnecessary. Instead, the physical dynamics inherently penalize computational delay through unmitigated state drift, thereby hindering high-frequency reactivity and reducing safety margins. We introduce this dynamic trade-off as the  physical  value of computation, formalized in Sec.   III  .

Robots must adapt their computation budget based on the situation that they are facing: (   s  s   ) deliberate longer to search for an optimal path, (    s  ′   s^{\prime}   ) react rapidly to noisy moving obstacles, or (    s  ′′   s^{\prime\prime}   ) balance planning and reactivity. Across real-world and simulated experiments, we show that adapting the computation budget across states within an episode is a fundamental capability for embodied intelligence.
As standard for computational efficiency, robots typically plan in receding-horizon: the system optimizes a trajectory over a finite horizon, executes the initial segment, and subsequently replans from the evolved state. Model predictive control (MPC)  [ 7 ,  8 ]  commits to a single control step, resolving the problem at every control period, providing high-rate feedback around a
nominal trajectory.
Alternatively, action-chunking architectures generate a sequence of actions
to execute in open-loop while the next plan is produced. This commitment can improve motion smoothness and sample efficiency  [ 9 ,  10 ,  11 ] , yet fundamentally limits the system’s ability to react to high-frequency disturbances or environmental changes. In both paradigms, the solver’s computational budget and execution horizon are statically defined prior to deployment. Consequently, the robot  lacks a principled mechanism to dynamically arbitrate when a state warrants extended deliberation for trajectory optimality versus when it necessitates rapid, reactive feedback (Fig.  1  ) .
Identifying an optimal static computation budget cannot be resolved via offline tuning. It is fundamentally a state-dependent quantity, dictated by the unmodelled  dynamics , the proximity to state-space  constraints , and the marginal  optimality  gained through additional solver iterations. Moreover, the budget choice cannot be assessed from the current state alone: its consequences propagate to future states throughout the episode, where they may influence later decisions and outcomes.
We address these challenges  by formulating adaptive computation budgets for real-time control
as a semi-MDP over decision epochs. At each epoch, one decision sets both the
open-loop execution horizon and the parallel computational resources allocated to solver. We then learn this decision from experience with an RL policy
(Fig.   2  ).

Related Work
We review the work most closely related to our setting.
Real-time and adaptive action chunking
Because a chunking policy emits several actions at once, a mechanism is required to cover the gap
while the next chunk is generated. RTC  [ 12 ]  keeps execution smooth
across that gap by conditioning the new chunk on the actions still running, and
DEFLECT  [ 13 ]  instead trains the policy to tolerate acting on
observations that have gone stale; both leave the chunk length fixed.
[ 14 ]  makes that length adaptive, while  [ 15 ]  pre-computes several candidate
chunks and follows whichever best matches the realised state. Unlike our approach, none of these methods couples the execution length with the solver’s computation budget, so a larger commitment cannot be leveraged to compute a better plan.
Adaptive receding-horizon MPC
Event- and self-triggered MPC frameworks dynamically determine when to replan rather than solving at every control step, preserving feasibility and stability of the system  [ 16 ] . Alternative approaches dictate the replanning interval based strictly on safety, defining it as the maximum duration the current trajectory can be executed while a collision-free fallback remains available  [ 17 ] . However, these triggering mechanisms are designed fundamentally to  conserve  computation subject to a baseline guarantee, treating the underlying optimizer as an oracle whose solution quality does not improve with additional allocated time.
Metareasoning and test-time computation
Metareasoning prices computation by the improvement in the decision quality it produces, net of the cost of running it  [ 5 ,  6 ] . This principle underlines anytime
algorithms, deliberation scheduling  [ 18 ,  19 ] , and learned computation budget  [ 20 ] . In these conventional formulations, the world is treated as frozen while the agent deliberates, so the cost of thinking is an explicit penalty chosen by the designer to trade against the expected benefit.
Learning planning budgets
Closest to our work,  [ 21 ]  learns how long to deliberate
in real time and casts the decision as a semi-MDP over budgeted options.
Their setting, however, is restricted to discrete game examples and therefore does not expose uncontrolled variation that arises when acting in the
real world. Furthermore, their method requires a separate reflex policy that acts in the open-loop phase, an assumption that our framework does not need.

By committing to this extended sequence, the robot executes the chunk open-loop, explicitly buying the required time window for the solver to concurrently compute the subsequent plan. While classic MPC formulations restrict this mapping to a single step (    h  =  1   h=1   ), our formulation extends    h  h    to leverage the solver’s monotonic improvement at the expense of delayed reactivity. We evaluate this framework using Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) and the Cross Entropy Method (CEM), two standard sampling-based receding horizon solvers, though our method accommodates any monotonic-improving optimizer.
Two  fundamental difficulties  arise when solving   1   online with a receding horizon time-monotone solver     ψ  h   \psi_{h}    whose computation time is non-negligible and proportional to the action chunk length    h  h   :
(C1)  Open-loop execution creates vulnerability to unmodelled dynamics and disturbances.
When the solver commits to an action chunk of length    h  h   , the system effectively operates without feedback for    h  h    timesteps. During this interval, stochasticity, external disturbances, or discrepancies in the transition model    𝒫  \mathcal{P}    cause the true system state to diverge from the solver’s predicted trajectory, leading to compounding errors or potential constraint violations.
(C2)  Finding the strict trade-off between plan optimality and system reactivity is a non-trivial problem.
Because the solver’s computational budget scales with the chunk length    h  h   , the problem formulation inherently couples how deeply the solver reasons with how frequently the system observes. A small    h  h    enables rapid feedback to counter the disturbances described in (C1), but heavily restricts the computational budget, resulting in myopic and suboptimal planning. Conversely, a large    h  h    allocates sufficient budget for deep, long-horizon deliberation but forces the system to remain open-loop for extended periods. A static    h  h    therefore forces a rigid compromise between optimality and robustness, failing to exploit the fact that  different states in the MDP require different levels of deliberation versus reaction .
We take up these challenges by adopting real-time chunking, which makes (C1) explicit rather than assuming it away, and by adapting the commitment length    h  h    (and consequentially budget of the solver) online through an RL policy learnt from experience, which turns the design constant of (C2) into a decision.

Our experimental evaluation is designed to validate the efficacy of adaptive computation budgets across diverse robotic domains. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions:
•
Q1   (Performance) : Does dynamic adaptation of the computational budget and execution horizon consistently outperform fixed-budget baselines in dynamic and constrained environments?
•
Q2   (Adaptation Mechanism) : Can the learned policy correctly identify state-dependent requirements for deliberation versus reactivity, and how critical are internal planner statistics to this arbitration?
•
Q3   (Boundary Behavior) : How does the adaptive policy behave in non-smooth cost landscapes where the optimal commitment horizon lacks strong state-dependent variation?
To quantitatively evaluate these questions, we report two primary metrics:
(1) task success rate , the fraction of evaluation episodes that satisfy
the task-specific goal criterion in goal-conditioned scenarios; and
(2) mean total return , the undiscounted sum of environment rewards
over an episode, averaged over all evaluation episodes, including failures.
Higher values are better for both metrics.
For navigation tasks, we additionally measure  harshness , a
dimensionless measure combining path tortuosity and motion jerk,
H  =    (   L  /  D   )   ​   [   1  +   log  ⁡   (   1  +   J  norm    )     ]     H=(L/D)[1+\log(1+J_{\mathrm{norm}})]   .
Here,    L  L    is the travelled path length, and    D  D    is the straight-line distance
between the robot’s initial and final positions. The normalized jerk
J  norm   J_{\mathrm{norm}}    measures how abruptly acceleration changes, using the
accumulated squared jerk normalized by episode duration and path length.
Lower harshness indicates a
more direct and smoother trajectory.

We deploy our framework across three distinct scenarios, systematically chosen to stress the computation-reactivity trade-off from different physical dimensions: holonomic ground navigation, deployed in the real-world; quadrotor with suspended cable payload; and contact-rich planar manipulation (3D Push-T). The properties of each system are described in the related paragraphs.
Implementation Details.
The hardware and software infrastructure is standardized across experiments to ensure consistent solver performance. The underlying control and communication stack is orchestrated via ROS 2. Hardware experiments are conducted on a DJI RoboMaster platform  [ 24 ]  for the holonomic ground navigation. Simulated environments utilize MuJoCo  [ 25 ]  for the quadrotor dynamics and the Push-T library using a  [ 26 ]  for planar manipulation. To achieve real-time, high-frequency planning, the predictive model and the Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) solver are implemented entirely in JAX and executed via MuJoCo MJX  [ 25 ] . All neural network inference and hardware-accelerated planning are executed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (40GB VRAM), ensuring the solver’s wall-clock latency strictly dictates the physical temporal cost of computation.
The control period is      d  ​  t   =   0.1  ​  s    dt=0.1\,\mathrm{s}    for navigation,      d  ​  t   =   0.02  ​  s    dt=0.02\,\mathrm{s}    for cable-payload flight, and      d  ​  t   =   0.04  ​  s    dt=0.04\,\mathrm{s}    for PushT; a commitment of    h  h    therefore corresponds to     h  ​  d  ​  t   h\,dt    seconds of open-loop execution.
The budget policy is a two-layer tanh MLP (128 units per layer), with a categorical actor and scalar critic. PPO uses     λ  =  0.95   \lambda=0.95   , learning rate     3  ×   10   −  4     3\times 10^{-4}   , minibatches of 256, four epochs, clip    0.2  0.2   , and gradient clipping at    0.5  0.5   . We train for 2,500 updates with 2,048 transitions per update.
In the experiments, MPPI scales its prediction horizon linearly with    h  h    and scales its samples linearly for navigation and as     h   \sqrt{h}    for cable flight; CEM on PushT scales both horizon and population linearly.

VII   Conclusion
Robot  metareasoning  is a fundamental capability of embodied
intelligence: an agent must decide how much to deliberate under
environmental uncertainty and temporal constraints. We introduce a semi-MDP framework for robot metareasoning that learns adaptive computation budgets for real-time control. By leveraging planner statistics, the policy dynamically balances plan execution length and planner computation time based on  plan quality  and  uncertainty .
Across three environments with distinct sources of difficulty, the policy adapts its budget to the demands of the task.
Our results show that online adaptation is  highly beneficial  when exploitable budget variation exists.
It discovers longer commitments for global route, smooth transit and shorter ones near dynamic obstacles, critical passage and, in cable-payload flight, near targets and disturbances.
In contact-rich planar manipulation, the learned
policy converges to a constant budget matching the best fixed baseline, indicating that adaptation is unnecessary when the nominal task provides little exploitable variation.
Future work  will explore richer, high-dimensional planner statistics, learned world models, and more expressive planning algorithms.
