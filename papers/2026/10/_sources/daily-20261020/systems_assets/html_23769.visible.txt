Abstract
Progress estimators play an essential role in providing dense reward signals for reinforcement learning in robotic manipulation, but accurately assessing task progress can be much challenging.
Existing methods heavily rely on expert data and naively assume that all actions within a demonstration contribute to task success.
This assumption often breaks down in real-world scenarios, especially in high-precision manipulation, where subtle geometric errors can lead to task failure.
Therefore, these methods can erroneously interpret human mistakes as meaningful progress, further corrupting progress learning.
We argue that a key limitation is the lack of a global prior regarding task evaluation criteria, which is crucial for distinguishing meaningful progress from human mistakes.
To this end, we propose PRIME, a novel progress estimator capable of learning precise progress estimation from imprecise demonstrations by aggregating experience across trajectories.
PRIME constructs a weighted graph over observed states, connecting states across trajectories based on spatial proximity and visual consistency. Edge weights encode spatial distances as transition costs, and shortest-path search approximates the geodesic distance to success. Changes in this distance naturally distinguish advancement, stagnation, and setbacks without assuming monotonic progress over time.
Extensive experiments in both simulation and real world on precise manipulation tasks show that PRIME is consistently effective across diverse tasks, and robust to data with varying degrees of suboptimality.
Notably, the advantage of PRIME gets more pronounced in higher-precision scenarios, yielding up to a     5  ×   5\times    improvement in policy success rate over the SFT baselines on charger insertion.

I   Introduction
Real-world Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an indispensable paradigm for advancing robotic manipulation from simple, repetitive actions to highly complex, precise skills  [ 1 ,  2 ,  3 ,  4 ] .
However, mastering these high-precision tasks demands feedback that goes beyond binary success signals to identify and rectify the inevitable imperfections within human-collected demonstrations and policy rollouts  [ 1 ,  2 ,  5 ] .
To bridge this gap, progress estimators have garnered extensive attention in the research community  [ 6 ,  7 ,  8 ,  9 ,  10 ] .
Despite recent progress, faithfully capturing subtle advancement, stagnation, and setbacks remains challenging for progress estimators, particularly in high-precision manipulation  [ 11 ,  12 ] .

The inefficiency of existing methods in high-precision tasks stems from their inability to distinguish genuine task progress from suboptimal behaviors such as drifting, overshooting, and idling (Fig.   1  , Bottom). These methods rely on a simple yet flawed assumption that all actions within a trajectory contribute equally to eventual task success  [ 8 ,  9 ] . Consequently, they depend heavily on expert data and discard a substantial proportion of collected demonstrations that fail to meet the required precision threshold.
However, discarding low-quality demonstrations is costly and overlooks that, in high-precision settings, even high-quality demonstrations may contain steps that do not advance the task. Although trajectory-level filtering improves the policy success rate by 16.7 percentage points in our SFT experiments (Fig.   1  , top), suboptimal behaviors within the retained demonstrations can still confound progress estimates. This motivates finer-grained, step-level progress estimation.
Our key observation is that accurate progress estimation can be achieved only if the underlying progress structure captures holistic task priors. Such priors cannot be obtained from a single trajectory.
For example, when learning to operate an entirely new machine, the human operators cannot determine whether the current operation is optimal and can only attempt to faithfully follow the demonstration.
However, after observing multiple demonstrations, the operator can distinguish between suboptimal and optimal actions and thus estimate the progress of the current operation.
Thus, we incorporate this holistic information into our progress labels by leveraging the collective experience across multiple demonstrations, rather than relying solely on the temporal position within an individual trajectory.
Building on this observation, we propose PRIME ( PR ecise progress estimation from  IM precise d E monstrations), a progress estimation method extracting collective experience from the collected suboptimal demonstrations to assess step-level quality. We adopt a graph search framework to approximate geodesic distances over the feasible manifold.
Each node represents an observed state comprising the robot configuration and multi-view visual observations.
Edges are established based on configuration proximity and visual consistency, while edge weights quantify geometric changes in robot configuration, which allows for transitions to alternative trajectories with only a fine-grained distance penalty. Shortest-path search on the resulting graph estimates the geodesic distance to success. Changes in this distance naturally distinguish advancement, stagnation, and setbacks without assuming monotonic progress over time.
We validate the effectiveness of PRIME through extensive experiments in both real-world and simulation scenarios, which demonstrate that a policy trained with PRIME significantly improves the success rate across diverse tasks and is robust to data with varying degrees of suboptimality.
In real world experiments, PRIME achieves consistently superior success rates especially on higher-precision tasks, outperforming the standard SFT baseline by up to     2.2  ×  −  5  ×   2.2\times-5\times    across 3 high-precision tasks: peg-in-hole (73.3% v.s. 33.3%), peg-in-hole Hard (70.0% v.s. 20.0%), and charger insertion (50.0% v.s. 10.0%).
We further construct a diagnostic benchmark covering stage understanding, local precision, and progress continuity. PRIME achieves 92.5% accuracy in local-precision discrimination, substantially outperforming the best baseline at 70.0%.
In the simulation environment, we evaluate PRIME (see Sec.   IV-A  ) via online reinforcement learning. Specifically,
our method achieves relative improvements of 10.9% in InD success rates (0.674 v.s. 0.608) and 13.5% in OOD success rates (0.353 v.s. 0.311), demonstrating its effectiveness in guiding policy learning.

II-A    Progress estimation in robotics
Obtaining an effective reward function is fundamental in robotic reinforcement learning to guide policy optimization and efficient exploration. This is especially crucial for high-precision tasks, where sparse feedback often leads to optimization collapse due to long credit-assignment horizons  [ 1 ] .
To derive dense rewards from demonstrations, existing methods commonly construct progress estimation from the temporal structure of trajectories.
Early approaches adopt linear temporal progress, such as normalized remaining-step estimation or linear interpolation over execution time  [ 13 ,  9 ,  8 ,  6 ,  14 ] .
More recent approaches introduce structured, non-uniform progress estimation  [ 15 ,  16 ] . SARM  [ 7 ]  introduces subtask-level progress allocation with linear interpolation within each subtask, while ProcVLM  [ 17 ]  further refines it by redistributing intra-subtask progress according to visual changes.
ARM  [ 16 ]  learns from human-annotated progress directions, which incurs substantial time and labor costs.
Although these designs relax the uniform-rate assumption of linear interpolation, their progress targets are still largely derived from temporal ordering in demonstrations, enforcing progress to increase monotonically along the trajectory. Such temporal monotonicity does not necessarily reflect effective progress in high-precision manipulation. Demonstrations may contain hesitation, overshooting, or redundant adjustments where later observations can be temporally closer to task completion yet physically farther from a successful state.
II-B    Learning precise manipulation
Reinforcement learning in high-precision tasks has enabled remarkable breakthroughs, such as screw installation  [ 1 ] , shoelace tying  [ 2 ] , in-hand manipulation  [ 18 ,  19 ] , band tying  [ 20 ] , belt assembly  [ 21 ] , and deformable object handling  [ 22 ] .
While these works improve precise manipulation, reliable dense progress supervision from offline demonstrations remains unaddressed.
Beyond these methods, researchers are also exploring efficient strategies for learning high-precision tasks.
Compared to these works that achieve high-precision manipulation with reweighted behavior cloning  [ 20 ,  2 ]  or pure reinforcement learning  [ 2 ,  18 ,  19 ,  21 ,  1 ] , PRIME distinguishes itself by focusing on accurate progress estimation learning, which further drives high-efficiency reinforcement learning.

III-A    Problem Analysis

Designing     l  task   l_{\text{task}}    can be more subtle. While most existing works adopt a simple Euclidean distance in the representation space, e.g.       l  task   ​   (   r  ⁡   (   s  t   )    ,   r  ⁡   (   s   t  ′    )    )    =    ‖    r  ⁡   (   s  t   )    −   r  ⁡   (   s   t  ′    )     ‖   2    l_{\text{task}}(r(s_{t}),r(s_{t^{\prime}}))=\|r(s_{t})-r(s_{t^{\prime}})\|_{2}    and      r  ⁡   (   s  t   )    =  t   r(s_{t})=t     [ 13 ,  9 ,  8 ]  or      r  ⁡   (   s  t   )    =   E  ​  n  ​  c  ​   (   o  t   )     r(s_{t})=Enc(o_{t})     [ 17 ] , we find the approach unsuitable for high-precision tasks as the latent paths indicated by Euclidean distances in the representation space of    r  r    are often infeasible in the physical world.
In such cases, geodesic distances along the feasible execution manifold are comparatively more appropriate. However, the feasible execution manifold is often unknown and difficult to model.
In this work, we propose to approximate the geodesic distance by Euclidean distance in neighborhood of representations.
Moreover, without a global prior regarding feasibility manifold, we propose to leverage the physical transitions collected in the dataset, and in this case, the minimum graph distance between states can be seen as a good approximation of the geodesic distance along the feasible execution manifold.
III-B    Progress Estimator Construction
III-B 1  Nearest Neighbor State Retrieval
To exploit collective experience across demonstrations, we construct graph connections from both observed transitions and state-compatible cross-trajectory transitions.
Specifically, within each demonstration, we preserve the temporal structure of the trajectory by connecting consecutive states;
across demonstrations, we connect states only when they are close in robot configuration and consistent in visual task state, i.e., the following conditions must be satisfied:

Benefiting from the distinguishing power and strong pose-aware priors of DINOv3, physically irreversible transitions tend to result in higher latent distances, guaranteeing the physical transferability between cross-trajectory edges.
We evaluate the similarity-based cross-trajectory edge forming mechanism in a real-world experiment. Specifically, we collect 20 new trajectories, compute the cross-trajectory edges between each new trajectory and the existing dataset (on average, more than 200 new edges per trajectory). For each trajectory, we replay the robotic arm to execute one-step transition between the new neighbor nodes formed by such cross-trajectory edges with environment unchanged. We find that over 99.4% of these cross-trajectory edges are physically feasible, meaning that the connected states are mutually reachable through one-step transitions. False positives are observed only when the grasped object rotates by less than 3°.

In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate the accuracy of PRIME’s progress estimates and their effectiveness for offline policy learning and online reinforcement learning. We further examine the contributions of the distance metric and the sensitivity to graph and sampling parameters. Our aim is to address the following research questions:
Q1
Can PRIME faithfully capture progress of high-precision tasks from human-collected trajectories interleaved with sub-optimal behaviors?
Q2
Can PRIME-based policy learn from sub-optimal trajectories in high-precision tasks?
Q3
Can PRIME provide more accurate supervision for progress estimation, thereby yielding more precise reward signals for RL training?
Q4
How do design choices and hyper-parameters affect PRIME?
IV-A    Experiment Setup
Real Robot
We evaluate PRIME on four real-world manipulation tasks using a Piper-X robot arm: drawer opening followed by object placement, peg-in-hole, peg-in-hole (Hard), and charger insertion (Fig.   7  ). We collect 20, 30, 50, and 50 demonstration episodes for these tasks, respectively. These demonstrations are used to construct the progress estimator and subsequently train the policy. Each trained policy is evaluated over 30 randomized trials per task.
Simulation
Our simulation environment, termed ExpandedManiSkill, builds upon the standard configuration of RL4VLA  [ 28 ] . Specifically, to verify the scalability of PRIME under substantial spatial variations and across a wide array of object geometries, we extend the initial positional workspace from       15  ​  cm   ×  15   ​  cm   15\text{cm}\times 15\text{cm}    to       15  ​  cm   ×  40   ​  cm   15\text{cm}\times 40\text{cm}    with more evaluation objects.
Baselines
We compare our approach against pure SFT and several advanced progress estimation baselines. In the real-world setting, we employ     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}     [ 29 ]  as our base model. We further evaluate PRIME against RECAP  [ 13 ] , SARM  [ 7 ] , RoboMeter  [ 8 ] , ViVa  [ 9 ] , GSR  [ 20 ]  and ProcVLM  [ 17 ] . In the simulated RL setting, we conduct comparative analyses against 0/1 sparse rewards and time-progress rewards. For fair comparison, the progress estimators mentioned above are trained using Qwen3-VL  [ 30 ]  in real-world scenarios, and their estimated progress is subsequently used in the same progress-prioritized policy training pipeline. In simulation, Qwen2.5-VL  [ 31 ]  is utilized as the progress-model backbone. During training, the model takes the robot configuration and observations as input.
IV-B    Can PRIME capture sub-optimal behaviors in human-collected dataset?
We evaluate PRIME on Peg-in-hole (Hard) through qualitative progress visualization and a quantitative diagnostic benchmark. As illustrated in Fig.   4  , the predicted curves show that PRIME captures setbacks during drifting and maintains stable estimates during idling.
To further quantify estimation quality, a professional data collector collected an additional 60 trajectories exhibiting clearly suboptimal behavior and 40 expert trajectories. From these demonstrations, the collector selected 120 stage-comparison pairs with clear differences in task stage, along with 30 expert trajectories for assessing temporal smoothness, quantified by the normalized root mean square (RMS) of the second-order differences in predicted progress. The collector also carefully selected 120 local-precision comparison pairs exhibiting subtle yet task-critical geometric differences during insertion (e.g., observations taken just above the opening and those taken after suboptimal actions have caused horizontal drift).
We aim to evaluate whether a progress estimator can correctly capture task-stage transitions, reflect suboptimal behaviors through changes in estimated progress, and provide smooth progress signals for high-quality trajectories. These properties are important for producing informative and stable reward signals for downstream RL training.
As stated in Table   II  , PRIME achieves 92.5% accuracy in local-precision discrimination, exceeding the best baseline by 22.5 percentage points while maintaining smooth progress predictions.

IV-C    Can PRIME-based policy learn from sub-optimal trajectories in high-precision tasks?
We consider an offline setting with two types of datasets: noisy data containing suboptimal trajectories (e.g., multiple insertion attempts), and expert data comprising high-quality demonstrations with inevitable human errors for four tasks: drawer opening followed by object placement, peg-in-hole, peg-in-hole (Hard), and charger insertion. The results in Table   I   illustrate that PRIME consistently and remarkably outperforms the advanced progress estimation methods.

IV-E    Effects of Design Choices and Hyper-parameters
In this subsection, we conduct ablation to investigate the critical design elements in PRIME on Peg-Hard and Drawer.
Ablation on Graph Construction and Progress Distance
We ablate the key components of PRIME to examine the roles of graph connectivity and distance computation.
As illustrated in Table   IV  , PRIME substantially outperforms all three variants.
PRIME w/o geometric edge costs retains the same graph connectivity but assigns unit edge costs, ignoring geometric transition magnitudes, thereby measuring only the minimum number of steps to the goal. PRIME w/o configuration-proximity constraint removes the configuration-proximity constraint when establishing cross-trajectory edges, relying solely on visual similarity while keeping the remaining graph formulation unchanged. This may connect visually similar states despite substantial differences in robot configuration, potentially introducing inappropriate cross-trajectory shortcuts. The Euclidean distance to goal discards the graph structure, potentially conflating equally distant states even at different task stages.
These results demonstrate the complementary importance of configuration-aware connectivity, fine-grained transition costs, and graph-constrained path structure.

V   Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce an improved paradigm for task progress estimation and policy learning in robotic manipulation, particularly for high-precision tasks. By combining human-collected trajectories into a graph-based framework, PRIME provides an estimate of the geodesic distance and treats it as a metric for progress estimation and as a reward for reinforcement learning. Based on such reward-labeling paradigm, our extensive evaluations on ExpandedManiSkill and in real-world deployments, comprising peg-in-hole, high-precision peg-in-hole, and charger insertion, demonstrate significant improvements in success rates (up to 5   ×  \times    for charger insertion and 3.5   ×  \times    for high-precision peg-in-hole). Furthermore, PRIME introduces modest computational overhead for graph construction and improves performance under the evaluated out-of-distribution conditions.
