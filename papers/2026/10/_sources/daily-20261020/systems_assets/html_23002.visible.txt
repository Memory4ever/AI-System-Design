Robotic Spatiotemporal Editing: A Unified Interface for Controllable Action Generation via Masked Action Reconstruction
Yanbang Li ,  Bu He ,  Yi Tian ,  Yu-Chiao Huang ,  Chenyu Zhang ,  Ruoqu Chen ,  Yuanzhe Liu ,  Yuxiang Gao ,  Mengdi Xu
Tsinghua University   CocoMatrix   Shanghai Qizhi Institute   UIUC
* Equal contribution Corresponding author
https://robotic-spatiotemporal-editing.github.io/rose.github.io/
Abstract
Generative policies can synthesize robot behaviors, yet controlling and editing them through diverse spatiotemporal references remains challenging.
Real-world manipulation requires robots to satisfy user-specified constraints on where, when, and how actions are executed.
Our key insight is that spatiotemporal control references, such as keypoints, flows, orientations, and keyposes, can be viewed as partial specifications of the same temporal Cartesian action space.
We introduce the  MAP (Masked Action Space)  interface to unify these action references and formulate controllable manipulation as an action inpainting problem.
Our  Inpainting Policy (IP)  reconstructs missing action dimensions of the end-effector, combining progress-guided alignment with multi-mask training to support unseen action constraint combinations.
We further adopt a training-free  RePaint  variant that iteratively inpaints the action during denoising to reduce control error.
On ManiSkill and LIBERO-10, our policy achieves average task success rates of     92.67  %   92.67\%    and     64.67  %   64.67\%   , respectively.
Real-world experiments demonstrate spatiotemporal control with average positional and rotational errors of     44.3  ​  mm   44.3\,\mathrm{mm}    and     7.8  ∘   7.8^{\circ}   , respectively.
Project page:  https://robotic-spatiotemporal-editing.github.io/rose.github.io/ .
1  INTRODUCTION
Figure 1:   Unified spatiotemporal control via action inpainting.
Top:  The  Masked Action Space (MAP)  interface unifies heterogeneous references from upstream models and human inputs as partial specifications of temporal Cartesian actions.
We use progress values instead of raw  timesteps .
Bottom:  Our  Inpainting Policy (IP)  completes these partial specifications through action inpainting, supporting potentially unlimited spatiotemporal reference types and unseen combinations.
Generative models have recently demonstrated remarkable progress in robotic manipulation, enabling policies to synthesize complex robot behaviors from visual observations and task descriptions  ( Chi et al., 2025 ;  Black et al., 2026 ;  Brohan et al., 2023 ) .
However, generating plausible behaviors alone is insufficient for deploying robots in the physical world.
Real-world tasks require robots not only to decide what to do but also to satisfy constraints on  where, when, and how  to execute an action   ( Rempe et al., 2026 ) .
Moreover, humans and intelligent systems often need to refine, modify, and compose robot behaviors by adding spatiotemporal constraints.
For example, when tidying a table, a user may use a hand gesture or a sketch instruction  ( Li et al., 2025b )  to specify where an object should be placed, rather than merely asking the robot to tidy the table.
While generative models can create robot behaviors, the next challenge is enabling humans and intelligent systems to control and edit them.
Meanwhile, robotics is gaining access to diverse spatiotemporal references from world models, 3D/4D videos, advanced agents, grasp models, and human demonstrations  ( Jang et al., 2025 ;  Gao et al., 2026 ;  Chen et al., 2026b ;  Murali et al., 2025 ;  Fan et al., 2025b ) , including keypoints  ( Huang et al., 2024 ) , flow  ( Xu et al., 2024 ) , keyposes  ( Yu et al., 2024 ) , and grasp proposals  ( Fang et al., 2023 ;  Hao et al., 2025 )  (see Figure   1  (A)).
These representations are physically grounded and low-dimensional, but they only provide partial information on future robot motion rather than complete executable actions.
Existing approaches typically address this gap with signal-specific policies or conditioning mechanisms.
This motivates the need for  a unified action interface  that can transform diverse spatiotemporal references into controllable robot behaviors.
Our key insight is that heterogeneous spatiotemporal control are not different control problems, but partial action tokens of the same underlying Temporal Cartesian Action Space.
We design  Masked Action Space (MAP)  as a shared interface that represents these references through partially specified action tokens and a binary mask (see Figure   1  (B,C)).
We formulate controllable action generation as  a masked action inpainting problem , and we propose  Inpainting Policy (IP)  to reconstruct masked components in MAP and turn them into a complete executable trajectory (see Section   3.3  ).
To enable this formulation, we design three components.
(1)  A  progress-guided sliding window  aligns control references with the current task stage using estimated progress value  ( Chen et al., 2026a )  rather than raw execution timesteps (Section   3.2  ).
(2)  A  two-stage joint-training strategy  uses random masks in the first stage and structured masks in the second stage to learn robust control over diverse and unseen control references (Section   3.4  ).
(3)  As a  training-free  alternative, we also adopt a  RePaint-style inpainting strategy  that can be applied during the inference-time denoising process of an off-the-shelf policy.
We conduct experiments on 15 tasks across the ManiSkill, LIBERO-10, and real-world environments, covering six standard control references and randomly structured references.
In sum, our method demonstrates several capabilities:
•
Versatile controllability.
Inpainting Policy supports diverse spatiotemporal references, including keypoints, flows, orientations, poses, and combinations of these reference types.
In real-world experiments, it attains mean position and rotation errors of     44.3  ​  mm   44.3\,\mathrm{mm}    and     7.8  ∘   7.8^{\circ}   , respectively.
Meanwhile, our method improves the success rate on LIBERO-10 by 4.67 percentage points compared with the baseline.
This proves that our policy enables arbitrary spatiotemporal controllability without compromising task completion.
•
Interpretability.
MAP (Masked Action Space) provides a human-readable and editable interface that bridges multi-dimensional spatiotemporal information from the physical world with robotic manipulation actions via the masked temporal Cartesian space.
It explicitly indicates which timesteps and action dimensions are constrained.
•
Practicality.
Our method can be directly applied to diffusion- or flow matching-based policies in simulated or real-world environments.
Built on a lightweight     49  ​  M   49\mathrm{M}   -parameter DiT model, it achieves a     92.67  %   92.67\%    success rate and a control error of    0.023  0.023    (Table   1  ) in ManiSkill experiments.
In real-world experiments, a     118  ​  M   118\mathrm{M}   -parameter diffusion model achieves a success rate above     90  %   90\%   .
2  RELATED WORK
2.1  HETEROGENEOUS SPATIOTEMPORAL SIGNALS FOR ROBOT CONTROL
Robotic manipulation increasingly uses spatially grounded intermediate representations, including keypoints, trajectories, and keyposes, to guide action generation.
Point-based methods provide sparse spatial guidance  ( Huang et al., 2024 ;  Liu et al., 2024 ) , while KALM uses task-relevant keypoints to define object-relative frames for action prediction  ( Fang et al., 2025 ) .
BiKC predicts keyposes to guide a low-level trajectory generator, and AnyGrasp provides grasp-pose proposals for downstream execution  ( Yu et al., 2024 ;  Fang et al., 2023 ) .
Gemini Robotics-ER supports spatial reasoning and trajectory prediction, while RoboBrain and A0 produce affordance and trajectory predictions to guide manipulation  ( Team et al., 2025 ;  Ji et al., 2025 ;  Xu et al., 2026 ) .
Beyond sparse references, trajectory-based interfaces provide denser motion guidance for downstream action policies  ( Xu et al., 2024 ;  Wen et al., 2024 ;  Gu et al., 2023 ;  Liu et al., 2025 ) .
Video-based models offer another route to control: V-JEPA 2-AC plans toward image goals through action-conditioned prediction in latent space, while UVA jointly learns video and action prediction  ( Assran et al., 2025 ;  Li et al., 2025a ) .
Our work focuses on  unifying heterogeneous control references  as specified entries in a shared Cartesian action representation, allowing a single policy to reconstruct the remaining action entries across different reference types and their combinations.
2.2  INPAINTING FOR ROBOT CONTROL
Visual masking and inpainting support several aspects of robot learning.
ROSIE augments robot observations through text-guided inpainting, while Mirage modifies robot appearance through cross-painting for embodiment transfer  ( Yu et al., 2023 ;  Chen et al., 2024 ) .
Cloak masks the end-effector in wrist-camera observations without generative inpainting, whereas Masked Visual Actions uses partially revealed video trajectories for forward and inverse world modeling  ( Piseno et al., 2026 ;  Alzayer et al., 2026 ) .
In motion generation, Kimodo supports pose-feature completion, Scheduled Inpainting balances motion preservation and editing, and MotionBricks performs latent in-betweening  ( Rempe et al., 2026 ;  Agrawal et al., 2026 ;  Wang et al., 2026b ) .
In action space, RTC and DiscreteRTC preserve committed action prefixes while generating subsequent actions, enabling asynchronous execution through inpainting  ( Black et al., 2025 ;  Wang et al., 2026a ) .
HDP and DISCO use inpainting to impose trajectory-endpoint and semantic-keyframe constraints, respectively  ( Ma et al., 2024 ;  Hao et al., 2025 ) .
3  METHOD
Our method first introduces a unified controllable interface, the  MAP (Masked Action Space) , for manipulation action generation, which converts heterogeneous spatiotemporal signals into specified action tokens and associated binary masks, thereby representing different control references in a common masked Cartesian action space (Section   3.1  ).
Based on this representation, we develop an  Inpainting Policy  that reconstructs the masked action tokens and generates executable action chunks (Section   3.3  ).
Our framework consists of three components.
(1)  A progress-guided sliding window aligns MAP with the current task progress and provides a temporally anchored MAP condition (Section   3.2  ).
(2)  Multi-modal joint training over heterogeneous mask families enables structured control and zero-shot generalization to unseen spatiotemporal compositions (Section   3.4  ).
(3)  We adopt RePaint  ( Lugmayr et al., 2022 )  as a training-free inference strategy, which repeatedly reinserts the specified action tokens during the denoising process of the diffusion-based policy, thereby improving adherence to the provided constraints (Section   3.5  ).

We formulate reference-guided robotic manipulation as reconstructing complete actions from partial specifications in a temporal Cartesian action space.
We use normalized task progress      p  ′   ∈   [   0  ,  1   ]    p^{\prime}\in[0,1]    as the temporal coordinate, with reference alignment detailed in Section   3.2  .

At each location, the action vector is defined as      a  p   =   (   x  p   ,   y  p   ,   z  p   ,   θ   x  ,  p    ,   θ   y  ,  p    ,   θ   z  ,  p    ,   d  p   )    a_{p}=(x_{p},y_{p},z_{p},\theta_{x,p},\theta_{y,p},\theta_{z,p},d_{p})   , where the first six dimensions describe the gripper pose and     d  p   d_{p}    denotes the gripper state.

The spatiotemporal references are represented by a binary mask     M  ∈    {   0  ,  1   }     T  ref   ×  7     M\in\{0,1\}^{T_{\mathrm{ref}}\times 7}    and the corresponding specified action tokens     M  ⊙   A  ref    M\odot A^{\mathrm{ref}}   , where      M   p  ,  d    =  1   M_{p,d}=1    indicates a supplied value and      M   p  ,  d    =  0   M_{p,d}=0    indicates an unspecified entry.
Mapping Control References to Masks.
The mapping     s  →   A   (  k  )     s\to A^{(k)}    converts control references    s  s    into MAP constraints on the action chunk (Figure   2  ).
As illustrated in Figure   1  (C), different distributions of    M  M    instantiate different control signals.

Structured mask types include 2D/3D keypoints, 2D/3D flow, keyposes, orientations, gripper states, and mixed-modality conditions formed by randomly combining these references.
Here, 2D references specify Cartesian     x  ,  y   x,y    coordinates and flow references denote position trajectories; image-space references must first be mapped into the action coordinate system.
For a reference at progress     p  ′   p^{\prime}   , let      p  ⋆   =    arg  ⁡   min  p    ​   |    p  p  ′   −   p  ′    |     p^{\star}=\arg\min_{p}|p_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}|    denote its discrete reference index.
For example, a 3D keypoint activates      M    p  ⋆   ,   {   1  ,  2  ,  3   }     =  1   M_{p^{\star},\{1,2,3\}}=1   , while an orientation reference activates      M    p  ⋆   ,   {   4  ,  5  ,  6   }     =  1   M_{p^{\star},\{4,5,6\}}=1   .
MAP also supports unstructured mask types, as shown in Figure   1  (B), such as specifying the gripper state at a particular manipulation stage, constraining the end-effector state along the    z  z   -axis, or specifying a rotation angle on a particular plane.

As illustrated in Figure   2  , IP generates complete Cartesian action chunks conditioned on visual observations, robot state, and the local MAP window selected in Section   3.2  .

3.4  MULTIMODAL JOINT TRAINING
To enable one model to handle diverse spatiotemporal conditions, we construct a multi-modal masked action dataset from demonstrations.
As illustrated in Figure   2  , we train the Inpainting Policy with three complementary mask patterns:    α  \alpha   ,    β  \beta   , and    γ  \gamma   , each targeting a distinct aspect of masked action reconstruction.
The    α  \alpha   -pattern consists of structured spatiotemporal control signals, including 2D keypoints/flow, 3D keypoints/flow, keyposes, orientation, and gripper-state conditions.
These masks represent common controllable action-generation interfaces, where partial Cartesian action tokens are specified as subgoals or constraints.
Joint training over    α  \alpha    enables the policy to follow diverse structured conditions within a unified action space.
The    β  \beta   -pattern corresponds to the no-known-context case, where no action reference is provided.
In this setting, the model reduces to plain action generation by the diffusion policy.
Including    β  \beta    encourages robustness when test-time conditions are sparse, such as low-frequency control signals.
The    γ  \gamma   -pattern introduces random token-level masks.
Since structured masks are special cases of random masking,    γ  \gamma    promotes token-level generalization across MAP, especially under OOD patterns.
However, experiments show that random-mask training may underperform structured joint training when training data is limited.
We sample a mask family     c  ∼   ρ  ⁡   (  c  )     c\sim\rho(c)   , where     c  ∈   {   α  ,  β  ,  γ   }    c\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}   , and then draw a mask     M  ∼   p  ⁡   (  M  ∣  c  )     M\sim p(M\mid c)   .
The distribution     ρ  ⁡   (  c  )    \rho(c)    controls the relative frequency of structured, no-reference, and random-mask examples during policy training.
Mask families and compositions are detailed in Supplementary Sections 3.4–3.5.
For LIBERO-10, we first train with random masks and then continue with a mixture of structured masks, as detailed in Supplementary Sections 2.2 and 1.4.
We find that, after joint training, the model can follow compositions of different spatiotemporal constraints and generaloize to an OOD mask pattern, formed by combining several standard patterns but never observed during training (Figure   5  ).

We adopt a RePaint-style inference strategy  ( Lugmayr et al., 2022 )  that repeatedly reinserts specified reference values during denoising without additional policy training.

This overwrite operation is applied at every denoising step, ensuring that the final sampled action chunk strictly preserves the specified reference values rather than merely conditioning on them.
4  EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments focus on three questions: (1) What information in physical references matters for action reconstruction?
(2) Can the model generalize to unseen, out-of-distribution mask patterns?
(3) How does each component contribute to the performance of our method?
We evaluate our policy on 15 tasks across ManiSkill, LIBERO-10, and real-world environments, demonstrating diverse manipulation skills and long-horizon capabilities.
Figure   3   provides an overview of the simulation and real-world tasks.
Figure 3:  Simulation and real-world tasks.
Dataset Construction.
For the simulation experiments, we construct action references by masking demonstration trajectories, without collecting additional reference annotations.
Pairing each demonstration with multiple mask configurations provides diverse reference conditions while preserving its observations and target actions.
As shown in Figure   1  (C), structured masks reveal modality-specific coordinate groups at selected reference locations for 2D/3D points, 7D poses, and orientations, or retain position coordinates along reference trajectories for 2D/3D flow.
Random masks instead reveal individual scalar action entries across reference locations and action dimensions (Section   3.1  ).
We additionally evaluate  Mix0 , a composition excluded from joint training that combines dense 2D flow, one 7D pose, and three 3D points (see Supplementary Section 2.4).
Progress Module.
Our progress module adapts SARM  ( Chen et al., 2026a )  to estimate task progress from frozen CLIP image and text features and proprioceptive states.
The module is trained separately from the policy and uses an eight-layer Transformer with a hidden dimension of 768 and a temporal sampling interval of two frames.
Metrics.
We report task success rate (SR) to evaluate task completion.
In addition to SR, we introduce  Control Error (CE)  to quantify how closely the generated trajectory follows the prescribed control signals.
Let     τ  =   (   a  1   ,   a  2   ,  …  ,   a  T   )    \tau=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{T})    denote the generated action trajectory, where      a  t   ∈   ℝ  7    a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}   , and let     𝒞  =   {    c  1   ,   c  2   ,  …  ,   c  K    }    \mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{K}\}    denote the control signals.
For each signal     c  ℓ   c_{\ell}   , a selection matrix     S  ℓ   S_{\ell}    extracts the corresponding action dimensions.
We define CE as the average distance from each control signal to its closest matching trajectory point:     CE  =    1  K   ​    ∑   ℓ  =  1   K     min   t  ∈   {   1  ,  …  ,  T   }     ⁡    ‖     S  ℓ   ⋅   a  t    −   c  ℓ    ‖   2       \mathrm{CE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\min_{t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}}\left\|S_{\ell}\cdot a_{t}-c_{\ell}\right\|_{2}   .
Lower CE indicates closer agreement with the prescribed control values.
Baseline.
We compare our method with the following baselines.
All methods are trained and evaluated using the same data, random seed, and initial states.  (1) Diffusion Policy (DP).
We use Diffusion Policy  ( Chi et al., 2025 )  with a U-Net backbone as the standard behavior cloning baseline, generating actions without control references.
(2) DiT Policy.
We replace the U-Net with a Diffusion Transformer (DiT)  ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 )  to isolate the effect of the backbone.
(3) Spatiotemporal Conditioning (STC).
Following prior work on trajectory-, keypose-, and motion-conditioned action generation  ( Huang et al., 2025 ;  Nasiriany et al., 2025 ;  Wen et al., 2024 ;  Gu et al., 2023 ;  Fan et al., 2025a ;  Xu et al., 2024 ;  Yu et al., 2024 ;  Ma et al., 2024 ) , STC flattens spatiotemporal signals and injects them into the diffusion transformer as conditioning features.
It shares the backbone and training hyperparameters of Inpainting Policy to assess the necessity of the Inpainting Policy design.

5  CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Conclusion.
We presented a unified framework for spatiotemporal action editing that augments policies with DiT or U-Net backbones with a unified control interface over heterogeneous spatiotemporal references.
Without requiring additional control-specific data or increasing the backbone training budget, our method preserves the original task completion ability while enabling users to edit and compose spatiotemporal action constraints through masked action inpainting.
Our inpainting formulation is model-agnostic and may be compatible with a broad range of foundation action models, including WAM and flow-matching-based VLAs, which we leave for future empirical study.
Limitations.
Our current inpainting implementation may not be optimal; alternatives such as CFG and ControlNet-style conditioning remain unexplored.
Future work will study large-scale BERT-style random-mask pretraining for foundation action models and evaluate zero-shot editing under more unstructured control specifications.

2  Additional Implementation Details
2.1  Benchmarks, Data, and Baselines
Benchmarks and dataset construction.
ManiSkill covers picking and placing objects, controlling object rotation, and pushing objects to target locations.
LIBERO-10 comprises ten long-horizon manipulation tasks with visual observations and language instructions.
Figure 3 (Simulation and real-world tasks) in the main paper summarizes the simulation and real-world tasks.
We construct training and test examples by masking demonstrations, without collecting additional control references.
Mask-wise data augmentation pairs each demonstration with multiple mask configurations while preserving its observations and target actions.
DP and DiT Policy.
DP uses a U-Net backbone for observation-to-action generation without an explicit spatiotemporal control interface  ( Chi et al., 2025 ) .
DiT Policy replaces the U-Net with a Diffusion Transformer  ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) , keeping the action prediction objective and training protocol unchanged to examine the effect of the backbone.
Spatiotemporal Conditioning (STC).
Following methods that condition action generation on trajectories, keyposes, or motion guidance  ( Huang et al., 2025 ;  Nasiriany et al., 2025 ;  Wen et al., 2024 ;  Gu et al., 2023 ;  Fan et al., 2025a ;  Xu et al., 2024 ;  Yu et al., 2024 ;  Ma et al., 2024 ) , STC flattens spatiotemporal control signals and injects them into a Diffusion Transformer as conditioning features.
It shares the backbone and training hyperparameters of Inpainting Policy, allowing a comparison between direct reference conditioning and masked-action reconstruction.
Within each comparison, methods use the same data, random seed, and initial states.
We additionally evaluate IP-RePaint as a training-free inference variant of Inpainting Policy.
2.2  Policy Architectures and Training
Mask ratio.
We investigate how the amount of visible control information affects action reconstruction by comparing structured constraints, represented by 3D points, with random constraints.
We quantify the information provided by either constraint type using the spatiotemporal information budget     N  ST   N_{\mathrm{ST}}   , the number of visible scalar action entries.
For a reference sequence of length    T  T    with action dimension     D  =  7   D=7   , 3D-point and random constraints provide approximately     3  ​  r  ​  T   3rT    and     r  ​  T  ​  D   rTD    visible entries, respectively.
Here, the structured-mask ratio is defined over reference locations, whereas the random-mask ratio is defined over scalar action entries, following the mask settings shown in the main paper.
Equal visible ratios therefore do not imply equal information budgets; Section   2.4   gives the precise definitions and budget-matched settings.
Mask category.
We next examine the effect of constraint structure while approximately matching the number of spatiotemporal tokens across constraint types.
Each token corresponds to one visible action coordinate at one reference location.
Thus, a 2D point contains two tokens, a 3D point contains three, and a 7D pose contains seven; complete 2D and 3D trajectories contain     2  ​  T   2T    and     3  ​  T   3T    tokens, respectively.
Random constraints contribute one token per revealed action coordinate.
We set      N  ST   =   0.2  ​  T  ​  D   =   1.4  ​  T    N_{\mathrm{ST}}=0.2TD=1.4T    and use visible ratios of    0.70  0.70   ,     7  /  15   7/15   ,    0.20  0.20   , and    0.20  0.20    for 2D points, 3D points, 7D poses, and random masks, respectively.
An orientation reference specifies three rotational coordinates and therefore uses the same visible ratio as a 3D point,      r  orientation   =   7  /  15    r_{\mathrm{orientation}}=7/15   .
For random constraints, the ratio is instead computed over all action entries:      r  random   =    N  ST   /   (   T  ​  D   )     r_{\mathrm{random}}=N_{\mathrm{ST}}/(TD)   .
Action-inpainting study.
The mask study uses a DiT with six encoder blocks, six decoder blocks, a hidden dimension of 512, and approximately 49M trainable parameters.
Each policy is trained on 100 demonstrations per task for 100,000 iterations, with a batch size of 64 and a learning rate of     10   −  4    10^{-4}   .
Each visibility and mask-type configuration is evaluated on 80 held-out episodes per task.
Single-task joint training on ManiSkill.
Each task uses a separate policy trained on 500 demonstrations with heterogeneous spatiotemporal masks.
The DiT architecture is the same as in the mask study.
Training uses 300,000 updates, a global batch size of 128, and a learning rate of     5  ×   10   −  5     5\times 10^{-5}   .
Multi-task joint training on LIBERO-10.
We train a shared language-conditioned policy on the official LIBERO-10 dataset.
The policy uses a U-Net-based Diffusion Policy with channel dimensions of 512, 1024, and 2048, and a lightweight Transformer language encoder.
Two-stage joint training first uses random masks across all ten tasks and then continues with a mixture of structured masks spanning different guidance modalities and sparsity levels.
Training uses 180,000 updates, a global batch size of 96, and a learning rate of     10   −  4    10^{-4}   .
The fixed test set contains 100 trajectories derived from the official LIBERO-10 pruned states; masking these trajectories produces the evaluation references.
Progress module.
We adapt SARM  ( Chen et al., 2026a )  to estimate task progress from frozen CLIP image and text features and proprioceptive states.
The module is trained separately and uses an eight-layer Transformer with a hidden dimension of 768 and a temporal sampling interval of two frames.
We refer to the progress module as STPM in the configuration descriptions and comparisons below.

2.6  Real-World Setup and Evaluation
Hardware and observations.
We use Franka R3 robotic arms equipped with two-finger grippers.
A calibrated RealSense D455 camera provides the third-person view, and a ZED-M camera provides the wrist view.
The policy operates at 15 Hz and outputs absolute end-effector commands.
Policies and training.
We train task-specific Diffusion Policy (DP) baselines and Inpainting Policies (IP) on the same demonstration datasets.
Both use frozen, pretrained DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoders for dual-camera RGB observations, together with proprioceptive inputs and a U-Net denoiser with channel dimensions of 256, 512, and 1024.
At inference time, IP additionally receives the user-provided action references through a 32-step MAP window.
DP serves as the base policy for observation-to-action generation.
Human-in-the-loop control.
The Robotic Spatiotemporal Editing system accepts a variable number of user-provided references.
Each reference specifies a reference type, the corresponding action values, and a normalized progress value      p  ′   ∈   [   0  ,  1   ]    p^{\prime}\in[0,1]    indicating the task stage at which the constraint should apply.
The system converts these inputs into visibility masks and partially specified action tokens in the Masked Action Space (MAP) interface.
At inference time, a progress model estimates the current task-progress anchor and selects the corresponding MAP window.
IP conditions on this window, visual observations, and proprioceptive state to reconstruct the unspecified action dimensions and predict a complete action chunk for execution.
Here, MAP denotes the reference interface, and IP denotes the learned policy used by the interactive system.
Tasks and reference configurations.
We evaluate whether references allow IP to satisfy additional spatial and orientational objectives while completing the underlying manipulation task.
Across the real-world evaluation, the system uses heterogeneous reference types, including keypoints, flow, poses, and orientations, under both sparse and dense reference configurations.
In cup arrangement, the underlying objective is to pick up a cup and place it on a plate; the references additionally control how the cup is grasped and where it is placed.
In controlled test-tube pouring, the underlying objective is to move a test tube above a beaker and pour liquid; orientation references specify the tube’s tilt angle to regulate the dispensed volume.
Figure 6 in the main paper illustrates the reference-guided behaviors and summarizes the reported outcomes.
Evaluation metrics.
We report task success rate together with task-specific physical reference errors.
For cup arrangement, the physical error is the cup-to-reference-point distance in millimeters; for pouring, it is the tube-tilt error in degrees.
These quantities describe task outcomes and are distinct from normalized action-space CE, as discussed in Section   2.3  .
The rollouts also provide qualitative evidence about motion smoothness and response to the requested references.
The per-configuration tables below additionally report normalized action-space CE from the recorded evaluations.

Practical tips for real-robot deployment.
Progress used for window selection should evolve smoothly and monotonically.
Discontinuous or backward updates can skip reference points or reactivate constraints from an earlier task stage.
A calibrated mapping from predicted progress to reference position, with limits on backward motion and abrupt advances, helps maintain consistent window selection.
Progress completion should be distinguished from successful physical task completion.
References should cover the task stages that matter for the requested behavior.
For cup arrangement, guidance during grasping and transport can complement a final placement target; for pouring, orientation references can specify the intended progression of tube tilt.
Because IP receives a local MAP window, a single late-stage reference may not become visible until execution approaches its associated progress value.
Reference positions and orientations should remain compatible with the training distribution and with one another.
Requests far outside the demonstrated range can fail even when they are representable through MAP.
IP reconstructs actions using learned behavior, so encoding a constraint in the interface does not by itself guarantee reliable execution of every requested behavior.
3  Additional Method Details
Throughout this section,    t  t    indexes policy execution,    p  p    indexes reference locations, and    k  k    indexes diffusion steps.
3.1  Progress-Guided MAP Alignment
Progress estimation.
We use normalized task progress rather than the raw execution timestep to align control references with policy execution.

The standard Inpainting Policy (IP) generates a complete action chunk conditioned on observations and the local MAP window.

3.4  Mask Families for Joint Training
We construct multiple masked-action examples from each demonstration.
The training distribution contains three complementary mask families: structured masks (   α  \alpha   ), no-reference masks (   β  \beta   ), and random masks (   γ  \gamma   ).
α  \alpha   : Structured control masks.
The structured family represents control references that naturally arise from external planning or human specification.
It includes:
•
2D position or trajectory constraints;
•
3D position or trajectory constraints;
•
7D poses comprising Cartesian position, orientation, and gripper state;
•
orientation-only constraints;
•
gripper-state constraints; and
•
sparse keypoints or keyposes.
These masks expose semantically meaningful subsets of the Cartesian action trajectory and teach the policy to interpret different reference modalities within a shared representation.
The first three coordinates specify Cartesian position, the next three specify orientation, and the seventh specifies the gripper state.
Here, 2D references constrain Cartesian     x  ,  y   x,y    coordinates, while flow references specify position trajectories across reference locations.
Image-space references must first be mapped into the action coordinate system.
For a reference at progress     p  ′   p^{\prime}   , we select      p  ⋆   =    arg  ⁡   min  p    ​   |    p  p  ′   −   p  ′    |     p^{\star}=\arg\min_{p}|p_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}|    and reveal the corresponding action coordinates in row     p  ⋆   p^{\star}    of    M  M   .
For example, a 3D point reveals coordinates     {  1  ,  2  ,  3  }   \{1,2,3\}   , while an orientation reference reveals     {  4  ,  5  ,  6  }   \{4,5,6\}   .
β  \beta   : No-reference masks.
For this family, the visibility ratio is zero:
M  =  𝟎   .   M=\mathbf{0}.
The policy therefore generates actions without an external action reference while retaining its observation conditioning.
Including this case trains the policy to operate when action references are unavailable.
γ  \gamma   : Random token masks.
Random masks reveal randomly selected scalar action entries across reference locations and action dimensions.
Unlike the structured family, these patterns are not tied to a predefined control modality.
They expose the model to a substantially larger space of partial action specifications and encourage token-level reconstruction beyond the structured patterns seen during training.
We sample a mask family and then a mask conditioned on that family:
c  ∼   ρ  ⁡   (  c  )     ,   c  ∈   {  α  ,  β  ,  γ  }    ,   M  ∼   p  ⁡   (  M  ∣  c  )      ,   c\sim\rho(c),\quad c\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\},\qquad M\sim p(M\mid c),
and apply    M  M    to the demonstration action trajectory to construct the reference condition for diffusion training.
The distribution     ρ  ⁡   (  c  )    \rho(c)    controls the relative frequency of structured, no-reference, and random-mask examples.
For LIBERO-10, the two-stage schedule first uses random masks and then continues with a mixture of structured masks spanning different reference modalities and sparsity levels.
The training settings and schedule comparison are described in Sections   2.2   and   1.4  , respectively.
3.5  Generalization to Unseen Mask Compositions
Because all control signals are represented as visible entries in the same      T  ref   ×  7   T_{\mathrm{ref}}\times 7    reference action space, compatible reference modalities can be combined by taking the union of their visible tokens.
For two masks     M  1   M_{1}    and     M  2   M_{2}    defined on the same reference progress grid, their composition is
M  mix   =    M  1   ∨   M  2     ,   M_{\mathrm{mix}}=M_{1}\lor M_{2},

with the corresponding visible action entries assembled from the two references.
This composition assumes that any overlapping specified entries agree.
This representation does not introduce a modality-specific input head or control interface.
Consequently, the policy can accept a mask composition that does not appear explicitly in the training set.
Our experiments evaluate its ability to follow such compositions using  Mix0 , a mixed-mask pattern excluded from training, without additional policy fine-tuning.
The construction of  Mix0  is defined in Section   2.4  .
3.6  Conditioning Architecture
The denoising model is conditioned on three information sources:

Here,     ϕ  vis   \phi_{\mathrm{vis}}    extracts visual features,     ϕ  state   \phi_{\mathrm{state}}    encodes robot proprioception, and     ϕ  MAP   \phi_{\mathrm{MAP}}    embeds the local masked-action window defined in Section   3.1  .
