Abstract
World-action models jointly predict actions and future world states, but long-horizon manipulation further requires language reasoning to plan subtask objectives, assess execution progress, and guide action selection. We present LAW, a closed-loop VLM–WAM framework that unifies language reasoning, action generation, and world modeling through semantic subtask progress. Within the LAW agent, a hybrid-expert vision-language model plans the remaining subtasks, estimates the values of and fuses actions proposed by a world-action model, and asynchronously assesses execution status to support continued execution and subtask transitions. Within the world-action model, Mixture-of-Transformer Experts integrates modality-specific and shared computation for joint video–action modeling. Dual-stream hierarchical visual memory maintains temporal context for both models, while subtask-local value learning aligns action evaluation with semantic objectives and preserves supervision from successful prefixes of failed trajectories. LAW achieves 94.21% average success across 50 RoboTwin tasks. Replacing the WAM backbone with OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    further raises the average success rate to 95.56%, establishing a new open-source state of the art on RoboTwin. Despite having only 2B parameters, our VLM also outperforms variants using Embodied-R1.5 and GPT-5.5 High. On three long-horizon real-robot tasks, LAW achieves 65% average success, compared with 28.33% for     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    and 25% for Fast-WAM.

1  Introduction
Long-horizon manipulation is a sequence of semantic subtasks, not merely a longer sequence of motor commands. After reaching for an object, a robot must decide whether the grasp actually succeeded; after a successful grasp, it must recognize that the task has entered a new stage; before executing the next motion, it must choose among several physically plausible actions according to the current objective  [ 23 ,  22 ] . Errors in any of these decisions compound over time. Consequently, local action quality alone does not determine task success. A capable agent must maintain a persistent account of task progress, connect that account to action generation, and revise its decisions when execution departs from expectation  [ 35 ,  44 ] .
Vision-language-action policies connect semantic representations to control  [ 60 ,  26 ,  8 ] , while world-action models learn action-conditioned scene evolution and executable behavior  [ 59 ,  57 ,  27 ,  51 ] . These models substantially improve action generation, but long-horizon deployment exposes a complementary systems problem: reasoning and control must remain aligned as the task evolves. A language plan generated once at the beginning cannot determine whether its intermediate steps were completed  [ 22 ] , and an action generator conditioned only on the current observation may not know which visual changes matter to the larger goal  [ 41 ] . Placing a planner before a policy therefore leaves three interfaces underspecified: how execution progress updates the plan, how candidate actions are judged against the current semantic objective, and how relevant history is retained without overwhelming either model.
Our premise is that semantic subtask progress should serve as the temporal interface between language reasoning and world-action modeling. Subtasks provide more than a decomposition of the instruction: they define when the agent should advance its plan, which outcomes a candidate action should optimize, and which observations should remain salient in memory. This view reveals that computations occur at different rates. State assessment should run continuously without blocking control, candidate evaluation should occur whenever the WAM proposes a new action chunk, and planning should operate at the semantic task scale rather than at every control step. The challenge is coordinating reasoning and control, rather than invoking a larger reasoner at every step.
We instantiate this principle in LAW, a closed-loop VLM–WAM framework that unifies language reasoning, action generation, and world modeling through semantic subtask progress, as summarized in Fig.   1  . The LAW agent uses a hybrid-expert VLM with role-selected planner, state, and value experts on a shared frozen backbone. The planner predicts the remaining semantic subtasks; conditioned on the current subtask, the WAM generates diverse action chunks; the value expert ranks and fuses them; and execution status is assessed asynchronously to determine subtask completion. Once the current subtask is completed, the planner predicts the next subtask and the agent switches to it; otherwise, the agent continues executing the current subtask. The LAW agent coordinates planning, action selection, and execution monitoring at different frequencies, using feedback to continue or switch subtasks.
To strengthen joint world and action modeling, we introduce Mixture-of-Transformer Experts (MoTE) into the WAM, yielding MoTE-WAM. MoT projects video and action features into a common attention space to exchange cross-modal information, but their subsequent transformations remain modality-specific. MoTE introduces shared experts to further transform these cross-modal representations, alongside video-private and action-private experts that preserve modality-specific computation. The shared experts learn common temporal and physical structure across predicted scene dynamics and robot actions, strengthening video–action joint modeling beyond information exchange through attention alone. Long-horizon execution also requires memory aligned with subtask transitions. Separate hierarchical memory streams for the VLM and WAM retain recent observations and full-resolution states at subtask boundaries, while compressing older history to preserve relevant context across subtasks. To better exploit failed trajectories when training the value expert, we introduce subtask-local value learning, which terminates returns at semantic boundaries. This preserves positive supervision from successfully completed subtasks within failed episodes, rather than assigning zero value to all their actions.
We evaluate LAW on 50 RoboTwin  [ 13 ]  tasks, the RoboDojo  [ 12 ]  capability benchmark, and three long-horizon real-robot tasks containing up to nine semantic subtasks. LAW achieves 94.21% average success on RoboTwin, 2.38 percentage points above Fast-WAM, without robot-data pretraining. Replacing the WAM backbone with OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    further raises the average success rate to 95.56%, establishing a new open-source state of the art on RoboTwin. Despite having only 2B parameters, our VLM outperforms variants using Embodied-R1.5 and GPT-5.5 High within the same LAW framework. On RoboDojo, LAW achieves leading performance across capability dimensions among methods without additional robot-domain or embodied-interaction pretraining. On the three long-horizon real-robot tasks, LAW achieves 65% average success, compared with 28.33% for     π  0.5   \pi_{0.5}    and 25% for Fast-WAM. We also conduct extensive ablation studies to validate the effectiveness of each component. Our contributions are:
•
We introduce LAW, a closed-loop VLM–WAM framework that unifies language reasoning, action generation, and world modeling through semantic subtask progress. The LAW agent coordinates planning, asynchronous completion assessment, and value-guided action fusion for long-horizon execution.
•
We introduce MoTE, dual-stream hierarchical memory, and subtask-local value learning to support progress-aware control: MoTE strengthens joint video–action modeling through shared and modality-specific experts; dual-stream hierarchical memory preserves task-relevant history and subtask-boundary observations; and subtask-local value learning retains positive supervision from completed stages of failed trajectories.
•
We demonstrate LAW’s effectiveness on RoboTwin, RoboDojo, and long-horizon real-world manipulation through extensive experiments and ablation studies. Integrating OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    achieves 95.56% average success on RoboTwin, establishing a new open-source state of the art and demonstrating LAW’s extensibility to stronger pretrained WAMs.
2  Related Work
2.1  Vision-Language-Action Models for Robot Control
Pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) provide semantic representations that vision-language-action (VLA) policies connect to executable control  [ 14 ,  60 ,  28 ,  26 ,  8 ] , supported by heterogeneous robot datasets and generalist pretraining  [ 39 ,  17 ] . RT-2 and OpenVLA represent actions as tokens, whereas     π  0   \pi_{0}    couples a VLM with a flow-matching expert for continuous action generation  [ 60 ,  26 ,  8 ] . Beyond action parameterization, CoT-VLA predicts intermediate visual subgoals  [ 54 ] , while VEGA aligns visual-encoder outputs with geometry-aware features before vision-language fusion  [ 45 ] . These approaches strengthen semantic transfer and spatial grounding, motivating complementary mechanisms for coordinating instructions, execution progress, and action selection over extended tasks.
Language-guided systems connect reasoning to execution through affordance-based skill selection, environmental feedback, action hierarchies, and spatial value maps  [ 23 ,  22 ,  4 ,  21 ] . SayCan grounds language plans in learned affordances, whereas VoxPoser translates language-specified objectives and constraints into spatial maps for trajectory optimization  [ 23 ,  21 ] . In navigation, NavCoT structures decisions through future imagination and observation matching  [ 33 ] , while Memoir retrieves environmental and behavioral experience using imagined states  [ 47 ] . Although navigation differs from continuous manipulation, these studies illustrate how intermediate reasoning and selective memory access support sequential decisions. For long-horizon robot policies, the corresponding challenge is to retain task-relevant history and execution feedback when semantic stages extend beyond individual action chunks  [ 41 ,  44 ] .
2.2  World Action Models and Video-Based Policy
World models support control through action-conditioned visual prediction or latent dynamics  [ 15 ,  19 ,  58 ,  1 ,  3 ] , while video generators also enable synthetic robot training trajectories  [ 24 ] . Visual Foresight optimizes actions through predicted observations, whereas Dreamer learns behavior through latent imagination, providing two complementary uses of predictive dynamics  [ 15 ,  19 ] . Recent world-action models (WAMs) connect future visual modeling with executable action generation through joint diffusion or future-latent alignment  [ 59 ,  57 ,  30 ,  32 ,  27 ,  51 ,  53 ] . Their designs differ in how predictive information is used during deployment: LingBot-VA combines causal video–action modeling with observation feedback and asynchronous execution  [ 27 ] , whereas Fast-WAM retains video co-training but omits test-time future prediction  [ 51 ] . WAM4D further introduces geometric supervision through spatial register tokens and modality-specific causal attention  [ 29 ] . These developments distinguish predictive supervision benefits from the cost of generating future observations online.
Reliable world-model-based control also requires faithful action consequences and informative evaluation signals. WorldEcho diagnoses failures to follow off-expert actions, and WorldSync improves action-conditioned generation through broader coverage and explicit action-effect alignment  [ 11 ] . Cosmos Policy jointly generates actions, future states, and values for planning  [ 25 ] ; MV-WAM integrates progress-value estimation with value-guided rollback  [ 10 ] ; and SafeDojo optimizes VLA policies using imagined trajectories with separate task-reward and safety-cost estimates  [ 42 ] . LAW builds on these ingredients by coordinating semantic planning, asynchronous state assessment, and value-guided WAM candidate selection, supported by hierarchical visual memory. Its modality-specific and shared experts strengthen video–action interaction, while separate hierarchical memory streams preserve temporal context for language-level reasoning and continuous robot action generation. Unlike the trajectory-level returns used by MV-WAM  [ 10 ] , LAW terminates value targets at semantic subtask boundaries, preserving supervision from completed prefixes of unsuccessful episodes. This links local action evaluation to progress-aware action selection and planning grounded in semantic subtasks.

3.2  Hybrid-Expert VLM for Progress-Aware Control
Hybrid-Expert VLM
To support planning, execution monitoring, and action evaluation within a single VLM, we introduce a hybrid-expert architecture with task-specific LoRA adapters  [ 20 ] . The planner, state, and value experts share a frozen VLM backbone, allowing them to reuse common visual-language representations while avoiding the cost of maintaining a separate full model for each control function. Each expert activates its own adapter and task-specific head, enabling specialization under distinct supervision objectives while isolating parameter updates and reducing interference across functions. Expert adapters are selected by control function rather than token-level routing.
The planner is trained with semantic subtask supervision. We construct ordered subtask annotations and temporal boundaries from the demonstrations, as illustrated in Fig.   3  . Additional task decompositions and segmented examples are provided in Appendix   E  . Given the global instruction, visual observation, and execution memory, the planner is trained to generate the remaining subtask sequence autoregressively:
ℒ  plan   =  −   ∑   i  =  1    |  𝒮  |    log   p  θ    (   s  i   ∣  g  ,   o  t   ,   ℳ  t  V   ,   s     <  i    )   .   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{plan}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\log p_{\theta}\!\left(s_{i}\mid g,o_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\mathrm{V}},s_{<i}\right).
(7)
The state expert is trained with subtask completion supervision and an auxiliary failure-prediction objective to improve completion assessment. Completion labels are derived from motion-based progress within annotated subtask intervals; the label construction is detailed in Appendix   C  . Failure trajectories are additionally reviewed to identify the first visually unambiguous manipulation error, together with its semantic stage and visual evidence. During training, dedicated completion and failure query tokens provide complementary supervision: the auxiliary failure head encourages the shared representation to distinguish unsuccessful execution from completed subtasks. The training objective is
ℒ  state   =     λ  d   ​  BCE  ​   (    d  ^   t   ,   d  t   )    +    λ  f   ​  BCE  ​   (    f  ^   t   ,   f  t   )      .   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{state}}=\lambda_{d}\,\mathrm{BCE}(\hat{d}_{t},d_{t})+\lambda_{f}\,\mathrm{BCE}(\hat{f}_{t},f_{t}).
(8)
where     d  t   d_{t}    and     f  t   f_{t}    are binary completion and failure labels, and      d  ^   t   \hat{d}_{t}    and      f  ^   t   \hat{f}_{t}    are the corresponding training predictions. The auxiliary failure loss improves the features used by the completion head. For value prediction, candidate action chunks     A  t   A_{t}    are encoded as tokens and processed jointly with the context     x  t   x_{t}    by the shared VLM. A learned query token aggregates these features for a 101-bin categorical value head, yielding

The value expert uses a LoRA adapter  [ 20 ]  initialized from the state expert; only this adapter, the action encoder, query embedding, and value head are trained, while the shared backbone, existing experts, and WAM remain frozen.

Mixed-Frequency Coordination
The three experts and the WAM operate at different frequencies, as illustrated in Fig.   4  . The planner is invoked at episode initialization and near subtask boundaries to produce or update the remaining plan. After the WAM generates candidate action chunks, the value expert synchronously scores and fuses them into the action chunk executed by the robot. In contrast, the state expert runs asynchronously during action execution. Each query-based state-expert forward pass takes approximately    60  60    ms and runs concurrently with action execution, enabling non-blocking completion assessment on the latest observations. The resulting pipelined parallelism between action execution and state assessment improves inference throughput by     15  %   15\%   . A valid prediction with completion probability at least    0.5  0.5    triggers a subtask switch at a control-loop boundary; validity is determined by the timestamp and subtask identifier as described in Algorithm   1  .

The state expert asynchronously estimates subtask completion during action execution and triggers the next planning decision when the current subtask is completed.
3.3  Joint Video–Action Modeling with MoTE
The WAM must jointly model the evolution of visual observations and the robot actions that cause these changes. In the original Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT)  [ 31 ] , video and action branches exchange information through mixed attention: their queries, keys, and values are concatenated for joint attention, after which the outputs are returned to the two modality-specific branches  [ 27 ,  51 ] . Mixed attention exchanges information, but modality-specific transformations limit shared video–action modeling.
Figure 5 :   MoTE architecture for joint video–action modeling. The video and action DiT branches exchange information through MoT mixed self-attention. After mixed attention, modality-specific top-1 routers select private experts or a shared expert available to both streams. The routed expert output is scaled by    α  \alpha    and added residually before the remaining Transformer transformations.
We introduce Mixture-of-Transformer Experts (MoTE) to strengthen this interaction, as illustrated in Fig.   5  . MoTE applies a residual mixture-of-experts layer to the mixed-attention representation. The router produces sparse expert weights for the current modality:

(10)
where    𝐇  \mathbf{H}    is the mixed-attention representation and     W  r   W_{r}    is a modality-specific router. The routed residual update is
𝐇  ~   =   𝐇  +   α  ​    ∑   e  ∈  ℰ      w  e   ​   E  e   ​   (   LN  ⁡   (  𝐇  )    )        ,   \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{H}+\alpha\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}w_{e}E_{e}\!\left(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{H})\right),

where    ℰ  \mathcal{E}    denotes the selected experts,     w  e   w_{e}    is the routing weight, and     E  e   E_{e}    is an expert network. The modality-specific experts preserve the distinct statistics of video and action tokens, while the shared experts capture their common temporal and physical dynamics. Because the MoTE layer is applied immediately after mixed attention, each token is routed to either a modality-private expert or the shared expert before entering the next Transformer block. The shared expert is available to both modalities; top-1 routing does not apply private and shared experts simultaneously to the same token.
In the default configuration, each modality has two private experts and both modalities share one additional expert. The router uses top-1 selection and the residual scale is set to    0.1  0.1   . The training objective combines video prediction, action prediction, and router regularization:

MoTE supplements mixed attention with modality-specific and shared nonlinear transformations to jointly model scene evolution and actions.
3.4  Dual-Stream Hierarchical Memory
Figure 6 :   Hierarchical visual memory and attention pattern in the WAM. Short-term and subtask-boundary observations are retained at full resolution, while older observations are spatially compressed.
Long-horizon manipulation requires historical context to assess task progress and maintain consistent action generation  [ 41 ,  44 ] . Retaining every past observation is computationally expensive, while compressing the entire history into a single representation can discard recent motion details and critical subtask transitions. LAW maintains separate VLM and WAM memory streams over shared observations.
At time    t  t   , we retain the previous five observations at a stride of four, giving the recent window     {    o   t  −  20    ,   o   t  −  16    ,   o   t  −  12    ,   o   t  −  8    ,   o   t  −  4     }   \{o_{t-20},\allowbreak o_{t-16},\allowbreak o_{t-12},\allowbreak o_{t-8},\allowbreak o_{t-4}\}   ; unavailable elements are omitted near the beginning of an episode, and the current observation     o  t   o_{t}    is provided separately. We also retain the initial observation and observations recorded at control-loop subtask transitions when they fall outside this recent window. Earlier frames are sampled every four steps within the current subtask and every 16 steps within completed subtasks. All selected timestamps share the phase     t  mod  4   t\bmod 4   , and a subtask-boundary frame is stored only once. Let      𝒞  t   =   (   c  1   ,  …  ,   c  m   )    \mathcal{C}_{t}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})    denote the resulting long-term timestamps and      ℬ  t   =   (   β  1   ,  …  ,   β  p   )    \mathcal{B}_{t}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})    the retained boundary timestamps, both in temporal order. Figure   6   illustrates how the WAM integrates short-term, subtask-boundary, and long-term visual memory with the current observation and action sequence.
The two streams encode these observations in their native token spaces. Boundary and recent observations retain their full spatial token sequences.

3.5  Subtask-Local Value Learning
Subtask-local value supervision.
Episode-level outcomes discard useful supervision when a trajectory completes several subtasks before failing. We instead assign reward    1  1    to the terminal action of each successfully completed subtask and    0  0    elsewhere. Completed prefixes therefore retain positive supervision, while the final failed subtask receives no positive reward and unobserved subtasks are excluded. For a chunk with     h  t   h_{t}    valid actions, the TD target is

(14)
where     A   t  +   h  t    data   A^{\mathrm{data}}_{t+h_{t}}    is the next recorded chunk and     θ  ¯   \bar{\theta}    denotes exponential-moving-average target parameters. Each chunk is truncated at the first subtask boundary, and     h  t   h_{t}    counts only its valid actions; actions beyond that boundary are masked out of the target. We set      b  t   =  1   b_{t}=1    only when the next recorded chunk exists within the same subtask and no terminal action is included; otherwise,      b  t   =  0   b_{t}=0   . Thus, terminal chunks and chunks without a valid within-subtask successor do not bootstrap.

4  Experiments
4.1  Training Details
Training datasets.
We use task-specific training data from RoboTwin  [ 13 ] , RoboDojo  [ 12 ] , and real-world manipulation. RoboTwin covers 50 bimanual manipulation tasks; its public Aloha-AgileX release provides 50 clean and 500 domain-randomized demonstrations per task. The official RoboDojo training release contains 3,500 trajectories across 35 task directories, with 100 trajectories per directory collected through automated synthesis or VR-based teleoperation. We augment the simulation trajectories with semantic subtask annotations and temporal boundaries for planning and completion assessment.
For real-world training, we collect 300 teleoperated trajectories per task on the AgileX Cobot Magic platform, totaling 900 trajectories, with one scene camera and two wrist cameras. Puzzle comprises three subtasks, each inserting one colored piece into its matching recess. Domino comprises five subtasks: standing four dominoes individually and then toppling the row. Organize comprises nine subtasks: opening the corresponding drawer, placing a colored block inside, and closing the drawer, repeated for three blocks. Each trajectory includes multiview observations, robot states, actions, language instructions, and semantic subtask boundaries. Successful and failed trajectories provide complementary supervision for value learning. Additional annotation and collection details are given in Appendices   E   and   B.2  .
Overall training pipeline.
LAW is trained in two stages. In the first stage, we train the planner and state experts sequentially on semantic subtask annotations, while training the WAM independently on paired video and robot-action sequences. In the second stage, we freeze these components and train the value expert on successful demonstrations and annotated failure trajectories using the subtask-local objective in Section   3.5  . All parameters are frozen for evaluation. This procedure prevents value learning from destabilizing the generator, planner, or state expert.

The planner and state adapters use rank     r  =  64   r=64   , while the value adapter uses rank     r  =  128   r=128   , adding approximately 100M parameters in total. The planner and state adapters use LoRA scale 128 and dropout 0.05. After training the planner, we freeze its adapter and train the state adapter together with its completion and auxiliary failure heads.
The value adapter is initialized from the state adapter, while the action encoder, value query, and categorical head are newly initialized. Each input contains a 32-step, 16-dimensional end-effector action chunk sampled at    0.06  0.06    seconds per step. Failure data construction.
We construct failure trajectories in simulation by replaying successful demonstrations and injecting task-specific perturbations at selected manipulation stages. Each task is assigned one to three applicable failure modes, including missed grasps, premature releases, incorrect placements, missed contacts, and incomplete or misaligned task-specific operations. For each task–failure-type pair, we target 35 clean trajectories and 100 domain-randomized trajectories. We then filter out semantically invalid, visually ambiguous, and low-quality samples.
World-action model.
The WAM is initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B  [ 43 ] , with the action branch initialized from the pretrained ActionDiT weights  [ 51 ] . We optimize the video and action DiTs, including the MoTE layers, while keeping the remaining pretrained components frozen. Training uses three camera views at     384  ×  384   384\times 384    resolution, an action horizon of 32, and subtask-conditioned text embeddings. We use AdamW with      β  1   =  0.9   \beta_{1}=0.9   ,      β  2   =  0.95   \beta_{2}=0.95   , weight decay     10   −  2    10^{-2}   , and gradient clipping at 1.0. The learning rate is linearly warmed up for the first 5% of training and then follows a cosine schedule.
Testing.
The WAM samples 32 candidate chunks using three denoising steps, without explicitly generating future images. The value expert retains the top eight candidates with temperature     T  =  1   T=1   , and the robot executes their fused prefix before resampling from updated observations.

5.1  WAM Backbone Extensibility

The same value expert is reused across Fast-WAM and OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    without backbone-specific adaptation. Despite the difference between recorded training actions and WAM-sampled inference candidates, it improves action selection without additional training on candidates generated by the deployment WAM. Table   6   evaluates this extensibility on RoboTwin using the default Fast-WAM backbone and OpenWAM-   α  \alpha     [ 46 ] . The OpenWAM variant uses the same LAW configuration as the Fast-WAM variant, with only the WAM backbone replaced. LAW improves the average success rate of Fast-WAM from 91.83% to 94.21%, and that of OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    from 93.60% to 95.56%. LAW + OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    establishes a new open-source state of the art on RoboTwin with 95.56% average success. These results demonstrate LAW’s extensibility across WAM backbones: its progress-aware control mechanisms improve both action generators, while integration with a stronger pretrained WAM further raises the framework’s achievable performance.

6  Conclusion
This paper presented LAW, a closed-loop VLM–WAM framework that unifies language reasoning, action generation, and world modeling through semantic subtask progress. The LAW agent coordinates specialized planning, completion assessment, and value-guided action fusion at complementary frequencies, connecting execution feedback to continued control and subtask transitions. MoTE strengthens joint video–action modeling through shared and modality-specific experts, while dual-stream hierarchical memory preserves recent context and semantic boundary observations. Subtask-local value learning further retains useful supervision from completed stages of failed trajectories. Integrating OpenWAM-   α  \alpha    raises RoboTwin success to 95.56%, establishing a new open-source state of the art and demonstrating LAW’s extensibility to stronger pretrained WAMs for further performance gains. These results highlight semantic subtask progress as an effective interface for agentic physical intelligence. Future work will investigate transfer across robot embodiments and adaptive subtask decomposition for longer and more diverse tasks.

Appendix B  Real-World Experimental Details
B.1  Robot Platform and Observations
Experiments are conducted on an AgileX (Songling) Cobot Magic platform equipped with two six-degree-of-freedom PiPER arms and parallel-jaw grippers. Figure   10   illustrates the hardware platform. Manipulation takes place in a fixed tabletop workspace. Visual observations comprise three RGB views: a scene camera covering the shared workspace and a wrist-mounted camera on each arm. The scene view provides task-level context, while the wrist views resolve local interactions between the grippers, objects, and target receptacles. Robot placement and camera mounting geometry are held fixed during data collection to maintain a consistent observation configuration.
Figure 10 :   AgileX Cobot Magic platform. Two follower arms perform bimanual manipulation, while two leader arms support demonstration collection. The observation configuration comprises one scene camera and two wrist cameras. Hardware reference image from  AgileX Robotics .
B.2  Demonstration Collection and Data Representation
Human demonstrations are collected through teleoperation. Across episodes, object positions and in-plane orientations are varied within the reachable workspace, subject to the geometric constraints of each task. This variation changes the required grasps and placements while preserving the task objective. Each trajectory associates multiview visual observations with bimanual robot states, action sequences, a language instruction, and an episode outcome. Subtask descriptions and boundaries supervise action learning, planning, and state estimation.
The collection protocol retains successful, partially completed, and failed executions. Failures are annotated by the stage at which they occur and their principal cause, preserving information about completed task prefixes. Recording validity is assessed separately from task success: episodes with missing visual or robot data, unusable temporal alignment, or severe occlusion of the manipulation are excluded. Collection uses no fixed success-to-failure ratio.
Trajectories are organized in LeRobot format with episode-level metadata and aligned camera streams. Recorded videos are stored at 30 frames per second. LAW uses the end-effector action representation described in the main paper: a three-dimensional position, a unit quaternion, and a scalar gripper command for each arm, giving a 16-dimensional bimanual action vector. Semantic boundaries align visual observations, actions, and subtask labels along the trajectory timeline.
B.3  Task Definitions
The evaluation comprises three tasks with complementary requirements for sequential manipulation, spatial alignment, and state-dependent interaction.
Domino.
The robot arranges four dominoes into an upright row and initiates their toppling. Successful execution requires stable placement and sufficient spatial alignment for the intended toppling sequence. The five stages comprise four placements and a final toppling action.
Puzzle.
The robot inserts three colored pieces into their corresponding recesses in a puzzle board. Each piece constitutes one semantic stage. The task requires matching and aligning each piece before insertion; success requires seating all three pieces.
Organize.
The robot sorts three colored blocks into the corresponding drawers of a three-level cabinet. For each block, the robot opens the target drawer, places the block inside, and closes the drawer, resulting in nine semantic stages. Success requires correct block–drawer assignments and closure of all drawers. The task combines object manipulation with repeated changes to drawer states.

We distinguish complete-task success from partial progress. We report
SR  =    100  N   ​    ∑   i  =  1   N    y  i      ,   Score  =    100   N  ​  K    ​    ∑   i  =  1   N     ∑   k  =  1   K    c   i  ,  k         .   \mathrm{SR}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i},\qquad\mathrm{Score}=\frac{100}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{i,k}.

This distinction captures intermediate progress in long-horizon execution without conflating it with achievement of the final objective.

Appendix D  Observation and Action Conventions
In the implementation, RGB inputs comprise one scene view and two wrist views, with separate historical visual-memory streams for the VLM and WAM. The WAM receives a 16-dimensional EEF state; the VLM state expert receives textual values for six joint positions and one gripper state per arm (14 dimensions), while the planner uses visual and task context. Actions are absolute 16-dimensional EEF targets ordered as left     (  x  ,  y  ,  z  ,   q  w   ,   q  x   ,   q  y   ,   q  z   ,  g  )   (x,y,z,q_{w},q_{x},q_{y},q_{z},g)    followed by right. Absolute poses use scene-world coordinates with the controller-specific end-effector frame convention; no relative-action conversion is applied. Gripper targets use the normalized environment scale, with zero closed and one open. WAM states and actions use per-dimension global z-score statistics from the configured training datasets, cached in  dataset_stats.json ; normalization uses      (   x  −  μ   )   /   (   σ  +   10   −  8     )    (x-\mu)/(\sigma+10^{-8})   , clipped to     [    −  5   ,  5   ]   [-5,5]   , rather than separate statistics for each action-horizon step. The same cached statistics are loaded for evaluation. Predicted actions are inverse-normalized before execution, and quaternion targets are renormalized to unit length. VLM joint-state values are supplied in their recorded scale.
