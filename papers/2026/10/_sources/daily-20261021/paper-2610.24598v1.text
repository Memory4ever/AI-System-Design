We propose AdamPM (Adam with Pooled Moments), a simple and memory-efficient variant of Adam that groups adjacent moment entries along the row or column dimension and represents each group by a single shared mean. AdamPM can well inherit Adam’s hyperparameter settings and is readily integrated into existing Adam optimizer implementations, with less memory overhead. 1 Introduction
Adam ( Kingma & Ba, 2015 ) and its decoupled weight-decay variant AdamW ( Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019 ) , which combine momentum with adaptive learning rates, have become the de facto standard for LLM training due to their strong convergence and stability. However, these advantages come at a substantial memory cost: Adam maintains first and second moment estimates for each parameter, requiring storage for two additional optimizer states per parameter.
As LLMs scale to trillions of parameters, this overhead becomes increasingly prohibitive. Against this backdrop, memory-efficient optimizers have emerged as an important research direction, aiming to reduce training memory consumption through optimizer design while preserving the performance of conventional adaptive methods. Representative approaches achieve this goal by projecting gradients into low-rank subspaces ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Robert et al., 2025 ) , factorizing the second moment state ( Luo et al., 2023 ) , sharing the moment statistics ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Wen et al., 2026b ) , and employing orthogonalization ( Liu et al., 2025 ) or sign-based update rules ( Chen et al., 2023 ) to eliminate the second moment state.
However, a recent survey from Stanford University ( Wen et al., 2026a ) notes that, despite numerous optimizers claiming faster training or lower memory consumption, Adam remains the robust default for LLM pre-training, with its primary limitation being the memory overhead of optimizer states. This raises a natural question:
Before an optimizer consistently surpasses Adam in both performance and robustness, would a memory-efficient variant with only minimal modifications to Adam be a better practical choice?
In this work, we systematically analyze the correlations and redundancy in Adam’s optimizer states, and propose AdamPM, a minimally modified Adam variant that groups adjacent second moment entries and shares one statistic within each group. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose AdamPM, a memory-efficient optimizer for LLM training that reduces optimizer-state memory without sacrificing model performance or increasing training time. AdamPM makes only a minimal modification to vanilla Adam by pooling the second moment state. It can directly reuse Adam’s hyperparameters, including the learning rate and decay rates, and is fully model-agnostic. These characteristics of AdamPM are validated by experiments on LLM pre-training, language-model and vision-language model fine-tuning.
• We systematically investigate the pooling of both first and second moments through experiments. We find that the first moment contains substantially more information and is highly sensitive to pooling, explaining why most memory-efficient optimizers primarily compress the second moment. We introduce cosine similarity and relative error to quantify deviations from vanilla Adam, and show that AdamPM’s preconditioners and parameter updates become more similar to those of Adam as model scale grows, suggesting that AdamPM offers greater memory-efficiency benefits over Adam for larger models.
2 Related Work
As LLMs scale to trillions of parameters, memory-efficient training has become a research priority. Existing memory-efficient optimizers reduce memory overhead primarily through matrix factorization, low-rank projection, constraint-based transformations, and parameter sharing.
Matrix factorization. These methods address reduction of memory by storing the factorized matrices, and the main differences involve to the optimization targets. For example, Adafactor ( Shazeer & Stern, 2018 ) and SMMF ( Park & Lee, 2025 ) factorize optimizer states, the LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) family factorizes weight updates, and LORO ( Mo et al., 2025 ) factorizes weight matrices. Although these methods improve memory efficiency, some are unsuitable for pre-training and limited to fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA), or have not yet been validated as extensively as Adam on LLMs with billions of parameters for pre-training (e.g., Adafactor and SMMF). Our AdamPM, like Adam, works well for both pre-training and fine-tuning, and is validated by experiments on large-scale LLMs and vision-language models.
Low-rank projection. These methods aim at reducing memory by maintaining optimizer states in lower-dimensional spaces. They achieve this goal by random projections in APOLLO ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) , or by gradient projection based on either SVD in GaLore ( Zhao et al., 2024 ) and Fira ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , or online PCA ( Liang et al., 2024 ) . Although these methods reduce memory consumption, they also incur additional computational overhead, such as periodic SVD computations in GaLore ( Zhao et al., 2024 ) , which increase training time. Besides, they often introduce new hyperparameters that are difficult to tune, such as the frequency of periodic subspace updates in Fira ( Chen et al., 2025 ) . Our proposed AdamPM makes only minimal changes to Adam and preserves its desirable optimization properties, while its parameter updates become increasingly similar to Adam’s as model size grows, as demonstrated in Figure 4 (d).
Constraint-based transformations. These methods aim to eliminate the need to store certain optimizer states by introducing constraint-based transformations, e.g., imposing orthogonality constraints on optimizer states in Muon ( Liu et al., 2025 ) and SUMO ( Refael et al., 2025 ) , normalizing gradients along the output feature dimension in SCALE ( Glentis et al., 2025 ) , and using sign-based momentum updates in Lion ( Chen et al., 2023 ) and OLion ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . These optimizers substantially modify Adam, preventing direct reuse of its hyperparameters and requiring additional tuning. AdamPM makes only minimal changes to Adam by using moment pooling, and can directly inherit Adam’s hyperparameters, including the learning rate and decay rates.
Parameter sharing. Adam-mini ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) and FOAM ( Wen et al., 2026b ) also reduce optimizer-state memory by sharing moment estimates. However, compared with Adam-mini ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , AdamPM offers a more general grouping scheme, pooling adjacent second moment entries along rows or columns with a configurable group size s s . Moreover, AdamPM is model-agnostic, whereas Adam-mini relies on model-specific information such as the hidden dimension and attention-head count. AdamPM also differs from FOAM ( Wen et al., 2026b ) , which restricts sharing to power-of-two-sized groups of adjacent elements within each row, applies the same compression to first moments, and introduces residual correction. These additional operations complicate optimizer instantiation, and FOAM cannot adopt Adam’s hyperparameter settings. Our AdamPM is fully model-agnostic, and can directly reuse Adam’s hyperparameters.

3.2 Local Similarity of Second Moment Statistics
Modern Transformer-based large language models widely adopt LayerNorm ( Ba et al., 2016 ) and RMSNorm ( Zhang & Sennrich, 2019 ) . By reducing scale discrepancies across different weights and rescaling the loss landscape, normalization partially plays a role similar to adaptive learning-rate adjustment, which is achieved in Adam through second moment estimation. Consequently, the effective learning rates of different parameters tend to become more similar.
We examine the structural redundancy in Adam’s optimizer states during the pretraining of LLaMA 130M model ( Touvron et al., 2023 ) . For illustration, Figure 1 (a) reports the effective ranks of the gradient, first and second moments for embed and the fifth Transformer block’s key . We define the effective rank of A A as erank ( A ) = exp ( − ∑ i p i log p i ) \operatorname{erank}(A)=\exp(-\sum_{i}p_{i}\log p_{i}) , where p i = σ i / ∑ j σ j p_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\sum_{j}\sigma_{j} and σ i \sigma_{i} denotes its i i -th singular value. Throughout training, the second moment consistently exhibits a substantially lower effective rank, indicating that its information is concentrated in only a few dominant directions. In contrast, the gradient and first moment contain richer directional information, with the first moment exhibiting an even higher effective rank than the gradient. This observation is consistent with their roles in Adam: the first moment primarily determines the update direction, whereas the second moment controls the coordinate-wise update magnitude through the diagonal preconditioner. The second moment can tolerate moderate approximation as long as the resulting update scale remains sufficiently accurate. Adam-mini ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) also supports this view by sharing a single second moment statistic within each parameter block while retaining competitive performance.
To assess local similarity during LLaMA 130M pretraining, we compute the coefficient of variation (CV) for the first moment, second moment, and diagonal preconditioner. For entries 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , CV is defined as CV ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) = σ 𝒳 / | μ 𝒳 | \mathrm{CV}(\mathcal{X})=\sigma_{\mathcal{X}}/|\mu_{\mathcal{X}}| , where μ 𝒳 \mu_{\mathcal{X}} and σ 𝒳 \sigma_{\mathcal{X}} are their mean and standard deviation, respectively.
Figure 1 (b) compares the CVs of the first and second moments for value and the MLP gate projection matrix gate in the fifth Transformer block, while Figure 1 (c) reports the preconditioner CVs for gate . The labels row and col denote mean row-wise and column-wise CVs, respectively, while s_ n n denotes the mean CV across non-overlapping groups of n n adjacent elements along the corresponding dimension. We observe that the first moment exhibits substantially higher CVs because it contains both positive and negative values and carries richer directional information, whereas the second moment has much lower CVs. Moreover, parameter updates depend directly on the diagonal preconditioner 1 V ^ t + ϵ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{t}}+\epsilon} , whose inverse-square-root transformation further reduces this variation. The preconditioner exhibits consistently low CVs throughout training, indicating strong local similarity among neighboring elements, with even lower variation in smaller group sizes.
3.3 AdamPM Optimizer
These observations motivate AdamPM, which reduces optimizer-state memory by applying group-wise moment pooling to Adam’s second moment. AdamPM pools every s s adjacent second moment entries within rows or columns, where s s denotes the group size. By default, AdamPM performs pooling within each row, with rows corresponding to output features. In particular, given a weight matrix W ∈ ℝ m × n W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} , each row is partitioned into q = n / s q=n/s groups if n n is divisible by s s . When n n is not divisible by s s , the final group of squared gradients is padded with its mean.

AdamPM performs moment pooling as follows. It first computes the element-wise squared gradient and reshapes it into m × q m\times q groups of s s adjacent elements. Then AdamPM computes the group means S ¯ t \overline{S}_{t} and applies an EMA to obtain the compressed second moment V ~ t \widetilde{V}_{t} . After bias correction, each group statistic is temporarily broadcast to its corresponding s s elements before the parameter update.
AdamPM pools second moments for all weight matrices, including embed , lm_head , attention, and MLP weights. One-dimensional parameters, such as RMSNorm weights, are pooled as single-row or single-column matrices. Note that, unlike FOAM ( Wen et al., 2026b ) , AdamPM does not pool the first moment and follows Adam’s element-wise update, since pooling the first moment naively leads to substantial performance degradation (Section 4.2 ).
The order of squared-gradient computation, pooling, and EMA accumulation is crucial for memory efficiency, as it is the EMA that incurs persistent optimizer-state storage overhead. By averaging squared gradients within each group before applying the EMA, AdamPM maintains only the compressed second-moment state V ~ t ∈ ℝ m × q \widetilde{V}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times q} throughout training, reducing second-moment memory usage to 1 / s 1/s of that required by Adam. It therefore stores m ​ n mn first moment and m ​ n / s mn/s second moment elements, totaling m ​ n ​ ( 1 + 1 / s ) mn(1+1/s) optimizer-state elements, compared with 2 ​ m ​ n 2mn for Adam.

4 Experiments
We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate AdamPM across diverse training scenarios. Our evaluation includes LLaMA ( Touvron et al., 2023 ) pre-training, language-model fine-tuning on the GLUE benchmark ( Wang et al., 2019 ) , and multimodal fine-tuning using TinyLLaVA ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) , a compact vision-language model. These experiments span different model architectures, parameter scales, and learning tasks, providing a systematic assessment of AdamPM in both pre-training and downstream adaptation. Throughout these experiments, AdamPM- s s denotes AdamPM with a group size of s s .
4.1 Pre-Training of LLaMA
Experimental setup.
We pre-train LLaMA models with 60M, 130M, 350M, and 1.3B parameters on the English subset of C4 ( Raffel et al., 2020 ) . Results are summarized in Table 1 . We evaluate performance using validation perplexity (PPL), where lower is better. We also report the combined memory footprint of model parameters and optimizer states, both stored in BF16.
For all model scales, we set β 1 = 0.9 \beta_{1}=0.9 , β 2 = 0.95 \beta_{2}=0.95 , and ϵ = 10 − 8 \epsilon=10^{-8} . For the 60M, 130M, 350M, and 1.3B models, both AdamPM and Adam use learning rates of 4 × 10 − 3 4\times 10^{-3} , 2 × 10 − 3 2\times 10^{-3} , 6 × 10 − 4 6\times 10^{-4} , and 3 × 10 − 4 3\times 10^{-4} , respectively. These values are selected for their robustness based on learning rate sweeps for Adam, such as those shown in Figure 2 . Detailed training configurations are provided in Appendix C.1 .
Baselines.
We compare AdamPM with vanilla Adam, Adam-mini ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , and several other representative memory-efficient optimizers, including GaLore ( Zhao et al., 2024 ) , which compresses optimizer states by projecting gradients into low-rank subspaces through SVD; APOLLO ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) , which uses random projection to construct subspaces; SUMO ( Refael et al., 2025 ) , which orthogonalizes the projected first moment; and LORO ( Mo et al., 2025 ) , which pre-trains low-rank-parameterized weight matrices through Riemannian optimization. We evaluate Adam-mini in our own experiments, using the same learning rates, moment decay rates, and other hyperparameters as Adam and AdamPM.
We carefully tune the learning rate of Adam for each model scale. As shown in Table 1 , the tuned Adam baseline achieves substantially stronger performance than the Adam baselines reported in several previous studies, such as SUMO ( Refael et al., 2025 ) and LORO ( Mo et al., 2025 ) . This observation highlights the importance of adequately tuning Adam when evaluating memory-efficient optimizers.
Main results.
As shown in Table 1 , AdamPM achieves perplexity comparable to that of carefully tuned vanilla Adam and frequently yields lower perplexity when the group size is not too large, while substantially reducing optimizer-state memory. In particular, AdamPM-8 and AdamPM-16 reduce second moment memory by 87.5% and 93.75%, respectively, relative to Adam. We further report the actual memory usage per GPU and compare the training time of AdamPM and Adam during LLaMA-1.3B pre-training in Appendix C.1 . These results demonstrate that Adam’s second moment statistics can be pooled to reduce memory usage without noticeably degrading pre-training performance. We additionally evaluate an extreme pooling strategy, denoted as AdamPM-inf, in which all second moment entries within each row share a single scalar. As shown in Table 1 , this aggressive pooling leads to modest performance degradation on the 350M and 1.3B models, while Adam-mini, which uses more aggressive second moment sharing, performs even worse on these two models. Overall, group sizes between 2 and 8 generally yield robust performance.

Flexibility of the pooling strategy.
Table 2 provides additional analyses of the group size and pooling direction. AdamPM-3 and AdamPM-5 achieve performance comparable to or even better than Adam. This result highlights AdamPM’s flexibility: padding the final incomplete group with the mean of its existing entries allows any positive integer group size, rather than only powers or multiples of two. We also evaluate column-wise second moment pooling, denoted as AdamPM-col. This variant also achieves performance comparable to that of Adam, demonstrating that pooling is effective in either direction.
First- versus second-order moment pooling.
We further evaluate the first moment pooling variant, denoted as AdamPM-M. Notably, directly pooling first moment entries leads to substantial performance degradation. As shown in Table 2 , AdamPM-M-2, which pools every two adjacent entries within each row of the first moment matrices, performs considerably worse than vanilla Adam.
Following the layer-wise strategy adopted by GaLore ( Zhao et al., 2024 ) and FRUGAL ( Zmushko et al., 2025 ) , we revert the embed , lm_head and normalization layers to vanilla Adam while applying first moment pooling to the remaining parameters; we denote this variant as AdamPM-M † . The results in Table 2 show that although this modification improves performance over pooling the first moments of all weight parameters, a substantial performance gap relative to vanilla Adam remains.
Combined with the higher effective ranks and larger coefficients of variation observed for the first moment matrices in Section 3.2 , these results indicate that the first moment carries substantial information regarding parameter update directions. Consequently, the first and second moments should not be considered equally compressible: the second moment exhibits considerable redundancy and can be effectively pooled, whereas directly pooling the first moment discards important optimization information and substantially degrades performance.

5 Conclusion
We introduced AdamPM, a simple memory-efficient Adam variant that only pools second moment estimates. AdamPM reuses Adam’s hyperparameters, remains model-agnostic, and incurs negligible training overhead. Our analysis shows that its preconditioners and parameter updates remain highly similar to Adam’s, particularly at larger model scales. Experiments on LLM pre-training, fine-tuning, and multimodal models demonstrate substantial memory savings while preserving Adam-level performance and training efficiency. These results establish second moment pooling used in AdamPM as a simple and practical approach to memory-efficient LLM training.
Limitations.
Due to computational constraints, we have not conducted full training runs on models with 7B or more parameters. Our study considers pooling only along a single direction, either row-wise or column-wise. Pooling over two-dimensional groups and selective pooling that retains elementwise second moments in certain layers or modules, along with their effects on optimization, remain unexplored.

Training Efficiency.
The implementation of AdamPM performs group-wise second moment pooling entirely through matrix-level tensor operations, including reshaping, averaging, and broadcasting, enabling efficient vectorized computation without element-wise Python for loops. Its computational cost is therefore nearly identical to that of vanilla Adam. In the pre-training of LLaMA 1.3B with a batch size of 32 and 4 × \times NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs, vanilla Adam and AdamPM require 116.2 and 116.4 hours, respectively, confirming that AdamPM introduces negligible training-time overhead. Moreover, the reduced memory footprint may allow a larger batch size under the same hardware constraints, improving hardware utilization and further accelerating training.
