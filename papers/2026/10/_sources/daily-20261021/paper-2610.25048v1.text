Lei Gao Hossein Entezari Zarch Chaoyi Jiang Murali Annavaram Affiliation: University of Southern California Affiliation: {leig, entezari, chaoyij, annavara}@usc.edu Abstract Fine-tuning language models on consumer GPUs is constrained by computation and memory. Although LoRA reduces trainable parameters and optimizer state, frozen projections remain expensive, and activations retained for backward can dominate memory usage. We present PACT, a LoRA fine-tuning system that accelerates frozen projections with native NVFP4 GEMMs while reducing quantization and dequantization overhead and limiting quantization error. To reduce activation memory, PACT reuses quantized RMSNorm outputs and saves gate/up activations in NVFP4 for backward. To limit numerical error, it preserves BF16 inputs for forward nonlinear operations and calibrates frozen weights offline using GPTQ adapted to NVFP4 scales and quantized activations. Fused nonlinear kernels and cached forward and backward weight layouts reduce quantization and dequantization overhead. Evaluations across multiple models and tasks on a 16 GB RTX 5080 show lower memory usage and faster LoRA fine-tuning with competitive accuracy, enabling model adaptation on resource-constrained consumer hardware.
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly fine-tuned to adapt to a user’s private data, personal writing style, or specialized downstream task ( Hu et al., 2022 ; Gao et al., 2025 ; Tan et al., 2024 ) . Performing this adaptation on the device that holds the data keeps sensitive data local, avoids the latency and recurring cost of cloud round trips, and enables continual learning ( Xu et al., 2024 ; Zhu et al., 2023 ) . In this setting, the practical target is often a small language model (SLM) in the 1 1 B to 3 3 B range, which can fit on a consumer GPU before fine-tuning but still faces severe training-time compute and memory pressure.
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) ( Hu et al., 2022 ) reduces the number of trainable parameters by freezing pretrained weights and optimizing small adapter matrices. This substantially reduces gradient and optimizer storage, but the frozen projections are still evaluated in the forward pass and used to propagate input gradients during backward. Standard BF16 LoRA therefore retains substantial computation, while saved activations become a major memory cost as the batch size and sequence length grow. Figure 1 illustrates this activation-dominated regime on a consumer GPU.
Figure 1: GPU memory composition during BF16 LoRA fine-tuning at batch size 4. Activations dominate memory at longer sequence lengths.
Existing methods address these costs through different mechanisms. QLoRA ( Dettmers et al., 2023 ) compresses frozen weights to four bits while retaining higher-precision computation and activations. Mixed-precision training accelerates matrix multiplications by quantizing their operands ( DeepSeek-AI, 2024 ; NVIDIA, 2025b ) , but it does retain quantized activations for backward computation. Consequently, retained activations can remain a substantial memory cost even when weights and computation use low precision. These approaches motivate jointly designing the computation and storage of a fine-tuning system.
NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs provide native NVFP4 matrix multiplication, offering a hardware basis for this design. At batch size 4 and sequence length 1,024, our representative linear-layer measurements show that NVFP4 GEMMs are approximately 6 6 – 7 × 7\times faster than BF16 GEMMs (Table 1 ). Realizing this benefit during training requires carefully designing the data flow to reduce the overhead of quantization and dequantization operations while limiting quantization error across both the forward and backward passes.

We introduce PACT , an NVFP4-based LoRA fine-tuning system that combines accelerated frozen projections with compressed activation storage. To accelerate computation, PACT uses native NVFP4 GEMMs for the forward and input-gradient computations of all seven frozen projections in each decoder layer. To reduce activation memory, PACT reuses quantized RMSNorm outputs for backward and saves separate NVFP4 copies of the gate/up activations. To reduce quantization error, SiLU-multiply uses the original BF16 gate/up values in forward, which are released after use. To reduce overhead caused by quantization and dequantization operations, fused kernels are implemented to produce both the compressed backward state and the quantized inputs required by subsequent projections, avoiding additional GPU memory traffic.
Frozen base weights provide a complementary opportunity to further reduce quantization error at the offline phase. PACT adapts GPTQ ( Frantar et al., 2023 ) to NVFP4 by evaluating representable block scales and constructing calibration statistics from quantized activations. This aligns weight calibration with the activation precision used during training. To further improve computation efficiency, PACT caches quantized weight representations in the layouts required by forward and input-gradient GEMMs, avoiding repeated weight quantization during training.
Our contributions are:
• We design an NVFP4 LoRA data flow that combines accelerated frozen projections with compressed activation storage. It reuses quantized RMSNorm outputs and saves gate/up activations in NVFP4 for backward while preserving BF16 inputs for forward nonlinear computation.
• We reduce quantization and dequantization overhead through fused Triton kernels and cached weight layouts. We also adapt GPTQ to NVFP4 block scales and quantized calibration inputs to reduce weight quantization error offline.
• We evaluate PACT across language and vision-language tasks on a 16 GB consumer GPU. Compared with BF16 LoRA, PACT reduces peak memory by 47% to 51% and achieves 1.7 × 1.7\times to 1.9 × 1.9\times training-step speedups at matched batch shapes, with an average classification accuracy gap of at most 2.6 percentage points.
2 Background
Quantization. Quantization ( Gholami et al., 2021 ; Nagel et al., 2021 ) reduces numerical precision by mapping a high-precision tensor 𝐗 \mathbf{X} to a finite set of discrete levels, thereby lowering memory footprint and computational cost. Formally, for a target integer or floating-point type with k k -bit codes having dynamic range [ − Q max , Q max ] [-Q_{\max},Q_{\max}] , each element is quantized as
x ~ i = round 𝒞 ⁡ ( x i s ) , s = max ⁡ ( | 𝐗 | ) Q max , \tilde{x}_{i}=\operatorname{round}_{\mathcal{C}}\!\left(\frac{x_{i}}{s}\right),\qquad s=\frac{\max(|\mathbf{X}|)}{Q_{\max}}, (1)
where s s is the per-tensor scaling factor and round 𝒞 \operatorname{round}_{\mathcal{C}} rounds to the nearest representable value in the target codebook 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . The code for x ~ i \tilde{x}_{i} is stored in k k bits.
Block-scaled quantization. The NVIDIA Blackwell architecture ( NVIDIA, 2025a ) introduces hardware acceleration for block-scaled formats. Unlike per-tensor methods, block-scaled quantization operates on small groups of elements, each with its own scaling factor. Specifically, MXFP4 groups 32 E2M1 elements under one E8M0 scale ( Rouhani et al., 2023 ) , whereas NVFP4 groups 16 E2M1 elements under one E4M3 scale ( NVIDIA, 2025b ) . Although Blackwell supports both formats, this work focuses on NVFP4 for its finer-grained block size and higher-precision scaling factor. A detailed comparison is provided in Appendix A .
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA). LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that freezes the pretrained weight 𝐖 0 ∈ ℝ d out × d in \mathbf{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}} and models its update Δ ​ 𝐖 ∈ ℝ d out × d in \Delta\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}} as a low-rank product, 𝐖 ′ = 𝐖 0 + Δ ​ 𝐖 = 𝐖 0 + 𝐁𝐀 , \mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}, where 𝐀 ∈ ℝ r × d in \mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}} and 𝐁 ∈ ℝ d out × r \mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r} are the trainable low-rank factors with r ≪ min ⁡ ( d in , d out ) r\ll\min(d_{\text{in}},d_{\text{out}}) , while 𝐖 0 \mathbf{W}_{0} remains frozen throughout training. The forward pass is computed as

where 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is initialized randomly and 𝐁 \mathbf{B} is initialized to zero, so that Δ ​ 𝐖 = 𝟎 \Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{0} at the start of fine-tuning and the pretrained behavior is preserved.
3 PACT
PACT uses native NVFP4 GEMMs to accelerate frozen projections (Section 3.1 ) and stores selected activations in NVFP4 for backward (Section 3.2 ), as shown in Figure 2 . To limit quantization error, it preserves BF16 inputs for forward SiLU-multiply and calibrates frozen weights offline (Section 3.3 ). Fused kernels combine nonlinear computation with quantization and dequantization to reduce conversion overhead and intermediate memory traffic. Cached forward and backward weight layouts further avoid repeated weight quantization during training.

3.2 NVFP4 Activation Storage Reduction
Beyond accelerating GEMMs, PACT reduces memory by storing selected backward state in NVFP4. RMSNorm reuses its quantized output, while SiLU-multiply uses BF16 gate/up inputs in forward and retains separate NVFP4 copies for backward. Customized RMSNorm and SiLU-multiply kernels limit the overhead of storing and reconstructing these compressed activations.
Reuse RMSNorm outputs for backward. At the pre-attention RMSNorm, standard LoRA training saves the normalization input for RMSNorm backward and its output for adapter weight gradients. PACT instead reuses the NVFP4 output already produced for the Q/K/V projections. Together with a per-token normalization statistic, this output supports both adapter weight gradients and approximate RMSNorm backward, eliminating the separately saved input. The pre-MLP RMSNorm likewise retains its NVFP4 output for normalization backward.

For elementwise nonzero 𝜸 \bm{\gamma} , the normalized input can be approximated as 𝐱 ^ ≃ 𝐲 ¯ / 𝜸 \hat{\mathbf{x}}\simeq\bar{\mathbf{y}}/\bm{\gamma} . To compress this state without adding quantization error to the forward inputs, PACT evaluates SiLU-multiply using the original BF16 values and saves separate NVFP4 copies. 3.3 NVFP4 Weight Quantization and Calibration
Frozen weights allow PACT to further reduce quantization error before training. PACT adapts GPTQ by selecting representable NVFP4 block scales and constructing calibration statistics from quantized activations. It then caches the calibrated weights in the layouts required by forward and input-gradient GEMMs. Since the weights remain fixed, both calibration and layout preparation are performed only once.
NVFP4-aware scale selection. NVFP4 represents weights using the E2M1 codebook, an FP32 tensor scale s W s_{W} , and UE4M3 scales shared by contiguous groups of 16 weights. Scale selection must therefore account for rounding in both the weight values and their block scales. Larger scales increase the representable range, while smaller scales improve resolution at the cost of clipping.
PACT evaluates candidate scales after rounding them to UE4M3, measuring reconstruction error with E2M1 weights. To reduce this output discrepancy, PACT quantizes columns sequentially and compensates the remaining weights for each column’s quantization error using inverse-Hessian updates. Both scale selection and error compensation therefore account for the numerical representation used by PACT’s forward GEMMs.
Forward and backward weight layouts. The forward GEMM uses NVFP4 𝐖 ~ 0 ⊤ \tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0}^{\top} , while the input-gradient GEMM uses a separately quantized representation 𝐖 ~ 0 , b \tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0,b} . However, NVFP4 groups weights along the GEMM reduction dimension, which changes from d in d_{\text{in}} in forward to d out d_{\text{out}} in backward. Simply transposing the packed forward representation therefore does not produce the required backward layout. PACT reconstructs and transposes the calibrated forward weights, then requantizes them into NVFP4 along the new reduction dimension using separate block and tensor scales. Caching both layouts removes weight transposition and requantization from the training loop, reducing conversion overhead in both forward and backward computation.

4.1 Models, Protocol, and Baselines
Models and datasets. We study three open-weight LLMs with 1 1 to 3 3 B parameters: Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) , and Qwen2.5-1.5B ( Yang et al., 2024 ) . We abbreviate the two Llama models as Llama-1B and Llama-3B. Our classification and multiple-choice benchmarks include SST-2 for sentiment classification ( Wang et al., 2019b ) , ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge for science question answering ( Clark et al., 2018 ) , and WinoGrande for commonsense reasoning ( Sakaguchi et al., 2021 ) . We also evaluate five SuperGLUE tasks ( Wang et al., 2019a ) : BoolQ for reading comprehension, RTE for textual entailment, WSC for coreference resolution, WiC for word-sense disambiguation, and COPA for causal reasoning. To evaluate open-ended generation, we use SQuAD for reading comprehension ( Rajpurkar et al., 2016 ) , GSM8K for mathematical reasoning ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , and SAMSum for dialogue summarization ( Gliwa et al., 2019 ) . For vision-language adaptation, we fine-tune the language decoder of Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct ( Bai et al., 2025 ) on the image-based questions in ScienceQA ( Lu et al., 2022 ) .
Baselines. We compare PACT with four baselines: zero-shot evaluation, BF16 LoRA as the higher-precision reference, QLoRA with 4 4 -bit NF4 frozen weights, BF16 activations and no gradient checkpointing, and NVFP4 TE. NVFP4 TE is NVIDIA’s TransformerEngine NVIDIA (2026) implementation of NVFP4 mixed-precision pretraining, which we modify to support LoRA fine-tuning. Its linear GEMMs run on NVFP4 tensor cores, while non-linear operators, the LoRA branch, and saved activations remain in BF16.
Hyperparameters and hardware. For the matched classification experiments, we use rank- 16 16 LoRA adapters on the query and value projections and train for up to 500 500 optimizer steps or two epochs, whichever comes first. For each task and method, we sweep learning rates { 10 − 5 , × 10 − 5 , 10 − 4 , × 10 − 4 } \{10^{-5},5\!\times\!10^{-5},10^{-4},5\!\times\!10^{-4}\} and select the rate with the highest mean accuracy across seeds 42, 43, and 44. All main experiments use PyTorch 2.11.0 2.11.0 , FlashAttention 2.8.3 2.8.3 , and CUDA 13.2 13.2 on a single RTX 5080 GPU with 16 16 GB of memory. Appendix C provides the task-specific training and evaluation settings.
4.2 Adaptation Quality
Classification. Table 2 shows that PACT preserves substantial fine-tuning gains, improving average accuracy over zero-shot evaluation by more than 11 percentage points across the three models. Its average accuracy exceeds TE on all three models and remains within 0.3 points of QLoRA on Llama-3B and Qwen2.5-1.5B, despite also quantizing activations and input-gradient GEMM operands. Relative to BF16 LoRA, the average gap is 0.9 to 1.3 points on these two models and 2.6 points on Llama-1B. Sensitivity remains task-dependent: Llama-1B shows larger losses on WSC and WiC, while slightly improving on RTE and matching BF16 on COPA.

5 Related Work
Mixed-Precision Training. The seminal work of Micikevicius et al. (2018) introduced FP16-based mixed-precision training: a forward pass casts higher-precision weights and activations to lower-precision at the GEMM boundary, executes the linear-layer GEMM in lower-precision, and writes back a higher-precision output. Recent works ( Micikevicius et al., 2022 ; DeepSeek-AI, 2024 ; NVIDIA, 2025b ) extend the approach to FP8 and FP4. Quantizing GEMM operands alone does not necessarily reduce the memory occupied by saved activations. PACT combines native NVFP4 GEMMs with NVFP4 storage of selected backward state.
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) and Quantization-Aware Training (QAT). PTQ ( Dettmers et al., 2022 ; Frantar et al., 2023 ; Xiao et al., 2023 ; Lin et al., 2024 ) converts pretrained models to low-bit representations for efficient inference, typically using a small calibration dataset. QAT ( Jacob et al., 2018 ; Nagel et al., 2021 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) instead simulates quantization during training so that parameters adapt to rounding error. Fake-quantization QAT retains higher-precision GEMMs and adds quantization overhead, rather than directly accelerating training. Both primarily target deployment efficiency. PACT uses offline calibration to prepare frozen weights, then executes base-projection GEMMs directly in NVFP4 during fine-tuning.
Memory-Efficient Edge Training. Lin et al. (2022) restrict trainable parameters to selected layers and channels, enabling on-device training within 256 KB. PockEngine ( Zhu et al., 2023 ) similarly reduces backward computation through sparse updates. FwdLLM ( Xu et al., 2023 ) avoids storing backward activations through backpropagation-free gradient estimation, while MobiZO ( Gao et al., 2025 ) uses parallelized zeroth-order optimization. These approaches reduce training work or change gradient estimation. PACT retains first-order LoRA optimization and targets computation and storage precision. HyC-LoRA ( Wang et al., 2025 ) reduces retained activation memory through hybrid compression, but the computation is still performed in higher-precision.
6 Conclusion
PACT accelerates LoRA fine-tuning with native NVFP4 GEMMs and reduces memory usage by compressing activations retained for backward. Its data flow is designed to reduce quantization and dequantization overhead while limiting quantization error across forward and backward computation. Experiments on a 16 GB RTX 5080 demonstrate faster training and lower memory usage with competitive adaptation quality across language and vision-language tasks. These results support practical model adaptation on memory-constrained consumer GPUs.
Limitations
This work focuses on SLMs in the 1 1 B to 3 3 B range, where activation memory is a major bottleneck for LoRA fine-tuning on memory-constrained edge hardware. Scaling PACT to larger models may introduce additional numerical and systems challenges. PACT currently retains attention computation in BF16. Extending NVFP4 computation to FlashAttention is a direction for future work. Our evaluation also uses a single memory-constrained GPU. Multi-GPU and datacenter settings, where communication, sharding, and pipeline parallelism alter the trade-offs, remain unexplored. Finally, PACT targets transformer decoders with LoRA adapters. Extending its low-precision computation and activation storage to other architectures and adaptation methods remains future work. This work develops an efficiency method for SLM fine-tuning and does not introduce new model capabilities or deployment risks.

Appendix A Comparison of MXFP4 and NVFP4
MXFP4 and NVFP4 pair four-bit E2M1 values with a shared block scale. The E2M1 codebook contains 15 distinct values,
{ 0 , ± 0.5 , ± 1 , ± 1.5 , ± 2 , ± 3 , ± 4 , ± 6 } . \{0,\,\pm 0.5,\,\pm 1,\,\pm 1.5,\,\pm 2,\,\pm 3,\,\pm 4,\,\pm 6\}. (17)
Table 6 compares their block sizes and scale formats. Including the eight-bit block scale, MXFP4 requires 4 + 8 / 32 = 4.25 4+8/32=4.25 bits per element, and NVFP4 requires 4 + 8 / 16 = 4.5 4+8/16=4.5 bits per element. This accounting excludes padding and the additional FP32 tensor scale used for weights and backward gradients in PACT.
Property MXFP4 NVFP4
Block size 32 16
Element format E2M1 E2M1
Block-scale format E8M0 E4M3
Bits per element 4.25 4.50
Table 6: MXFP4 and NVFP4 format parameters, showing that NVFP4 uses smaller groups and more precise block scales at a slightly higher storage cost.
Block size. Sharing a scale among fewer elements limits the number of values affected by an outlier within a block. PACT uses NVFP4’s groups of 16 along the reduction dimension of each GEMM, allowing adjacent groups to use different scales.
Scale precision. E8M0 represents power-of-two scales, while E4M3 provides three mantissa bits within each exponent. NVFP4 therefore permits finer scale adjustment, which PACT accounts for when selecting weight block scales. NVFP4 activation storage is approximately 4.5 / 16 = 28 % 4.5/16=28\% of BF16 before padding and auxiliary statistics.

The quantized pre-attention RMSNorm output is shared by the Q/K/V projections, the Q/V adapters, and RMSNorm backward. The MLP retains its quantized RMSNorm output and gate/up copies for the nonlinear backward kernels. The down-projection input can be released after forward because the frozen down projection needs only its weight and output gradient during backward. The quantized input to the frozen attention output projection can also be released after forward, although the BF16 attention output remains part of the attention backward state. These lifetimes explain the retained-state terms in Equation 12 .
B.2 Quantizer and Weight Preparation
Weight scales. For a frozen weight 𝐖 0 \mathbf{W}_{0} , PACT uses the FP32 tensor scale s W = max ⁡ | 𝐖 0 | / ( 448 ⋅ 6 ) s_{W}=\max|\mathbf{W}_{0}|/(448\cdot 6) , with a positive floor for zero-valued inputs. Each row is partitioned into groups of 16 along the forward reduction dimension. The scale search in Section 3.3 evaluates nine factors: 1.5, 1.25, 1.0, 0.95, 0.90, 0.85, 0.80, 0.75, and 0.70. Each candidate scale is rounded to UE4M3 before measuring reconstruction error. The selected group scale remains fixed during subsequent column quantization and error compensation.
Calibration settings. GPTQ uses processing blocks of 128 columns, groups of 16 columns, and diagonal damping equal to 1% of the mean Hessian diagonal. Projections are processed in the order Q/K/V, attention output, gate/up, and down. Text calibration uses 128 sequences of length 512 sampled from the WikiText-2 training split with seed zero. The input Hessian is accumulated from reconstructed NVFP4 activations, so weight calibration uses the activation precision of the forward GEMMs. ScienceQA image-prompt calibration uses 128 training prompts containing images. Weight preparation is performed once and cached for subsequent fine-tuning runs.
Backward layout. PACT reconstructs the calibrated forward weight, transposes it, casts it to BF16, and applies round-to-nearest NVFP4 quantization along the new reduction dimension. The resulting representation 𝐖 ~ 0 , b \tilde{\mathbf{W}}_{0,b} has its own tensor and block scales. Both packed layouts are cached and reused throughout fine-tuning. Their reconstructed values may differ because their quantization groups follow different dimensions, so the base input-gradient GEMM approximates the backward pass of the forward weight representation.
Activation and gradient scales. The fused RMSNorm and SiLU-multiply forward kernels use block scales without an additional tensor scale in the default configuration. Before an input-gradient GEMM, the backward quantizer uses
s ∇ Y = max ⁡ | ∇ 𝐘 | 448 ⋅ 6 s_{\nabla Y}=\frac{\max|\nabla\mathbf{Y}|}{448\cdot 6} (18)
with a positive floor, followed by UE4M3 scales for groups of 16 values. The GEMM epilogue applies the product of the gradient and weight tensor scales. Keeping these scales on the device avoids a host-side scalar read during each GEMM. Tensor scaling adapts the quantizer to the gradient range, although sufficiently small values can still round to zero.

Appendix C Training and Evaluation Protocol
Compute budget. We estimate the total computational cost of this study at approximately 200 GPU hours on an RTX 5080, including calibration, hyperparameter search, training, evaluation, and profiling.
C.1 Task Formulation and Prompts
We formulate fine-tuning as conditional generation and compute next-token cross-entropy only on the target tokens. Table 8 summarizes the input and target structures. Classification uses short verbalized labels, while COPA and WinoGrande use candidate continuations. Generation tasks use an answer, reasoning solution, or summary as the target. Prompt and padding positions are masked out of the loss.

C.2 Classification and Multiple Choice
The main classification protocol uses rank-16 adapters on Q/V projections with adapter scaling equal to one. Fine-tuning uses AdamW and a constant learning rate selected from the sweep in Section 4.1 . Only adapter parameters are updated. Dataset order and subsampling follow seeds 42, 43, and 44. We first average the scores for a learning rate across seeds, then select the best mean for each task and method. The Avg. column of Table 2 weights the nine tasks equally, and zero-shot scores are reported before adaptation. For label-based tasks, evaluation selects the most likely candidate answer token. COPA and WinoGrande compare the mean log-probability of target tokens for each candidate continuation, with paired candidates kept together.
C.3 Open-Ended Generation
Generation runs use 500 optimizer steps, up to 4,000 training examples, and 500 evaluation examples per seed. We select the learning rate with the highest mean score among rates with completed runs for all three seeds, using candidates 10 − 4 10^{-4} and 5 × 10 − 4 5\times 10^{-4} . Evaluation is greedy, with no sampling and one beam. SQuAD generates at most 50 tokens and is scored with normalized token F1. GSM8K generates at most 320 tokens and uses exact match of the extracted final number. SAMSum generates at most 80 tokens and uses ROUGE-L F1. Generation stops at the configured newline or end-of-sequence token.
C.4 Vision-Language Adaptation
ScienceQA uses image-bearing training and validation examples and evaluates answer-letter accuracy on 1,000 examples. The image encoder and merger remain frozen, and adaptation changes only the decoder adapters. PACT uses micro-batch size 4 with four accumulation steps, while BF16 uses micro-batch size 2 with eight accumulation steps, giving the same effective batch size of 16. Runs train for 500 steps. Text calibration is evaluated with learning rates 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} , 10 − 4 10^{-4} , and 5 × 10 − 4 5\times 10^{-4} . The image-prompt calibration result averages three runs at 5 × 10 − 4 5\times 10^{-4} . Calibration prompts come from the training split.
