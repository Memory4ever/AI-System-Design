Abstract
Many modern language models have adopted hybrid architectures that incorporate both linear-time recurrence and softmax attention. Modern recurrence layers provide a highly expressive mixing mechanism while ensuring high throughput by compressing contextual information into a fixed-size state, but underperform softmax attention in contextual information retrieval. In this paper, we reveal a Competing Learning phenomenon where different kinds of mixers compete to learn the same function, and when any layer learns a function, it suppresses the corresponding learning signal for other layers. Layer attribution analysis reveals that under typical data distributions, modern recurrence learns retrieval functions faster than softmax attention, causing attention to fail to learn retrieval in most cases. Stopping weight updates in recurrence in the middle of training helps attention learn retrieval. Based on these results, we propose a staged training procedure for pre-training hybrid architectures on natural language. Empirical results on models up to 1B parameters show that our method results in substantial improvement in language modeling loss, common-sense reasoning, as well as a wide range of retrieval-related tasks across different context lengths. 1 1

1 Introduction
Recently, many open-weight large language models (LLMs) have adopted hybrid architectures that combine two distinct types of token mixers: linear recurrence (e.g., state space models (SSMs) and linear attention) and softmax attention ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) . Such models have demonstrated strong language modeling capabilities while offering improved training and inference efficiency, scaling to models with up to trillions of parameters. Representative examples include Kimi K3 ( Team Kimi et al., 2026 ) , GLM-5.3-Flash ( GLM Team, 2026 ) , Qwen3.8-Flash-Next ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) , and Nemotron-3-Ultra ( NVIDIA Team, 2026 ) .
Prior work has shown that, in well-trained LLMs, different token mixers—and/or different heads within the same mixer—can specialize in distinct mixing patterns. For instance, Xiao et al. (2025) show that a subset of attention heads in Transformer models ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) , referred to as retrieval heads , learn long-distance mixing patterns, whereas other heads primarily capture short-distance interactions. Similarly, Bick et al. (2025) show that different heads within a linear recurrence layer exhibit retrieval behaviors over different distances. In hybrid architectures, Chen et al. (2026) further show that assigning long-distance retrieval to softmax attention and short-distance mixing to recurrent layers is beneficial across a wide range of downstream tasks. Together, these findings motivate a division of labor in hybrid models, where softmax attention specializes in long-distance mixing while recurrent layers specialize in short-distance mixing.
In this work, we identify Competing Learning , a phenomenon in which layers that acquire retrieval capabilities earlier inhibit the development of these capabilities in other layers. In the hybrid models we study, recurrent layers tend to gain this early advantage, taking over retrieval-related functions that softmax attention layers are intended to specialize in. Consequently, training hybrid models using standard practices results in softmax attention’s retrieval learning being limited by recurrent layers, leading to suboptimal division of labor.

Concretely, we provide an analysis based on layer-wise contribution attribution on synthetic retrieval tasks to identify the speed of retrieval learning of different mixers in a hybrid model. The analysis provides evidence to illustrate how the learning of retrieval functions in softmax attention is inhibited by the retrieval learning of recurrent layers. Furthermore, manually stopping recurrent layers from learning retrieval at different points in the training process reveals that softmax attention starts learning retrieval as soon as recurrent layers’ learning is stopped. We also discover that under typical training distributions, with more short-distance patterns than long-distance ones, recurrence learns retrieval faster than softmax attention, resulting in suboptimal division of labor.
To address this issue, we propose Better Layer Division (BLD), a training pipeline that introduces a preliminary training stage before standard pretraining to give full-attention layers an early advantage in learning retrieval. This intervention is designed to steer Competing Learning in favor of full-attention layers, encouraging them to assume the long-range retrieval role during subsequent pretraining. We evaluate BLD on widely used benchmarks (e.g. Common-sense reasoning (CSR), recall-intensive tasks, LongPPL ( Fang et al., 2025 ) .) using hybrid models based on Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) 2 2

Widely-used RNN architecture adopted by frontier models (e.g. Kimi K3, GLM-5.3-Flash). ( Kimi Team et al., 2025 ) at multiple scales, and observe improvements in benchmarks including CSR, recall-intensive tasks, RULER, LongPPL and training loss over vanilla ones.
Our main contributions are as follows:
•
We investigate the training dynamics of layer-wise functional specialization in hybrid models, focusing on the allocation of retrieval-related functions between recurrent and full-attention layers.
•
We identify and characterize Competing Learning through empirical analyses, revealing how early differences in retrieval learning influence subsequent layer specialization.
•
We propose BLD, an LLM training pipeline that guides functional specialization by freezing the parameters of selected components during the early stage of pretraining, and verify its effectiveness on hybrid models at multiple scales and with different recurrent architectures. Empirical results show that BLD produces pretrained LLMs with performance gains on a wide range of tasks.
2 Related Work
Hybrid Architectures
Recent LLMs increasingly adopt hybrid architectures that combine softmax attention with linear attention, including GLM-5.3-Flash ( GLM Team, 2026 ) , Kimi K3 ( Team Kimi et al., 2026 ) , Qwen3.8-Flash-Next ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) , and Nemotron-3-Ultra ( NVIDIA Team, 2026 ) . Such designs combine the retrieval strength of softmax attention with the computational efficiency of linear attention. Prior work has shown that linear or recurrent models can underperform softmax attention on precise in-context retrieval ( Arora et al., 2024 ; Bick et al., 2025 ; Jelassi et al., 2024 ) . Several works improve the memory or retrieval capabilities of linear attention through architectural or state-space enhancements ( Yang et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2025b ; Qin et al., 2024 ; Shen et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, our work does not enhance the retrieval capacity of linear attention itself, but improves the pre-training of hybrid models without architectural modifications. Hybrid architectures instead retain softmax-attention layers for long-range retrieval while using efficient linear attention for most computation ( Kimi Team et al., 2025 ) . Our work focuses on better realizing this architectural complementarity during pre-training. Most of our experiments are conducted on Kimi-Linear-style models based on Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) ( Kimi Team et al., 2025 ) .
Labor Division in Non-Hybrid Models
Prior work has shown that only a small subset of attention heads develops strong long-context retrieval behavior ( Wu et al., 2025 ) . DuoAttention exploits this functional specialization by distinguishing retrieval heads from streaming heads for efficient inference ( Xiao et al., 2025 ) . Other works leverage already-formed retrieval heads to construct post-training signals for improving long-context performance and contextual faithfulness ( Huang et al., 2025 ; Ma and Okazaki, 2026 ) . Recent studies further analyze how retrieval heads are learned or evolve during context extension and long-context continual pre-training ( Zhao et al., 2024 ; Liang et al., 2026 ) . These works mainly study or exploit specialization among homogeneous softmax-attention heads, rather than the allocation of retrieval capability across heterogeneous attention components. In hybrid models, however, such allocation is critical: if all retrieval heads emerge in linear-attention components, the intended architectural advantage of combining efficient linear attention with the stronger retrieval capability of softmax attention would be largely undermined.
Labor Division in Hybrid Models
Prior work has observed functional specialization between attention and recurrent components in hybrid models, with attention playing a particularly important role in exact retrieval and recall ( Bick et al., 2025 ; Afendulev et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . These works mainly characterize specialization after it has emerged, while other studies exploit such specialization to construct hybrid models from pretrained Transformers ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Bick et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, we study how this division of labor forms during hybrid-model pre-training and how it can be actively optimized. In the work most closely related to ours, Qiao et al. (2026) show that efficient-attention modules can weaken the optimization pressure for full-attention layers and delay retrieval-head formation. Our work further identifies the underlying competition in retrieval-specific learning signals: linear-attention and softmax-attention layers both gradually acquire retrieval capabilities, and layers that learn retrieval earlier reduce the retrieval-specific prediction error, thereby weakening the learning signal available to other layers. We refer to this as the Competing Learning phenomenon and use it to guide pre-training toward stronger retrieval specialization in softmax-attention layers.
3 Competing Learning in Hybrid Attention Models
Central to our paper is the Competing Learning phenomenon, which is stated as follows:
Competing Learning Phenomenon
During hybrid-model pre-training, both recurrent and
softmax-attention layers can acquire in-context retrieval
capabilities. If some layers learn retrieval earlier, they can reduce the retrieval-specific prediction error, weakning the training signal for other layers, slowing their
acquisition of the same capability.
To show the causes and effects of this phenomenon, we conduct a series of layer attribution and gradient intervention experiments to monitor the retrieval learning of each layer in a hybrid model. In the following sections, we first propose a synthetic retrieval task (Section 3.1 ). Then, we propose a novel metric for determining how much a layer contributes to the model’s retrieval abilities (Section 3.2 ). Finally, we train a series of hybrid models on this task and inspect the contributions of each layer to monitor their retrieval learning over time (Section 3.3 ).
Concretely, we conduct the following three sets of experiments:
•
Section 3.3.1 : We provide the experimental evidence for the Competing Learning phenomenon with weight freezing.
•
Section 3.3.2 : We provide direct gradient evidence for the Competing Learning phenomenon through weight freezing and unfreezing.
•
Section 3.3.3 : We show how the distribution of short and long-distance patterns chooses the winner of Competing Learning phenomenon.
Altogether, we demonstrate the existence of Competing Learning in hybrid models and that KDA layers learn modeling short-distance patterns faster than attention layers. Since short-distance patterns are more frequent than long-distance patterns in natural language pretraining ( Futrell et al., 2015 ) , this leads to a suboptimal labor division in direct pretraining of hybrid models.

3.3 Analysis Experiments
With the above task and estimation method, we can now perform a series of experiments to analyze the phenomenon of Competing Learning for hybrid models, which is central to our paper.
Experimental Configuration
We train several hybrid models on the Spaced Retrieval task. Each model has 3.6M parameters and consists of two KDA (a widely-used linear recurrence architecture) layers and two attention layers. Unless specified otherwise, we use 1:1 mixtures of examples with g = 64 g=64 and g = 8192 g=8192 . During training, we only compute the loss on output tokens using loss masks. Here, we report the results for g = 8192 g=8192 , but we emphasize that the results of g = 64 g=64 are similar. The results for g = 64 g=64 and more experimental details can be found in Appendix A .
3.3.1 Suppressing Learning in One Token Mixer Stimulates Learning in Another

in a hybrid model trained on Spaced Retrieval.
Figure 3: The retrieval ability of the two different kinds of layer
in a hybrid model, trained on Spaced Retrieval with different data mixtures.
Figure 4: Demonstration of Competing Learning. The curves indicate the retrieval ability of attention layers in a hybrid model consisting of attention and recurrent layers (KDA), trained on our retrieval task, Spaced Retrieval (Section 3.1 ).
Without intervention, KDA layers become dominant in terms of retrieval ability in this setting, as shown in Figure 3 . To investigate how their dominance affects the attention layers, we freeze all KDA layers at different training steps and continue training the remaining model components. As shown in the left part of Figure 4 , the attention layers’ retrieval ability increases sharply immediately after each freezing intervention. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that, once KDA layers acquire some retrieval capability, they suppress the acquisition of the same capability by attention layers, providing evidence for the Competing Learning phenomenon.
Furthermore, we explore how unfreezing KDA layers at a later training step influences learning competition. In the right plot of Figure 4 , runs in which KDA is frozen earlier than step 125 have higher retrieval ability than when the KDA remains frozen for the rest of the training, while runs in which KDA is frozen later than step 125 have lower retrieval ability than when the KDA remains frozen. Moreover, these changes appear almost immediately after KDA is unfrozen. This clear polarization may be attributed to the fact that attention layers in checkpoints where freezing began earlier than step 125 have enough retrieval advantages by freezing KDA layers long enough.
At these early freezing checkpoints, attention layers have already accumulated a sufficient advantage in retrieval performance, substantially outperforming KDA layers. Consequently, once freezing is lifted, attention layers tend to further reinforce their retrieval capabilities, while KDA layers are driven to specialize in other functions. This is reflected in Figure 4 : at checkpoints where attention layers have established a sufficiently strong advantage, their retrieval ability further improves after unfreezing. In contrast, at checkpoints where this advantage is insufficient, attention layers are once again outcompeted by KDA layers, leading to a decline in their retrieval ability.
Takeaways
In hybrid models, KDA and attention layers compete to learn retrieval, with KDA initially gaining an advantage due to its stronger inductive bias toward short-range patterns. Temporarily freezing the dominant KDA layers can shift—and, with sufficient duration, even reverse—the winner of this competition.
3.3.2 Gradient Evidence of Competing Learning
Figure 5: Relative gradient magnitude changes in the Attention layers compared to the baseline without KDA freezing. KDA is frozen at steps 100, 150, or 175, with an unfreezing branch introduced 50 steps later. Across these timings, keeping KDA frozen progressively amplifies attention gradient norms relative to the baseline, whereas unfreezing KDA substantially attenuates this amplification.
We further investigate how the freezing intervention affects gradient dynamics. As shown in Figure 5 , the gradient norm of the attention layers in the frozen checkpoint reaches approximately 10 × 10\times that of the baseline checkpoint, which has never undergone freezing. Moreover, the gap between the dotted and solid curves indicates that unfreezing the KDA layers mitigates this gradient amplification. Notably, the gradient norm increases progressively rather than abruptly, suggesting that this amplification emerges from the evolving optimization dynamics rather than as an immediate consequence of freezing. This observation further supports Competing Learning, suggesting a self-reinforcing optimization mechanism in which the current parameter configuration determines the allocation of learning signals, progressively amplifying the advantages of already dominant components. See Appendix I.1 for more details.
Takeaways
Freezing KDA progressively amplifies attention layers’ gradients, while unfreezing mitigates this effect. This supports Competing Learning: learning signals depend on the current ability gap of components, reinforcing the advantages of already dominant components.
3.3.3 Different Training Data Prefer Different Architectures

Figure 7: The retrieval accuracy when masking a fraction of the heads.
We further investigate how the distribution of gap ( g ) (g) in the training data influences the learnability of retrieval in different mixers.
To this end, we train the hybrid model using different mixture proportions of short-distance ( g = 64 g=64 ) and long-distance ( g = 8192 g=8192 ) examples. As shown in Figure 3 , attention layers become more important as the proportion of long-distance examples increases, whereas KDA layers are more likely to dominate when the long-distance patterns do not appear overwhelmingly more than short-distance patterns. This is because KDA initializes forget gates with low values, which gives this architecture a short-distance inductive bias (See Appendix B for a more specific explanation).
Takeaways
Different mixers learn retrieval at different speeds, depending on the data distribution. While both mixers can acquire the same retrieval abilities, the mixer that learns faster will become dominant, limiting the learning of the other mixer.
Implications
Applying the traditional training recipe to a hybrid model can lead to a suboptimal division of labor. Because short-distance patterns occur more frequently than long-distance patterns in natural language ( Futrell et al., 2015 ) , the training objective may encourage KDA to dominate retrieval, suppressing the training of attention layers. This issue is less problematic in homogeneous architectures such as Transformers, where all layers share the same architecture and, consequently, have the same potential for retrieval.
4 LLM Pretraining Experiments
In this section, we show that we can leverage insights about Competing Learning to achieve improved LLM performance in natural language pre-training.
4.1 Method: Better Layer Division
Based on the analysis results in Section 3.3.1 , we propose Better Layer Division (BLD), which freezes RNN layers’ weights during the early stage of pretraining, thereby giving the attention layers a first-mover advantage in Competing Learning. Importantly, this intervention establishes a corrected division of labor between the two components, which is subsequently preserved and further reinforced throughout the remainder of pretraining. This better labor division translates to improved model performance.
4.2 Experimental Details
Model Configuration
We train a family of KDA–attention hybrid models ranging from
180M to 1B total parameters, including embeddings.
All models share a repeating four-layer pattern consisting of
one attention layer followed by three KDA layers, maintaining
a fixed 3:1 KDA-to-attention ratio across scales.
The layers are stacked in the form of “Attention-KDA-KDA-KDA”, and all layers are using NoPE (not adopting RoPE, which is reported to be better than using RoPE in KDA-based hybrid architecture ( Kimi Team et al., 2025 ) ).
We scale the models by varying their depth and hidden width.
At each scale, BLD and vanilla pre-training use identical
architectures.
Detailed model configurations are provided in
Appendix C .

We compare BLD against vanilla pre-training by training
models from scratch with token budgets scaled proportionally
to their non-embedding parameter counts, following the
Chinchilla scaling principle (training tokens are roughly 20 × \times of parameters) ( Hoffmann et al., 2022 ) .
All models are trained on DCLM ( Li et al., 2024 ) , a high-quality open-source pre-training corpus,
using the Qwen3 tokenizer ( Yang et al., 2025a ) and a context length of 16,384 tokens.
For BLD, the KDA blocks remain frozen for approximately 1 / 10 1/10 of the subsequently unfrozen pretraining part.
The vanilla baseline updates all parameters,
using the same training data, token budgets, and optimization

We use AdamW during the initial portion of pre-training,

4.3 Main Results
Downstream Tasks
As shown in Table 1 , BLD models achieve notably lower LM losses. They also show a modest improvement in CSR performance, while exhibiting substantial gains on retrieval-oriented tasks, including Recall and RULER. Moreover, the PPL metrics, which reflect overall long-context modeling capability, consistently favor BLD by a large margin. The specific subtask scores are included in Appendix F .
Context Utilization
We evaluate the long-context utilization of our models as Sun et al. (2024) by computing LM loss as a function of context length. Specifically, we calculated next-token prediction loss on Books3,BookCorpus2 ( Gao et al., 2020 ) as a function of token positions, up to 16K. Figure 7 shows that BLD model has a lower loss especially in the later position, highlighting better context utilization abilities by BLD models. The results of all model sizes and other details are included in Appendix G .
Scaling Laws
We also fit the loss as a power-law function of model size to verify that BLD is scalable to larger model scales (shown in Figure 8 ), and the fitted curves suggest that BLD retains its loss advantage well beyond the evaluated model scales.

Takeaways
The advantages of BLD, lower training loss and better downstream task performance, are transferable to other linear recurrence architectures.
4.5 Evidence for Labor Division Change in Attention Scores
Figure 9: The α \alpha values of attention heads. With GQA, query heads 0–3 and 4–7 share a KV head within each group and therefore share the same KV-head-level α \alpha value. Greater α \alpha values indicate heads that are important for long-distance retrieval.
We further provide direct evidence that BLD induces stronger functional differentiation. Specifically, following Xiao et al. (2025) , we analyze 510M KDA-attention hybrid models by training α \alpha values to distinguish retrieval heads , which are responsible for long-distance information retrieval, from streaming heads , which primarily perform short-distance token mixing.
α \alpha measures a head’s reliance on full-context attention, with larger values indicating greater reliance. In other words, heads with greater α \alpha values are more important for long-distance retrieval. The α \alpha values are trained for 1,000 steps. More experimental details and results are reported in Appendix I .
As shown in Figure 9 , the attention heads in the BLD model exhibit higher α \alpha values than those in the vanilla model, indicating that BLD contains more retrieval heads that attend to long-range tokens. Figure 7 reports model’s retrieval accuracy as attention heads are progressively masked in ascending order of their α \alpha values. For BLD, the accuracy remains largely unchanged as low- α \alpha heads are masked and drops sharply only when the final three heads, which have the highest α \alpha values, are removed. This suggests that these three heads play a critical role in retrieval, and we therefore identify them as retrieval heads. In contrast, the vanilla model exhibits a substantial accuracy drop only when its single highest- α \alpha head is masked, indicating that it contains only one retrieval head.
Takeaways
BLD promotes a greater degree of functional specialization, with more attention heads specializing in long-range retrieval. This behavior aligns with the motivation underlying our method.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we first identify the Competing Learning phenomenon and provide supporting experimental evidence. We then show that directly pretraining hybrid models can lead to a suboptimal division of labor among different architectural components. To address this issue, we propose BLD and validate its effectiveness through scalable experiments. By promoting a more effective division of labor, BLD consistently improves model capabilities across a wide range of dimensions.
Nevertheless, BLD represents only a preliminary approach to addressing the Competing Learning phenomenon in the training of hybrid models. We believe that a deeper understanding of the Competing Learning phenomenon will provide further insights into the division of labor within hybrid architectures and inspire the development of more advanced neural architectures and training strategies that better account for this phenomenon and achieve stronger performance.

Appendix E Evaluation Details
E.1 Checkpoint and metric selection
We evaluate the final pretraining checkpoint without fine-tuning, using zero
demonstrations for the downstream language-model evaluation tasks. The decoding strategy for generation tasks is greedy decoding.
Language Modeling Loss
We report the mean training loss over the final 100 optimizer updates.
Common-Sense Reasoning
CSR is the macro average of seven tasks: ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge ( Clark et al., 2018 ) ,
HellaSwag ( Zellers et al., 2019 ) , and PIQA ( Bisk et al., 2020 ) use normalized
accuracy; BoolQ ( Clark et al., 2019 ) , WinoGrande ( Sakaguchi et al., 2019 ) , and LAMBADA ( Paperno et al., 2016 ) use accuracy.
Recall
We use the Based evaluation suite ( Arora et al., 2024 ) and average the
contains scores over SWDE ( Lockard et al., 2019 ) ,
FDA ( Arora et al., 2023 ) , SQuAD ( Rajpurkar et al., 2018 ) ,
TriviaQA ( Joshi et al., 2017 ) , DROP ( Dua et al., 2019 ) , and
Natural Questions ( Kwiatkowski et al., 2019 ) with a 2,048-token context.
RULER
We evaluate RULER ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) at 1K, 2K, 4K, 8K, and 16K tokens, with 100 examples per
task–length combination. These lengths include 10, 11, 12, 12, and 12 tasks,
respectively; QA-1 is included from 2K and QA-2 from 4K. The overall score
averages all 57 task–length combinations.
CSR, Recall, and RULER scores are reported as percentages.
WikiText-2
We report word-level perplexity on WikiText-2 ( Merity et al., 2016 ) using the evaluator’s
word_perplexity metric.
LongPPL
Following LongPPL ( Fang et al., 2025 ) , we report pooled perplexity over tokens selected using fixed reference-model

Appendix I Retrieval Head Identification and Retrieval Evaluation
Retrieval Head identification.
We apply a DuoAttention-style retrieval head identification procedure ( Xiao et al., 2025 ) to the final 510M
Vanilla and BLD checkpoints. Each model has six attention layers, with two
KV heads and eight query heads per layer. We learn one gate
α ℓ , g ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \alpha_{\ell,g}\in[0,1] per KV head (12 gates in total), shared by its four

I.1 Details of the Gradient Dynamics Experiment
Figure 5 compares a never-frozen
baseline with a KDA-freezing intervention under the
settings in Table 24 .
At the branching point, the frozen trajectory is split
into continued-freezing and unfreezing branches with
identical model, optimizer, scheduler.
Unfreezing resumes the paused KDA Adam states without
resetting the optimizer. Within each seed, all compared
branches follow the same training-data sequence.
Training uses full backward passes and the same
step-specific baseline clipping coefficient before
frozen updates are masked.
We measure unclipped attention gradients using the same
gradient-enabled diagnostic graph across conditions.
For each attention mixer, we average the per-example
gradient vectors before taking their ℓ 2 \ell_{2} norm,
then divide by the corresponding baseline norm at the
same step. The plotted statistic is the arithmetic
mean of the two attention mixers’ trajectories, each
computed as the geometric mean of paired ratios across
seeds. The left panel enlarges the freezing phase of
the full trajectory shown on the right.
