First-Order LoRA Training with Phone NPUs
Xu Zheng 1 Wei Cheng 2 Zhuomin Chen 1 Dongkuan Xu 3 Mo Sha 1 Jingchao Ni 4 Dongsheng Luo 5
† † footnotetext: 1 Florida International University, Miami, FL, USA 2 NEC Laboratories America, Princeton, NJ, USA 3 North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC, USA 4 University of Houston, Houston, TX, USA 5 Singapore Management University, Singapore. Correspondence to: Dongsheng Luo <luodongsheng01@gmail.com>. Preprint. Abstract On-device fine-tuning lets a large language model (LLM) learn from its user’s private data without uploading it. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) reduces the trainable parameters, but each first-order training step still multiplies by the frozen base-model weights during the forward pass, activation recomputation, and input-gradient computation. A phone’s neural processing unit (NPU) already holds these weights for inference; using it for training also requires computing input gradients, which its inference interface does not support. We present a system that runs all three frozen-weight products on the NPU while keeping the training graph, adapters, and optimizer on the CPU. Its input-gradient kernels reuse the resident weight tiles and the forward dequantizer, avoiding a transposed weight copy and ensuring forward and backward use the same effective weights. To preserve usable gradients, the system selects precision by gradient sensitivity and applies a per-row range guard for half-precision (FP16) outputs. Its runtime fuses the remaining CPU operators and overlaps NPU execution with adapter computation, while resumable execution and thermal pacing let training proceed as a background job. We evaluate it on two Qualcomm Snapdragon platforms using four LLMs from the Qwen and Llama families. Our workloads cover nightly personalization with labels supplied on the phone and the public LaMP-2 benchmark; comparisons include an optimized CPU trainer, a phone-GPU trainer, forward-only training, and GPU-trained references.
1 Introduction
Phones now run large language models (LLMs) locally, and deployed assistants specialize a shared base model with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) Gunter et al. (2024) . Such a model serves its user better once it learns from that user’s private examples, which accumulate on the phone. Training where the data lives avoids uploading it and leaves room for federated aggregation McMahan et al. (2017) . Making on-device adaptation practical requires training to fit within the phone’s compute, memory, and thermal limits.
LoRA reduces training memory by freezing the base model and updating only small adapters Hu et al. (2022) . However, the frozen layers still do much of the computation. Each frozen projection uses its weights W W three times per training step: once in the forward pass, once to recompute activations during checkpointing Chen et al. (2016) , and once to compute the input gradient d ​ Y ​ W \mathrm{d}YW during backpropagation. We call these three matrix multiplications the frozen path; all three read the same weights (Figure 1 b). Even with LoRA, each training step still requires all three frozen-path multiplications (Section 2.1 ).
The phone’s neural processing unit (NPU) is built for these matrix products and already serves on-device LLM inference Xu et al. (2025) . Its inference engine computes the first two products from resident weight tiles, and the third is the same product with the operand roles exchanged. The software stack, however, exposes only inference: forward products with resident, quantized weights and half-precision (FP16) outputs. Existing routes give up backpropagation, the NPU, or model scale. Forward-only methods run on inference engines because they estimate gradients from perturbed forward passes Xu et al. (2024) ; Gao et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2026) ; Malladi et al. (2023) . First-order LLM fine-tuning has run on an embedded GPU Zhu et al. (2023) and phone GPUs Tether Data (2026a) , leaving the NPU idle. Mandheling Xu et al. (2022) and HexaTrain yuubinnkyoku (2026) train on mobile digital signal processors (DSPs) and NPUs, but only networks far smaller than billion-parameter LLMs. If the NPU also computes d ​ Y ​ W \mathrm{d}YW from its resident tiles, the frozen path can run there, while the small adapter and its optimizer state stay on the CPU (Figure 1 c).

Building a trainer around this idea presents three challenges. Direction: inference kernels and weight layouts serve X ​ W ⊤ XW^{\top} , whereas backward traverses the other dimension; a separately quantized transpose would add resident storage and change the effective weights the gradient sees. Precision and range: in the engine’s FP16 results, small gradients underflow and partial sums can overflow, and inference weight formats target outputs, not gradients. In models that interleave recurrent Gated DeltaNet (GDN) layers with attention, such as Qwen3.5 Qwen Team (2026) ; Yang et al. (2025b) , quantization error in a recurrent projection enters a state that every later token reuses. System cost: once the NPU runs the frozen path, the remaining CPU operators and the CPU–NPU boundary limit each step, and an update runs for hours on a phone whose memory is shared, whose temperature rises, and which its user continues to use.
We present a system that trains an on-device LLM with first-order LoRA and runs the frozen path on the NPU’s inference engine. Its input-gradient kernels read resident FP16 tiles and 8-bit block-quantized (Q8) tiles, which store each block of 32 weights as 8-bit integers with one FP16 scale; for Q8, they exchange the matrix engine’s operand roles so the forward dequantizer yields W ⊤ W^{\top} . To keep gradients usable, a gradient-sensitive policy keeps recurrent projections in FP16 and activations in floating point, and a range guard scales each gradient row by a power of two, with overflow flags and bounded retries. On the CPU side, fused operators shrink residual work, asynchronous requests overlap NPU products with adapter gradients, and the job commits at optimizer-step boundaries to yield to the user, pause under thermal limits, and resume. Our contributions are:
• A split of first-order LoRA training between a CPU training graph and a resident NPU service that runs the frozen path from the inference weight tiles (Sections 3 and 6 ).
• Frozen-path backpropagation on an inference NPU: input-gradient kernels that reuse the resident weights and the forward dequantizer, a gradient-sensitive precision policy, and a per-row range guard (Section 4 ).
• A CPU–NPU training runtime for hours-long background jobs: fused residual operators, asynchronous NPU requests, a checkpoint budget, and step-boundary commit, yield, and thermal pacing (Section 5 ).
• An evaluation on two Qualcomm Snapdragon platforms (8 Elite and 8 Elite Gen 5, with Hexagon V79 and V81 NPUs) with four LLMs from two model families, on a nightly personalization update with on-phone labels and on LaMP-2 Salemi et al. (2024) , against an optimized CPU trainer, a phone-GPU trainer, forward-only training, and GPU-trained references (Section 7 ).
2 Background and Motivation
2.1 Frozen-Weight Computation
For a frozen projection W ∈ ℝ n out × n in W\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rm out}\times n_{\rm in}} and tokens X ∈ ℝ M × n in X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times n_{\rm in}} , a LoRA layer Hu et al. (2022) computes
Y = X ​ W ⊤ + s ⁡ ( X ​ A ⊤ ) ​ B ⊤ , Y=XW^{\top}+s(XA^{\top})B^{\top}, (1)
with adapters A ∈ ℝ r × n in A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n_{\rm in}} and B ∈ ℝ n out × r B\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rm out}\times r} and scale s s . Its base input gradient is
d ​ X base = d ​ Y ​ W . \mathrm{d}X_{\rm base}=\mathrm{d}YW. (2)
Freezing W W removes its parameter gradient and optimizer state, but not its products: X ​ W ⊤ XW^{\top} in forward, again when checkpointing recomputes the layer Chen et al. (2016) , and d ​ Y ​ W \mathrm{d}YW for the input gradient. Each costs n in ​ n out n_{\rm in}n_{\rm out} multiply-adds per token, against r ⁡ ( n in + n out ) r(n_{\rm in}+n_{\rm out}) for the adapter branch, with r r far below both (Section 7.1 ). Because the frozen path dominates the arithmetic, a projection-level interface to the NPU suffices; the training graph can stay on the CPU.
2.2 On-Device Training Bottleneck
We profile a CPU LoRA trainer on an older phone, P1 (Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite, Hexagon V79 NPU), and a newer one, P2 (Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5, Hexagon V81 NPU), both detailed in Section 7.1 . Even after basic CPU fixes, the frozen path takes most of the self CPU time of one training example on both phones (Figure 2 a and Section 7.3 ).
Figure 2: The frozen path dominates CPU training, and inference precision is not gradient precision. (a) Self CPU time of a training example on P2 with and without the frozen path (replaced by zeros), and the remaining time by operator; (b) LoRA-gradient error against FP32 by frozen-weight format, where + keeps the gate or the recurrent (GDN) projections of Q8 in FP16 (dark blue: our system’s format; dashed: the error with BF16 weights).
The NPU is built for this work: at the same forward shape, its matrix engine, Hexagon Matrix Extensions (HMX), far outpaces floating-point matrix multiplication on the CPU with Arm’s Scalable Matrix Extension. Effective offload should also reduce the remaining CPU work: without the frozen path, small operators, casts and copies, adapter products, and GDN recurrence still take a large share of CPU time (Figure 2 a).
2.3 Numerical Precision in Backpropagation
Inference weight formats are not automatically safe for backward. Figure 2 b compares the relative L 2 L_{2} error of concatenated LoRA gradients of Qwen3.5-4B (Q3.5-4B) Qwen Team (2026) under frozen-weight formats against a single-precision (FP32) reference in a harness that emulates the engine’s arithmetic (Appendix A ); its dashed line marks bfloat16 (BF16) weights. Q8, the engine’s inference format, raises the error well above that of FP16 weights, and its 4-bit counterpart (Q4) destroys the gradient. The error concentrates in the recurrent projections: keeping only their gates in FP16 does not help, but restoring only the recurrent projection types to FP16 returns it to the FP16 level (Section 7.4 ). We attribute this to the recurrent GDN state, which carries a weight perturbation into every later token.
2.4 Limitations of the NPU Inference Interface
On both phones’ NPUs, HMX works with Hexagon Vector Extensions (HVX), DMA, and an on-chip vector tightly coupled memory (VTCM). HMX accepts FP16 operands, accumulates in wider cells, and writes FP16 results Qualcomm Technologies (2026) , so an FP32 CPU buffer cannot recover values lost in the engine’s output conversion. The inference interface and its Q8 tiles serve forward products; backward traverses the other dimension, where a separately quantized transpose would add storage and change the effective matrix.
These findings require our system to (R1) reuse the forward weights and their dequantization arithmetic in backward, (R2) keep gradients usable within the engine’s FP16 precision and range, and (R3) shrink and hide the CPU work and CPU–NPU boundary cost that offload exposes, for an hours-long job in the phone’s memory and thermal budget.
3 System Overview
To make first-order LoRA training practical on a phone, the system should accelerate the dominant frozen-weight computation through an inference-only NPU interface, preserve the gradients needed to train the adapters, and sustain execution within the phone’s memory and thermal limits. We propose a CPU–NPU training framework that addresses these requirements by organizing execution around frozen projections. It combines a CPU trainer that owns the training graph and mutable state, a resident NPU service that supplies frozen-weight products in both directions, and a job controller that manages progress and pauses. This division lets forward, activation recomputation, and backpropagation share the NPU’s resident weights throughout an update.
The framework delegates frozen-weight computation to the resident NPU service while the CPU trainer retains control of training. By extending the inference engine to support backpropagation with the same resident weights, the service enables NPU acceleration across the frozen path without an additional weight copy. Numerical safeguards preserve usable gradients, allowing the CPU trainer to learn the adapters from the service’s results. The CPU–NPU runtime organizes the trainer and service into a coordinated training process. It reduces the remaining CPU work and overlaps independent computation across the two processors, helping translate NPU acceleration into faster training steps. It also manages activation memory so training can fit within the phone’s available memory.
The job controller extends this execution model across an hours-long update. At optimizer-step boundaries, when no NPU request is in flight, it saves the CPU-owned training state so execution can resume after a pause or service restart. Foreground use and thermal limits can therefore interrupt training without losing committed progress.

On the NPU, there are two tasks in the frozen-path backpropagation: compute d ​ Y ​ W \mathrm{d}YW from the resident weight tiles and keep it usable under quantized weights and FP16 outputs.
4.1 A projection-level interface
The NPU’s inference API runs forward graphs, but a training graph is dynamic, may contain recurrent layers, and owns an optimizer; porting it to the NPU’s compiler would mean rewriting all three. The service instead exposes one operation per frozen projection with a direction flag: forward ( X ​ W ⊤ XW^{\top} ) or input gradient ( d ​ Y ​ W \mathrm{d}YW , Equation ( 2 )), so the graph and its optimizer never leave the CPU. The client runs the device’s kernel planner, so it knows at startup which projection shapes each direction supports on the NPU. Shapes outside that set, such as gate projections with only a few outputs, run instead in an explicit floating-point CPU implementation that returns complete outputs and gradients.
4.2 Input gradients from resident FP16 weights
Backward traverses the other dimension of W W . A transposed FP16 copy for it would double resident storage, so our FP16 backward kernel reads W W in place and transposes it on chip, one window at a time (Figure 3 a). DMA streams row windows of the resident W W into VTCM, HVX converts each window into the tiles that the transposed product needs, and the output gradient takes the engine’s activation conversion path. The kernel planner chooses reduction segments, window width, and token tiles within the VTCM budget.
Segmenting also decides where the result is rounded. Large projections split the contraction dimension into segments, and each segment yields an FP16 partial result that HVX expands and accumulates in FP32. Because each partial result is rounded before the FP32 sum, later cancellation cannot repair an overflowed partial result, so the range guard detects it before accumulation and resends the row (Section 4.5 ).
4.3 Operand exchange for Q8 tiles
For Q8, backward uses forward’s effective matrix by reusing both the quantized values and the forward dequantizer’s arithmetic. A separately quantized transposed copy would change the values, and even a separate conversion of the same integers changes the effective matrix, because the vector dequantizer can round a quantized value q q with scale d d differently from a conventional FP16 rounding of q ​ d qd . We therefore exchange the two HMX operand roles (Figure 3 b): the dequantized weight tile becomes the activation operand and the output-gradient tile the weight operand, which computes
( d ​ X base ) ⊤ = W ⊤ ​ d ​ Y ⊤ . (\mathrm{d}X_{\rm base})^{\top}=W^{\top}\mathrm{d}Y^{\top}. (3)
The forward dequantizer already produces the FP16 layout the W ⊤ W^{\top} activation tile needs, so only the gradients are transposed: the kernel transposes d ​ Y \mathrm{d}Y in registers into the weight layout and each output tile as it stores d ​ X \mathrm{d}X . The logical transpose of Equation ( 3 ) thus happens at tile boundaries, without a full FP16 expansion or a second quantized model, so backward reads forward’s effective weights (Section 7.4 ).

4.4 A gradient-sensitive precision policy
Reusing the forward dequantizer makes forward and backward use the same effective weights, but it does not fix Q8’s gradient error. Section 2.3 traces that error to projections whose quantization error a recurrent state carries forward, not to gates alone. Keeping every projection in FP16 would increase resident weight storage, so our policy sets precision per projection type from measured gradient sensitivity: FP16 for the sensitive types, such as all recurrent projection types where a model has them, and Q8 for the rest, such as attention and feed-forward (MLP) projections; attention-only models use Q8 throughout. Activations stay in floating point when weights are quantized, separating storage and computation precision as QLoRA does Dettmers et al. (2023) . Because a CPU Q8 path can quantize activations, startup validation keeps every shape off it; unsupported shapes take the floating-point CPU implementation of Section 4.1 .
4.5 Range guard at the request boundary
The engine’s FP16 outputs limit range at both ends: small gradients underflow, and large partial results overflow. A global loss scale Micikevicius et al. (2018) cannot serve rows whose magnitudes differ widely, so the client scales each row by a power of two, which adds no rounding (Figure 3 c). For each finite, nonzero row d ​ Y i \mathrm{d}Y_{i} , the client chooses a factor c i c_{i} that places max j ⁡ | c i ​ d ​ Y i ​ j | \max_{j}|c_{i}\mathrm{d}Y_{ij}| near 2 12 2^{12} ; the service computes the scaled product, and the client divides its FP32 result by c i c_{i} . Since c i c_{i} cannot bound every FP16 partial result, the kernel flags rows whose partial result is nonfinite or reaches 60,000, below FP16’s maximum of 65,504, and the client resends only those rows, scaling c i c_{i} by 2 − 8 2^{-8} per retry, up to three times.
5 A CPU–NPU Training Runtime
The NPU service of Section 4 supplies frozen-path outputs and input gradients; the CPU retains the surrounding computation and training state. The runtime organizes this division into complete training steps by reducing CPU work and scheduling projection requests and activation recomputation within a memory budget. Completed optimizer steps provide boundaries for saving state, allowing training to pause and resume under changing phone conditions.
5.1 Reducing CPU work between NPU requests
GDN recurrence and LoRA branches contain many small operations, and the vocabulary head also remains on the CPU after offload (Figure 2 a). We fuse each into a separate autograd node, keeping frozen projections outside for NPU execution. The GDN node combines causal convolution, SiLU, normalization, gates, delta update, and gated RMS normalization between its projections. It keeps recurrent state in FP32 while preserving BF16 rounding boundaries. Internal state checkpoints every 16 tokens let backward replay each segment forward and differentiate it in reverse. Partitioning by value head and 64-column slices exposes CPU parallelism without retaining per-token intermediates.
Each LoRA node combines rank-16 products, dropout, scaling, and addition. Its separation from the frozen projection allows overlap with NPU execution. With answer-only supervision, the vocabulary head computes logits only at supervised answer positions; we fuse its BF16 matrix path for forward and backward and keep logits in FP32.
5.2 Scheduling training with memory budget
Resident weights, training state, and foreground apps share the phone’s memory, so retaining every layer’s activations can leave insufficient space for backward temporaries. The trainer therefore bounds activation storage by the smaller of a configured cap and currently available memory after reserving space for those temporaries. Layers whose activations cannot be retained within this budget are recomputed through the same NPU projection interface. The resulting schedule follows the training graph’s dependencies and includes the forward requests needed for recomputation.
Within this schedule, the LoRA and frozen branches can execute independently until their results are combined. In backward, the client submits d ​ Y ​ W \mathrm{d}YW , computes adapter gradients, and waits only to add the branches’ input gradients (Figure 4 a). In forward, it submits projections sharing an input ahead of use. The fused LoRA node thus supplies CPU work to overlap with NPU requests.
One request is in flight at a time, and every result, including recomputation results, is consumed exactly once at its intended graph position. After the accumulated microbatches, the CPU updates the adapters with no request outstanding, establishing the boundary for progress saving.
5.3 Pausing and resuming across training steps
To support pauses and restarts, the job saves CPU-owned training state at optimizer-step boundaries, when no NPU request is outstanding. This includes the adapter parameters, optimizer state, etc. On resumption, the trainer restores the last commit and repeats only uncommitted work; a supervisor restarts the NPU service if it has failed (Figure 4 b).
To manage heat and limit interference with foreground use, the trainer can run at lower priority with threads pinned to a subset of cores. A thermal controller monitors Android’s thermal status and battery temperature. Training pauses when either reaches its pause threshold and resumes only when both fall to or below their lower resume thresholds.

6 Implementation
Software stack. The CPU trainer uses PyTorch 2.12.1 Paszke et al. (2019) and CPython 3.12 in user-space Linux. Arm C++ extensions implement fused operators; a custom autograd.Function connects frozen projections to the NPU service. The service extends a modified llama.cpp ggml Hexagon backend ggml-org contributors (2026) ; its Q8_0 and Q4_0 formats correspond to Q8 and Q4. FP16 backward builds on ggml’s OUT_PROD, while added Q8 kernels implement operand exchange.
Startup validation. Client and device share one kernel planner. Startup checks validate supported shapes, token buckets, and directions against a reference, probe determinism and overflow, and exclude ggml’s CPU Q8 path, which quantizes activations. Graphs and tensor metadata are then reused throughout training.
Model integration. Supported models are Q3.5-4B (248 projections), Qwen3.5-2B (Q3.5-2B), Qwen3.5-0.8B ( Q3.5-0.8B ), Qwen3-4B Yang et al. (2025a) (Q3-4B; 252), and Llama-3.2-3B Meta (2024) (L3.2-3B; 196). Smaller Qwen3.5 models run 16-output gates on the CPU with complete forward and backward. We integrate a model by supplying its projection list, checkpoint names, CPU operators for non-projection layers, and precision configuration.
Runtime configuration. Nightly updates use a 3000 MB activation cap and 4000 MB backward reserve. Thermal pacing pauses at Android thermal status 2 (moderate) or battery temperature 40 ∘ C, resuming only at status 1 or below and battery temperature at most 38 ∘ C.
7 Evaluation
We evaluate the system through six research questions:
• RQ1: Can the system complete on-device fine-tuning with task quality comparable to GPU-trained references?
• RQ2: How do NPU offload, CPU fusion, and asynchronous execution contribute to training performance?
• RQ3: How do inference weight formats and FP16 arithmetic affect numerical fidelity?
• RQ4: What energy and memory costs does training incur?
• RQ5: Can training sustain progress under thermal limits and resume correctly after pauses or service failures?
• RQ6: How does the system compare with phone-GPU training and forward-only training methods?
We first describe the experimental setup and then address each question in turn.
7.1 Setup
Platforms. P1 is a 16 GB OnePlus 13T and P2 a 16 GB OnePlus 15, with Adreno 830 and 840 GPUs. GPU references use an NVIDIA RTX 5090. Table 1 lists each experiment’s conditions; the screen wakes during parts of both P1 overlap runs of the nightly update (Appendix D ).
Models and workloads.
We evaluate hybrid Q3.5-4B and Q3.5-2B and attention-only Q3-4B and L3.2-3B, with Q3.5-0.8B for scaling tests. The workloads cover a nightly personalization update using on-phone labels and the public LaMP-2 movie-tagging benchmark Salemi et al. (2024) . Both use first-order LoRA with answer-only supervision; the nightly update continues an existing adapter, while LaMP-2 trains a fresh one. 7.2 End-to-end on-device fine-tuning (RQ1)
RQ1 asks whether a complete update finishes on the phone and yields an adapter as useful as a GPU-trained one.
Completion. Both phones complete all 222 optimizer steps of the nightly update (Table 2 ). With overlap, the update takes 7.82 and 7.87 active hours in two P1 runs and 8.09 on P2 with six pinned threads. The system also trains a fresh adapter for a full LaMP-2 epoch on all three models, taking 3.09 to 9.06 active hours depending on model size.
Reproducibility. All six nightly runs, across both phones and three runtime configurations, produce bit-identical losses at every step and the same final adapter, with no service restarts, row retries, or fused-operator fallbacks. Switching phones, thread counts, or overlap leaves each step’s computation unchanged; overlap changes only when each product runs, not the result it returns (Section 5.2 ).
Adapter quality. The phone-trained adapters are close to their GPU-trained references and far better than the base model. On LaMP-2 (Table 4 ), they land within 1.01 percentage points of GPU-trained adapters on all three models and gain 21 to 29 points over the base model (Figure 5 a). They also match the GPU seeds in macro-F1 (Table B.1 ), exceeding both seeds on Q3.5-2B and L3.2-3B and staying close on Q3-4B. Exact paired McNemar tests of our adapters against the two GPU seeds give p = 1.00 p=1.00 and 0.23 0.23 on Q3.5-2B , 0.38 0.38 and 1.00 1.00 on Q3-4B, and 1.00 1.00 for both on L3.2-3B, so no model’s adapter differs significantly from either seed. On the 175-item next-day set, the phone-trained nightly adapter answers 103 items correctly (58.9%), against 108 (61.7%) for a same-recipe GPU reference ( p = 0.23 p=0.23 ; Table 3 ).
7.3 Where the speed comes from (RQ2)
Offload after CPU optimization. We first ask whether the NPU still helps after CPU optimization. We run every configuration on one 3,431-token batch on P1 and add one mechanism at a time (Figure 5 b). Fusing GDN, LoRA, and the head shortens the CPU step from 361.0 to 251.4 s (1.44 × \times ). Ours (sync), which keeps those fused operators and moves the frozen path to the NPU with synchronous requests, shortens it to 62.4 s, a further 4.03 × \times and 5.79 × \times overall. Recorded clocks are similar across configurations, and the two rounds run in opposite orders from the same 42 ∘ C start (Appendix C ). Two findings explain the gain: after four basic CPU fixes, the frozen path still takes 55% of self CPU time for one training example on P1 and about 40 of 68.6 s on P2 (Figure 2 a), and at the same forward shape, HMX reaches 4.65 TFLOPS on P1 and about 5.3 on P2 with FP16 weights, against 0.178 and 0.780 on the CPU.
Fused CPU operators. Fusion shrinks the CPU residual that offload exposes. On P2, a zero-returning stub for the frozen path leaves 26.7 s of self CPU time per training example, led by small operators (9.1 s), casts and copies (6.2 s), adapter products (4.8 s), and GDN recurrence (4.3 s). Operator tests reduce recurrent cost by about 5.8 × \times on P1 and 3.8 × \times on P2. Within a layer on a 744-token CPU microbenchmark, the fused LoRA operator reduces time from 9.4 to 2.2 s. The fused vocabulary head takes 216 ms for forward and backward at 38 answer positions, against 595 ms for unfused slicing at 32 positions. Arithmetic, mask, threading, and cross-platform checks validate all fused operators.
Overlap. Overlap hides adapter work behind NPU requests (Section 5.2 ) and adds to offload: in two cold-step trials in opposite orders, it cuts step time from 65.3 to 59.8 s on P1 and from 60.2 to 52.4 s on P2, with identical losses and no unconsumed requests. In a profile of a 744-token P2 example, overlap cuts the exposed backward-call time from 1.00 s to 0.20 s. In Table 2 , updates with overlap take 7.82 hours on P1 (8.51 without) and 8.57 on P2 (9.01 without). A six-thread P2 repeat that starts at full battery and stays at thermal status 0 takes 8.09 active hours (Appendix D ).
After offload. To see what remains after offload, we profile a 744-token P2 example with overlap: client calls, including exposed NPU waiting, account for 15% of self CPU time, against 56% for fused CPU operators. The example takes 13.55 s with profiling enabled and records 13.3 s of self CPU time, of which client calls take 2.05 s and fused recurrence, adapters, and elementwise operations take 7.43 s. In this profile, the CPU–NPU boundary thus takes a small share of self CPU time, and the fused CPU work the largest. The input-gradient kernels take 0.51–5.60 ms per projection at 840 tokens (Table 5 ); on V81, they reach 13.0 TFLOPS in FP16 at 840 tokens and 17.0 in Q8 at 2,048 tokens.

7.5 Energy and memory (RQ4)
Offload cuts energy per token by more than two thirds relative to C3. On the same 3,431-token batch on battery power, our system with overlap uses 0.2275 J per token above idle, 32.2% of C3’s energy per token and 25.3% of C0’s (Figure 5 c). It finishes the batch soon enough that energy per token falls although its whole-device power draw rises to 13.3–13.8 W, from about 11.0 W for C3 (Appendix D ).
The precision policy also shrinks the resident model: the service holds 4.74 GB of Q3.5-4B weights (2.02 GB FP16 recurrent, 2.72 GB Q8), against 7.14 GB in all-FP16. In the six updates, the trainer peaks at 4.25–4.56 GB, minimum available memory is 1.80 GB on P1 and 1.74 GB on P2, and the first optimizer step starts 45–77 s after launch, once the NPU service loads its weights and validates its shapes.

7.6 Training as a background job (RQ5)
Training can also run without a cooler. With passive cooling only, a 339,380-token update on P2 completes in 2.64 hours with synchronous requests. The full nightly update needs pacing: under fixed load without a cooler, it drives P2 to Android thermal status 3 (severe) after about 18 minutes, and thermal pacing turns this into steady progress (Figure 6 ).
With thermal pacing on P2, six pinned threads, and no cooler, the paced run trains 313,592 tokens over 46 steps in 3.22 wall-clock hours and averages 27.0 tokens/s at a 64% duty cycle, 53% of the cooled six-thread run’s rate on the same steps. Thermal status 2 starts all 81 pauses, and the battery stays at or below 38.3 ∘ C and the CPU at or below 72.2 ∘ C. Pausing and resuming do not change the computation: all 58 committed losses, including those of the 12 fixed-load steps before pacing starts, match the cooled runs bit for bit.
The job also yields and recovers: a P1 update that yields twice finishes in 7.82 hours with the same adapter as an uninterrupted repeat (7.87 hours). In service-kill tests on both phones, the training job restores the last commit and reproduces the subsequent losses and the final adapter; on P2, the restarted service listens again 54 s after the failure.

8 Related Work
Forward-only methods train through inference engines by estimating gradients from forward passes Malladi et al. (2023) , also on phones Gao et al. (2025) ; we keep backpropagation. First-order trainers run it on edge and phone GPUs Zhu et al. (2023) ; Tether Data (2026a) , and smaller networks train on phone DSPs and NPUs Xu et al. (2022) ; yuubinnkyoku (2026) . We add the input-gradient product to the NPU’s inference engine, so the frozen path of billion-parameter models backpropagates on the phone’s NPU.
We build on LoRA Hu et al. (2022) and activation checkpointing Chen et al. (2016) . Unlike weight quantization for output quality Frantar et al. (2023) ; Lin et al. (2024) , we set each projection’s precision by gradient sensitivity, and unlike a global loss scale Micikevicius et al. (2018) , our range guard scales each row. Appendix E details each route.
9 Conclusion
An on-device LLM should learn from its user’s data where that data lives. The phone’s inference NPU can carry the expensive part of first-order LoRA training: the frozen path in both directions, computed from its resident inference weights. Around this offload, our system adds a gradient-sensitive precision policy, a per-row range guard, and a resumable runtime that fuses residual CPU work and overlaps requests. It completes nightly personalization updates of a 4B model on P1 and P2 with an external cooler and sustains thermally paced training on P2 without one. Its LaMP-2 adapters land within 1.01 points of GPU-trained ones on all three models, and on P1 it runs a controlled step 4.03 × \times faster than an optimized CPU trainer. Because training asks the NPU only for products over the weights its inference engine already holds, the weights that serve an on-device LLM’s inference also serve its fine-tuning.

Appendix A Numerical and Kernel Validation
A.1 Precision policy measurements
The precision study of Section 2.3 compares concatenated LoRA gradients g g of Q3.5-4B for four evaluation-mode examples with an FP32 reference. It emulates block quantization, row scaling, FP16 operands and outputs, and FP32 accumulation. Table 6 in Section 7.4 reports the relative error ‖ g − g FP32 ‖ 2 / ‖ g FP32 ‖ 2 \|g-g_{\rm FP32}\|_{2}/\|g_{\rm FP32}\|_{2} , the global cosine similarity, and the worst (minimum) cosine over modules. Its BF16 row stores frozen weights in BF16 without autocast, and its Q4 row quantizes them to 4-bit blocks with one scale each.
A.2 Operand exchange
Both NPUs have an 8 MB VTCM, and HMX accumulates in 37-bit cells Qualcomm Technologies (2026) . Q8 stores blocks of 32 signed 8-bit values with one FP16 scale, and device weights occupy 32 × 32 32\times 32 tiles of 1,088 bytes, including 64 scale bytes. One-hot probes recover the forward effective weights bit for bit on both phones for tested shapes.
A.3 Nonfinite inputs and row retries
HMX can return finite outputs for input rows that contain NaN. The CPU adapter branch receives the same input and propagates nonfinite values through the integrated layer. Integrated checks exercise nonfinite inputs and row retries.

Appendix E Detailed Related Work
Parameter-efficient tuning.
LoRA Hu et al. (2022) and adapter layers Houlsby et al. (2019) shrink the trainable state. QLoRA Dettmers et al. (2023) keeps the frozen base in low precision while computing in higher precision, and MeSP restructures adapter backward to save memory while keeping gradients mathematically identical Park et al. (2026) . They reduce what training stores; we execute what remains, the frozen path, on a phone’s inference NPU.
Updates through inference engines.
FwdLLM fine-tunes LLMs for federated learning with perturbed inferences Xu et al. (2024) , MobiZO and MobiEdit run forward-only updates through mobile inference engines Gao et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2026) , and MeZO estimates gradients from forward passes to save optimizer memory Malladi et al. (2023) . These methods avoid backward kernels; we instead add the input-gradient product to the NPU’s inference engine, so training on the phone keeps backpropagated gradients.
Training on mobile and edge hardware.
On phones, offloading prefill to the NPU already speeds up LLM inference Xu et al. (2025) . TinyTL freezes weights and learns small modules to cut activation memory Cai et al. (2020) , and training under 256 KB brings learning to microcontrollers Lin et al. (2022) . Mandheling trains deep neural networks with mixed precision across a phone’s CPU and DSP Xu et al. (2022) . PockEngine compiles sparse backpropagation for edge devices and fine-tunes Llama-2-7B on a Jetson GPU Zhu et al. (2023) . The open-source HexaTrain project trains small transformers on Hexagon NPUs yuubinnkyoku (2026) , and Fabric trains LoRA adapters on phone GPUs through Vulkan Tether Data (2026a) . We run backpropagation through the frozen path of billion-parameter base models on the phone’s inference NPU.
Rematerialization and offloading.
Activation checkpointing recomputes intermediates to save memory Chen et al. (2016) , and Checkmate and POET schedule rematerialization optimally or together with paging Jain et al. (2020) ; Patil et al. (2022) ; they complement our checkpoint budget. ZeRO-Offload divides training state and work between GPU and CPU Ren et al. (2021) , work on AMD client NPUs offloads training’s matrix products to the NPU Rösti & Franz (2025) , and Flying Serving switches an LLM server between data and tensor parallelism at run time as load changes Gao et al. (2026) . Our partition also reuses resident inference weights and handles the engine’s FP16 output range.
Quantization and gradient range.
GPTQ and AWQ quantize weights Frantar et al. (2023) ; Lin et al. (2024) , and LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant handle activation outliers Dettmers et al. (2022) ; Xiao et al. (2023) , all for output quality; we set weight precision per projection by gradient sensitivity. Mixed-precision training keeps FP32 master state and scales the loss Micikevicius et al. (2018) ; our range guard acts per row and retries partial-result overflow.

The model set covers hybrid Qwen3.5 backbones Qwen Team (2026) , which interleave GDN recurrence and attention, and attention-only Qwen3 Yang et al. (2025a) and Llama Meta (2024) backbones. Table F.1 summarizes their roles. Qwen3.5-9B is used for the weight-residency check in Section 7.5 .

Q3.5-4B has 32 layers: 24 GDN and eight attention layers. Its text backbone has hidden width 2,560, MLP intermediate width 9,216, 16 attention heads, four key/value heads, and a vocabulary of 248,320 tokens. The 248 frozen projections cost 3.569 billion multiply-adds per token per forward pass. Three full traversals for forward, activation recomputation, and input gradients therefore require about 21.4 GFLOP per token. Frozen-weight formats follow the gradient-sensitive policy in Section 4.4 ; the smaller Qwen3.5 models execute their 16-output gates on the CPU with complete forward and backward.
F.2 Workloads and data
Nightly personalization. The nightly workload continues the previous night’s Q3.5-4B adapter on 882 examples labeled on the phone. Two epochs contain 1,483,048 training tokens and 222 optimizer steps. Evaluation uses a 175-item next-day set and a 510-item held-out set, as described in Appendix B .
LaMP-2. LaMP-2 Salemi et al. (2024) is a movie-tag classification benchmark with 3,820 training records and 692 development records. Each example includes two profile examples retrieved by BM25. Q3.5-2B, Q3-4B, and L3.2-3B each train a fresh adapter for one epoch, giving 478 optimizer steps. Task-quality metrics and GPU-reference comparisons follow Section 7.1 and Appendix B .
