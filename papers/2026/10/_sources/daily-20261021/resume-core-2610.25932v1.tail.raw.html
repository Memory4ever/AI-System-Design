5.2 Where the gain comes from

Table 2 reports surviving lineages beside distinct outputs and coverage, which bounds what any selection rule can reach.

Lineages increase at every step in all eight rows and coverage in seven; the exception is Dream on GSM8K, where Chopthin alone costs a little coverage before the floor more than recovers it. Under multinomial resampling Dream is close to a single surviving lineage on every benchmark, so the floor has more to recover there, and Chopthin without the floor barely moves it. Distinct outputs rise with lineages in every row, so the extra lineages carry different completions, not duplicates. Nor is the gain obtained by resampling less often: LP-SMC triggers the gate as often or more often than multinomial resampling in all eight settings while losing fewer than half as many particles per event, 4.014.01 against 11.6611.66 on Dream GSM8K, and its loss per event varies little across models and benchmarks where the multinomial loss tracks how concentrated the weights happen to be.

Table 2 adds the components in one order; crossing the ancestor draw with the floor on GSM8K (Table 3, Appendix B.3) tests whether either alone would suffice. Systematic resampling raises surviving lineages but leaves coverage at 90.8%90.8\%, so drawing the ancestors more evenly is not a substitute for bounding the weights that enter the draw, and Chopthin is ahead of it on every column of that table, with and without the floor. The floor raises coverage on its own under both draws, so it addresses a distinct failure mode; nevertheless no single-component configuration reaches LP-SMC. Accuracy and surviving lineages both rise monotonically with the depth of the floor on MATH-500 (Figure 3, Appendix B.3); we keep κ=ln⁡N\kappa=\ln N throughout because equation 6 admits it for every N≥6N\geq 6 with no tuning, although 1/81/8 does better on that benchmark. Among correctly answered LP-SMC runs, many correct lineages had a high hypothetical deletion probability under multinomial resampling at their recorded weights (Appendix D).

5.3 Selection rules

Table 4 (Appendix B.4) applies four selection rules — confidence, majority, distinct-output consensus, and the best-of-NN reward model of Table 1 (Brown et al., 2024; Stroebl et al., 2024) — to the same saved populations, so selector quality is not confounded with population quality; each rule is defined in full there.

Coverage improves consistently; what varies is how much of it each rule converts into accuracy. The one rule that favours the baseline is the decoder’s own confidence, which by construction undervalues the low-confidence lineages the floor exists to keep; the populations are not weaker, and coverage rises in every setting regardless. On Dream-7B the ordering is less uniform and the consensus rules split (Table 5, Appendix 4); the two models are compared within a table, not across tables, since Dream is a base checkpoint and LLaDA-1.5 is aligned.

6 Related work

Particle decoding for language models.

Sequential Monte Carlo has steered autoregressive language models through probabilistic programs (Lew et al., 2023), learned twists (Zhao et al., 2024), syntactic and semantic constraints (Loula et al., 2025), and particle-based inference-time reasoning (Puri et al., 2025; Markovic-Voronov et al., 2026; Giannone et al., 2025; Giannone et al., 2026; Tran et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2026). Parallel work sharpens the base-model distribution or its answer marginal rather than steering toward an external reward (Karan & Du, 2026; Azizi et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Arzhantsev et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2023). Feynman–Kac steering has also been applied to image and text diffusion models using intermediate rewards (Singhal et al., 2025). For diffusion LLMs, SR-SMC uses accumulated decoder confidence (Luo et al., 2026), S3S^{3} uses a composite verifier (Bilal et al., 2026), POKE-SMC uses lookahead path likelihood (Liu et al., 2026), and TRI-TSMC learns twisting functions (Wang et al., 2026). PG-DLM instead addresses one-shot weight degeneracy by refining complete trajectories with particle Gibbs (Dang et al., 2026). These methods modify the proposal, potential, target or outer refinement procedure; we hold the guidance fixed and study lineage loss at a resampling event.

Resampling, genealogy and final-answer selection.

Stratified, residual and systematic resampling reduce variance relative to multinomial resampling and conventionally return equal weights (Kitagawa, 1996; Liu & Chen, 1998; Carpenter et al., 1999; Douc et al., 2005). Fearnhead & Clifford (2003) resample only the lighter particles, a thresholding principle related to Chopthin’s pass-through region (Gandy & Lau, 2016), which Ahmadi et al. (2026) applied to autoregressive power sampling; our lineage-based formulation, the input weight floor, and their integration with block-wise diffusion SMC are new here. The loss of ancestral diversity under repeated resampling is classical (Doucet et al., 2001; Del Moral, 2004); we make its mismatch with effective sample size explicit in diffusion LLM decoding. Selecting a final answer is a separate problem, addressed through voting or output clustering (Wang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023b; Kuhn et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023a) or through verifiers and reward models (Cobbe et al., 2021; Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025); Section 5.3 compares selection rules on a fixed population.

7 Conclusion

Multinomial resampling can remove useful lineages while resetting the effective sample size, so high ESS does not imply genealogical diversity. LP-SMC addresses this mismatch by combining Chopthin’s unequal-weight resampling with an input weight floor. Across two diffusion LLMs and four reasoning and code-generation benchmarks it increases oracle coverage in every setting and accuracy under a common reward-model selector in eleven of twelve, and the mechanism is visible in the populations themselves: surviving lineages rise with the decoder left unchanged. Population management can therefore widen the set of problems on which inference-time search produces a correct candidate; how much of that becomes final accuracy depends on the selection rule; Appendix F states the limitations.

Ethics statement

This work studies inference-time decoding for open-weight language models. No human subjects were involved and no new data were collected. Experiments use publicly available benchmarks (MATH-500, GSM8K, HumanEval, and MBPP), publicly released model weights (LLaDA-1.5 and Dream-7B), and publicly available reward models. The proposed method operates at decoding time and does not train or modify the underlying model weights; consequently, generated outputs remain subject to the limitations and failure modes of the underlying models and reward signals. Evaluation on HumanEval and MBPP requires execution of model-generated code; this execution is performed only through the evaluation harness’s sandboxed execution path on compute controlled by the authors.

B.1 Chopthin’s offspring draw, and the proof of Proposition 1

The offspring function haηh^{\eta}_{a} of equation 3 (Section 2.3) sets the expected number of offspring of particle ii, with aa chosen so that the expected offspring counts sum to NN, ∑i=1Nhaη​(wi)=N\sum_{i=1}^{N}h^{\eta}_{a}(w_{i})=N. Proof of Proposition 1.

(i) After the reset every weight equals 1/N1/N, so ESS=N\mathrm{ESS}=N. Lineage ρ\rho survives if and only if at least one of the NN independent ancestor draws falls in ρ\rho, which has probability 1−(1−wρ)N1-(1-w_{\rho})^{N}; summing over lineages gives the expectation. For uniform weights put wi=1/Nw_{i}=1/N; the sequence (1−1/N)N(1-1/N)^{N} increases to e−1e^{-1}. (ii) As a function of aa the total ∑ihaη​(wi)\sum_{i}h^{\eta}_{a}(w_{i}) is continuous and never increases. B.2 The ESS lower bound implied by a weight-ratio bound

Lemma 2 (ESS lower bound).

Let w1,…,wN>0w_{1},\dots,w_{N}>0 with maxi⁡wi/mini⁡wi≤η\max_{i}w_{i}/\min_{i}w_{i}\leq\eta for some η≥1\eta\geq 1. Then

B.4 Selection rules by model

The four selection rules of Section 5.3 are: confidence, the argmax of the decoder’s cumulative confidence and the rule of the released SR-SMC code; majority, a vote over all NN particles; distinct, a vote over distinct outputs merged by the grader’s answer equivalence (Ahmadi et al., 2026; conventions in Appendix B.6); and reward model, the argmax of Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B on mathematics or AceCodeRM-7B on code, applied identically to both populations. Table 4 reports all four on LLaDA-1.5; Table 5 reports the same for Dream-7B.

Two conventions differ from Table 4 and are stated here. First, 40.4%40.4\% of Dream’s particles on MATH-500 finish without a \boxed expression, against 3.5%3.5\% for LLaDA; empty answers are excluded from the vote in both tables, but the exclusion removes far more of the population here. Second, answer equivalence on code is exact program identity after AST canonicalisation, as in Table 4, except that the key is the sanitised program the grader executes rather than the raw generation. Dream continues into the next few-shot example after finishing, so its raw text does not parse and the raw key would measure the parser, not the selector. The code reward model, AceCodeRM-7B, is scored on the sanitised program for Dream for the same reason.

B.5 The external potential, exactly

Section 5 writes the externally guided weight as U(i)=exp⁡s⁡(x(i))U^{(i)}=\exp s(x^{(i)}). This section states ss exactly, since the reward model’s raw output is a per-step probability, not a log-weight.

At a block boundary the committed prefix of particle ii is decoded to text and split into steps on blank lines; empty steps are dropped, and a prefix with no non-empty step is assigned p=0.5p=0.5. A chat sequence is built with the system prompt “Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.”, the question as the user turn, and the steps joined by the separator token <extra_0> with a trailing separator as the assistant turn. The question is the final few-shot example only: the prompt is split on the last occurrence of its family’s start marker, so the in-context examples are not scored. The sequence is truncated to its first 40964096 tokens.

Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B emits a two-way distribution at every <extra_0> position. Let pkp_{k} be the positive-class probability at step kk and p=meank⁡pkp=\operatorname{mean}_{k}p_{k}, clamped to [10−3,1−10−3][10^{-3},1-10^{-3}]. The index kk runs over all steps of the prefix, that is over every <extra_0> position surviving the 40964096-token truncation, so the mean is unweighted over the whole committed prefix, not the score of the final step alone. Then

s⁡(x)=β​log⁡p1−p,β=1,s(x)\;=\;\beta\,\log\frac{p}{1-p},\qquad\beta=1, (8)

so ss is a log-odds and U=exp⁡s=(p/(1−p))βU=\exp s=(p/(1-p))^{\beta}. We use the absolute form: at each boundary log⁡w\log w is replaced by ss instead of incremented by it, which is the boundary-wise reweighting noted in Section 5.

B.6 Evaluation protocol

The shot counts of Section 5 are the harness defaults for each benchmark; they are identical across configurations and were not tuned. Mathematics answers are taken from the last boxed expression and compared by symbolic equivalence; code is graded by executing the candidate, truncated at the task’s stop marker and then reduced to its longest parsable prefix, against the benchmark tests. A path τ\tau, its output x0x_{0} and the answer the grader extracts are distinct objects: several paths can give one output and several outputs one answer, and distinct-output counts are counts of x0x_{0}. For distinct-output consensus, the NN outputs of the last block are merged into their distinct outputs, which are grouped into answers by the grader’s equivalence, numeric or symbolic on mathematics and exact program identity after AST canonicalisation on code, with benchmark execution used only for final grading; the answer held by the largest number of distinct outputs is returned (Ahmadi et al., 2026). Appendix 4 states the conventions specific to Dream-7B.

The population-management operations introduce no direct decoder or reward-model evaluations. Measured end to end on GSM8K with LLaDA-1.5, the mean number of network evaluations per problem is 701.6701.6 for SR-SMC, 692.1692.1 for Chopthin and 708.0708.0 for LP-SMC, and the mean wall time is 120.8120.8, 121.1121.1 and 119.6119.6 seconds on the same hardware.

Appendix C Reward models for code generation

The reward model used on MATH-500 and GSM8K, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B, is trained on mathematical reasoning and is not validated for Python. This appendix records what is available instead, since the availability of a step-level code reward model determined which experiments were feasible. We did not audit either reward model for overlap with the evaluation sets, and neither model card documents its training corpus at a level that would settle the question. Overlap would affect the absolute selected accuracies.

No identified step-level code reward model meets all requirements.

Most of the open-weight code reward models we identified are outcome models: they are trained to score completed programs, so they can rerank a final population but provide no prefix-level signal for the block-boundary resampling weights used here. Step-level code reward models do exist, and we identified two, but neither simultaneously satisfies the three requirements a usable one must meet here: released weights, a declared license, and step-level scoring demonstrated on function completion. CodePRM (Li et al., 2025) ships two LoRA adapters in a source repository with no declared license, and SWE-Shepherd (Dihan & Khan, 2026) releases an adapter whose model card declares no license and which targets repository-agent actions on SWE-Bench. Our decision rests on licensing and task fit, not on training-distribution mismatch: the runs reported below use a mathematics process reward model on Python, which is further from its training distribution than a code model evaluated on other code benchmarks would be. Of the outcome models we use AceCodeRM-7B (Zeng et al., 2025) to rank finished programs (Table 1).

Execution as a reward, and why it is not applicable here.

A reward can be obtained by running the candidate instead of scoring it with a model, and Markovic-Voronov et al. (2026) do exactly this for HumanEval, combining unit-test execution with an auxiliary syntax term. Execution needs a complete program, which they obtain by rolling each particle forward to completion before scoring it. That is a change of method rather than of reward: our sampler adds no model evaluations beyond decoding (Section 4.1), and lookahead rollouts would multiply that cost by the remaining sequence length. The tests would also have to be separated from the evaluator, which is the same difficulty as selecting on the visible tests below. A syntax-only reward avoids both problems and is computable on an unfinished prefix, but it scores well-formedness, not correctness, so we do not report it.

The resampler under a task-mismatched reward.

The absence of a usable code reward model leaves open the question: does the resampler’s effect on the population depend on the reward being well matched to the task? We can test that directly, because a mathematics process reward model can be placed in the weight on code even though it is strongly out of distribution there. Table 7 reports both configurations on HumanEval and MBPP with Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B in the weight, on the same problems, so the two configurations differ only in the population step (Chopthin resampling with the floor). Coverage rises in both benchmarks, by 4.34.3 points on HumanEval and 2.32.3 on MBPP. Accuracy under confidence selection falls in both out-of-distribution guided code experiments. These rows are reported here and not in Table 1 because the reward is not a code reward model, and describing it as external code guidance would overstate the experiment.

Why we do not select with the visible tests.

Running each candidate against the tests shown in the prompt would cost less than a reward model, but in MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) the three assertions shown in the prompt are the test cases used for grading, and in HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) the docstring examples recur among the assertions of the hidden check function, so selecting on them would reuse information from the benchmark’s own evaluator and break the separation between selection and evaluation. Rerankers that execute model-generated tests instead of the benchmark’s own tests avoid this by construction (Ma et al., 2025).

Appendix D Lineage deletion risk

For each problem the ancestor lists recorded at every block are replayed forward to recover which founding lineage each particle slot belongs to at each resampling event. The final answers are graded, the lineages that produced a correct answer are identified, and for each resampling event the total normalized weight of such a lineage, wρw_{\rho}, is used to evaluate the probability that multinomial resampling would have left it without offspring, (1−wρ)N(1-w_{\rho})^{N}. The higher median risk under LP-SMC is expected: LP-SMC admits correct lineages whose intermediate weights are precisely those multinomial resampling would have been likely to remove. Table 8 reports, for each problem, the largest such value over the joint range of both indices: over every boundary at which the ESS gate actually fired, and over every lineage that produced a correct answer. Where several correct lineages exist they are neither pooled nor selected between, so the statistic describes the most endangered correct lineage at its most dangerous event rather than the fate of the problem’s coverage. Weights are pooled only within a lineage. Only problems with at least one correct final answer enter the statistic, so the denominator differs between configurations. Under LP-SMC the correct lineage faced better-than-even odds of deletion on 95.7%95.7\% of the problems Dream answers correctly and 87.4%87.4\% of those LLaDA answers correctly, with median worst-case risk 0.790.79 and 0.780.78. No correct lineage in either floored configuration faced a deletion probability above 0.90.9, compared with 2.7%2.7\% and 9.5%9.5\% of them under multinomial resampling and 6.2%6.2\% and 23.4%23.4\% under Chopthin without the floor: flooring a weight at 1/N1/N of the maximum weight bounds (1−wρ)N(1-w_{\rho})^{N} away from one. The controlled contrast is the last pair: holding the ancestor draw fixed at Chopthin, adding the floor takes the fraction above 0.90.9 from 6.2%6.2\% to zero on Dream-7B and from 23.4%23.4\% to zero on LLaDA-1.5, so this is the floor’s effect and not the resampler’s. Chopthin alone raises the fraction above the multinomial baseline. These percentages are taken over different denominators (column Correct of Table 8), and each configuration is measured on its own weights, not as a paired counterfactual.

Appendix F Limitations

All experiments sit at one operating point: two base models, N=16N=16 particles and L=256L=256. A sweep over the particle count would mean re-decoding every benchmark under every configuration, and at the measured cost of roughly two minutes per problem per configuration (Appendix B.6) that grid was beyond the compute we could allocate to the full test sets. We therefore report the operating point we can support in full, and note that the mechanism is not specific to it: the expected fractional lineage loss under multinomial resampling is 1−(1−1/N)N1-(1-1/N)^{N} for any N≥4N\geq 4 (Proposition 1) and the floor κ=ln⁡N\kappa=\ln N is defined relative to NN, so both components are stated for general NN. Whether the measured margin holds at other NN is untested.

Task-appropriate external process-reward guidance is evaluated only on mathematics, because no step-level code reward model met our criteria (Appendix C); the deliberately out-of-distribution test of Table 7 is a substitute for it, not an equivalent.

The floor explicitly changes the weighted measure, so LP-SMC is unbiased for the floored target and not for the original unfloored one, and at a boundary the floor can move at most (N−1)/(2​N−1)(N-1)/(2N-1) of the normalized mass, 0.480.48 at N=16N=16; the measured displacement is about an order of magnitude smaller (Appendix B.3).

Finally, coverage gains do not always become accuracy gains. The conversion depends on the selection rule and is least reliable for the consensus rules on code, where merging by answer equivalence has less to work with than on mathematics (Tables 4 and 5).
