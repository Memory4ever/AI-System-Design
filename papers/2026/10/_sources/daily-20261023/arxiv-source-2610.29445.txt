Deciding Scale Placement in MXFP4 Dot Products with Integer Arithmetic
Shreyas Vinayakumar
Abstract
Block-scaled formats represent a group of values as low-bit elements
multiplied by one shared scale. The Open Compute Project’s microscaling
(MX) family includes MXFP4, which stores 32 four-bit elements with a shared
power-of-two scale. While lowering a dot-product reduction for execution,
a compiler can apply these scales before the reduction, or defer scaling until afterwards.
Although equivalent over real numbers, these formulations can produce
different IEEE binary32 results through overflow or underflow, with
equivalence also depending on the reduction tree. We study the legality of compiler
rewrites in either direction by deriving conditions under
which the two formulations are bitwise equivalent.
To decide rewrite legality, we construct an integer state machine
that exploits the finite element alphabet and shared power-of-two
scales, modeling overflow, NaN, and subnormal rounding.
We prove its faithfulness under bounded scale codes and integer
coefficients, yielding an exact encoding of the equivalence query
in quantifier-free linear integer arithmetic( QF_LIA ).
For MXFP4, we derive a closed-form sufficient upper bound on the sum
of scale exponents for any fixed binary reduction tree within the supported
coefficient range. This bound depends on the size of the largest proper subtree.
For four tree shapes at block lengths k ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 , 8 , 16 , 32 } k\in\{2,3,4,8,16,32\} and
two shapes at k = 64 k=64 , SMT finds counterexamples when this upper
bound is increased by one exponent step.
We evaluate the computational cost of this approach against the standard direct encoding in quantifier-free floating-point
arithmetic ( QF_FP ). On matched queries, cvc5 proves the integer version in seconds but reaches
a configured one-CPU-hour limit on the floating-point proof even at k = 2 k=2 .
The implementation was cross-checked using an
independent floating-point encoding, exhaustive and stratified binary32 execution, and
GPU replay of counterexamples. An MLIR lowering prototype demonstrates
selection between the two formulations using the certified conditions as
static or runtime guards.
Keywords: floating point microscaling MXFP4 compiler
correctness SMT equivalence checking
1 Introduction
As ML models, particularly large language models, continue to grow in size,
they require substantial memory and computational resources to store and process their parameters
and intermediate activations. Reducing the number of bits used to represent each value
can improve throughput and reduce energy use on supporting hardware compared to full
32-bit floating-point arithmetic, by reducing data movement and enabling efficient arithmetic.
However, reducing bit width affects precision and dynamic range,
and requires care to preserve numerical accuracy and to avoid overflow or underflow.
Block scaling formats like Open Compute Project’s microscaling (MX) formats, which
store a tensor as blocks of narrow elements that share one scale [ 24 , 27 ] ,
extend the dynamic range of the elements while keeping storage costs moderate.
These formats have become so important that major vendors
have added native support: NVIDIA’s Blackwell tensor cores and AMD’s matrix cores support multiplying
block-scaled matrices [ 23 , 5 ] .
On the software end, frameworks like Triton expose the operation as dot_scaled
and emulate it on other targets [ 31 ] , and torchao emulates it by
dequantizing to a wide type and calling an ordinary matrix
multiply [ 26 ] .
In MXFP4, a block holds 32 four-bit E2M1(2 bit exponent, 1 bit mantissa) elements and one eight-bit E8M0
scale, and the scale is a power of two. For an E2M1 element with sign bit S S , exponent field E E , and mantissa bit M M ,
its nonzero normalized decoded value is
( − 1 ) S ​ 2 E − 1 ​ ( 1 + M 2 ) , (-1)^{S}2^{E-1}\left(1+\frac{M}{2}\right),
which is then multiplied by the block scale. An E8M0 scale has the decoded value 2 e − 127 2^{e-127} ,
where e e is the unsigned integer stored in the scale byte.

elements a 1 , … , a k a_{1},\dots,a_{k} and b 1 , … , b k b_{1},\dots,b_{k} . A way to compute the dot product is to dequantize
elements from each block, and perform a reduction as shown below:

Under real arithmetic, the distributive law makes the two forms equivalent.
This identity motivates scale hoisting as a compiler optimization.

Existing compilers have two ways to deal with this today. Under strict IEEE semantics,
rewriting is forbidden, because it can change the returned value. Under
fast-math flags it is allowed, together with every other reassociation. For example,
LLVM’s language reference warns that reassociation may “dramatically change
results” [ 20 ] . Neither option uses the unique constraints known
about this particular operation: the element alphabet has sixteen codes(4 bits per element), the
scale is a power of two, the block length is fixed by the format, and the
compiler itself chooses the reduction tree. We derive sufficient guards for
rewrites between the direct and hoisted forms under a fixed reduction tree,
and establish sharpness for the configurations evaluated here.
Bit-precise SMT supports bounded equivalence checking of floating-point
programs [ 22 , 21 ] , but the direct encoding
is expensive even for small instances of this family
(Section 2.6 ). Our contribution is an exact reduction to
quantifier-free linear integer arithmetic( QF_LIA )
that exposes the arithmetic structure of these queries.
Every E2M1 product is a quarter-integer of
magnitude at most 36, and the scales are powers of two. Under an upper bound on
each scale code and a bound on the magnitudes of intermediate integer
coefficients, both programs can be represented by a state machine consisting of
an integer coefficient and a tag in { fin , + ∞ , − ∞ , NaN } \{\mathrm{fin},+\infty,-\infty,\mathrm{NaN}\} .
These bounds ensure exactness in the normal range, while overflow
and subnormal rounding can be expressed as conditions on integers.
The resulting query in QF_LIA is
equisatisfiable with the floating-point query under these bounds,
and counterexamples translate constructively in both directions
(Theorem 3.1 , Section 3 ).
We use the following vocabulary throughout: A lane is an index
i ∈ { 1 , … , k } i\in\{1,\dots,k\} , one position in a block. A scale code is the
eight-bit E8M0 field e e of a block, and its scale is 2 e − 127 2^{e-127} . The
exponent sum t = ( e A − 127 ) + ( e B − 127 ) t=(e_{A}-127)+(e_{B}-127) is the exponent of the combined
scale s A ​ s B s_{A}s_{B} . A band is an interval of exponent sums. The
guard is the condition on the pair of scale codes under which we prove
the rewrite exact; it consists of an upper cap on each code and a band on the
exponent sum. A band is certified for a block length and a tree when
the solver has proved that no block inside it separates the two forms, and it
is sharp when widening it by one exponent step admits a
counterexample. A witness is a concrete block, elements and scale
codes together, on which the two forms return different binary32 values.
This paper makes the following contributions.
•
An exact reduction of a family of floating-point equivalence queries to
QF_LIA , with a faithfulness proof and constructive counterexample
translations on the domain of interest (Section 3 ).
•
A closed-form sufficient guard using MXFP4’s element and scale encodings
for any fixed binary reduction tree
within the coefficient budget, with solver-established sharpness for
four shapes at block lengths 2,3,4,8,16,32 lanes and for two shapes at 64 lanes
(Sections 4 and 5.3 ).
•
A prototype implementation of a rewrite guard based on the certified
bands (Section 6.2 ) in an MLIR lowering pass, and
a description of which parts of the construction carry over to
other block formats(Section 6 ).
•
A comparison of the two theories,
evaluating the relative cost using the same solver and
validating the encoding against an independently written floating-point
encoding, against exhaustive and stratified binary32 execution, and by
replaying witnesses on a GPU(Section 5 ).
2 The Rewrite and Its Legality Question
2.1 Formats
An E2M1 element has a sign bit, two exponent bits, and one mantissa bit. Its
sixteen codes denote 0 0 , ± 0.5 \pm 0.5 , ± 1 \pm 1 , ± 1.5 \pm 1.5 , ± 2 \pm 2 , ± 3 \pm 3 , ± 4 \pm 4 ,
and ± 6 \pm 6 , with separate codes for + 0 +0 and − 0 -0 ; there are no codes for
infinity or NaN. We write an element as a = m / 2 a=m/2 with
m ∈ M = { 0 , ± 1 , ± 2 , ± 3 , ± 4 , ± 6 , ± 8 , ± 12 } , m\in M=\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3,\pm 4,\pm 6,\pm 8,\pm 12\},
and note that every nonzero | m | |m| has at most two significant bits.
An E8M0 scale is an eight-bit exponent without sign or mantissa. Code
e ∈ [ 0,254 ] e\in[0,254] denotes 2 e − 127 2^{e-127} , so the scales run from 2 − 127 2^{-127} to
2 127 2^{127} ; code 255 is NaN. We denote x = e A − 127 x=e_{A}-127 and y = e B − 127 y=e_{B}-127 for the two
unbiased exponents, which implies s A = 2 x s_{A}=2^{x} and s B = 2 y s_{B}=2^{y} , and t = x + y t=x+y for the
exponent sum. The OCP block length for MXFP4 is k = 32 k=32 [ 24 ] .

All arithmetic is IEEE binary32 with round to nearest, ties to even, and
gradual underflow [ 15 ] . We denote fl ⁡ ( v ) \operatorname{fl}(v) for the correctly rounded value of a
real number v v ; every operation returns fl \operatorname{fl} of its exact result. We use
the following facts:
The smallest positive value is δ = 2 − 149 \delta=2^{-149} , and every subnormal value is

Infinity and NaN follow IEEE rules: ∞ + ( − ∞ ) \infty+(-\infty) and 0 ⋅ ∞ 0\cdot\infty produce NaN,
an infinity absorbs any finite value, and NaN propagates through every operation.

2.4 The equivalence relation
We compare results with a relation ≃ \simeq under which + 0 ≃ − 0 +0\simeq-0 , any two
NaNs are equal, and finite values and infinities must match exactly.
In SMT-LIB this is expressed by fp.eq augmented with a test
that both operands are NaN [ 30 ] .
A checker for scalar IEEE optimizations must also distinguish the sign of
zero [ 22 ] ; we write ≡ \equiv for that stricter
relation. We state results for ≃ \simeq and show in
Section 4.4 that the two relations agree on the certified
domain, so the choice does not affect the guard.

3 An Exact Integer Semantics
The floating-point encoding asks the solver to reason about 32 pairs of
four-bit codes, two scale codes, and a network of roughly a hundred binary32
operations, each of which can in principle round. On the capped domain
very few of them do. We identify a single power-of-two grid
on which every value of both programs lies,
reduce each operation to integer arithmetic on that grid plus an overflow test and a
subnormal rounding step, and prove that the reduced semantics computes
exactly what binary32 computes.

6.1 The rewrite rule
The compiler rule, in terms of the eight-bit scale fields, is
e A ≤ 252 e B ≤ 252 107 ≤ e A + e B ≤ 254 + B ^ ρ ​ ( k ) r D ρ ↝ r H ρ \frac{e_{A}\leq 252\qquad e_{B}\leq 252\qquad 107\leq e_{A}+e_{B}\leq 254+\widehat{B}_{\rho}(k)}{r_{\mathrm{D}}^{\rho}\;\rightsquigarrow\;r_{\mathrm{H}}^{\rho}}
(11)
where r D ρ ↝ r H ρ r_{\mathrm{D}}^{\rho}\rightsquigarrow r_{\mathrm{H}}^{\rho} means that the direct form may be
replaced by the hoisted form, and the replacement preserves ≃ \simeq on every
block whose codes satisfy the condition. Since ≃ \simeq is symmetric, the same

for a fold and ≤ 372 \leq 372 for a balanced tree.
The solver certifies bounds for a specific format, block length, reduction tree and
accumulator semantics. These choices can vary across kernel configurations,
like tiling, split-K and autotuning, meaning certification may not simply be once per format.
Our low-cost certification allows us to check the guard for each configuration efficiently,
allowing it to serve as a legality oracle for automated kernel generation and
rewrite search where many candidates need to be checked.
In our prototype, the bound B ^ ρ ​ ( k ) \widehat{B}_{\rho}(k) is computed offline,
when a lowering rule for a given block length and tree is written, and stored with the rule; the solver
is not on the compilation path. At lowering time the condition is checked
in one of two ways. With static range metadata , a quantizer or
runtime states a guaranteed range for each operand’s scale codes, range
propagation checks the condition once, and the compiler emits the hoisted form
with no branch. Without static ranges, the compiler emits a runtime
check of the four comparisons per block, or per group of blocks, taking the
hoisted form when it passes and the direct form otherwise.
This rule differs from attaching reassoc , nnan , and
ninf flags. When the condition holds, it is guaranteed that the rewrite
preserves the value, including the exceptional values; if it doesn’t the
compiler may still rewrite under an approximate policy, but cannot guarantee that the
result is value-preserving.

6.3 Beyond MXFP4
The construction of Section 3 uses three structural
assumptions: element products lie on a common power-of-two grid with a bounded
integer coefficients, scales are powers of two, and the reduction tree is
fixed. Coefficient bounds are the primary format-dependent property.
The state machine can be applied by representing elements
as m ​ 2 − f m2^{-f} with | m | ≤ M |m|\leq M . The coefficient budget for k k lanes is M 2 ​ k M^{2}k ,
with E = t − 2 ​ f E=t-2f , provided that M 2 ​ k < 2 24 M^{2}k<2^{24}
and that the cap is adjusted so that M ​ 2 x − f M2^{x-f} stays below Ω \Omega .
Raw products and individually scaled operands must be exactly representable.
E2M1 exactness is enabled by its two significant bits and its narrow range:
its nonzero magnitudes range from 0.5 0.5 to 6 6 , represented with f = 1 f=1 , yielding M = 12 M=12 .
The OCP formats MXFP6 (E2M3 and E3M2 elements) and MXINT8 also satisfy the budget at k = 32 k=32 .
The exactness argument does not extend to MXFP8:
E4M3 elements range over eighteen binades, from 2 − 9 2^{-9} to
448 448 , so two lane products in one block can differ in scale by more than 2 35 2^{35} ,
and a sum of such products need not fit in 24 bits even though each product
alone does.
NVFP4 uses an E4M3 scale instead of the power-of-two scale,
whose nontrivial significands can introduce rounding in subsequent operations.
Appendix 0.A shows that, for positive finite E4M3 scales
and k ≤ 16 k\leq 16 , every operation in both forms is exact in the single-level
model, so the rewrite is legal without a band. Including the per-tensor binary32 scale, as
NVFP4 uses in practice, permits addition rounding at every node.
The present exactness argument does not cover this setting.
The implementation and certified bounds presented here cover MXFP4 with E2M1 elements and E8M0 scales.
7 Related Work
Microscaling.
The OCP specification defines the MX formats and leaves the internal
precision and operation order of a dot product to the
implementation [ 24 ] ; Rouhani et al. evaluate them for training
and inference [ 27 ] . Cuyckens et al. treat the
reduction tree of an MX datapath as a hardware design
parameter [ 7 ] ; our results say that the parameter
also changes which rewrites are legal. MX+ exploits the shared exponent to
detect outliers during serving [ 18 ] . Rout and Tine describe
a fused dot-product unit for mixed-precision tensor cores that scales
operands before the multiply-add [ 28 ] , which is the direct
form.
Verifying floating-point optimizations.
LifeJacket checks LLVM’s floating-point peephole rules with bit-precise SMT
and found incorrect ones [ 22 ] ; Alive2 performs bounded
translation validation for LLVM IR [ 21 ] . Both reason
about scalar rules over arbitrary floating-point inputs and utilize floating-point theory.
We fix the element alphabet, scale format, and
tree, which allow bypassing the theory, and we produce a parametric region
rather than a decision on a single rule.
Icing provides a verified semantics for fast-math rewrites inside the CakeML
compiler [ 2 ] - the programmer indicates which rewrites are allowed
through annotations. Our guard instead proves that the specific rewrite is value-preserving
on its domain. Valpey et al. formalize the mixed-precision dot products of
three tensor-core generations in SMT [ 32 ] ; such bit-precise
hardware models are complementary to our work, supplying the accumulator semantics under
which a band would have to be re-derived (Section 6.3 ).
Volta checks the equivalence of GPU
kernels, including matrix multiplication and attention, over expressions in
the real numbers [ 10 ] ; over reals the direct and
hoisted forms are equal by distributivity, and the witness of
Section 1 does not exist.
Floating-point decision procedures.
Alternative routes include FP-to-bit-vector encoding [ 12 ] ,
abstract CDCL over floating-point intervals in
MathSAT [ 3 ] , real-arithmetic axiomatization with
rounding lemmas [ 6 ] , and a lossless reduction to
exact arithmetic [ 19 ] , and translation of mixed real–floating-point formulas into
equisatisfiable real-arithmetic ones [ 29 ] .
Our method trades their
generality for an exact linear-integer model of a restricted family.
Leeser et al. ignore subnormals and non-real results, whereas overflow,
NaN, and subnormal rounding are central to the rewrite studied here.
Gadelha et al. report performance differences across solver backends.
To control for this, we compare the floating-point and
integer encodings using the same solver.
Exact reasoning about floating-point networks.
SELTZO verifies floating-point accumulation networks with a
precision-independent abstraction that reduces to integer
reasoning [ 33 ] ; our reduction is in the same spirit but
exploits a finite alphabet and a shared exponent, and it is exact rather than
approximate on its domain. FLoPS formalizes P3109 scalar formats and their
operations in Lean [ 4 ] ; it does not model MX blocks or
reductions.
Herbie and Daisy improve or bound rounding
error [ 25 , 8 ] ; our property is
equality, including of infinities and NaN, which error bounds do not express.
Fasi and Mikaitis emulate stochastic rounding on IEEE
hardware [ 11 ] ; a stochastically rounded implementation
would need a distributional rather than a bitwise notion of equivalence.
Mixed precision and silent errors.
Low-precision arithmetic has spread beyond ML into numerical linear
algebra [ 13 ] and is being adopted in scientific
codes [ 16 ] , where results are checked against references
rather than validation scores.
Fleet-scale result checking for silent data corruptions [ 14 , 9 ]
has been used for detecting rare, input-dependent,
and quiet divergences like the one in
Section 1 . In contrast, a miscompilation is deterministic, and
its condition can be proved once rather than watched for unlike a hardware fault.
8 Limitations
The guarantees in this paper apply to a specific execution model:
each operation evaluated separately in binary32 with round
to nearest ties-to-even and gradual underflow. Within the certified band every lane
product is exactly representable in binary32, so replacing the multiplication followed by addition
with a fused multiply-add removes no rounding and preserves the result.
However, wider accumulators, flush-to-zero, or a reduction tree other than the one
certified can change the behavior and require a separate equivalence argument.
In particular, the lower endpoint − 147 -147 relies on gradual underflow which would not hold under flush-to-zero.
In a full matrix multiplication, there is a cross-block accumulation, and the block-local
rewrite composes with it only if both implementations use the same outer accumulation order and arithmetic.
Theorems 3.1
and 4.1 have mathematical proofs supported by exhaustive and differential checks,
but haven’t been mechanized. Formalizing the integer semantics and its connection to an
existing IEEE formalization is future work. Sharpness is
established using the solver for the specific trees and lengths in
Tables 3 and 4 .
The MLIR prototype generates reference scalar IR implementing the semantics.
The rewrite proof does not verify subsequent LLVM transformations or machine-code
generation. The prototype does not provide a production kernel.
9 Conclusion
An exact reduction to linear integer arithmetic makes a specific family
of floating-point equivalence queries tractable, while preserving the
behavior at range boundaries that determines legality. Implementing
the reduction for MXFP4 helps derive a sufficient condition
for the legality of rewrites in the form of a cap on each scale code and
a band for their sum, depending only on
the size of the largest proper subtree of the reduction tree.
Solver queries establish that the condition is also necessary for
several representative configurations. An MLIR prototype utilizes the results
in static or runtime checks consisting of a few simple conditions
to verify the legality of rewrites.
Retaining the format and reduction structure in the IR
enables checking legality conditions to guide lowering choices.
