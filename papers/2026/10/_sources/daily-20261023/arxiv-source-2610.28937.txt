Abstract
Conformal prediction (CP) for language models is typically evaluated under a matched protocol, in which calibration and deployment use the same post-training inference configuration, such as quantization mode and inference temperature. In deployment, these choices often differ from those used at calibration to meet memory or throughput constraints or serving policies. We argue that this exposes a limitation in current CP evaluation protocols for language models. We formalize the underlying phenomenon as inference-time model shift : a change in the conformal score distribution induced by inference configuration rather than by the input–label distribution, and theoretically characterize how it breaks split-conformal exchangeability even when the task distribution and trained model are unchanged. We then evaluate its consequences across 8 8 language models, 4 4 finite-label benchmarks, and 12 12 inference configurations. We find that matched evaluation can substantially overstate deployment-time coverage, and that the effect is heterogeneous across models and datasets. We argue that inference configuration should be reported as a first-class experimental axis in CP evaluations for language-model systems.
1 Introduction
Conformal prediction (CP) is a distribution-free framework for uncertainty quantification: given a trained predictor and a held-out calibration set, it returns a prediction set guaranteed to contain the true label with a user-specified probability 1 − α 1-\alpha in finite samples Vovk et al. (2005) ; Angelopoulos and Bates (2023) . The guarantee is obtained by calibrating a threshold on nonconformity scores, which are real-valued scores of disagreement between the predictor’s output and a candidate label, and rests on an important assumption: that calibration and test scores are exchangeable. A substantial literature studies how this assumption fails when the data-generating distribution shifts, including covariate shift, label shift, online drift, and other forms of non-exchangeability Tibshirani et al. (2019) ; Gibbs and Candès (2021) ; Oliveira et al. (2024) . In each case, the source of failure is a change in the joint distribution P X ​ Y P_{XY} between calibration and deployment.
For language models, calibration and test scores depend not only on P X ​ Y P_{XY} but also on the inference configuration used to generate the predictive distribution from which scores are computed: post-training choices that shape the predictive distribution without altering the trained model itself, including numerical representation (e.g., quantization mode) and inference parameters (e.g., temperature). In deployed systems, the inference configuration is not always held fixed for reasons unrelated to the data or the model: quantization is changed to satisfy memory or throughput constraints, and inference temperature varies with system defaults, serving policies, or user-facing controls. Existing CP evaluations for language models, however, typically adopt a matched protocol, in which calibration and test points are generated under the same inference configuration; reported coverage then estimates the reliability of one fixed score-generating procedure, a valid target only when the deployment configuration is fixed and known in advance Kumar et al. (2023) ; Quach et al. (2024) ; Mohri and Hashimoto (2024) ; Cherian et al. (2024) ; Su et al. (2024) ; Ulmer et al. (2024b) ; Gui et al. (2024) ; Zhong et al. (2025) .
When the calibration and deployment configurations differ, even with the trained model and the task distribution unchanged, calibration and test scores are generated by different mechanisms, and the exchangeability assumption underlying split-conformal validity need not hold. We refer to this phenomenon as inference-time model shift : a change in the conformal score distribution induced by inference configuration rather than by the input–label distribution. It is distinct from classical dataset shift because P X ​ Y P_{XY} is fixed; the shift lives in the mapping from the input to the resulting predictive distribution, and hence in the induced score distribution.

The empirical consequences of inference-time model shift are nontrivial. Figure 1 reports coverage on MMLU-Pro when deployment is fixed to a single configuration ( nf4 quantization, temperature T = 0.5 T{=}0.5 ) and calibration uses, in turn, each of the twelve configurations in a 3 × 4 3{\times}4 grid of quantization modes and temperatures. Matched calibration tracks the nominal 0.90 0.90 target across eight small language models, with mean coverage 0.902 0.902 . Mismatched calibration produces mean coverage 0.639 0.639 and worst-case coverage 0.508 0.508 . The task distribution and the trained model are unchanged across all twelve calibrations; the degradation is attributable solely to the change in score-generating procedure between calibration and deployment. Matched evaluation, by construction, cannot surface this degradation, which exposes a limitation of matched-only CP evaluation protocols for language models.
We characterize this setting theoretically and audit it empirically. We formalize inference-time model shift as a property of the conformal score distribution, and we show that matched validity does not transport across inference configurations once score-level exchangeability is broken. The empirical audit covers 8 8 language models on 4 4 finite-label benchmarks (multiple-choice QA and classification) across a 12 12 -configuration grid. We restrict the empirical scope to discrete-label tasks because they admit a controlled comparison: candidate labels, predictive distributions, and conformal scores are directly comparable across inference configurations, isolating inference-configuration mismatch from confounds introduced by open-ended generation.
Two findings emerge. First, matched evaluation can substantially overstate deployment-time coverage, with the failures concentrated in low-temperature regimes and amplified, but not solely caused, by aggressive quantization. Second, and more consequential for evaluation practice, the effect is heterogeneous : the same type of calibration–deployment mismatch produces severe undercoverage in one setting and mild degradation, or even overcoverage, in another. This implies that inference configuration is an experimental axis on the same footing as the dataset and the model.
Contributions. We make the following contributions.
•
We identify a limitation in current CP evaluation protocols for language models and formalize the underlying phenomenon as inference-time model shift , a distinct source of non-exchangeability characterized by a change in the score-generating procedure under a fixed task distribution, under which matched validity is not transportable across inference configurations.
•
We provide an empirical audit across 8 8 language models, 4 4 finite-label benchmarks, and 12 12 inference configurations, finding that mismatch effects are large but neither monotone in configuration nor consistent across models and datasets, and propose an evaluation protocol that reports matched validity alongside mismatch sensitivity and deployment-assumption-specific baselines.
2 Related Work
Conformal prediction under shift and non-exchangeability.
CP provides distribution-free uncertainty quantification under exchangeability, with modern developments covering regression, classification, adaptive coverage, and broader risk-controlled prediction sets Doula et al. (2025) ; Lei et al. (2018) ; Sadinle et al. (2019) ; Romano et al. (2020) ; Angelopoulos et al. (2021) ; Angelopoulos et al. (2024) . A substantial literature studies what happens when the matched i.i.d. setting fails, including covariate shift, label shift, online distribution drift, and other forms of non-exchangeability Doula (2026) ; Tibshirani et al. (2019) ; Gibbs and Candès (2021) ; Gibbs and Candès (2024) ; Podkopaev and Ramdas (2021) ; Prinster et al. (2022) ; Oliveira et al. (2024) ; Kasa et al. (2025) . These works are closest in spirit to ours because they analyze when standard conformal guarantees degrade outside matched calibration and test conditions. However, their source of failure is typically a change in the data-generating distribution or dependence structure. In contrast, we keep the task distribution fixed and study a different source of non-exchangeability: deployment-time changes in inference configuration. The same checkpoint and the same inputs can therefore induce different conformal score distributions under different temperatures or quantization modes.
Conformal prediction and uncertainty for language models.
Recent work has brought CP to language and foundation models, including multiple-choice
question answering Kumar et al. (2023) , conformal language modeling for generated
candidate sets Quach et al. (2024) , conformal factuality and claim filtering Mohri and Hashimoto (2024) ; Cherian et al. (2024) ,
black-box or API-only settings Su et al. (2024) , correctness-coverage guarantees for open-ended
generation Wang et al. (2024b) , non-exchangeable conformal language generation Ulmer et al. (2024b) ,
selective uncertainty testing Wang et al. (2025) , and trust- or alignment-oriented selection for
foundation-model outputs Gui et al. (2024) . These works establish CP as a promising tool
for language-model reliability, but their evaluation targets differ from ours: they typically calibrate
and evaluate under a fixed inference setup, or study other sources of uncertainty and
non-exchangeability. Our focus is the evaluation protocol itself: whether a conformal predictor
calibrated under one post-training inference configuration remains reliable when deployment uses
another. Appendix B provides a more detailed summary of this distinction.
Calibration, temperature, and quantized language models.
A parallel literature studies calibration and uncertainty estimation for neural networks and language
models. Temperature scaling remains a standard post-hoc calibration baseline Guo et al. (2017) ,
and calibration is also central for pretrained transformers and question-answering language models Desai and Durrett (2020) ; Jiang et al. (2021) .
Recent work further studies uncertainty estimation for free-form or long-form LLM generations Ulmer et al. (2024a) ; Huang et al. (2024) .
On the systems side, quantization has become a standard deployment mechanism for LLM serving,
with work on low-bit inference and quantization-aware training Lin et al. (2024) ; Liu et al. (2024) ;
recent work also shows that quantization can materially affect calibration itself Zhong et al. (2025) .
This literature motivates temperature and quantization as practically relevant inference axes, but it
does not formulate calibration-to-deployment inference mismatch as a distinct conformal-validity
problem. Our contribution is to connect these deployment choices to exchangeability of conformal
scores and to treat inference configuration as part of the CP evaluation design.
3 A Theoretical Characterization of Inference-Time Model Shift
Standard matched CP evaluation estimates reliability for a fixed inference pipeline: calibration and deployment use the same score-generating procedure. In deployed language-model systems, however, the inference configuration may change after calibration. We formalize this mismatch and show why matched validity need not describe deployment-time reliability even when the task distribution is unchanged.

4 Reliability Targets Under Deployment Uncertainty
Once inference configuration is allowed to vary after calibration, “is CP valid?” is no longer a single question. The relevant reliability target depends on the deployment assumption. If deployment configuration is fixed and known in advance, matched calibration is the correct target. If deployment follows a stable mixture over configurations, mixture-marginal validity is the relevant target; we include pooled calibration as a baseline for that setting and defer its formal treatment to the appendix. The main text focuses on the stronger regime in which deployment configuration is unobserved at test time and reliability should remain meaningful for individual latent deployment states.
We study two conservative baselines for that regime. MaxThr provides worst-case protection across configurations by hedging against the largest configuration-specific threshold. Latent-Robust uses the observed predictive vector to restrict that hedge to a conformal set of plausible latent configurations. These baselines are not intended as universally superior replacements for matched CP; they correspond to stricter deployment targets and, in our default construction, Latent-Robust can be even more conservative because the total error budget is split across two stages.

5 Evaluation
We study inference-time model shift as an evaluation problem through three questions. First, can matched evaluation materially misstate deployment-time coverage under calibration–deployment mismatch? Second, how much of this effect is attributable to temperature and to quantization, respectively? Third, how do calibration procedures aligned with different deployment assumptions compare in coverage under these regimes?

CommonsenseQA follows the same qualitative pattern more mildly: all eight models fall below target under the three mismatch summaries, but the corresponding average drops are smaller (matched 0.912 0.912 ; mismatch mean 0.827 0.827 ; mismatch worst 0.789 0.789 ; pooled 0.818 0.818 ).
DBPedia-14 differs in kind rather than degree. The descriptive averages are comparatively close to target ( 0.901 / 0.863 / 0.827 / 0.867 0.901/0.863/0.827/0.867 ), but Mismatch Mean spans a wide model-level range ( 0.795 0.795 to 0.946 0.946 ): the same summary statistic conceals substantial degradation for some model families and near-matched behavior for others. MobileActions is the most stable dataset, with average mismatch effects within a few points of target, but it is not invariant: Mismatch Worst still falls below target for 6 of 8 models, indicating that even comparatively stable settings retain adverse calibration–deployment pairs. Full per-model tables are in Appendix D.1 .
Two observations cut across the four datasets. First, the gap between Mismatch Mean and Mismatch Worst is consistently large, so any summary that averages over off-diagonal calibration choices understates the worst-case reliability cost. Second, the heterogeneity is structural: MMLU-Pro and CommonsenseQA fail uniformly across models while DBPedia-14 fails selectively, and a single reporting protocol that fixes one calibration choice cannot capture both regimes.

5.4 Experiment 3: Temperature Effects at Fixed Deployment Quantization
Finally, we isolate the role of temperature by fixing deployment quantization to nf4 and averaging over deployment temperatures. This complements Experiment 2 by asking whether the mismatch effect persists when quantization is held fixed and only the inference regime varies. Figure 4 reports coverage separately for each dataset–model pair. For each model and run, coverage is first averaged over deployment temperatures at fixed nf4 , and the plotted bar is then averaged over runs.
The mismatch effect persists, but more selectively than in Experiments 1 and 2. The clearest sign is that Mismatch Worst still falls below target for all 8 of 8 models on every dataset, whereas Mismatch Mean now falls below target on only some datasets: 8 of 8 on MMLU-Pro , 1 of 8 on CommonsenseQA , and an intermediate count on DBPedia-14 and MobileActions . MMLU-Pro again shows the strongest degradation: descriptive averages over models drop from 0.901 0.901 ( Matched ) to 0.842 0.842 ( Mismatch Mean ) and 0.719 0.719 ( Mismatch Worst ). 6 Discussion
The central claim of this paper is evaluative: for deployed language-model systems, inference configuration is part of the validity problem itself, not an implementation detail to be held fixed or averaged away. Standard CP evaluations ask whether the guarantee holds when calibration and deployment share an inference pipeline. Our results show this is insufficient. Even with the task distribution and trained model fixed, changing the inference configuration between calibration and deployment can move the conformal score distribution enough to invalidate split-conformal validity, and matched-only evaluation cannot, by construction, surface this failure.
The empirical picture shows the challenging nature of inference-time model shift. In our experiments, low-temperature deployment is consistently the most mismatch-sensitive regime, and aggressive quantization can amplify the effect, but the failures admit no monotone ordering over inference configurations. Their magnitude varies with the dataset and model family, and the direction of the effect can change across settings.
Implications for evaluation practice. Our findings motivate a concrete revision of reporting practice. We argue that evaluations of CP for language models should treat inference configuration as a first-class experimental axis, on the same footing as the dataset and the model. Concretely, this entails three reporting steps: (i) state the deployment assumption being targeted: fixed configuration, stable mixture, or unobserved at test time, since each implies a different reliability target; (ii) report sensitivity to calibration–deployment mismatch along the inference axes that may vary at deployment, rather than matched coverage alone; and (iii) where the deployment configuration is not known in advance, report a baseline aligned with the targeted deployment regime. In the setting studied here, these reporting steps require no new theoretical machinery, but they do require treating inference configuration as part of the experimental design. We view this as a necessary step toward conformal guarantees that remain meaningful when language-model systems are actually deployed.
Limitations. Our analysis is primarily a characterization rather than a mechanistic account: we identify when mismatch matters but do not provide a predictive theory of when a given configuration change will induce undercoverage, overcoverage, or only mild effects. The audit is carried out on a finite deployment grid, which captures many realistic serving choices but does not address richer deployment variation, including continuous temperatures or temporal drift in serving policies.
7 Conclusion
We identify inference-time model shift as a distinct failure mode for conformal prediction in language models: even under a fixed task distribution, changing deployment-time inference configuration can alter the conformal score distribution and break the assumptions behind matched calibration. Across eight small language models, four datasets, and a grid of quantization and temperature settings, this effect leads matched-only evaluation to substantially overstate deployment-time reliability. These results motivate a simple change in practice: conformal evaluations for language models should treat inference configuration as part of the experimental design, not as a fixed implementation detail.

Tables 23 – 26 clarify how Latent-Robust achieves coverage recovery. Besides final coverage and average prediction-set size, they report the empirical coverage of the latent configuration-set predictor Γ ^ ​ ( Z ​ ( X ) ) \widehat{\Gamma}(Z(X)) and the average size of the predicted configuration set. Interpreting these diagnostics jointly is important. When the inferred configuration set is large, the method hedges more broadly across plausible latent deployment states and correspondingly pays a larger efficiency cost. Conversely, smaller inferred configuration sets allow a less conservative threshold.
The diagnostics in Tables 23 – 26 therefore show that the method’s conservativeness is not arbitrary. It is mediated by how difficult it is to infer the latent deployment configuration from the observed predictive vector. Generally, the adaptive hedge is driven primarily by latent-configuration predictions rather than repeated reversion to the global worst-case threshold. The attached standard deviations also make clear that these diagnostic quantities are themselves reasonably stable across runs.
NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1.
Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes] .
Justification: The abstract and introduction state the main contribution: identifying inference-time model shift as a source of non-exchangeability for conformal prediction in language models and evaluating its empirical consequences across models, datasets, and inference configurations. The scope is also clearly limited to finite-label benchmarks and post-training inference-configuration changes.

Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes] .
Justification: Section 6 discusses limitations, including that the work is primarily a characterization rather than a mechanistic theory, that the empirical study uses a finite deployment grid, and that it does not cover richer deployment variation such as continuous temperatures or temporal drift in serving policies.
