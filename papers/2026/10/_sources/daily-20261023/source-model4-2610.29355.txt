Abstract
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) models improve scalability by routing input tokens to only a subset of expert networks, but they often suffer from representation collapse, in which multiple experts converge toward similar functions and lose their intended diversity. Prior work has addressed this limitation either by improving router architectures or by introducing auxiliary loss terms that implicitly penalize similarity among experts. However, router-based approaches remain vulnerable to routing collapses, and loss-based approaches can conflict with the task’s original objective during optimization. In this work, we propose GaP-MoE (Gradient Accumulation and Projection in MoE), which induces expert specialization by directly manipulating expert output gradients rather than modifying the loss function. At each training step, GaP-MoE stochastically selects one of the T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK routed experts as a leader based on normalized routing scores, and projects the gradients of the remaining follower experts onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by the leader’s recent gradients. This removes directional overlap between expert gradients without altering the task loss, allowing specialization to emerge as a direct consequence of the optimization dynamics rather than an externally imposed constraint. We evaluate GaP-MoE in both fine-tuning settings (BERT-based, on GLUE) and from-scratch pretraining settings (GPT-2-based, on WikiText-103, enwik8, and text8), and further confirm its compatibility as a drop-in module for existing SMoE architectures. Experimental results show that GaP-MoE achieves performance comparable to or better than standard SMoE.

1 Introduction
With the development of Transformer-based models ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) , numerous deep learning models have made remarkable progress across various tasks in recent years. The simplest way to improve the performance of deep learning models for each task is to follow the so-called “scaling law,” which involves increasing both the model size and the dataset size ( Kaplan and others, 2020 ; Brown and others, 2020 ) . However, this also implies an increase in computational cost.
A commonly used approach to address this issue is the Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ) . Instead of a single neural network, SMoE uses multiple independent networks called experts to increase the overall model capacity ( Jacobs et al., 1991 ) , and applies T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK routing , which selects the K K experts best suited to process each input token. Each selected expert processes the token using its own parameters, and the router combines their outputs as a weighted sum using the corresponding routing scores, which were originally used for T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK routing .
However, SMoE does not inherently guarantee that different experts learn distinct functions. For example, if the router collapses, tokens may be assigned disproportionately to only a small subset of experts or distributed without meaningful expert preference, preventing the experts from receiving sufficiently differentiated training signals ( Krishnamurthy et al., 2023 ) . Even when the router does not collapse, experts may remain insufficiently specialized if they observe similar inputs or evolve from similar initialization conditions ( Dai et al., 2024 ) .
Therefore, regardless of whether insufficient specialization originates from the routing process or the optimization dynamics of the experts, encouraging expert specialization remains important for fully utilizing the capacity of SMoE.
Consequently, various studies have been conducted to promote expert specialization. Some approaches identify low-dimensional degeneration of expert representations as a cause of insufficient specialization and encourage low-dimensional operations at the router level ( Chi et al., 2022 ) .
Other approaches dynamically adjust the number of experts used for routing ( Pham et al., 2024 ) , efficiently measure and regulate parameter similarity ( Do et al., 2025 ) , or directly orthogonalize token output representations ( Liu et al., 2024 ) .
More recently, this line of research has extended to gradient regulation, where auxiliary loss functions are introduced to prevent gradient conflicts among tokens within the same expert ( Yang et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) .
However, existing specialization methods have potential drawbacks. Router-based methods primarily improve routing decisions and encourage specialization indirectly by modifying the geometry or distribution of token assignments, rather than directly constraining how the selected experts learn their representations ( Wang et al., 2026 ) .
Methods that regulate expert parameters or representations through auxiliary loss functions can more directly induce specialization. However, such auxiliary objectives may conflict with the original task loss used to train the model parameters, potentially hindering both learning processes ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) .
Accordingly, we propose Gradient Accumulation and Projection in Mixture-of-Experts, or GaP-MoE , a learning algorithm that retains the standard training procedure of SMoE while explicitly applying orthogonality constraints to already-computed expert output gradients.
Rather than introducing an additional loss term, GaP-MoE directly modifies redundant gradient directions after the task gradients have been generated, thereby preserving the original task objective while promoting expert specialization.
Specifically, we store the recent output gradients received by each expert as an orthonormal basis using the Gram–Schmidt process. Among the T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK selected experts, one expert is probabilistically designated as a leader according to the normalized routing scores, while the remaining experts are treated as followers. The leader retains its original task gradient, whereas each follower removes the component of its output gradient that overlaps with the subspace spanned by the leader’s accumulated gradients. In this way, GaP-MoE encourages co-routed experts to follow differentiated update directions without requiring additional forward or backward passes.
The key benefits of GaP-MoE are as follows.
•
Explicitly induces expert specialization through gradient orthogonalization.
•
Reuses already computed expert output gradients, avoiding additional forward/backward passes and gradient recomputation.
•
Its simple structure ensures compatibility with various existing router architectures and auxiliary loss functions.
2 Related Works
2.1 Sparse Mixture-of-Experts
Following the conventional SMoE formulation ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ) , a Mixture-of-Experts module consists of a set of N N neural networks, denoted as E 1 , … , E N E_{1},\ldots,E_{N} , each with independent parameters. It is generally used to replace the FFN layer in Transformer models, in which each expert is typically implemented as a two-layer MLP ( Lepikhin et al., 2020 ; Fedus et al., 2022 )
y i ( x ) = W i 2 ( σ ( W i 1 x + b i 1 ) ) + b i 2 , i ∈ 1 , … , N y_{i}(x)=W^{2}_{i}(\sigma(W^{1}_{i}x+b^{1}_{i}))+b^{2}_{i},i\in{1,\ldots,N}
where x x is the input token, and ( W i 1 , W i 2 ) (W^{1}_{i},W^{2}_{i}) and ( b i 1 , b i 2 ) (b^{1}_{i},b^{2}_{i}) denote the weights and biases of the i i -th expert. However, this form is not fixed, and various types of experts and corresponding architectures are being studied ( Zhang et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2022 ) .
SMoE additionally employs a gating network, or router, G ⁡ ( x ) G(x) that computes a routing score for each expert w.r.t. token x x . Based on these scores, the router selects the K K highest-scoring experts and retains their scores, while suppressing the scores of all unselected experts to zero. The token is then passed to the selected expert, and their outputs are combined using the corresponding routing scores as
S ​ M ​ o ​ E ​ ( x ) = ∑ i = 1 N G i ​ ( x ) ​ y i ​ ( x ) SMoE(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}(x)y_{i}(x)
(1)
Since G i ​ ( x ) = 0 G_{i}(x)=0 for experts not included in the T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK , only a subset of experts is activated for each token, thereby achieving sparse computation.
2.2 Expert Specialization and Representation Collapse
Chi et al. (2022) describe representation collapse as the degeneration of representations during neural network training and investigate this phenomenon in sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) models. Their analysis suggests that routing-induced updates can constrain token hidden representations toward a low-dimensional subspace spanned by expert embeddings and draw representations of tokens routed to the same expert closer together, potentially limiting their expressive capacity.
Subsequent studies have used the term representation collapse more broadly to describe several forms of insufficient differentiation among experts. These include cases in which multiple experts learn similar representations, thereby limiting specialization, and cases in which the router assigns most tokens to only a small subset of experts ( Pham et al., 2024 ; Do et al., 2025 ; Do et al., 2023 ) .
Research that aims to make individual experts learn distinct representations or functions is generally studied under the notion of expert specialization ( Krishnamurthy et al., 2023 ; Dai et al., 2024 ) .
Recent studies have employed various approaches to promote expert specialization and alleviate representation collapse. A prominent example is router-based methods, which prevent tokens from being concentrated on specific experts and encourage more differentiated routing decisions. XMoE ( Chi et al., 2022 ) maps routing representations to a low-dimensional space to mitigate representational degeneration.
( Nguyen et al., 2025 ) further analyzed how perturbing a cosine router, which has recently emerged as an alternative to the conventional linear router, can help prevent representation collapse.
Other studies address representation collapse by directly regulating expert weights, outputs, or gradients. Guo et al. (2026) promote expert specialization using a loss that orthogonalizes expert parameters, alongside a loss that promotes more diverse routing. SimSMoE ( Do et al., 2025 ) induces differentiation by reducing the similarity between the output representations produced by different experts. SD-MoE ( Huang et al., 2026 ) decomposes expert and gradient matrices via SVD into dominant and expert-specific components, facilitating the learning of an expert-specific subspace.
However, existing methods encourage expert outputs to explore diverse subspaces either implicitly through routing mechanisms or explicitly through additional loss functions. In contrast, our method, GaP-MoE, directly differentiates the update directions of expert outputs by projecting follower gradients away from the recent gradient subspace of a dynamically selected leader. This promotes expert specialization without introducing an additional optimization objective, while preserving the original task gradient of the selected leader.

3.2 Gradient Projection
Gradient projection modifies a gradient direction by removing its component along a specified subspace. It has been widely used to mitigate gradient interference in continual and multi-task learning ( Yu et al., 2020 ; Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017 ) .
GaP-MoE adopts this principle to reduce overlapping update directions between co-routed experts, rather than between different tasks ( Farajtabar et al., 2020 ) .

Role-dependent Buffer Update
Within a mini-batch, the same expert pair ( E j , E k ) (E_{j},E_{k}) may take opposite roles for different tokens, appearing as ( E j l , E k f ) (E_{j}^{l},E_{k}^{f}) for one token and ( E j f , E k l ) (E_{j}^{f},E_{k}^{l}) for another token.
In this case, only the gradient received as the leader g j l g_{j}^{l} is used to update Q j Q_{j} , while the projected gradient g j f ∗ g_{j}^{f*} is not used. This is to prevent the direction altered by the projection from being immediately reflected in the buffer, which would make the training process noisy.

For K > 2 K>2 , GaP-MoE selects one leader from the T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK experts and treats the remaining K − 1 K-1 experts as followers. The leader buffer is then used to project each follower gradient independently.
3.4 Theoretical Justification of GaP-MoE
GaP-MoE aims to prevent co-routed experts from being repeatedly updated in similar directions. As illustrated in Figure 2(a) , when two experts receive highly aligned gradients for the same token, their output representations may continue to evolve toward similar functions.
In contrast, Figure 2(b) shows how GaP-MoE projects the follower gradient away from the leader’s recent gradient subspace, thereby directing the follower toward a different update direction. GaP-MoE therefore reduces the gradient components shared by co-routed experts rather than directly constraining their output representations.

Orthogonalizing the output gradients serves to reduce the alignment between the two different expert output vectors through which a single token passes.
It should be noted that we do not perform direct optimization for our reduction goal. Aggressively minimizing this similarity could drive the two representations toward opposite directions, i.e., ψ = π \psi=\pi . In such a case, their contributions may cancel each other in the weighted sum of Eq. ( 1 ), potentially hindering MoE optimization.
Instead, GaP-MoE orthogonalizes the follower update with respect to the leader gradient subspace, thereby alleviating excessive output alignment without explicitly forcing the expert outputs apart.
Proposition 1 characterizes this mechanism by showing that the projection removes the follower-gradient component shared with the leader subspace, while minimally altering the original task gradient.
4 Experiments
Although MoE is being applied in various fields thanks to its scalability ( Riquelme et al., 2021 ; You et al., 2021 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) , this paper focuses on natural language tasks.
Since GaP-MoE is designed to deliver relative performance improvements when combined with other models, we compare it to the baseline SMoE ( Shazeer et al., 2017 ) across various tasks and subsequently compare the performance changes when combined with various models already published for specific tasks. For all tasks, each feed-forward sub-layer of the baseline models is replaced with a MoE layer consisting of four experts—composed of two linear layers and a GELU—and the T ​ o ​ p ​ 2 Top2 routing method is employed.
For more detailed experimental setup, see Appendix C .
The hyperparameter controlling the projection intensity is set to its maximum value of 1 for all tasks; however, during the first epoch, it is set to increase linearly from 0.1 to 1.0 in steps to mitigate the impact of noisy gradient changes. Additionally, the projection threshold γ \gamma for GaP-MoE is set to -1 and 0.7, respectively, to represent both full projection and minimal projection scenarios.
We evaluate GaP-MoE in both fine-tuning and from-scratch scenarios.
4.1 Fine-tuning
Based on BERT ( Devlin et al., 2019 ) , we use the publicly available pretrained version, bert-base-uncased, obtained via the HuggingFace Transformers library ( Wolf et al., 2020 ) .
We replace the 12 feed-forward layers with MoE layers consisting of four experts of the same parameter size, and initialize the experts identically based on the pretrained parameters. For the tasks, we use the GLUE benchmark ( Wang et al., 2018 ) , which includes a total of eight NLP tasks such as sentence reasoning and sentiment classification, and evaluate the model using task-specific metrics such as accuracy and F1 score. After training for five epochs, we summarize the final results based on the best performance.

4.2 From-scratch
We use GPT-2 ( Radford et al., 2019 ) as the backbone model. To fit our computational budget, we adopt a reduced GPT-2 Small configuration with 6 transformer layers, 8 attention heads, and an intermediate dimension of 2048. The original GPT-2 Small consists of 12 layers, 12 attention heads, and an intermediate dimension of 3072. Additionally, for the from-scratch task, we replaced only the last two layers with GaP-MoE—which exhibit relatively stronger gradient consistency, as will be discussed in 4.6 —while retaining the standard SMoE layers for the rest of the network.
The expert parameters are randomly initialized. We conduct experiments on WikiText-103 ( Merity et al., 2016 ) , enwik8, and text8 ( Mahoney, 2011 ) , all of which are evaluated on the next-token prediction task. WikiText-103 is evaluated using perplexity (PPL), whereas enwik8 and text8 are evaluated using bits per character (BPC). The validation loss is evaluated every 1,000 training steps, and training is terminated if the validation loss does not improve for three consecutive evaluations.
Table 2 presents the from-scratch evaluation results. Compared to the baseline SMoE, from-scratch experiments also showed that GaP-MoE generally performed better. However, on text8—a relatively easy dataset created by retaining only alphabetic characters and spaces from enwik8—the performance improvement wasn’t as significant as it was for the other two tasks.
4.3 Expert Specialization Without Router Training
GaP-MoE is particularly robust to router failure because it induces expert specialization directly through the gradients generated during training. Table 4 shows the difference in model performance on the WikiText-103 dataset when the router was not trained at all, and instead two experts were randomly selected and trained. Although performance decreased significantly overall compared to Table 2 , the performance drop is considerably smaller for GaP-MoE. In other words, this suggests that GaP-MoE explicitly guides the process to prevent expert specialization from failing.
4.4 Generalizability with Different MoE
One of the novelties of GaP-MoE is that it can be easily applied to various MoE architectures. We conducted additional experiments by applying two models—XMoE ( Chi et al., 2022 ) and SimSMoE ( Do et al., 2025 ) , which address the same problem to our GaP-MoE experimental framework.
Since no official implementation of XMoE is publicly available, and the official SimSMoE implementation is designed for pre-training rather than our fine-tuning setting, we implemented the core mechanisms of both methods within our unified experimental framework.
To efficiently evaluate the various experimental results, we categorized the tasks in the GLUE benchmark into three groups and conducted one experiment for each task. These are CoLA for the single-sentence classification task, STS-B for the sentence pair similarity task, and RTE for the natural language inference task.
Table 4 shows the results of evaluating each model by applying the GaP-MoE architecture to three fine-tuning tasks.
Interestingly, while applying GaP-MoE resulted in improved performance on most tasks, it was found to be more significantly affected by γ \gamma than the GaP-MoE-only model. Identifying the appropriate γ \gamma value for each task and model should be addressed in future research.
4.5 Expert Output Differentiation
Figure 4 shows the cosine similarity between expert outputs in the final layer for all tokens in an input sequence, whereas Figure 4 visualizes the angles between expert outputs in the sixth layer using a single input token.
When the projection threshold γ \gamma was set to − 1 -1 , the similarity decreased substantially. In contrast, setting γ \gamma to 0.7 0.7 resulted in only a marginal reduction in similarity, as projection was applied only when the similarity exceeded the threshold. This suggests that gradient projection can be used to differentiate expert outputs.
4.6 Short-term Gradient Consistency
To empirically examine whether expert gradients exhibit the short-term gradient consistency assumed in the theoretical justification, we track for each expert of the first and the last layer, the cosine similarity between its current output gradient and the orthonormal basis accumulated t t steps earlier ( t ∈ { 1 , 3 , 5 , 10 } t\in\{1,3,5,10\} ) whenever that expert is selected as leader.
Figure 5 visualizes these trajectories across layers and gap sizes. In the first step, the cosine similarity values are generally greater than 0, but they fluctuate significantly. In contrast, when the model reaches the intermediate step, the cosine similarity stabilizes. To avoid noisy updates, it is recommended to gradually increase the projection intensity α \alpha from a low level to a high level during the initial training phase.
Once the updates have stabilized to some extent, the variance in similarity decreases. At this point, the early layers maintain a similarity level above 0 but converge toward 0 as the gap widens. Conversely, the later layers consistently maintain a high level of similarity. In other words, since there are some differences in short-term consistency across layers, theoretical performance differences may exist between layers; therefore, we expect that performance will be enhanced by not unnecessarily increasing the buffer size. In our experiments, we used buffer sizes of 8 and 16 (for training from scratch and fine-tuning, respectively).

5 Conclusion and Future Work
This study proposed GaP-MoE, which retains the standard training procedure of sparse MoE with T ​ o ​ p ​ K TopK routing while imposing explicit orthogonalization constraints only on pre-computed expert output gradients. Through dynamic leader selection and gradient projection, GaP-MoE encourages experts to learn more diverse output representations without introducing additional parameter constraints or requiring gradient recomputation. Experimental results demonstrated that GaP-MoE achieves competitive or improved performance over the baseline SMoE and can be effectively combined with existing specialization methods, demonstrating its compatibility with different MoE architectures.
One limitation of this work is that the hyperparameters introduced by GaP-MoE, including the buffer size h h , the choice of layers to which GaP-MoE is applied during fine-tuning, projection threshold γ \gamma , and projection intensity α \alpha , were not systematically explored. Consequently, the reported settings may not be optimal, and the relationship between these hyperparameters and model performance remains insufficiently understood. In addition, our evaluation was limited to relatively small-scale models. Future work will therefore focus on systematic hyperparameter optimization and validating GaP-MoE on larger-scale language models.
