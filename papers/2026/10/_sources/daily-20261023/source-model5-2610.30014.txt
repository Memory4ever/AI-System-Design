Abstract
LLM agents with similar success rates can follow different action sequences. An Agent Behavior Map represents LLM–harness combinations using centered action log-likelihoods on shared reference trajectories, without requiring new environment rollouts from the target agent. We derive error bounds relating squared coordinate distances to trajectory KL under explicit assumptions, and across eight cross-scored models the distances track the directly measured trajectory KL in rank order. Across 100 LLMs and four environments, ALFWorld-based coordinates support performance prediction and identify behavioral similarities. In ALFWorld, these similarities persist after controlling for success rates and model size, including across model families. As an extension, harness-component displacements approximately compose, with accuracy varying across components and environments.

Comparing LLM agents is a matter not only of how often they succeed but also of how they act. Agents with similar success rates can differ in the tools they choose, the order of their actions, and how often they repeat an operation. Such behavior depends on the combination of the underlying LLM and the harness that manages history, planning, and reasoning ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Yao et al., 2026 ; Vats and Golev, 2026 ; Fan et al., 2026 ) . Running every combination in the environment reveals both performance and behavior, but each new agent to compare requires collecting new trajectories. This paper asks whether a representation that captures similarity of run-time behavior can be built without new environment runs of the target agent.
When agents are compared by their log-likelihoods on shared reference trajectories, the difficulty is that the distribution of the histories on which the likelihoods are conditioned itself depends on the agent’s policy. The histories contained in the reference trajectories need not coincide with those that an agent reaches through its own actions. Whether similarity of action probabilities measured on shared histories corresponds to similarity of trajectory distributions and behavior at run time is therefore not obvious. How much of these on-policy properties can be captured by off-policy observations on fixed reference trajectories is the central question of this work.
Language model maps, which vectorize log-likelihoods on shared texts, represent the relations between models in a common coordinate space ( Oyama et al., 2025 ) . Their extension to prompt–response distributions has examined performance prediction from conditional log-likelihoods and the approximate additivity of the coordinate displacements caused by prompt changes ( Oyama et al., 2026 ) . This paper applies the framework to agents that interact sequentially with an environment and answers the question above with theory and experiments.
An Agent Behavior Map treats each agent as a pair of an LLM and a harness. Each agent rebuilds its input with its own harness from the shared external history in the reference trajectories, namely the task, the observations, and the past actions. The recorded actions are teacher-forced under this input, and the resulting log-likelihood vector is centered to give the agent’s coordinates. Because the reference trajectories are reused, the coordinates of a new agent can be computed without collecting any additional environment trajectories from it. Figure 1 shows an example in which agents that are close in these coordinates take similar action sequences when run. We validate the representation from two angles.
•
Theoretical and empirical relation to the trajectory distribution (§ 2 , § 3.1 ). Under assumptions on the locality of the two policies and on the mismatch with the reference distribution, we derive error bounds that relate squared coordinate distances to the KL divergence between trajectory distributions. The error decomposes into local approximation, reference mismatch, centering, and finite samples, and the decomposition shows how the step-level and trajectory-level representations differ in their dependence on trajectory length. In a cross-scoring experiment with eight models, we compare the coordinate distances with the directly measured trajectory KL, the target itself, and find a high rank correlation.
•
Validation on performance and behavior (§ 3.2 , § 3.3 ). We apply a common harness to 100 LLMs and evaluate coordinates built from ALFWorld reference trajectories alone on ALFWorld, TextWorld, BFCL, and MiniWoB++. Besides predicting the success rates of unevaluated models from the evaluation results of the others, we ask whether neighbors on the map generate similar action sequences. In ALFWorld in particular, the similarity of action sequences remains when compared with neighbors chosen by success rate or by size, and when the candidates are restricted to models of other families. These results support the view that the coordinates carry behavioral information that neither performance level nor model family explains. The accuracy of performance prediction and the strength of behavioral similarity among neighbors, however, depend on the environment and the measure.
Finally, we represent the addition of a harness component as a displacement on the map and ask whether such displacements add up within an LLM (§ 4 ). Comparison with measured coordinates shows approximate additivity, with an accuracy that depends on the component and the environment.

3 What does the map capture about an agent?
This section fixes the harness to a standard configuration (BASE: a history window of the last four steps, no planning, direct action; § 4.1 ) and places each LLM as one point. The question is what the coordinates capture about an agent. LM maps showed a correlation between coordinates and downstream performance ( Oyama et al., 2025 ) . We answer with three quantities: the target itself, the KL between the trajectory distributions of the agents when run (§ 3.1 ); one projection of it, the success rate (§ 3.2 ); and the behavior itself when run (§ 3.3 ).
3.1 Does distance track the on-policy divergence?
Theorem 1 applies only in the local regime of small ε \varepsilon , that is, to nearly identical policies, whereas actual pairs of models may be far apart. 3.2 Performance: predicting without running
Setup.
From here on we use 100 open-weight LLMs. The reference trajectories are 500 trajectories with 10,388 steps, generated by two models (Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) and Gemma4-E4B ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) ) on 250 ALFWorld train tasks; every model scores them, and its centered step coordinates are computed from these scores. Only the two generators ran the reference trajectories; the other models only score them. Success rates and action sequences are obtained by running each model itself on different tasks, so the material for the coordinates and the material for the evaluation do not overlap. Scoring uses raw log-likelihoods without the grammar constraint; rescoring with the constraint barely moves the coordinates (Appendix B.1 ).
Question.
Can the downstream success rate of a model that has only computed likelihoods on the reference trajectories, and has never been run in the environment, be predicted from its coordinates? We hold out one model at a time and predict it from the remaining 99. The downstream tasks are 50 ALFWorld valid_unseen tasks, the environment of the coordinates, and 50 tasks each from different environments , TextWorld ( Côté et al., 2018 ) , BFCL ( Patil et al., 2025 ) , and MiniWoB++ ( Shi et al., 2017 ; Liu et al., 2018 ) , plus the averages over the four benchmarks and over the three other environments (Appendix B.3 ). The baselines use only information available without running : a regression on the log parameter count (size), the mean success rate of the same family (family), and a regression on the mean log-likelihood ℓ ¯ \bar{\ell} (the component removed by centering). On the coordinate side we use kNN, Ridge ( Hoerl and Kennard, 1970 ) , and Ridge with size as a covariate, with hyperparameters chosen by inner leave-one-out (Appendix B.3 ).

3.3 Behavior: do neighbors act alike?
In § 3.2 , models that are close in coordinates had similar success rates. Do neighbors also share their behavior when run? Behavior can only be measured as a distance between a pair of models, so the question becomes whether the behavioral distance from a model to its coordinate neighbors is smaller than its distance to the remaining models. We look at neighbors rather than at the correlation over all pairs because the guarantee covers only close pairs (§ 2.4 ). Coordinate neighbors are compared with two kinds of neighbors. The first are neighbors chosen by size, family, or ℓ ¯ \bar{\ell} , which, as in Table 2 , use only information available without running. The second are neighbors chosen by the measured success rate on that benchmark, a reference that is available only after running. Agents with the same success rate do not necessarily solve tasks in the same way, so the gap to the second kind shows what the coordinates capture beyond closeness in performance.
Setup.
We use the action sequences that each model itself produced with the BASE harness: ALFWorld valid_unseen (50 tasks), the environment of the coordinates, and the other environments TextWorld (50), BFCL multi_turn (25), and MiniWoB++ (50), all for the 100 models. The behavioral distance between two models is the distance between their trajectories on the same task, averaged over tasks. Since similar behavior is not a single number, we measure it in four aspects from coarse to fine (definitions in Appendix B.4 ):
•
Composition (which actions are used how often, ignoring order): Jensen–Shannon distance between the distributions of action types ( Endres and Schindelin, 2003 ) (type JS)
•
Global (agreement of the whole action sequence): normalized Levenshtein distance ( Levenshtein, 1966 ; Rabanser et al., 2026 ) (edit distance)
•
Local (what follows a given action): Jaccard distance between the sets of action 2-grams (2-gram)
•
Early (whether the same first move is made from the same state): disagreement rate of the first action from the same initial state ( Bouchard and Chauhan, 2026 ; Gonuguntla, 2026 ) (first action)
The difference in success rate is measured in the same form, which puts the results of § 3.2 on the same scale. The effect size for a model’s five coordinate neighbors is the mean behavioral distance to the remaining models minus the mean distance to the neighbors, divided by the standard deviation over all pairs (positive: neighbors more similar). Figure 1 (c) applies the same definition to the k k -th nearest model alone, in coordinates and in size, averaged over the 100 models and the four aspects.

Models that behave alike can be found without running them.
The five coordinate neighbors not only have similar success rates ( 0.39 0.39 – 0.61 0.61 ) but also behave alike on all four benchmarks (Table 3 ). The effect is roughly twice that of size neighbors (edit distance on ALFWorld 0.88 0.88 versus 0.40 0.40 ; 2-gram on MiniWoB++ 0.50 0.50 versus 0.27 0.27 ), and ℓ ¯ \bar{\ell} neighbors are weak ( 0.03 0.03 – 0.20 0.20 ). Family is a stronger competitor than size: coordinates exceed it on ALFWorld, TextWorld, and MiniWoB++ (except for the first action on TextWorld) and tie with it on BFCL. The effect remains when the coordinate neighbors are restricted to other families (edit distance on ALFWorld 0.60 0.60 ), so it is not produced by same-family dependence. Similarity decreases with rank: the first coordinate neighbor is the most similar and the effect returns to the overall mean around rank 30, whereas size neighbors are nearly flat beyond the first (Figure 1 (c)). The divergence position and the profile of idiosyncrasies show the same pattern, while for the success pattern, neighbors chosen by family or size are about as close as or closer than coordinate neighbors (Appendix B.4 ).
Beyond closeness in performance.
In ALFWorld, the environment of the coordinates, coordinate neighbors behave more alike than neighbors chosen by the measured success rate in all four aspects ( † \dagger in Table 3 ); in the other environments the two are roughly on a par, with coordinates significantly closer only for composition on TextWorld and MiniWoB++ and for the first action on MiniWoB++. We also regress out performance and size (Appendix B.4 ): because strong models may converge on the shortest procedures, we regress each pair’s behavioral distance on the difference in success rate, the pair’s mean success rate and its square, and the difference in log parameter count, and measure the neighbor effect on the residuals. In ALFWorld the effect remains (edit distance 0.61 0.61 , 2-gram 0.68 0.68 ) and stays positive when the candidates are restricted to other families ( 0.31 0.31 , 0.31 0.31 ); in the other environments it is weak, remains mainly in the local and early measures, and for some measures vanishes or changes sign. This linear control shows only that an association remains that the observed success rates and sizes do not explain. That the coordinates are built from the likelihood of the next action in a fixed state is consistent with the effect remaining in the local and early measures.
4 Composing Harness Effects in Agent Behavior Maps

Section 3 fixed the harness; this section fixes the LLM, varies the harness, and asks what structure the map has with respect to harnesses . A harness combines components for context management, planning, and acting. If adding a component appears as a displacement within an LLM and these displacements add up, the map reads as the position of the LLM plus a sum of component vectors, and the coordinates of an unscored combination follow from the scores of single components. We test whether the approximate additivity observed for prompt changes in LM maps ( Oyama et al., 2026 ) also holds for agent harnesses (§ 4.2 ).
4.1 Harness interventions and setup
Harnesses.
A harness splits the rule C i C_{i} that builds the LLM’s input from the external history h t h_{t} (§ 2.1 ) into three slots.
•
Context management (which part of the history is shown): windowed passes the initial observation and the last four steps verbatim; retrieve adds past steps retrieved for their relevance; summarize replaces old history with a structured summary.
•
Planning (whether a plan is rewritten before acting): none / replan.
•
Acting (whether free-form reasoning is written before the action): direct / think.
System instructions, tool definitions, and the initial observation are shared by all harnesses; plans and reasoning enter only the current step’s input, so the external history consists of actions and observations only and every harness builds its input from the same h t h_{t} , both when running and when scoring. The 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 3\times 2\times 2=12 combinations include BASE (windowed/none/direct), four single-component harnesses that add one component to BASE, and seven composed harnesses that add two or more (specifications in Appendix C.1 ).
Harness vectors.
For the same LLM m m , the difference between the coordinates of BASE and of the harness that adds component A A , Δ A ( m ) := z ⁡ ( m , A ) − z ⁡ ( m , base ) \Delta^{(m)}_{A}:=z(m,A)-z(m,\mathrm{base}) , is the harness vector , and the sum of harness vectors predicts the coordinates of a composition, z ^ ​ ( m , A + B ) := z ⁡ ( m , base ) + Δ A ( m ) + Δ B ( m ) \hat{z}(m,A{+}B):=z(m,\mathrm{base})+\Delta^{(m)}_{A}+\Delta^{(m)}_{B} .
Since the coordinates are centered log-likelihoods on the common reference trajectories, Δ A \Delta_{A} represents how component A A changes the relative likelihood of the reference actions; the change in the mean log-likelihood is reported separately as a scalar (Appendix C.1 ).
Setup.
All 12 configurations are scored, but only the coordinates of the five configurations BASE and single components enter the composition; the coordinates of the remaining seven are constructed as their sums. With 12 LLMs (listed in Appendix C.1 ), the coordinates of 60 agents thus yield composed coordinates for 84. The measured coordinates of the composed harnesses are used only for validation. The coordinates are obtained by teacher forcing on 359 HAL ( Kapoor et al., 2026 ) traces of τ \tau -bench ( Yao et al., 2025 ) ; ALFWorld and SWE-Bench (6 LLMs) are in Appendix C .
4.2 Predicting coordinates of unscored compositions
Does the sum of the single-component displacements approach the measured displacement z ⁡ ( m , A + B ) − z ⁡ ( m , base ) z(m,A{+}B)-z(m,\mathrm{base}) of the composition? Figure 3 shows one composition, retrieve + think, as an example; all seven are in Figure 5 (Appendix C.2 ). For all compositions, Table 12 in the appendix reports, within each LLM, the cosine between the measured displacement and the predicted displacement Δ A + Δ B \Delta_{A}+\Delta_{B} , the norm ratio (measured / predicted), and the relative reconstruction error ∥ measured − predicted ∥ / ∥ measured ∥ \lVert\text{measured}-\text{predicted}\rVert/\lVert\text{measured}\rVert , as medians over LLMs. We compare displacements from BASE because absolute coordinates share a large component common to the LLM, which alone would make them look close. The arrows of the same component also look parallel across LLMs in Figure 3 ; this cross-LLM agreement of directions is not analyzed in this paper.
For two-component compositions, the median cosine over the 60 cases (12 LLMs × \times 5 compositions) is 0.90 0.90 and the median norm ratio 0.98 0.98 , so the sum roughly matches in direction and length. The agreement varies by composition: retrieve with planning or reasoning agrees best (cosine 0.97 0.97 / 0.94 0.94 , relative error 0.25 0.25 / 0.34 0.34 ), compositions with summarize keep the direction but deviate more (cosine 0.91 0.91 / 0.87 0.87 ), and replan + think is weakest (cosine 0.81 0.81 , norm ratio 0.74 0.74 : the measured displacement is shorter than predicted). Three-component compositions reach cosine 0.82 0.82 and norm ratio 0.87 0.87 (median over 2 × 12 2\times 12 cases). On ALFWorld and SWE-Bench (6 LLMs) the agreement weakens to median two-component cosines of 0.63 0.63 and 0.81 0.81 (Table 12 ). Additivity thus depends on the components and the environment, but for context management combined with planning or reasoning, the direction and magnitude of the composed displacement can be approximated from single-component displacements.
5 Related Work
Maps of language models and policies.
LM maps ( Oyama et al., 2025 ) place models in a common coordinate system by their log-likelihoods on fixed texts and have been extended to prompt–response distributions ( Oyama et al., 2026 ) ; other works embed models by their outputs or correctness ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Yax et al., 2025 ; Zhuang et al., 2025 ) . All of them address static LMs. In reinforcement learning, policies are compared by their actions on a fixed set of states ( Parker-Holder et al., 2020 ; Pacchiano et al., 2020 ; Konyushkova et al., 2021 ) . We extend LM maps to closed-loop agents and quantify the cost of measuring on fixed trajectories as the term λ \lambda , which accumulates over steps like the compounding error of imitation learning ( Ross and Bagnell, 2010 ; Ross et al., 2011 ) . Predicting performance from scores on cheaper benchmarks ( Ruan et al., 2024 ; Song et al., 2026 ) shares our goal but needs the target model’s scores, whereas our coordinates need neither scores nor environment runs.
Harness effects and agent behavior.
Harness effects have been measured as changes in success rate by running every LLM–harness combination ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Yao et al., 2026 ; Fan et al., 2026 ; Cho, 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) or decomposed into LLM and scaffold abilities with IRT ( Ge et al., 2026 ) ; we represent a component as a displacement on the map, obtained without running the composed agent, and ask whether displacements add (§ 4 ). Evaluations increasingly report consistency across trials, cost, and behaviors found in logs alongside success rates ( Yao et al., 2025 ; Kapoor et al., 2025 ; Kapoor et al., 2026 ) ; harnesses with similar success rates can differ greatly in token use and failure patterns ( Vats and Golev, 2026 ) , and agent behavior carries fingerprints of the underlying LLM ( Sun et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Oderinwale, 2026 ; Lugoloobi et al., 2026 ) . All of these works run the agents and compare the resulting behavior through aggregate statistics or classifiers. We measure behavioral differences by distances obtained without running the agents, relate them to the trajectory KL with explicit error bounds (§ 2 ), and show that pairs at small distance behave alike when run (§ 3.3 ).
6 Conclusion
Agent Behavior Maps, obtained by teacher forcing on common reference trajectories, locate agents without running them. Their coordinates predict the success rates of models that were never run, on the benchmark the map was built from and, combined with size, in other environments. Neighbors on the map take similar action sequences when run. The theory decomposes the gap between the distances and the trajectory KL into four error sources and limits the guarantee to close pairs. Harness components appear as displacements within an LLM whose sums approximate the coordinates of unscored combinations, with an accuracy that depends on the component and the environment. The map represents not only a ranking by performance but also how close agents are in what they do.
Limitations
Locality.
Both the theoretical guarantee and the empirical correspondence are limited to close pairs. The KL can be read off the distance only for pairs in the local regime of small ε \varepsilon and λ \lambda , and pairs of different LLMs are not guaranteed to fall in it: the estimates exceed the measured KL by a median factor of 7.5, although their order agrees (§ 3.1 ). The correspondence with behavioral distance holds for neighbors and fades beyond about the 30th neighbor (Figure 1 (c)); we have not shown that the magnitude of the distance between far-apart pairs measures how different their behavior is. The map answers who is similar to whom, not by how much.
Dependence on the reference trajectories.
Differences between policies in regions that Q Q never visits cannot be measured, and the reference mismatch λ \lambda remains a sensitivity parameter that the likelihood matrix alone does not determine. A general theory of the reference policy is left for future work.
Estimation of the KL.
The trajectory log-likelihood ratios in § 3.1 are based on the probability of the canonical token sequence and differ from action probabilities that marginalize over all tokenizations. In environments where the candidate set can be enumerated, this can be resolved by renormalization, but in general it is a limitation.
Access to log-likelihoods.
The coordinates require the target model’s log-likelihood of a given action sequence. Models that are available only through an API that does not return the log-probabilities of given text cannot be placed on the map, so this study is limited to open-weight models.
Sample size of behavior.
The behavioral comparisons are based on one rollout per task, and the behavior of the same model varies from run to run ( Bjarnason et al., 2026 ; Gonuguntla, 2026 ) . We have not compared the size of this variation with the behavioral distance to neighbors.
Scope of the harness experiment.
The harnesses in § 4 are limited to the 12 configurations of our own three-slot design (context management, planning, acting) and to 12 LLMs; the main additivity results are on τ \tau -bench, and ALFWorld and SWE-Bench cover 6 LLMs. Whether the same additivity holds for other scaffolds with differently designed components has not been tested.

A.1 Grammar-constrained action probabilities
We define action probabilities under grammar-constrained decoding. A deployed agent can execute only outputs that match the environment’s action format, so its outputs are typically constrained to an action grammar, e.g., by constrained decoding. Our grammar constraint reflects this setting: the constrained policy is the distribution of actions the agent actually sends to the environment.
Logged actions are serialized by a common normalization map 𝒩 \mathcal{N} that preserves their effect on the environment. Let 𝒜 ⁡ ( h ) \mathcal{A}(h) be the set of action strings that satisfy the grammar under history h h . Agent i i reconstructs its input C i ​ ( h ) C_{i}(h) from h h with its rule C i C_{i} (§ 2 ). Let q i ​ ( v ∣ C i ​ ( h ) , v < k ) q_{i}(v\mid C_{i}(h),v_{<k}) be the model’s next-token probability given this input and a prefix v < k v_{<k} , and let 𝒱 ⁡ ( h , v < k ) \mathcal{V}(h,v_{<k}) be the set of tokens allowed by the grammar after v < k v_{<k} . Grammar-constrained decoding sets the probabilities of disallowed tokens to zero and renormalizes the rest: for v k ∈ 𝒱 ⁡ ( h , v < k ) v_{k}\in\mathcal{V}(h,v_{<k}) ,
q i 𝒢 ​ ( v k ∣ h , v < k ) = q i ​ ( v k ∣ C i ​ ( h ) , v < k ) ∑ v ∈ 𝒱 ⁡ ( h , v < k ) q i ​ ( v ∣ C i ​ ( h ) , v < k ) . q_{i}^{\mathcal{G}}(v_{k}\mid h,v_{<k})=\frac{q_{i}(v_{k}\mid C_{i}(h),v_{<k})}{\sum\limits_{v\in\mathcal{V}(h,v_{<k})}q_{i}(v\mid C_{i}(h),v_{<k})}.

B.4 Behavior: data, metrics, and additional results
This section defines the action sequences, distances, neighbors, and tests of § 3.3 , and reports measures, controls, and robustness checks not shown in the main-text table.
B.4.1 Definitions
Action sequences.
We use one trajectory per model and task; run conditions are listed in Table 4 . For the 50 ALFWorld valid_unseen tasks, the trajectories come from the same runs as the performance labels. An action sequence is the sequence of commands for the 50 TextWorld treasure-hunter tasks, the sequence of function names extracted from model outputs for the 25 BFCL multi_turn tasks, and the sequence of action strings for the 50 MiniWoB++ tasks. The action type is the leading verb, function name, or tool name. An action is judged invalid by the environment’s set of admissible actions in ALFWorld, by the environment’s feedback that it could not parse the command in TextWorld, by a turn without a function call in BFCL, and by a tool error in MiniWoB++.
Behavioral distances.
The four aspects in the main text are the base-2 Jensen–Shannon distance between action-type frequency distributions ( Endres and Schindelin, 2003 ) , the Levenshtein distance divided by the longer length ( Levenshtein, 1966 ; Rabanser et al., 2026 ) , the Jaccard distance between sets of action 2-grams, and the disagreement rate of the first action from the same initial state ( Bouchard and Chauhan, 2026 ; Gonuguntla, 2026 ) . The first three are computed per task and averaged over the tasks, at least 10, recorded for both models. The appendix tables add four more measures. The divergence position is 1 − k / min ⁡ ( T i , T j ) 1-k/\min(T_{i},T_{j}) , where k k is the number of steps before the first differing action. The conditional disagreement counts disagreement at step t t only for pairs whose action sequences agree up to step t − 1 t-1 , summed over t = 2 , … , 5 t=2,\dots,5 ; only pairs with at least 10 such cases are used. For the first action, conditional disagreement, and divergence position, two invalid actions are not counted as agreeing. The profile distance is the Euclidean distance between per-model vectors standardized within each benchmark; each vector contains task averages of the repetition rate T − | distinct actions | T − 1 \frac{T-|\text{distinct actions}|}{T-1} ( Gioacchini et al., 2024 ) , the consecutive repetition rate, exploration breadth measured as distinct actions / T /T , the invalid-action rate, which is the complement of grounding accuracy ( Ma et al., 2024 ) , the share of information-gathering actions, the format-error rate, steps / / limit, the rate of hitting the step limit, and the early-termination rate. For BFCL we also include the rate of empty turns. The success pattern is the fraction of tasks on which the two models’ outcomes differ, 1 | 𝒯 | ​ ∑ t | s i ​ t − s j ​ t | \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t}\lvert s_{it}-s_{jt}\rvert .
Choosing neighbors and testing.
Each model’s five neighbors are the closest models in centered coordinates by Euclidean distance, in size by the difference in log ⁡ | θ | \log|\theta| , in ℓ ¯ \bar{\ell} by the difference in mean log-likelihood on the reference trajectories, in family by taking models of the same family in order of size, and in success rate by the difference in measured success rate on that benchmark. For families with fewer than five other members, the remaining neighbors are taken from other families in order of size. The effect size is the mean behavioral distance to the remaining models minus the mean distance to the five neighbors, averaged over models and divided by the standard deviation of the behavioral distance over all pairs. Figure 1 (c) applies the same definition to the k k -th nearest model alone; ranks k ≤ 5 k\leq 5 are shown individually and larger ranks as bin averages. Significance is assessed by a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test ( Wilcoxon, 1945 ) paired by model. The comparison between coordinate neighbors and success-rate neighbors, marked † \dagger in the main-text table, uses the same paired test: it asks whether the distance to success-rate neighbors minus the distance to coordinate neighbors, divided by the all-pairs standard deviation, is positive. These are paired tests with models as the unit; pairs share models and no correction for multiple comparisons is applied, so the significance markers are descriptive guides rather than exact error rates. The main conclusions rest on effects that are large (at least 0.5 0.5 for coordinate neighbors in ALFWorld) and consistent across measures rather than on individual markers; small differences marked as significant, such as those between coordinate and success-rate neighbors in the other environments, should be read as suggestive.
B.4.2 Robustness of the neighbor effect
The main conclusion is that coordinate neighbors behave alike when run. We check that this does not depend on the choice of behavioral distance, on how performance is controlled, or on how the coordinates are built.
Does it hold for other behavioral distances?
Part A of Table 8 adds the divergence position, conditional disagreement, profile, and success pattern to the four distances of the main text. The divergence position and conditional disagreement, which measure divergence early in the episode, follow the same pattern as the first action. For the profile, which aggregates repetition and invalid-action rates, and for the success pattern, neighbors chosen by success rate are closer than coordinate neighbors. These measures reflect how often an agent fails and are therefore tied closely to its success rate.
Does it survive regressing out performance?
Instead of comparing with neighbors chosen by success rate, part B of Table 8 removes the effect of performance by regression. We regress each behavioral distance on the difference in success rate, the pair’s mean success rate and its square, and the difference in log ⁡ | θ | \log|\theta| , and compute the effect of coordinate neighbors on the residuals. We control for the mean success rate as well because strong models converge on the shortest solutions and therefore act alike. For the success pattern, we instead subtract its expectation under a null distribution generated by the curveball algorithm ( Strona et al., 2014 ) , which shuffles outcomes while keeping each model’s success rate and each task’s difficulty fixed. After this control, the effect of coordinate neighbors remains on every aspect in ALFWorld, the environment of the coordinates, and mainly on the local and early-step measures in the other environments.

Input construction.
The input is assembled in the order fixed part, history selected by context management, plan and reasoning for the current step, and action instruction. The fixed part consists of the system instructions, tool definitions, and initial observation (including the task instruction) and is shared by all harnesses. One block of the external history is an executed action and the resulting observation; past plans and reasoning are not included. The budget for the history part is 8,192 tokens, and the whole input is at most 16,384 tokens including the reserve for the output; when the budget is exceeded, the oldest blocks are dropped first and, if necessary, the latest observation is truncated to its head and tail.
Components.
•
Context management : windowed passes the initial observation and the last four blocks verbatim. retrieve additionally selects, from the older history, blocks relevant to the current observation by BM25 ( Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009 ) (weight 0.7) and recency (0.3) and inserts up to 1,536 tokens of them. summarize splits the older history, summarizes the parts, and merges them hierarchically into a JSON memory of at most 1,024 tokens that replaces the history. If the history has at most four blocks and uses at most 75% of the budget, neither retrieval nor summarization is applied and the history is passed raw.
•
Planning : replan rewrites from scratch, at every step and in at most 256 tokens, the confirmed progress, the next subgoal, and the observation that would confirm its achievement, and adds them to the input of the current step.
•
Acting : think generates, for the next single action, step-by-step reasoning of at most 512 tokens that organizes the goal, constraints, assumptions, and expected effect of the action, and adds it to the input of the current step (the plan, if any, is also included in the input).
Plans and reasoning are not carried over to the next step.
Generation and scoring.
Generation (rollouts) and scoring (teacher forcing) use the same construction; for scoring, each harness builds its input from the same reference history h t h_{t} , and the raw log-likelihood of the fixed reference action is computed. The action being scored, future observations, and rewards are not used in the input.
LLMs.
The 12 LLMs are Qwen3-8B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) , Qwen3.5-27B/9B/2B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) , Gemma4-E4B/12B/31B ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) , GPT-OSS-20B/120B ( OpenAI, 2025 ) , GLM-4.7-Flash ( Z.ai, 2026 ) , and Devstral-Small-2-24B ( Rastogi et al., 2025 ) , all among the 100 models of § 3.2 . SWE-Bench was scored with 6 of them (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3.8-27B, Gemma4-E4B, GPT-OSS-20B/120B, GLM-4.7-Flash).
