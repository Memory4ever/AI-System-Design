  1  Introduction 

 
      
  Figure 1:  End-to-end comparison of native test-case generation and validation

Figure   3   defines the oracle decisions and validity accounting. 
 
 
 LLM judges now assign benchmark scores, curate supervised fine-tuning data, provide preference and
reward signals for post-training, and inform deployment gates for models and prompts
 ( Zheng et al., 2023 ;  Liu et al., 2023 ;  Gu et al., 2024 ) . Their decisions are useful only when a scoring
implementation measures its stated criterion reliably. Agreement with human annotations on a
held-out benchmark provides important evidence, but an aggregate agreement score does not
characterize how a judge responds to input variations that should preserve or alter that criterion.
Behavioral testing applies controlled changes to characterize these responses before comparable
input variation occurs in deployment. 
 
 
 Behavioral testing evaluates whether a model satisfies focused, task-relevant
behavioral properties rather than only summarizing its average performance on held-out data. It
treats the model as a black box and evaluates its responses to targeted examples and controlled
input changes. These tests primarily
use two categories: minimum functionality tests, and metamorphic tests with invariance or
directional expectations. Minimum functionality tests evaluate
individual capabilities on targeted examples.
Invariance and directional tests state whether outputs should remain unchanged or move in an
expected direction after controlled input transformations  ( Ribeiro et al., 2020 ) .
In a reference-free setting, metamorphic
testing specifies the relation that should hold between scorer outputs before and after a controlled
input transformation  ( Chen et al., 1998 ;  Segura et al., 2016 ) . 
 
 
 Many metamorphic testing methods begin from fixed, human-authored recipes, which constrain
the available perturbations and the scoring criteria to which they apply.
CheckList established a fixed, reusable testing inventory for
NLP  ( Ribeiro et al., 2020 ) . Humans 
 
 
 specify capabilities and tests, then use the framework’s 
 
 
 templates, lexicons, and perturbations to generate concrete test cases. FBI adapts this approach to evaluator
LLMs.   Doddapaneni et al. (2024)   generate
perturbations for 22 human-designed categories and manually validate every generated perturbation,
producing a 2,400-case benchmark.  Jrh  instead provides an open-source harness that automatically
generates and executes judge-reliability tests from a
fixed, human-authored recipe library, with optional human review
 ( Dev et al., 2026 ) . Across these systems, automation begins only after
humans define the testing inventory.  Jrh , the closest prior system, draws from fixed tests for label
changes, formatting, paraphrase, verbosity, repeatability, and ordinal calibration. 
 
 
 A fixed recipe library works when each selected transformation has the same interpretation for
every scorer to which it is applied. This condition does not hold in general. Formatting changes may
be irrelevant to a faithfulness scorer but directly relevant to a professionalism scorer. Adding
relevant detail should reduce a concision score, but increase a coverage score when the detail
supplies missing salient information. A generic verbosity recipe cannot assign one expected relation
to both. The required perturbation and score relation therefore depend on the scorer specification,
its output contract, and the data being evaluated. 
 
 
 We introduce  Sane , a reference-free agent-based framework that automates capability
identification, scorer-specific recipe ideation, test-case generation, and validation. 
 
 
  Sane  organizes testing around six fixed behavioral requirements: monotonicity, subadditivity,
repeatability, robustness, dynamic range, and calibration. It encodes these requirements as reusable
agent instructions. The requirements remain fixed across scorers, while the capabilities,
perturbations, and expected relations that operationalize them depend on scorer intent. For each
scorer, the agent reads the specification, implementation, and representative inputs, identifies
capabilities pertinent to the scoring criterion, and ideates recipes and expected relations for each
capability.  Sane  next instantiates test cases for each recipe and validates the complete
progression from design to execution: whether the recipe agrees with scorer intent, whether the
generated text realizes the requested perturbation, and whether the expected relation applies to the
realized instance. Behavioral testing and metamorphic testing are well-explored foundations of
 Sane . We do not claim novelty in their behavioral requirements or relation types. Our
contribution replaces human-authored recipe design with an agent that
determines how general behavioral requirements apply
to each scorer and derives the corresponding perturbations and expected relations to target its
specific scoring domain. Since our
framework makes no assumptions about scorer type, the same procedure could be applied to multi-agent evaluators
and statistical metrics in addition to single-model LLM judges; we do not evaluate these
scorer types here. 
 
 
 We compare  Sane  against  Jrh , a recent open-source harness
for judge reliability and the most directly comparable prior system  ( Dev et al., 2026 ) , across
faithfulness, fluency, coherence, professionalism, and toxicity.
Figure   1   compares the two harness architectures and their
automation boundaries. Both methods use Claude Opus 5 and the same balanced 1,200-example
generation dataset, composed of 300 examples each from AggreFact-CNN, AggreFact-XSum,
TofuEval-MediaS, and TofuEval-MeetB.
To isolate the fully automated pipelines, neither method uses human review during generation. We
pass every generated recipe and test case to automated validation without manual editing, rejection,
or selection.  Sane  identifies each scorer’s capabilities and uses the six requirements
to derive scorer-specific perturbations and expected relations. Its aligned recipe
inventory therefore expands with the scorer set.  Jrh  continues to draw from its fixed human-authored
library. Across five domains,  Sane  accumulates 108 unique aligned recipes, while  Jrh  yields
seven. After test-case validation,  Sane  produces 3.7 times as many validated test families and
detects scorer
deficiencies in all five domains,
including professionalism and toxicity, where  Jrh  fails to detect
any under the evaluated configuration. 
 
 
 We make four contributions. First, we automate the scorer-specific reasoning required
to translate fixed general behavioral requirements into capabilities, perturbations, and expected
relations. This design expands the aligned recipe inventory as scorers are added. Second, we define a
formal, scorer-independent validation framework that separates recipe alignment, perturbation
realization, and instance-level relation applicability. Third, we show that  Sane  detects scorer deficiencies
with tests adapted to faithfulness, fluency, coherence, professionalism, and toxicity, including
scorer-dependent expected relations that  Jrh ’s fixed recipes did not express. Fourth, we evaluate
human alignment at the judge-ranking level and find that
 Sane ’s faithfulness test suite ranks 11 judge
models consistently with human-labeled FaithBench performance. Model-level rank concordance is
the relevant unit because we evaluate LLM judges themselves, not individual model outputs. 
 
 
 
  2  Related work 

 
 Behavioral testing in NLP and LLMs. 

 
 Behavioral testing evaluates focused, task-relevant properties through targeted examples
and controlled input changes rather than relying only on aggregate held-out performance.
CheckList adapted this approach to NLP through a fixed, human-authored matrix of linguistic capabilities and three test
types: minimum functionality, invariance, and directional expectation
 ( Ribeiro et al., 2020 ) . AdaTest later used language models to propose tests within a
user-guided topic tree. Users select valid suggestions and organize the tree to direct subsequent
generation  ( Ribeiro and Lundberg, 2022 ) . Recent systems automate more aspects of test-case
construction.   Ferrando et al. (2023)   generate source sentences and candidate
translations for predefined machine-translation capabilities. SynthEval uses controlled language-model
generation to find difficult cases, after which experts identify recurring failures and manually
design test templates  ( Zhao et al., 2024 ) . These methods reduce the cost of
instantiating and expanding tests, but retain human selection of capabilities, topics, or templates.
SANE automates the preceding scorer-specific decisions: it identifies pertinent capabilities and
derives recipes from the scorer specification, implementation, and representative inputs. 
 
 
 
 Metamorphic testing and automated relation design. 

 
 Metamorphic testing addresses the oracle problem by specifying relations that should hold between
source and follow-up executions  ( Chen et al., 1998 ;  Segura et al., 2016 ) .
Relation identification, input transformation, follow-up generation, and relation checking
are separate parts of this process. LLM-based systems now automate several of them. MR-Adopt
recovers a reusable input transformation from a developer-written test that already contains a
source input, follow-up input, and output assertion  ( Xu et al., 2024 ) .
  Cho et al. (2025)   collect 191 relations from prior NLP research and execute 38 of
them across four LLMs, demonstrating the breadth of applicable relations while retaining a curated
relation inventory.   Cañizares et al. (2026)   propose a domain-independent assistant
that infers new relations and generates follow-up cases, with validation and repair stages, for three
software domains. Their system operationalizes relations within a metamorphic-testing environment
and accepts domain context through a conversational interface. These results establish LLM assistance
for relation inference and follow-up validation outside judge testing. SANE instead defines and
evaluates a judge-specific entry point: given an arbitrary scorer, it derives the capabilities,
recipes, perturbations, and expected score relations required to test that scorer. 
 
 
 
 LLM judges as measurement instruments. 

 
 LLM judges can agree well with human preferences on summarization, dialogue, and pairwise model
comparison  ( Liu et al., 2023 ;  Zheng et al., 2023 ) . Agreement with human annotations does
not, by itself, establish that a judge measures its stated criterion consistently. Measurement
theory separates validity, which concerns whether scores represent the intended construct, from
reliability, which concerns variation under repeated application of the measurement procedure
 ( Chehbouni et al., 2025 ) . Empirical work confirms that these questions require
separate tests.   Lee et al. (2025)   measure self-consistency and agreement across
scoring scales, while Rating Roulette measures intra-rater reliability across repeated calls
 ( Haldar and Hockenmaier, 2025 ) . Recent work on contemporary proprietary judges shows that
an overconfidence advisory and single-call self-debate improve the calibration and score-distribution
spread of verbalized confidence  ( Hsiao, 2026 ) .   Thakur et al. (2025)   show that percent agreement can conceal
large score differences and report sensitivity to prompt complexity and lenient scoring. This line
of work establishes repeated execution, prompt variation, scale choice, and criterion validity as
distinct properties of a judge as a measurement instrument. 
 
 
 
 Behavioral testing of LLM judges. 

 
 Judge-specific test suites convert these reliability questions into controlled
evaluations. FBI defines 22 human-designed perturbation categories across factuality, instruction
following, long-form coherence, and reasoning. It generates 2,400 cases and manually validates
every perturbation  ( Doddapaneni et al., 2024 ) . CALM defines 12 judge biases and automates
principle-guided response modifications for each category  ( Ye et al., 2025 ) .  Jrh  generates and validates cases for a fixed suite covering label changes, formatting invariance,
semantic paraphrase, verbosity, stochastic stability, and ordinal calibration
 ( Dev et al., 2026 ) . It also supports human review before judge execution and is the closest
engineering comparison to SANE. CheckList, FBI, CALM, and JRH
demonstrate the value of targeted tests and automatic perturbation generation. In each system, the
test inventory remains fixed after its authors define the capabilities, test types, bias categories,
or recipes. 
 
 
 SANE automates scorer-specific metamorphic test design. Its reusable behavioral
requirements remain constant. The agent then determines how they apply to each scorer and produces a
new recipe inventory. SANE then separates three validity questions: whether a recipe agrees with
scorer intent, whether a generated case realizes the recipe, and whether the expected relation
applies to that realized case. Our contribution is an agent pipeline that reasons from general behavioral requirements
to derive scorer-specific capabilities, metamorphic recipes, and expected relations, then explicitly
accounts for validity from candidate recipe to validated test family. 
 
 
 
 
  3  SANE 

 
 Given a scorer specification and representative examples,  Sane  performs two
generation steps, as shown in Figure   1  . First, the agent identifies key
capabilities implied by the scorer and derives perturbation
 recipes  that target them. Each recipe states a perturbation and its expected effect on
scorer outputs. Second, the agent instantiates test cases intended to realize each recipe’s
perturbation. A recipe together with its generated test cases is a
 candidate test family . The collection of a scorer’s
candidate test families is its  candidate test suite . 
 
 
 Generation alone does not establish that a candidate test family measures its intended
claim. First, the recipe and expected relation must agree with scorer intent. The recipe-alignment
oracle shown in Figure   1   and detailed in
Figure   4  (a) predicts this condition, denoted by     A  r   A_{r}   . Second, an
effective test case must realize the recipe from which it was generated, and the expected relation
must apply for the test case. These two conditions, denoted by     Z   t  ,  r    Z_{t,r}    and
    I   t  ,  r    I_{t,r}   , are independently predicted by the case-realization oracle in
Figure   1   and detailed in Figure   4  (b).
Equations   4  –  6   formalize the three
decisions. A test case is valid exactly when it is joint-valid, meaning that all three are true,

 For each aligned recipe,  Sane  samples    n  n    test cases. Depending on computational budget

 validated test suite . Given this generation
and joint validation, the suite measures whether a scorer behaves reliably on capabilities

  4  Experimental design 

 
 Our experiments proceed in three phases. First, test-suite preparation applies the
generation and validation procedures from Section 3: we generate candidate test families, evaluate
the three oracle conditions in Equations   4  –  6  , and retain
joint-valid test cases and validated families (Figures   1  ,   3  , and
  4  ). Second, we execute the prepared test cases against their target scorers and
identify cases whose expected relations do not hold. Because oracle validation requires agreement on alignment, realization, and
applicability, we interpret a violated relation as a deficiency in the targeted capability and call
it a  fault . A test
case is  fault detecting  when its scorer outputs violate the expected relation, and a validated
family is  fault detecting  when at least one joint-valid case detects a fault. Third, we measure
human alignment in the form of whether harness
pass rates rank judge models consistently with an independent
human-labeled benchmark. These phases test whether modern agents can derive scorer-aligned
metamorphic recipes, whether those recipes yield validated and fault-detecting test families, and
whether the resulting test suites differentiate judge models consistently with human evidence. 
 
 
 Scorers and source data. 

 
 The experiment covers faithfulness, fluency, coherence, professionalism, and toxicity scorers. We
construct a balanced generation dataset from four summarization sources in LLM-AggreFact:
AggreFact-CNN, AggreFact-XSum, TofuEval-MediaS, and TofuEval-MeetB
 ( Tang et al., 2024 ) . We sample 300 examples per source, for 1,200 total. These sources span
news, media interviews, and council meetings while retaining a common summarization task. This
choice varies source genre without confounding scorer comparisons with a change in the underlying
task. Every method receives the same source examples. The primary analysis
excludes repeatability because identical-input replication does not require scorer-specific text and
would not measure recipe adaptation. 
 
 
 
 Generation conditions. 

 
  Sane  uses Claude Opus 5 for its agent pipeline. We regenerate the complete  Jrh  condition with
the same model for both synthetic generation and validation LLM stages. This control
prevents generator capability from determining the comparison. We preserve  Jrh ’s released recipes,
prompts, and expected relations. Both methods run without human intervention. We disable optional
human review and do not manually edit, reject, or select generated recipes or cases. This gives both
methods the same fully automated generation condition. 
 
 
 
 Oracle validation. 

 
 Oracle judges measure each of the three criteria necessary for a joint-valid test case in
Equations   4  –  6  . The recipe oracle receives the scorer
specification, generated recipe, and expected relation. The
case oracle receives the scorer specification, original and perturbed text, recipe, and expected
relation. It predicts recipe realization and relation applicability separately. We collect three
independent GPT-5.2 predictions for each decision and use majority voting over the panel.
The oracle inputs use the same fields for  Sane  and  Jrh  and include generated text verbatim.
We set     n  =  10   n=10    for every aligned scorer-recipe pair, which gives     τ  =  6   \tau=6    under the
strict-majority family rule. Appendix   C   evaluates every
threshold from     τ  =  5   \tau=5    through     τ  =  10   \tau=10    and shows that the central family-count and cross-domain
conclusions hold throughout. This choice satisfies budget constraints while retaining
enough repeated cases per recipe to estimate each judge’s criterion-specific test-suite pass rate.
Case-level fault rates and judge-ranking agreement use every joint-valid sampled case from aligned
recipes, including cases from families below the family threshold. The family threshold measures
consistency across generated cases; it does not invalidate a case that independently passes all
three oracle decisions. Cases with      J   t  ,  r    =  0   J_{t,r}=0    do not contribute to either analysis.
We count unresolved oracle predictions as invalid and unresolved scorer predictions as not
fault detecting, yielding conservative point estimates. 
 
   
  Figure 4:  Oracle inputs and decisions. (a) The recipe-alignment oracle determines whether a candidate
recipe and its expected relation agree with the scorer specification. For each aligned recipe,
(b) The case-realization oracle separately determines whether the generated text implements the
requested perturbation and whether the expected relation applies to that instance. 
 
 
 
 Fault detection. 

 
 We execute each scorer once per input using its designated model configuration. Before
analyzing faults, we validate every scorer against human labels from a domain-matched benchmark.
Appendix   A   reports the datasets, metrics, and construct scope. These
results provide external evidence that the scorer implementations measure their intended criteria. 
 
 
 
 Human alignment. 

 
 Our meta-evaluation asks whether validated test suites differentiate judge models according
to independent human evidence. We select faithfulness as a target domain because JRH’s
fixed recipes include applicable faithfulness transformations, making the domain representable
by both harnesses. At the judge-model
level, we measure two independent summaries of scorer quality. FaithBench AUROC summarizes how well
the model’s continuous faithfulness scores distinguish human-labeled faithful and unfaithful
examples  ( Bao et al., 2025 ) , while each harness’s pass rate summarizes how often the model
satisfies expected relations over joint-valid faithfulness test cases.
The faithfulness scorer elicits
verbalized confidence using an overconfidence advisory and single-call self-debate
 ( Hsiao, 2026 ) . We beta-calibrate the resulting scores to probabilities on     [  0  ,  1  ]   [0,1]   
using parameters fit on labeled AggreFact data separate from FaithBench. These metrics
measure different quantities, so we do not compare their numerical values. We instead measure
agreement between the judge-model rankings they induce using Spearman    ρ  \rho    and Kendall     τ  b   \tau_{b}   .
The primary panel contains 11 models and excludes Opus 5, which generated both test
corpora. GPT-5.2, which serves as the validation oracle, is also not a panel member. Panel results are computed from
777 FaithBench examples and the downsampled faithfulness test sets, which comprise 165  Sane  and 55  Jrh  joint-valid test cases. We use a
two-sided exact permutation test for Spearman    ρ  \rho   .

  5.2  Validated and fault-detecting test families 

 
 A test family groups one recipe with the test cases instantiated from it. 
 
 
 A case is fault detecting if its scorer outputs violate the case’s expected relation. A validated test family is
fault detecting if at least one joint-valid case violates its relation or a relation defined across
the family fails. 
 
 
  Sane  yields 77 validated test families from 133 candidates, compared with 21 from 35 for  Jrh  (Figure   6  (a)). The corresponding end-to-end rates are 57.9% and 60.0%. The rates are
similar, but  Sane  propagates this yield through a larger inventory of generated aligned
recipes, producing 3.7 times as many validated families. 
 
 
 Of these families, 31  Sane  families and 10  Jrh  families detect at least one scorer fault.
 Sane  produces fault-detecting families for all five scorers.  Jrh  produces
none for professionalism or toxicity. For coherence,  Jrh  detects faults in three of four validated
families and  Sane  detects faults in four of eleven. This shows that  Jrh  recipes can be
effective when they apply. The result is a more comprehensive scorer-specific test suite:
 Sane  creates more applicable families and detects faults across all five evaluated scorers.
 Sane ’s aggregate family-count advantage is independent of the evaluated threshold,
although its magnitude decreases at stricter settings. Across all measured per-scorer thresholds,
 Sane  retains fault-detecting families in all five domains
(Appendix   C  ).

  6  Limitations 

 
 Both harnesses are regenerated with the same model, so the comparison controls for generator
capability. What remains open is whether the absolute generation and realization rates transfer to
weaker agent models. While GPT-5.2 panels validate recipes and test cases at scale, human annotation
would provide an independent estimate of oracle error, which may influence reported
scorer-deficiency counts. Judge-ranking alignment is evaluated for
faithfulness, the criterion supported by both harnesses and an independent human-labeled benchmark.
Extending this analysis requires comparable benchmarks for other scorer criteria. 
 
 
 Future work should test additional generator models and human validation panels. A
retrieval library of validated scorer problems, capabilities, and recipes could reuse successful
designs across related scorer domains while preserving scorer-specific reasoning. 
 
 
 
  7  Conclusion 

 
  Sane  automates scorer-specific metamorphic recipe ideation from general behavioral requirements
and validates both recipes and generated cases before test execution. Across five scorers, this
procedure produces more aligned, validated, and fault-detecting test families than  Jrh ,
the leading open-source harness for judge reliability. On faithfulness,  Sane  test-suite scores provide human-aligned differentiation of judge models, supporting automated
scorer-specific testing as a practical method for LLM-judge reliability.
