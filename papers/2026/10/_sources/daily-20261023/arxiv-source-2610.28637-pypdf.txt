Abstract
DisaggregatedLLMserving separates prefill and decode into
distinct node pools, interposing a network fabric between
the moment a key–value (KV) cache is computed and the
moment it is consumed. This architectural shift invalidates
a core assumption of classical cache policies: that the cost
of a miss is simply recomputation on the same device. In
disaggregated systems, a miss triggers bothrecomputation
on a prefill node and anetwork transferof the resultingKV
block to the decode node—costs that differ by an order of
magnitude and depend on prefix length, model width, and
fabric bandwidth. Meanwhile,admittinga block to the global
KVpool requires an additional transfer at compute time, so
a poorly chosen keep decision wastes both memory and
bandwidth even before reuse occurs.
We presentKVLearn, a learning-based retention frame-
work that makes keep/evict decisions as first-class cost-
optimization choices in disaggregatedLLMserving. KVLearn
consists of three components: (i) a lightweightPrefix Reuse
Predictor(PRP) that estimates reuse probability from struc-
tural and temporal prefix features without touching model
weights; (ii) aCost-A ware Retention Score(CARS) that trans-
lates reuse probability into a keep/admit signal by account-
ing for per-block recompute, transfer, and storage costs;

1 Introduction
Large language model inference increasingly runs ondisag-
gregatedserving stacks that physically separate the prefill
phase (computing full key–value attention states for the
prompt) from the decode phase (generating tokens one by
one) [22, 23, 34]. Disaggregation improves cluster utilization:

prefill is compute-bound and benefits from tightly packed
kernels, while decode is memory-bound and benefits from
large batches of independent sequences. Production disag-
gregated deployments now route requests through dedi-
cated prefill node pools (P-nodes) and decode node pools
(D-nodes) [23, 34], with the computedKVtensors migrat-
ing over a high-speed fabric (InfiniBand, NVLink, or RoCE)
between them.
The retention gap in disaggregated systems.Prefix
caching is well-motivated even in single-node deployments:
when many requests share a common prompt (a system in-
struction, an image encoding, or a few-shot example), reusing
theKVblocks for that prefix avoids redundant quadratic at-
tention computation [8, 33]. Standard policies—LRU, LFU, or
radix-tree eviction—handle this by retaining recently or fre-
quently accessed blocks in on-device HBM.Disaggregation
breaks the cost model underlying these policies.In a disaggre-
gated cluster, a cache miss now entails:
1. Dispatching the request to a P-node for recomputation;
and
2. Transferring the resultingKVblock over the fabric to the
D-node before decoding can begin.
Both costs depend on prefix length, model depth, and fabric
bandwidth—none of which LRU/LFU tracks. Conversely,ad-
mittinga block to the sharedKVpool at compute time costs
an additional transfer (P-node → pool), so indiscriminate
caching wastes bandwidth and pool memory even for pre-
fixes that will never be reused. The fundamental asymmetry
is that in disaggregated serving, keeping a block and not
keeping a block arebothpolicy choices with concrete, mea-
surable costs—not an oversight that any uniform threshold
can fix.
Multimodal workloads amplify the problem.Mul-
timodalLLMs encode visual inputs (images, video frames)
into long token sequences before autoregressive decoding [1,
9, 18]. A single 448×448 image typically contributes 256–576
visual tokens; high-resolution images or short video clips
may produce 4k+ tokens. TheKVfootprint for such prefixes
is large (128–512 MB for a 13B model in bfloat16), the re-
compute cost is correspondingly high (∼100–400 ms on an
A100), yet reuse patterns are uneven: popular images (prod-
uct thumbnails, dataset evaluation images) are queried many
times per hour, while user-uploaded photos may never recur.
A policy that treats all visual prefixes identically—keeping
all or evicting all—leaves most of the available reuse value
on the table.
Our approach: learn the keep decision.We observe
that prefix reuse is not uniformly random: structural features
(prefix length, modality, radix-tree depth, session metadata)
and temporal features (access recency, frequency, time-of-
day patterns) carry substantial predictive signal for whether
a block will be requested again within a given horizon. We
build a lightweight online predictor—trained in the serving
critical path without any offline profiling—that scores each
prefix with a reuse probability, and combine it with a closed-
form cost model to produce a keep/evict signal that is directly
calibrated to the fabric and memory configuration.
Contributions.We make four contributions:
1. We formalize thedisaggregatedKVretention problem(§3):
a joint admission and eviction decision under heteroge-
neous recompute, transfer, and storage costs. We prove
when the optimal admission threshold must vary with
prefix length (𝑞> 1idealization; Appendix A)—beyond
what LRU/LFU can express.
2. We designKVLearn(§4): a three-component framework
comprisingPRP(prefix reuse predictor),CARS(cost-
aware retention score), andATC(adaptive threshold
controller).
3. We establish that prefix reuse probability islearnable on-
linefrom lightweight structural and temporal features
with high accuracy (§5), validating the feasibility of the
learning-based approach.
4. We demonstrate end-to-end gains on both language and
multimodal workloads (§5): up to 56% TTFT reduction vs.
No-Cache (38% vs. LRU-Pool, 33% vs. Mooncake-LRU),
53% inter-node transfer volume reduction vs. LRU-Pool,
and near-Oracle throughput on MM-Session at realistic
pool sizes.
2 Background and Related Work
2.1 Disaggregated LLM serving
Splitwise [22], DistServe [34], and Mooncake [23] each sep-
arate prefill and decode to different GPU pools and manage
KVmigration explicitly. Mooncake introduces a dedicated
KVcache layer between P- and D-nodes, enabling cache-first
disaggregation: prefill results are stored in a pooled cache
and transferred to D-nodes on demand. Our work targets
exactly this cache layer—specifically,what to keep in itrather
than how to transfer what has been decided to keep. Prior
disaggregated work either caches everything (bounded by
pool capacity with LRU fallback) or mirrors the policy from
single-node stacks; neither accounts for the asymmetric cost
structure we formalize in §3. LMCache [3] targets fast pre-
fill via cross-request cache reuse, InfiniStore [ 2] provides
RDMA-oriented distributedKVstorage (e.g., integrated with
vLLM through LMCache), and CXL-based serving systems
such as CXL-SpecKV [11], SAC [20], and TraCT [30] disag-
gregateKVonto shared or FPGA-attached CXL memory.

2.2 Prefix and radix caches
Open serving stacks—vLLM [ 8], SGLang [33], and TensorRT-
LLM [21]—focus heavily on single-nodeKVefficiency. Page-
dAttention in vLLM virtualizesKVblocks to reduce frag-
mentation. SGLang’s RadixAttention [33] cachesKVin a
radix tree for automatic prefix sharing, with LRU-based evic-
tion at capacity. CacheBlend [29] selectively recomputes a
subset ofKVtokens to amortize staleness. At the serving-
system layer, TinyServe [10] selects query-relevantKVpages
at decode time, and PiKV [12] manages expert-shardedKV
for MoE inference. These single-node systems share the as-
sumption that miss cost ≈ recompute cost; in disaggregated
deployments, miss cost also includes network transfer time
andP-nodedispatch latency. KVLearn is complementary: it
can replace the eviction policy inside any radix-tree cache
layer, including Mooncake’s pool, without changing the radix
structure.
2.3 Cache admission and online learning
Admission filters for web caches (TinyLFU [5], LeCaR [26])
use lightweight frequency sketches or reinforcement learn-
ing to decide which objects enter cache. Learned caching
(Belady-inspired predictors [17], CACHEUS [24]) trains of-
fline on traces. We target anonlinesetting—no offline corpus
of serving traces is available at deploy time—and incorpo-
rate a cost-heterogeneous objective specific toKVblocks
in transformer inference. The closest learning-based work
is LRB [25] (learning to predict the next-access time); we
differ in online adaptivity, explicit cost modeling, and the
multimodal inference context.
2.4 KV compression and quantization
Orthogonal to retention, quantization lines reduce bytes
per block [ 6, 7]. Streaming attention [ 27, 28] discards
distant tokens within a sequence, and algorithmic cache
designs reshape in-model reuse—e.g., hardware-aware
memory-efficient caching [14, 32] and learnable/adaptive
activation caching for diffusion transformers [ 13, 15, 16].
These techniques changehow mucheach block costs orhow
activations are reused; KVLearn changeswhichblocks are
retained. They compose naturally: a quantized block in the
pool has the same admissibility decision structure, just with
smaller|KV 𝑏 |.

4 The KVLearn Framework
4.1 Overview
Figure 2 shows the KVLearn architecture within a disaggre-
gated serving stack. KVLearn adds a thinretention control
planethat runs on the same hosts as the KV pool coordina-
tor. The control plane intercepts two events: (1)block arrive,
when a P-node finishes prefilling a new or updated prefix
and must decide whether to admit the block to the pool;
and (2)pool full, when the pool reaches capacity and must
choose one or more blocks to evict. All decisions are made
in the critical path of a request, subject to a latency budget
of≤0.5ms per block.

Pool data structure and eviction ordering.The pool

Lazy score refresh.CARS (𝑏) becomes stale as PRP ac-
cumulates new labels and pool-state features drift. We adopt
a lazy refresh policy: when a block is considered for evic-
tion, its score is recomputed if the last PRP inference is older
than a staleness threshold 𝜏stale (default: 30 s). In practice,
re-scoring the bottom 10% of the heap at each eviction pass
keeps ordering error below 5% of blocks; a full heap rebuild
is triggered only when the PRP model is updated (every 100
gradient steps,≈200 ms on a co-located CPU core).

5.1 Setup
Hardware.We use two cluster configurations: (a)Small:

ordered pairs; the resulting per-image access frequency
is heavy-tailed (empirical Zipf exponent ˆ𝛼=1.08, fitted
via MLE), consistent with image-access distributions
reported for production multimodal serving [19]. Average
visual prefix: 576 tokens (LLaVA-1.5 ViT-L/14); average
text continuation: 128 tokens.

P-node hardware; heterogeneous clusters (mixing A100 and
H100 nodes) require per-nodeR calibration. The ATC PI con-
troller may oscillate under bursty workloads if gains𝐾 𝑃, 𝐾𝐼

traces (LLaVA-Instruct-150K, ActivityNet-QA, ShareGPT)
rather than proprietary production logs; while the access-
skew (§5.8) and horizon (§5.9) sensitivity sweeps confirm the
results are robust across a wide parameter range, integration
with a live Mooncake or DistServe cluster and validation
against closed production traces are reserved for future work.
Hardware design perspective.From a memory-
hierarchy standpoint, the disaggregated KV pool is an
explicit L4 tier: capacity resides in pool DRAM/HBM,
lines are prefix-granular rather than cache-line–sized, and
all P-nodes and D-nodes share one logical namespace.
Proposition 1 implies that utility-maximizing retention
islength-heterogeneous: at equal predicted reuse, long
visual prefixes should rank lower for eviction because
R (𝑏) − T (𝑏) grows with 𝐿𝑏—something LRU/LFU cannot
capture with recency or frequency alone. KVLearn encodes
this bias in per-block CARS scores; hardware LLCs pursue a
similar goal through utility- or size-aware replacement and
victim structures for large lines. A future pool controller
could attach footprint and calibrated (R,T ) tags to each
block and evaluate retention in the memory fabric (e.g.,
CXL expanders), avoiding per-admission control-plane
round-trips—a direction we leave to future work.
Caching per-prefix KV blocks may retain sensitive con-
tent (user images, PII in prompts) beyond the originating
request’s lifetime. Deployments must apply retention poli-
cies consistent with data protection regulations: KVLearn’s
admission API accepts per-prefix TTL overrides, allowing
operators to force eviction of sensitive-prefix blocks on a
schedule.
7 Conclusion
We identified and formalized theKVcache retention problem
in disaggregatedLLMserving: a joint admission–eviction
decision whose optimal policy depends heterogeneously
11
PAGE 12
SYSTOR ’26, September 8–9, 2026, Virtual Event, Israel Liu et al.
on prefix length, fabric bandwidth, and recompute cost—
making classical LRU/LFU inadequate. KVLearn addresses
this through a lightweight online predictor (PRP), a cost-
aware scoring function (CARS), and a closed-loop threshold
controller (ATC). Evaluations on language-only and multi-
modal workloads show up to 56% TTFT reduction vs. No-
Cache (38% vs. LRU-Pool), 53% inter-node transfer volume
reduction vs. LRU-Pool, and near-oracle throughput on MM-
Session at realistic pool sizes (modestly larger gaps on other
workloads)—all without modifying model weights or chang-
ing the serving routing logic.
The broader takeaway is thatwherea KV block is com-
puted andwhereit will be consumed are no longer the same
node in modern disaggregated deployments, and retention
policies should account for the intervening network as a first-
class cost. As visual token lengths grow (4k–16k for high-
resolution and video models) and serving clusters scale to
hundreds of nodes, the gap between intelligent and naive re-
tention will only widen. Future directions include hardware
memory-side retention primitives, joint KV-compression and
retention co-optimization, and federated reuse prediction
across cluster boundaries.
