Abstract
World models predict how embodied systems evolve under actions, but their accuracy can degrade when physical conditions move outside the training distribution. We study two components of causal modeling: which inputs a predictor may use, and how its mechanism computes their effects. In a synthetic benchmark with shortcut features, a frozen dependency mask derived from paired interventions reduces mean out-of-distribution (OOD) RMSE from 1.769 for a fully connected predictor to 0.697, close to the generator-oracle mask (0.694). For robot link-mass extrapolation in CausalWorld, with disjoint training and test mass ranges, we develop finger-local world models in which link masses enter inertia and dynamics-bias terms through explicit affine coefficients, actions act through either a learned torque network or the known PD controller, and learned residuals capture contact-related effects. Compared with a separately trained control whose mass inputs are fixed at training means, the known-PD model turns out-of-range masses into a consistent predictive gain: median time-averaged joint R 2 R^{2} over 150-step rollouts rises from 0.609 to 0.727, with improvement in all ten task–seed runs (exact two-sided sign test, p ≈ 0.002 p\approx 0.002 ). In a shared 100-step forecast, the learned-torque model is competitive with the strongest recurrent robotic world model (RWM) configuration (median joint R 2 R^{2} of 0.755–0.768 versus 0.752), whereas supplying out-of-range masses sharply degrades the MLP and recurrent predictors. These results indicate that how a physical parameter enters the dynamics mechanism determines whether its out-of-range value helps or harms prediction.
1 Introduction
World models are used to predict the evolution of embodied systems as they execute actions [ 1 ] . Their prediction accuracy may degrade when physical conditions deviate from the training environment. Recent theory connects generalization across multi-step goals to learned predictive world models [ 2 ] . Embodied applications require these models to remain useful under changing conditions, such as when a robot handles a different payload or operates with different physical properties. Pearl’s structural causal model (SCM) framework separates structural dependencies from the functions governing those relationships [ 3 ] . This separation motivates two questions for a world model: which inputs should a predictor use, and how should its dynamics mechanism compute their effects?
Causal dynamics models, such as CDL [ 4 ] and recent structure-distribution approaches [ 5 ] , learn or estimate dependencies between current state, action, and next state, and use them to restrict irrelevant inputs to the dynamics predictor. Such constraints remove direct prediction paths from excluded shortcut inputs. However, if the OOD shift involves a true causal factor, such as a heavier robot link, the relevant dependency must remain. The computation performed after reading the shifted value is then determined by the mechanism. Input selection alone does not specify how this function should extrapolate. Recent context-aware mechanical models and structured physical world models further motivate examining how parameter information enters the dynamics mechanism [ 6 , 7 ] .
We develop a robot world-model configuration that assigns physical parameters and control inputs distinct roles: link masses enter inertia and dynamics bias through explicit affine coefficients, actions produce torque through the known PD controller, and a neural residual models contact-related effects without directly receiving robot masses. The dependencies are fixed to each finger’s local state and action, with shared object context. We compare this configuration with an MLP and mass-affine dynamics with learned torque under the same input routing. Each model is paired with a separately trained mass-information control: mass inputs are removed from the MLP or fixed at their training means in the structured models. A complementary synthetic experiment uses an intervention-derived mask to evaluate shortcut dependence. Figure 1 summarizes the robot architecture and prediction procedure.
Figure 1: Overview of the robot world model. Three finger mechanisms predict joint accelerations under fixed input dependencies; one mechanism family is used per model. A separate object MLP predicts object-state changes. Joint integration and object updates produce future states, which are fed back for rollout prediction. Training uses simulator trajectories; evaluation uses link masses outside the training range.
The experiments connect dependency selection to physical parameter extrapolation. Frozen intervention-derived masks reduce mean synthetic OOD RMSE by 60.6%. In CausalWorld, supplying out-of-range link masses to an unstructured MLP or to a recurrent robotic world model sharply degrades prediction, whereas the known-PD mass-affine model improves its median time-averaged joint R 2 R^{2} over 150 steps from 0.609 for its fixed-mass-input control to 0.727. The median within-run benefit of mass information is 0.121 under the heavy-mass shift, compared with 0.009 within the training mass range. In a shared 100-step benchmark, the learned-torque model is competitive with the strongest recurrent baseline (median joint R 2 R^{2} of 0.755 with actual masses and 0.768 with fixed mass inputs, versus 0.752), and the known-PD model retains a positive mass benefit in every run.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
•
A mass-informed robot world-model design. We develop finger-local mass-affine dynamics with learned-torque and known-PD variants. Masses enter inertia and bias through explicit coefficients, while torque and residual networks use mass-excluded inputs. Joint integration and a separate object-state predictor generate future trajectories.
•
Competitive OOD prediction in a shared CausalWorld benchmark. Over a common 100-step forecast, the learned-torque model reaches median joint R 2 R^{2} of 0.755 with actual masses and 0.768 with fixed mass inputs, compared with 0.752 for the strongest tested RWM configuration, with slightly lower joint NRMSE and object-position RMSE and without using observation history. The accuracy margins are small; the larger difference lies in how the models respond to out-of-range mass inputs, which sharply degrade the MLP and recurrent predictors.
•
Consistent predictive benefit from out-of-range masses. Separately trained mass-information controls show that the known-PD design improves median 150-step mean joint R 2 R^{2} from 0.609 to 0.727. Actual masses improve prediction in all ten task–seed runs in both the 150-step ablation and the 100-step benchmark; the latter yields a median paired mass gain of 0.071 (exact two-sided sign test, p ≈ 0.002 p\approx 0.002 in each case).
2 Problem Statement
Given state s t s_{t} , action a t a_{t} , robot link masses m m , and other physical context c c , we predict
s ^ t + 1 = F θ , G ​ ( s t , a t , m , c ) , \hat{s}_{t+1}=F_{\theta,G}(s_{t},a_{t},m,c),
(1)
where G G specifies permitted input dependencies and θ \theta parameterizes the mechanisms. The robot mass study assumes that the current link masses and the simulator’s low-level control law are available. The networks learn dynamics coefficients from state-prediction supervision, using the current mass values to specify the physical condition. A rollout starts from an observed state and repeatedly applies F θ , G F_{\theta,G} using predicted states and the recorded action sequence.
We distinguish two sources of distribution shift. In a shortcut shift , an input correlated with an output during training changes its relationship to that output; excluding an irrelevant input removes that prediction path. In physical parameter extrapolation , a relevant parameter takes values outside the training support. The model must retain its dependence on that parameter and compute its effect at unseen values. The synthetic study evaluates fixed masks in a benchmark containing shortcut shifts. The robot study isolates a link-mass shift under a shared dependency structure.
The robot evaluation asks both whether predictions are accurate and whether actual mass information improves them. For mechanism family r r and task–seed run b b , we define the predictive benefit of mass as
Δ r , b = R 2 ¯ r , b actual − R 2 ¯ r , b control , \Delta_{r,b}=\overline{R^{2}}_{r,b}^{\mathrm{actual}}-\overline{R^{2}}_{r,b}^{\mathrm{control}},
(2)
where R 2 ¯ \overline{R^{2}} is the mean joint R 2 R^{2} across the evaluated rollout steps. The controls and score aggregation are specified below. This separates absolute prediction accuracy from the additional value of providing the physical parameter.
3 Related Work
Causal dynamics and dependency constraints.
SCMs separate dependencies from the mechanisms generating variables [ 3 ] . Theoretical studies connect robustness under specified distribution shifts to causal world-model knowledge, including settings in which actions affect the environment [ 8 , 9 ] . CDL learns causal input dependencies for dynamics prediction and task-independent state abstraction [ 4 ] ; FCDL refines the structure through quantized state–action subgroups [ 10 ] . More recently, CADY estimates a distribution over causal structures and samples masks for a probabilistic dynamics model, evaluating robustness to corrupted inputs and changes in robot dynamics [ 5 ] . CoDA uses local causal structure for counterfactual data augmentation [ 11 ] , while BECAUSE learns causal representations to address confounding in offline model-based reinforcement learning [ 12 ] . We fix the dependency structure to study the functional response to a supplied physical parameter outside its training range.
Composable and masked world models.
WM3C uses language-guided compositional causal components to model unseen environments [ 13 ] . Causal-JEPA uses object-level latent masking to encourage interaction-dependent prediction and evaluates visual reasoning and predictive control [ 14 ] . These approaches organize latent information and interactions. Our study uses recorded physical states and known link masses, focusing on how a dynamics mechanism turns an out-of-range parameter into a useful change in its predicted trajectory.
Structured mechanics and physical parameterization.
DeLaN embeds Lagrangian mechanics into learned dynamics [ 15 , 16 ] ; classical inertial identification uses the linear dependence of inverse dynamics on inertial parameters [ 17 ] . Structured Mechanical Models and APHYNITY combine known physical structure with learned components [ 18 , 19 ] . Recent extensions are particularly relevant: Context-Aware DeLaN combines recurrent online identification with contextual mechanical models and residual dynamics for a robot arm under varying loads [ 6 ] ; Floating-Base DeLaN imposes branch-induced sparsity and physical consistency on learned inertia matrices [ 20 ] . Both directly build on DeLaN. Our configuration makes measured masses explicit affine coefficients of inertia and bias, routes actions through a known controller, and assesses parameter utility using separately trained mass-information controls.
Physical world models and extrapolation.
Architectural functional form influences neural extrapolation [ 21 ] . Wang et al. study structured physical world models with learned energy, dissipation, known drive channels, and separable intervention-parameter maps in electromagnetic and fluid systems [ 7 ] . This combines imposed physical structure with learned system-specific functions. We study contact-rich robot prediction under link-mass changes, using a mass-affine joint model with a separately learned state- and velocity-dependent bias and an object-state predictor. The target is the predictive benefit of known masses over finite robot rollouts.
History-based adaptation and robotic prediction.
UP-OSI estimates physical parameters from state–action history, while CaDM conditions dynamics on a learned context; RMA uses an adaptation module for policy control [ 22 , 23 , 24 ] . Context-Aware DeLaN further couples online identification to structured mechanics [ 6 ] . RWM uses recurrent history and autoregressive training for long-horizon robotic prediction [ 1 ] . These methods motivate separating inference of unknown context from use of a supplied parameter: in our mass experiments the actual link masses and low-level control law are available, and the question is how the dynamics representation uses them.
Evaluation and baseline methods.
PETS combines probabilistic ensemble dynamics, trajectory sampling, and model-predictive control [ 25 ] . CausalWorld provides configurable robot and object properties for embodied transfer studies [ 26 ] . We use CausalWorld for both environment-transfer prediction with a PETS-style dynamics baseline and the link-mass mechanism study, with separate metrics for the two protocols. Appendix A identifies the sources of established components and our design choices.
4 Methodology
4.1 Fixed dependencies and model decomposition
The robot world model contains three separately parameterized finger mechanisms and one object-state MLP. Each finger uses its own three joint positions, three velocities, three actions, four mass entries, and shared object state and context. Its mechanism predicts three joint accelerations, which are integrated to update that finger’s joint state. The object MLP reads all joint positions, velocities, and actions together with the object state and context, and predicts a 13-dimensional state increment. It receives robot-mass effects through the evolving state. This input routing is fixed across the three mechanism families; each model learns its own network weights.
For the synthetic dependency experiment, the mask is estimated from paired interventions. For each intervened input i i , we average the absolute output change and normalize each output column:

4.4 Training and mass-information controls
Models are first trained on one-step transitions and then fine-tuned on free-running trajectories. The multi-step objective combines teacher-forced one-step error with rollout error:
ℒ = 𝔼 ​ MSE ⁡ ( s ^ t + 1 TF − s t + 1 σ Δ ​ s ) + λ H ​ ∑ k = 1 H 𝔼 ​ MSE ⁡ ( s ^ t + k roll − s t + k σ s ) , \mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}\,\operatorname{MSE}\!\left(\frac{\hat{s}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{TF}}-s_{t+1}}{\sigma_{\Delta s}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{H}\sum_{k=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}\,\operatorname{MSE}\!\left(\frac{\hat{s}_{t+k}^{\mathrm{roll}}-s_{t+k}}{\sigma_{s}}\right),
(11)
where MSE \operatorname{MSE} averages squared components, λ = 1 \lambda=1 , and normalization statistics are fitted to training data. Each rollout begins with an observed state and then uses its own predictions. Model selection uses validation trajectories from the training mass domain.
We use three complementary mass-information controls. First, a separately trained MLP removes robot-mass inputs. Second, each structured model has a separately trained counterpart in which mass inputs are overwritten by their training means during both training and evaluation. These counterparts use the same varying-mass simulator trajectories as the corresponding actual-mass models. Third, an inference-time diagnostic replaces the trained MLP’s test mass inputs with training means while retaining its weights. This last comparison measures the effect of the shifted input on an already fitted predictor.
5 Simulation Results
5.1 Experimental setup and metrics
The synthetic benchmark has two interacting objects, 32 inputs, six next-state outputs, and 17 distractor inputs. Its OOD split changes physical inputs and shortcut relationships. We compare dense, random, intervention-derived, and generator-oracle masks using the same predictor architecture, data, and loss. Random masks match the oracle’s edge count. Inputs and outputs are standardized using training statistics, and RMSE is computed in those standardized output coordinates over ten seeds.
The robot experiment uses CausalWorld pushing and picking tasks [ 26 ] . Nine movable-link masses vary, while three base masses remain fixed, giving 12 mass inputs. Each varying mass is sampled within its simulator space A bounds ( ℓ r , h r ) (\ell_{r},h_{r}) as
m r = ℓ r + u r ​ ( h r − ℓ r ) , u r ∼ U ⁡ ( 0 , 0.4 ) ​ for training , u r ∼ U ⁡ ( 0.6 , 1 ) ​ for OOD testing . m_{r}=\ell_{r}+u_{r}(h_{r}-\ell_{r}),\qquad u_{r}\sim U(0,0.4)\ \text{for training},\quad u_{r}\sim U(0.6,1)\ \text{for OOD testing}.
(12)
The mean test-to-training mass ratio is approximately 1.85. Both domains are inside platform space A, with a gap between the light and heavy training/test subranges. Per task–seed run, 1,200 light-mass episodes are split into 864 training, 96 validation, and 240 in-distribution (ID) test episodes; 300 separate heavy-mass episodes form the OOD test. Each episode has 150 steps at 250 Hz, and action targets change every 50 steps.
The 31-dimensional state contains nine joint positions, nine velocities, and the object’s position, quaternion, linear velocity, and angular velocity. Other context consists of object mass, three size coordinates, and object, floor, and stage friction. Network inputs are standardized using training statistics; the PD rule uses joint positions and velocities in physical units. Finger and coefficient networks have two SiLU hidden layers of width 128; the object MLP has three hidden layers of width 256. Acceleration limits use ten times the training 0.999 quantile of absolute velocity increments divided by Δ ​ t \Delta t ; velocity limits use three times the maximum absolute training velocity. Actual mass improves R 2 ¯ \overline{R^{2}} in all ten task–seed runs, with a mean within-run gain of 0.120 (median 0.121; exact two-sided sign test, p ≈ 0.002 p\approx 0.002 ). The learned-torque model achieves medians of 0.734 and 0.733 for actual and fixed mass, respectively, with positive within-run mass benefit in three of ten runs. The known-PD configuration consistently converts supplied out-of-range mass into predictive benefit under this protocol. Its fixed-mass-input control is, however, markedly weaker than the learned-torque control (0.609 versus 0.733). One plausible explanation is that, with torque fixed by the known controller, the inertia and bias terms are the only channels through which the model can represent heavier links, so the PD mechanism relies on mass information more than the learned-torque model does. The mass benefit should therefore be read together with the absolute scores. Comparisons between mechanism families concern complete configurations, including their scaling and training settings; the matched controls quantify the benefit of mass information within each family.
5.4 Mass benefit across domains and rollout horizons
Figure 2 shows the known-PD mass ablation over the full prediction horizon. Actual mass has a higher median score than fixed mass at every reported step. At step 150, the medians are 0.518 and 0.439, respectively. The positive benefit therefore extends to the end of the evaluated rollout.
The benefit is larger when the parameter moves outside the training range. Taking the actual-minus-control difference within each run and then its median gives 0.121 on heavy-mass episodes and 0.009 on ID episodes. The OOD benefit exceeds the ID benefit in every task–seed run. This connects the result to the intended application: when the robot’s physical parameters change beyond the observed range, the structured mechanism makes the known parameter useful for trajectory prediction.

We compare the structured mechanisms with Robotic World Model (RWM) [ 1 ] , a recurrent predictor trained with autoregressive state feedback. We evaluate training forecast lengths of 8 and 50 steps, each with actual-mass and no-mass inputs. This evaluates recurrent prediction alongside explicit physical parameterization; Appendix B.2 gives the adaptation and training settings.

RWM additionally receives the preceding observed history. The forecast contains 100 steps at 250 Hz (0.4 s), with predicted states fed back throughout. All models use the same episode splits and training-derived normalization. Its fixed-mass-input variant obtains the best absolute scores in Table 4 : 0.768, 0.440, and 2.650 cm. The paired mass comparisons identify the distinctive result of the known-PD design: actual masses improve prediction consistently, while supplying them to the recurrent predictors or the learned-torque model does not yield a positive median mass benefit in this benchmark.

5.6 Environment-transfer comparison with a probabilistic ensemble baseline
To test dependency constraints with an established dynamics-model family, we compare an intervention-masked predictor with a PETS-style probabilistic ensemble [ 25 ] on CausalWorld pushing and picking. Each prediction maps an initial physical description and a nine-dimensional action to the simulator observation after holding that action for 50 environment steps. Data collection retains examples in which the object moves at least 5 mm; action pools are constructed separately in each domain using the same movement criterion. 6 Conclusions and Future Work
We developed a robot world-model design combining mass-affine dynamics, alternative torque mechanisms, and learned residuals under fixed finger-local dependencies. Frozen intervention-derived masks improve synthetic OOD prediction. In the shared CausalWorld benchmark, the learned-torque configuration is competitive with the strongest tested RWM configuration, with or without mass inputs. Supplying out-of-range masses sharply degrades the MLP and recurrent predictors, whereas the known-PD configuration makes actual mass information consistently useful: median 150-step mean joint R 2 R^{2} increases from 0.609 to 0.727 relative to its fixed-mass-input control, and positive mass benefit is observed in all ten runs of both the 150-step ablation and the 100-step benchmark.
The present evaluation assumes available link masses and a known low-level controller for the PD variant, and covers forecasts of 0.4–0.6 s. The robot dependency structure is specified by finger-local routing rather than learned, and the evaluation uses two tasks with five seeds each. Longer deployments, uncertain parameter estimates, and downstream control remain to be evaluated. Future work will examine intervention-based learning objectives and energy-consistent relationships between inertia and bias, with matched torque-model comparisons to isolate how controller knowledge affects mass use. These results show that explicit mechanism design can turn out-of-range physical parameters into useful information for embodied prediction.

Appendix B Complementary Benchmark Details
B.1 Environment-transfer predictors and metric
The environment-transfer data record simulator state variables and physical descriptors together with nine action coordinates. Constant source features are removed, and remaining inputs and output observations are standardized using source-training statistics. The two tasks use ten seeds each. Per run, 20% of the 8,000 source examples form an ID test set; the remaining examples are split 85:15 for model fitting and validation. A separate pool of 4,000 paired source interventions estimates dependencies. The effect threshold is 0.03 times the largest measured effect, and action dependencies are kept open because the paired pool intervenes on state variables rather than actions. No target prediction scores are used in model validation.
The PETS-style baseline has five bootstrapped probabilistic MLPs, each with two hidden layers of width 200 and SiLU activations. Each network predicts a mean and diagonal log variance, is trained with Gaussian negative log likelihood, and uses Adam with learning rate 10 − 3 10^{-3} , batch size 512, at most 300 epochs, and validation patience 30. Point predictions average the five network means. Its graph variant retains an input if the estimated mask connects it to at least one output; the masked predictor instead uses output-specific dependencies. All variants use the same source observations and target observations. Graph variants additionally use the independent source intervention pool, and the comparison evaluates their complete predictive configurations.
The intervention-masked predictor uses an eight-dimensional scalar encoding, 16-dimensional edge messages, and encoder and output-head hidden widths of 64. It is trained with squared prediction error, Adam at learning rate 3 × 10 − 3 3\times 10^{-3} , batch size 512, at most 300 epochs, and validation patience 30. The reported 0.6122 configuration does not add a paired-response loss. The paired data are used to estimate its mask, and the mask is held fixed during predictor training.

B.2 RWM implementation and shared-window evaluation
The comparison adapts the official RWM state-dynamics module [ 1 , 28 ] at commit 18eebcdd7145284c8d5eed5d8ed1a4b96c649693 . Its two-layer, 256-unit GRU feeds separate mean and log-standard-deviation heads, each with a 128-unit ReLU hidden layer. The adapter uses the official sampled autoregressive regression and variance-bound losses. The input has 59 coordinates: 31 states, nine actions, 12 robot masses, and seven object/environment context values. No-mass variants zero all 12 standardized mass slots during training and evaluation, retaining the input dimension and initialization of their actual-mass counterparts. This benchmark evaluates the state predictor; policy optimization and auxiliary reward or termination heads are outside its prediction task.
RWM receives 32 observed state–action pairs and trains to forecast either 8 or 50 future states. Adam uses learning rate 10 − 4 10^{-4} , weight decay 10 − 5 10^{-5} , batch size 1,024, a gradient-norm limit of 1.0, and variance-bound weight 1.0. Each configuration has a budget of 50,000 gradient updates, with ID validation every 250 updates. Early stopping uses 20 validation checks without improvement after at least 2,000 updates. Selection minimizes joint NRMSE over the common 100-step ID validation forecast. Both training horizons use this selection protocol. The four structured configurations use their existing validation-selected checkpoints from Table 1 .

RWM feeds back its predicted mean at evaluation, and the shared evaluation applies quaternion normalization. The hold-state reference repeats s 32 s_{32} at every forecast step.
