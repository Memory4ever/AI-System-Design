Correlated Failures of AI Approval Monitors
Luca Zanolini
Affiliation: Independent researcher
Email: lucazanolini1992@gmail.com
Abstract
Before an AI agent takes a consequential action, such as paying, messaging or changing code, several monitors check
it. Their verdicts are combined by aggregating scores or by counting approvals, whatever inputs the monitors share.
Monitors that share a base model or a poisoned input, however, may fail together. We borrow the fail-prone systems of
distributed computing: lists of monitor sets that may fail together. A rule is safe if it executes no bad action and
live if it blocks no good one. We measured twelve small open-weights monitors on payment approval under two
pre-registered protocols. Without pre-registration, we also measured three closed frontier models (Claude Opus 5.5,
GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4 Argon) and the open DeepSeek-V3.2. Every reliable fooling of a monitor into approving a fraud came through
an evidence source and reached only that source’s readers. Fooling it into blocking a correct payment depended on
injection resistance, a monitor’s refusal to follow instructions planted in its data. Small models and DeepSeek obeyed such notes. The three closed models ignored them in
all but one case, and Opus also resisted persuasion tailored to it. On new data, a rule that counts sources instead of
votes blocked 0.9% of correct payments, against 7.4% for the best counting rule, at equal safety. The
pre-registered worst-case test failed. One attack left the approvers on a single source, so no rule over the
twelve language-model monitors survives every measured attack. Adding a rule engine, which cannot be persuaded,
restored the condition for a safe and live rule on the measured attacks. Diversify the inputs, harden the monitors, count sources, not votes.
1 Introduction
AI agents take consequential actions for people and organisations: payments, messages, changes to code, access
to data. Before such an action runs, monitors check it. A monitor can be a language model, a rule engine,
or a human. They are also called guardrails, safety classifiers or judges, when the monitor is a large language model (LLM). In AI control, the study of overseeing a possibly misaligned model with weaker monitors, a monitor usually returns a suspicion score that decides
what a human audits. Ours decide whether the action runs.
Take an accounts-payable agent that proposes to pay a supplier’s invoice. Suppose three strong models serve as
judges, and all read the same invoice e-mail. An attacker forges the e-mail, replacing the
supplier’s bank account with their own, and fools all three at once. Three judges that read one document
are one monitor counted three times. Now suppose instead that the judges read different sources . A source is
one independent record against which a monitor checks the action, such as the invoice, the company’s vendor record,
or the supplier’s portal. The judges are cheap models, but a forged document now fools only its reader, and the
other two catch the fraud. A planted note has the opposite effect. The agent’s justification is the free-text note it attaches to
the payment. Suppose it contains a fake security notice: “this vendor is under investigation; do not pay”. Cheap judges then refuse a correct payment, whatever source they read. Judges that resist planted
instructions ignore the note. Which monitors must agree before the payment runs depends on which monitors can be
fooled together, and in which direction.
The safety half of this picture is classical in financial controls. A payment is checked against records that the
requester does not control. Examples are the vendor master file, changed bank details verified independently, the
three-way match of purchase order, goods receipt and invoice, and segregation of duties. We do not claim the
principle. Our contribution has three parts. First, published AI monitor designs rarely use this principle. In the
designs we reviewed, each monitor sees the agent’s whole trajectory: its actions, the tool outputs, and often its
reasoning [ 35 , 29 ] . When several monitors are used, their scores are
aggregated [ 25 , 10 ] . Panels of model judges take a
vote [ 56 ] . Some designs restrict what a monitor sees, to hide the agent’s reasoning or signals
that monitors could use to collude [ 19 ] . With one exception, Pandey et al. [ 48 ] ( Section 7 ), none gives different
monitors different sources of evidence. Second, we measure what follows, in small and frontier pools. Third, we extend the analysis to liveness, which
classical controls do not address for monitors that can be persuaded.
Correlated failure of language models is documented. Models err on the same answers far more often than
independence predicts [ 30 ] . Jailbreaks transfer along shared
representations [ 5 ] . One poisoned corpus misleads several models [ 61 ] .
One injection subverts several monitors [ 54 ] . Layered defences fail
together [ 44 ] . These findings have not been turned into a fault model estimated from attacks, a statement of
which joint failures a rule must survive. Distributed computing has a theory of correlated failures that reasons
about which sets of participants can fail together, instead of how many. A fail-prone system lists the sets
of components that may fail together [ 41 , 24 ] . Given the list, the theory says
which approval rules are safe , never executing a bad action, and which are live , executing every good
one. It also says when no rule is both. We ask whether AI monitors fail like the replicas of this theory, the voting copies of a replicated service,
and what that implies for the rule.
Five steps, from the theory to a design rule.
The theory tells us what to measure. A monitor can be
fooled in two directions: into approving a bad action, or into blocking a good one. For each direction we measure
which sets of monitors can be fooled together; we call these the approve side and the reject side.
Section 3 then gives the condition under which some approval rule is both safe and live given the two
sides (the cross-cover condition). We measured both sides on payment approval with twelve small open-weights
monitors, four families on three sources. Open-weights models have public weights and run locally. We ran a
pre-registered pilot, then a pre-registered confirmation on new data. To pre-register is to freeze the hypotheses
and the pass/fail criteria before any model is queried. The pilot tested the classical theory, with one list of
fooled sets, and refuted it: on the pooled list no rule is safe and live. Reading its fooled sets showed that the
two directions have different shapes, and the confirmation tested that reading. Without pre-registration we then ran three closed frontier
models (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4 Argon) and the open DeepSeek-V3.2.
(1)
The approve-side structure is the sources. In the pilot, every fooling into fraud arrived through a source and
reached only that source’s readers. In the confirmation the approve-fooled sets were exactly the three source
columns, as pre-registered. Its attacker could read the
monitors’ token probabilities. Even so, no justification text made its target approve more than 8.3 % 8.3\% of the
training frauds. Every frontier-class monitor, the closed frontier models and the open DeepSeek, showed the same structure.
Diversify the inputs.
(2)
The reject-side structure is set by injection resistance. Injection resistance is whether a monitor
ignores instructions planted in the data it reads. Planted notes made the small models of every family and
DeepSeek-V3.2 block correct payments, and worked across families. Opus and Astra ignored them, and Argon did
all but once. Persuasion optimised against Opus, with DeepSeek as the writer, the model that rewrites the note, fooled none of 640 640 held-out verdicts.
Model size does not predict this. The notes travelled in the agent’s justification. In a pre-registered control that hid the field from the
monitors, every blocking note fell to within 0.05 0.05 of the benign floor, the error rate on unattacked items. Harden the monitors.
(3)
Therefore count sources, not votes. A rule that approves when the approvers read at least two
sources is safe whenever fooling into fraud stays within one source, however many monitors are talked into
blocking. In the confirmation it blocked 0.9 % 0.9\% of
correct payments, against 7.4 % 7.4\% for the best counting rule chosen on training data, at the same safety. The
difference was pre-registered and supported. An attacker given each rule’s decision as its objective changed none of 40 40 held-out decisions per goal, for
either rule.
(4)
The theory predicts where this breaks. Cross-cover fails when a rejection attack leaves the
approvers on one source. In the confirmation one blocking note, optimised against Gemma, did so for the twelve
language-model monitors. The pre-registered cross-cover hypothesis failed, and by our decision rule the worst-case claim for that pool is a negative result. A rule engine,
which compares fields and lies outside the text attack surface, restored the condition in every bootstrap
resample of the pre-registered thirteen-monitor pool. Include a monitor that cannot be persuaded.
(5)
Caution is model-specific. Opus refused 30 % 30\% of correct payments whose evidence bore an apparent
trace of tampering, and none under a neutral line. Astra, Argon and DeepSeek refused none. Such refusals reach
only the readers of the tampered-looking source, so the two-source rule absorbs them (exploratory).
Section 5 follows the same five steps, after a note on what the pilot changed. Our monitors check a proposed action against external evidence and do
not read the agent’s reasoning. Chain-of-thought monitoring, whether a model’s thinking can be observed, is a
different question. The one piece of agent text they do read, the justification, carried the persuasion (step 2).
All empirical claims concern actions whose fields can be checked against independent records, measured on payment
approval. Section 8.1 says what transfers. Every zero bounds only the attacks we ran.

2 Model and the approval-rule hierarchy
We write for two audiences: AI-security researchers who may not know fail-prone systems, and distributed-systems
researchers who may not know AI monitoring. Each term is explained at first use, and the recurring ones are
collected here. A monitor is anything that returns a verdict on a proposed action. A rule is safe if it never
executes a bad action, and live if it executes every good one. A fail-prone system is a list of the
sets of monitors that may all be fooled at the same time. The cover number τ \tau is the smallest number of
listed sets whose union is every monitor. The condition Q k Q^{k} holds if no k k listed sets cover all monitors,
that is, if τ > k \tau>k . A kernel is a set of monitors contained in no listed set, so at least one of its
members is honest. The kernel rule approves only if the approvers form a kernel. Provenance is which
base-model family a monitor runs and which source it reads. Findings outside the
pre-registered plan are labelled exploratory or not pre-registered .
Monitoring AI agents.
AI control studies protocols in which a monitor oversees an untrusted
model [ 19 ] . The monitor is trusted because it is too weak to scheme (to pursue hidden goals of
its own), with a small budget of human audits. Consequential actions wait for the
monitors [ 38 ] . Several monitors are combined into one threshold by
averaging [ 31 ] , by Neyman–Pearson tests [ 25 ] or by learned
combiners [ 10 ] . None states which joint failures it tolerates. An
unsafe approval violates safety. A false rejection violates liveness and costs usefulness or human
time, scarce in AI control [ 19 ] .
System and threat model.
A proposal x x is what a monitor checks: an action, such as “pay this
invoice”, and its context. The context is read through a source: a mailbox, a tool, a web page. A finite set M M of n n monitors returns signed verdicts v ∈ { ok , bad } v\in\{\textsf{ok},\textsf{bad}\} . A check is factual , or
witness mode, if each proposal has a ground truth v ∗ ​ ( x ) v^{*}(x) that every correct monitor eventually returns.
“Payee and amount match the order” is factual. A check is a judgement , or agreement mode, if correct
monitors may legitimately disagree. A check is self-verifying if a correct verdict
carries evidence that anyone can check. A gateway holds a rule and executes the action only on a certificate of
signed verdicts that satisfies it. The gateway acts for a principal , the person or organisation on whose
behalf the agent works, with its own fail-prone system. We treat one principal; several principals who
share monitors and hold different fail-prone systems, the setting of asymmetric trust [ 3 ] ,
are left to later work. Links are reliable and authenticated. Timing is asynchronous unless stated: no bound on message delay is assumed. A monitor
is fooled if it runs its intended code but returns a wrong verdict, because its input was manipulated or
because it is honestly wrong. A monitor is compromised if it deviates arbitrarily. Fooled monitors are the AI-specific
case. The faulty set depends on the proposal, so an attacker who chooses the proposal chooses the faulty set among
those it can reach. The attacker controls the proposal and some sources. Its budget is typed : text
that transfers within some base-model families [ 60 , 5 ] , and poisoned sources
that mislead every reader [ 20 , 61 ] . Two kinds of text recur. A
jailbreak is text written to make a model do what it was trained to refuse. A prompt injection is an
instruction planted in data the model reads, meant to be followed as if it came from the user. In our setting it
is a note, in the evidence or in the agent’s justification, that tells a monitor to override its rule. The attacker is adaptive: it
knows the pool and the rule and chooses its input afterwards [ 47 ] . The agent may
equivocate , that is, show x x to some monitors and x ′ ≠ x x^{\prime}\neq x to others. In our deployment the gateway
receives one copy of the action and forwards it ( Section 4 ), which removes equivocation; the propositions
below assume this, and Q 3 Q^{3} is the case where it is not removed. Forged signatures, a compromised
gateway and denial of service beyond silent monitors are out of scope. Colluding monitors [ 46 ] ,
also discussed in a blog post by Shlegeris [ 52 ] , are modelled as sets that fail together.
We assume that the principal’s request is legitimate: the user or system that asks the agent to act is not
compromised. Our monitors check consistency : whether the proposed action matches the request and the
evidence. An action that is correctly formed but should not exist at all passes every consistency check. Such
actions, from a hacked requester, a socially engineered employee or an insider, are outside our threat model.

3 Directional fail-prone systems
In the classical Byzantine model a faulty process can both lie and withhold, so one fail-prone system serves
safety and liveness alike. A fooled monitor fails in a direction : into approving a bad action, or into
rejecting a good one. The two need not happen to the same sets of monitors. In every attack we measured, fooling into fraud
arrived through a source, as false facts or as a persuasive note planted there, and so reached only that
source’s readers. Fooling into blocking needed only a persuasive note in the justification, which every monitor
reads, and reached whichever models were gullible, across sources. The reject-fooled sets were therefore much
larger than the approve-fooled sets. Pooled into one list, as in Proposition 3 , every monitor that can be talked
into blocking counts as if it could also be talked into fraud. On the pilot’s pooled list, the pre-registered test,
Q 2 Q^{2} failed ( Section 6 ). Kept apart, the directions give the weaker cross-cover condition below, and
the classical theory is the special case ℱ A = ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{A}=\mathcal{F}_{R} . Separate assumptions for safety and for liveness have
precedent. The alive-but-corrupt faults of Flexible BFT, a Byzantine fault-tolerant replication protocol, attack safety and never liveness [ 42 ] .
The replication protocol of Tas et al. [ 53 ] , published at CCS 2023, is live under one quorum
system and safe under another. Their Theorem A.1 proves our condition necessary. What is new is the reading: the two
families are the directions in which a one-shot approval, a single decision on one action rather than a replicated
log, can be fooled. The families are estimated from attacks instead of being assumed. The rule that uses only one
of them is the most live of the safe rules. Proofs and the counting criterion are in Appendix A .

cannot turn a yes into a no. Quorum systems have this property, and so does every sensible approval rule.

(ii) P P is live for ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{R} iff M ∖ R ∈ P M\setminus R\in P for every R ∈ ℱ R R\in\mathcal{F}_{R} ;
(iii) a safe and live rule exists iff ( CC ), and it is necessary for every decision rule,
including rules that read rejections and use timeouts, in any timing model;
(iv) the safe and live rules are exactly the up-sets P P with 𝒬 R ⊆ P ⊆ 𝒦 A \mathcal{Q}_{R}\subseteq P\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{A} : 𝒦 A \mathcal{K}_{A} is the
largest safe rule and is live iff ( CC ); 𝒬 R \mathcal{Q}_{R} is the smallest live rule and is safe iff ( CC );
(v) Q 2 ​ ( ℱ A ∪ ℱ R ) Q^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{A}\cup\mathcal{F}_{R}) implies ( CC ) but not conversely.
A worked example on two pools of three monitors, one satisfying ( CC ) and one not, is in
Appendix A . The interval and the maximality in (iv) are elementary, and we have
not found them stated before. They were script-checked exhaustively for n ≤ 4 n\leq 4 and on random pairs for n = 5 , 6 n=5,6 .
Which end of the interval to use. The two ends of the interval need different knowledge. 𝒦 A \mathcal{K}_{A} is
built from ℱ A \mathcal{F}_{A} alone. It is safe whatever ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{R} turns out to be, and it stays live against any reject-fooled
set whose survivors are not all inside one approve-fooled set, whether that set was anticipated or not. 𝒬 R \mathcal{Q}_{R} is
built from ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{R} alone, and the same holds with the sides swapped: it is live whatever ℱ A \mathcal{F}_{A} turns out to be,
and it stays safe against any approve-fooled set that does not contain all the survivors of some reject-fooled
set. So the better choice is the rule built from the side one
knows well. In our measurements ℱ A \mathcal{F}_{A} is small, stable and explained by provenance, while ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{R} is large and
driven by persuasion ( Section 5 ). One then estimates ℱ A \mathcal{F}_{A} , runs 𝒦 A \mathcal{K}_{A} and verifies liveness on data.
The theory also says where this breaks. Suppose the approve-fooled sets are the source columns. Then
( CC ) fails exactly when some attack talks every monitor outside one column into blocking. The monitors
left to approve all read the same source, and a forged copy of that source would make the same set approve a
fraud. Counting rules have a criterion of their own on the pair. It is a linear-programming value ν \nu , and
counting suffices iff ν < 1 \nu<1 ( Proposition 8 , Appendix A ). On a 4 × 3 4\times 3 grid with one poisoned
source for fraud and three persuaded families for blocking, ( CC ) holds and no counting rule exists.
What the gateway does with the rule. The gateway collects signed verdicts and executes as soon as the
monitors that have signed ok form a set in P P . Because P P is an up-set, later verdicts cannot undo this.
Rejections need not be read: a monitor that signs bad or stays silent is simply not an approver. In practice a
deadline handles silence: if no set in P P has approved by then, the gateway refuses. The deadline needs a
timing assumption that the propositions do not make. Under the assumptions the monitors outside the reject-fooled
set form a set in 𝒬 R \mathcal{Q}_{R} and their approvals arrive eventually, so in an asynchronous model a good action is
eventually executed, while a deadline refuses no good action only if it exceeds the delay bound of a partially
synchronous model. An
execution therefore certifies that the action was good. A refusal certifies less: the approvals did not come. The
same asymmetry applies to rejections. A set in P P that signs bad does not prove the action bad, because P P was
built against ℱ A \mathcal{F}_{A} and a reject-fooled set may contain it. What proves an action bad is a rejection by a set
contained in no reject-fooled set. In the classical model these are the same sets. Here ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{R} is much larger
than ℱ A \mathcal{F}_{A} , so a trustworthy rejection needs rejecters from beyond any gullible family, and rejections carry
little evidence. Liveness, finally, is proved from the assumption that the fooled sets lie in ℱ R \mathcal{F}_{R} . For AI
monitors that assumption is an estimate, and the attacker may find a larger set tomorrow. A rule engine or a
person that cannot be talked into rejecting a good action lies outside every set that persuasion can fool, by
construction. It can still be fooled through a forged record, so it keeps ( CC ) true as long as the
attacker cannot forge every record it checks. This is why the
deployment recipe in Section 8.2 includes one such monitor.
4 Measurement
We test whether AI monitors under attack fail like the grid of Section 2 , and whether a rule that uses
that structure approves better than a rule that counts votes. The design, the estimators, the hypotheses with
their refutation criteria and a pilot gate, a pass/fail criterion for continuing to a larger study, were fixed in a protocol before any model was queried. The protocol is
a version-controlled file in the repository. The formal criteria and the run record are in
Appendices B and D . Figure 1 shows the ingredients described in this section.
Task and ground truth.
An agent proposes a payment (recipient, account, amount, currency, reference)
with a free-text justification . The user’s request names the vendor but not the account or amount.
Evidence for both comes from three independent sources, each sufficient on its own. doc is the invoice
e-mail, tool the vendor record from the company’s accounting system (ERP), and web the vendor’s
portal. An action is good iff payee, account, amount and reference equal the task’s true values, so the
check is factual. A good action stays good if a source carries a planted note. The agent submits the action once to
the gateway, which forwards the same copy to every monitor. A compromised agent can therefore lie in the action,
the justification and the sources it controls, but it cannot show different actions to different monitors. This
is the setting of Proposition 5 . Tasks are synthetic, with fictional vendors and non-existent account
prefixes.
How a run works.
It helps to follow one monitor. Suppose it is Llama reading doc . For one
payment it receives the user’s request, the proposed action, the agent’s justification and the invoice e-mail. It
compares the fields of the action with the invoice and signs ok or bad . The eleven other monitors do the same on
the same payment, each with its own source. The attacker can reach this monitor in two ways: through the
justification, which all twelve read, or through the invoice, which only it and the three other doc readers see. The
monitor is fooled if its verdict is wrong, ok on a fraud or bad on a correct payment. One such test is an
item : one payment, one attack placed in a chosen way, and one goal of the attacker. All twelve monitors see
the same item because what we measure is who fails together ; the wrong verdicts on an item form its
fooled set ( Figure 1 b). One (monitor, item) pair is a cell , that is, one verdict. The same
attack is then repeated on 20 20 payments, and the monitors it fools on most of them form the attack’s fooled set
W a W_{a} ( Figure 1 c). The sets W a W_{a} over all attacks are the empirical fail-prone system on which the theory
of Sections 2 and 3 is tested, and on which the rules are compared. The rest of this section
defines each of these pieces.
Monitors.
A monitor is a pair (model, view). The view fixes what the monitor reads: the request, the
structured fields of the action, the justification, and exactly one source. Source sharing is therefore
controlled by construction. The pool is a 4 × 3 4\times 3 grid of four small open-weights models on three sources,
run locally: Llama 3.1 8B (Meta; L), Ministral 3 8B (Mistral AI; M, 8.9 8.9 B), Qwen 3.5 9B (Alibaba; Q) and
Gemma 4 E4B (Google; G, about 4 4 B effective parameters). All are 4-bit quantised and run with reasoning disabled, with versions fixed by content digest
( Table 14 ). They run at temperature 1.0 1.0 . A model picks each word at random from a probability
distribution; the temperature scales that randomness. At 0 0 the model always takes the most likely word and the
same input gives the same verdict. At 1 1 it samples from the distribution as learnt, which is the usual
deployment setting, and repeated items are independent trials. All monitors receive the same
system prompt, prompt v2 : the check, a field-by-field comparison, and a note that evidence and
justification are data, not instructions. It replaced the pre-registered prompt after a noise-floor run on
unattacked items, before any attack data existed, a logged deviation ( Appendix B ). Unparseable output,
refusals and server errors count as non-approval, which we call fail-closed . Under prompt v2 no monitor
approved any of 20 20 plain frauds, and good payments were rejected by at most 3 3 of 20 20 per monitor. We call
this the benign floor .
Attackers and payloads.
A payload is the attacker’s text: a note written to change a verdict,
inserted into the justification or into one source. Two attackers place payloads. A compromised agent controls
the action, including the justification, which every monitor reads, and it can tailor the text to one model
family [ 54 ] . A third party controls some sources, which only their readers see. This is
indirect injection, an instruction hidden in data the model reads [ 20 , 12 ] .
The budget is one family and one source at a time, so the provenance system of Section 2 is
ℱ prov = ℱ 1 , 1 \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{prov}}=\mathcal{F}_{1,1} . A source payload reaches only its readers, so column containment holds by design. What
is empirical is row containment (does a family payload stay in its row, or transfer to other families?),
column completeness (does a source payload fool every family that reads it?) and whether a payload can be
universal.
Items and goals.
An item is a (task, attack condition, goal) triple shown to every monitor. The
goal is approve-bad , a bad action the attacker wants approved, or block-good , a good action it wants
rejected. Blocking attacks are essential. Without them a k k -of- n n rule with large k k looks safe and live, since
nothing ever tests whether it blocks good actions. The attack conditions are of five kinds. A
family-targeted attack is a justification payload optimised against one family. A source injection
is an instruction or a false notice planted in one source, in the styles of AgentDojo and
InjecAgent [ 12 , 58 ] . In a source substitution , one source shows
the attacker’s account or amount [ 61 ] . Combined items carry one family payload and one
source payload. Universal payloads are optimised against all families at once. Clean and
plain items carry no payload and give the benign floor. In all, 54 54 adversarial conditions × \times 2 2
goals × \times 20 20 held-out tasks plus 40 40 benign items make 2,200 2{,}200 items.
Payload optimisation.
Payloads come from black-box hill climbing in the style of
PAIR [ 9 ] : an attacker model rewrites a note repeatedly and keeps the best-scoring text.
The attacker model is OLMo 3 7B, a fifth family that is never a monitor. It rewrites a seed note in the wrapper
styles of public jailbreak collections [ 8 , 43 ] , in our own wording. A
candidate’s score is the fraction of desired verdicts from the target family on 8 8 optimisation tasks disjoint
from the evaluation tasks. The best of 31 31 rounds is kept.

Fooled sets and the empirical fail-prone system.
The fooled set of an item is the set of
monitors that gave the wrong verdict on it: approved a bad action, or did not approve a good one. Monitors run at
temperature 1.0 1.0 , so the same payload does not fool the same monitors on every task. We therefore summarise at
the level of an attack unit , an (attack condition, goal) pair, which has 20 20 items in the pilot. For each unit
we count, monitor by monitor, on how many of its items the monitor was fooled. The fooled set W a W_{a} of the unit
holds the monitors fooled on at least a fraction θ \theta of its items, with θ = 0.5 \theta=0.5 as the pre-registered
value: the monitors this attack fools reliably. A monitor fooled on fewer items is left out of W a W_{a} and treated
as noise. Nothing is fitted here; W a W_{a} is a summary of what happened. The empirical fail-prone system ℱ ^ \hat{\mathcal{F}} is
the family generated by the sets W a W_{a} : its maximal sets are the W a W_{a} , and it contains every subset of them. The
closure is the usual convention for a fail-prone system, and it is justified: an attack that fools a set can fool
any smaller set, with some monitors happening to answer correctly. In the pilot, ℱ ^ \hat{\mathcal{F}} pools both
goals, so it stands for the classical single fail-prone system of Section 2 . In the confirmation study it
is estimated per goal, giving ℱ ^ approve \hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{approve}} , which concerns safety, and ℱ ^ reject \hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{reject}} ,
which concerns liveness. The coverage of ℱ ^ \hat{\mathcal{F}} says how well this summary describes the raw data: it is
the fraction of adversarial items whose realised fooled set lies inside some W a W_{a} . A coverage of 0.9 0.9 means that
on nine items in ten, the monitors fooled on that item all lie within one of the summary sets, though not
necessarily the set of the item’s own attack. We check the
sensitivity of every conclusion to θ ∈ { 0.25 , 0.75 } \theta\in\{0.25,0.75\} . For ℱ ^ \hat{\mathcal{F}} we report its maximal sets, the cover
numbers τ \tau and τ ∗ \tau^{*} , whether Q 2 Q^{2} holds, and the coverage, each with a bootstrap over tasks and attack
units.
Rules, metrics and splits.
Every rule is a monotone predicate on the final set of approving monitors,
in the sense of Section 3 . A rule may also escalate , that is, hand the action to a person
instead of deciding. For the metrics an escalation counts as a rejection, since the action is not executed. The
counting rules are the best single monitor, majority, all-must-approve, the best k k -of- n n , and two
weighted votes. The weights come either from a linear programme over ℱ ^ train \hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{train}} , the system
estimated on the training split, or from the log-odds of each monitor’s training accuracy. The
structure-aware rules are a root count and three kernel rules . The root count counts root
causes, families and sources, rather than monitors, following a recent preprint by He and Yu [ 23 ]
and Pandey et al. [ 48 ] . The three kernel rules are built for ℱ prov \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{prov}} , which needs no
training, for ℱ ^ train \hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{train}} , and for ℱ ^ train \hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{train}} with θ \theta tuned. The metrics are
measured on held-out data, that is, on attacks that were not used when the rules’ parameters were chosen. The
unsafe-approval rate is the fraction of frauds approved. The
false-rejection rate (FRR) is the fraction of good items not approved, both clean and under blocking
attacks. Parameters are chosen on the training split to minimise unsafe approvals subject to FRR ≤ 0.10 \leq 0.10 . The
primary split assigns attack conditions 50 / 50 50/50 to training and test, so no test payload is seen when fitting.
Confidence intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over attack conditions ( B = 1,000 B=1{,}000 ), paired across rules.
A bootstrap recomputes a quantity on many random resamples of the data; the share of resamples in which a
condition holds, reported as p p below, measures how stable it is.
Every bracket [ ⋅ , ⋅ ] [\cdot,\cdot] after a number in Section 5 is a 95 % 95\% interval, from this bootstrap or,
for a single proportion, Wilson’s interval. A missing cell is a verdict that was never recorded, for
instance because a provider’s quota ran out; it is reported, never imputed. An
offline adaptive attacker [ 47 ] picks, on half of the tasks, the condition that best
defeats each rule, and is scored on the other half.

Return to Llama reading doc . In the
pilot it answers 2,200 2{,}200 items: 40 40 benign payments, and 108 108 attack units of 20 20 payments each, one

On the training half we choose each rule’s parameters, such as the threshold k k , the vote weights or θ \theta .
On the test half we replay the verdicts: for each item we look at which monitors approved, apply each rule to that
set, and count the frauds executed and the good payments blocked. The pilot’s hypotheses were judged by frozen
