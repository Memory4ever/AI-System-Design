 Is Refusal Direction Universal across Low-Resourced Languages?
Anastasiia Ivanova
MaiNLP, LMU Munich
anastasiia.ivanova@campus.lmu.de
Abstract
Refusal directions have been shown to trans-
fer cross-lingually with near-perfect effective-
ness across safety-aligned languages (Wang
et al., 2025), reportedly regardless of resource
level. However, the only truly low-resourced
language evaluated is Yoruba, which is strongly
misaligned and shows limited transfer, leav-
ing open how refusal directions behave for
other low-resourced languages. We extend
PolyRefuse to three additional low-resourced
languages (Belarusian, Bashkir, and Tajik)1 and
replicate the pipeline of Wang et al. (2025) on
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct. We find that universal-
ity is graded rather than binary: directions from
safety-aligned and partially-aligned languages
transfer broadly, while those from strongly mis-
aligned languages fail to reliably modulate re-
fusal behavior on any target tested, including
other misaligned languages, once ceiling ef-
fects are accounted for. Geometric alignment
and behavioral transfer are further shown to be
partially dissociated. The findings complicate
the universality claim and suggest that users of
the least-resourced, most strongly misaligned
languages may face weaker safety guarantees.
1 Introduction
Safety alignment in large language models (LLMs)
relies on the model’s ability to recognize and refuse
harmful, unethical, or policy-violating requests.
Arditi et al. (2024) showed that this refusal be-
havior is mediated by a single linear direction in a
model’s activation space, and that ablating this di-
rection reliably bypasses refusal – suggesting safety
representations can be manipulated or transferred.
Wang et al. (2025) extend this to a multilingual
setting, introducing PolyRefuse, a dataset of harm-
ful and benign prompts translated from English into
13 languages spanning diverse families and scripts.

2 Background
Refusal directions.Mechanistic interpretability
seeks to explain the behavior of neural networks
by analyzing their internal representations, activa-
tion patterns, and learned features through tech-
niques such as probing, feature attribution, and
linear subspace analysis (Olah et al., 2020; Elhage
et al., 2021). Recent work on representation steer-
ing has shown that specific linear directions in a
model’s activation space can correspond to behav-
iors or attributes, which can be detected, amplified,
or suppressed at the inference time through lin-
ear interventions (Zou et al., 2025; Turner et al.,
2024). Arditi et al. (2024) showed that refusal be-
havior is mediated by a single such direction, and
that ablating it reliably bypasses refusal. Refusal
directions are computed using the difference-in-
means method (Marks and Tegmark, 2023): the
mean activation difference between harmful and
harmless prompts is calculated across layers, and
the direction with the highest discriminative power
is selected. The practical motivation for studying
refusals is clear: an ideal model should avoid as-
sisting users in illegal activities, causing harm to
others, or engaging in unethical behavior (Amodei
et al., 2016). At the same time, what constitutes
a refusal-worthy request can vary across cultural,
legal, and linguistic contexts (Yam et al., 2023).
WildGuard.WildGuard (Han et al., 2024) is an
open-source moderation tool for evaluating LLM
safety. It assesses model responses across three di-
mensions: harmfulness of the prompt, refusal, and
compliance. Since compliance rate is the comple-
ment of refusal rate (compliance= 1−refusal ),
we report compliance rate throughout this paper, as
our central claim concerns the rate at which mod-
els produce unsafe outputs rather than the rate at
which they correctly refuse. We use WildGuard’s
refusal/compliance judgments to evaluate whether
a language is safety-aligned, following the proce-
dure of Wang et al. (2025): a language is consid-
ered safety-aligned if its compliance rate is consis-
tently low and its refusal rate is high on harmful
prompts.
Low-resourced languages.These are the lan-
guages that lack sufficient data to train powerful
models, and this data imbalance creates biases in
LLMs in favor of the linguistic and cultural prefer-
ences that dominate their training data (Joshi et al.,
2020; Blasi et al., 2022). There is also a certain
inconsistency in the NLP literature regarding what
constitutes a low-resourced language (Nigatu et al.,
2024) – a terminology issue we revisit in Section 3.
3 Analysis of Wang et al. (2025)
Wang et al. (2025) expand previous findings to
non-English languages by creating PolyRefuse, a
collection of English-language safety datasets au-
tomatically translated into 13 languages (14 includ-
ing English): English (en), German (de), Spanish
(es), French (fr), Italian (it), Dutch (nl), Japanese
(ja), Polish (pl), Russian (ru), Chinese (zh), Ko-
rean (ko), Arabic (ar), Thai (th), and Yoruba (yo).
The underlying English prompts originate from
Arditi et al. (2024): the harmful set combines
AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023), MaliciousInstruct
(Huang et al., 2023),and TDC2023 (Mazeika et al.,
2023), while the harmless set draws from ALPACA
(Taori et al., 2023). The authors extract refusal
vectors from models’ activation patterns and eval-
uate their cross-lingual transferability using cross-
lingual cosine similarity and PCA visualizations
of harmful and harmless representations. The key

 finding is that a refusal direction derived from En-
glish can effectively bypass refusal behavior in
other languages with near-perfect success, even
without fine-tuning, and that refusal vectors ex-
tracted from any safety-aligned language transfer
seamlessly across all tested languages. This trans-
ferability is attributed to the geometric parallelism
of refusal vectors across languages in the embed-
ding space. Despite this universality, models re-
main vulnerable to cross-lingual jailbreaks because
harmful and harmless prompts are often poorly sep-
arated in non-English representation spaces.
The authors do not discuss the resource status
of the chosen languages in the main text, men-
tioning Polish, Thai, and Yoruba as mid- and low-
resourced only in a footnote. However, resource
status is multidimensional – Nigatu et al. (2024)
note that it encompasses socio-political factors, dig-
ital data availability, linguistic infrastructure, and
community agency. Without explicit criteria, the
classification is unclear: Yoruba is considered low-
resourced mainly due to a scarcity of computational
tools and infrastructure (Jimoh et al., 2025), de-
spite having 47 million speakers and institutional
status, while Thai is under-resourced primarily in
terms of available digital data and corpora (Ar-
reerard et al., 2022). This matters for interpreting
the universality claim – Polish and Thai have sub-
stantially more NLP infrastructure than Yoruba,
making Yoruba the only language in PolyRefuse
that is unambiguously low-resourced. It remains
unclear whether this strongly-misaligned effect is
specific to Yoruba or a general characteristic of
low-resource languages.

4 Extending PolyRefuse
To test whether the findings for Yoruba generalize
to other low-resourced languages and to strengthen
claims about safety-aligned languages, more exper-
iments with low-resourced languages are needed.
We choseBelarusian(be),Bashkir(ba), andTajik
(tg) as test languages because we can manually ver-
ify translation quality for each. See Appendix A
for the translation examples.
Belarusian is an East Slavic language closely re-
lated to Russian and Ukrainian, sharing enough
lexical and grammatical structure with Russian
to enable manual evaluation by Russian speak-
ers. Bashkir is a Kipchak Turkic language spo-
ken primarily in Bashkortostan, Russia, and con-
tains numerous Russian loanwords from prolonged
language contact. Tajik is an Iranian language of
the Persian subgroup, largely mutually intelligible
with Farsi despite using Cyrillic rather than Ara-
bic script. This proximity to Farsi allows Persian
speakers to evaluate Tajik translations.
All three languages are classified as low-
resourced for different reasons. For Belarusian,
Aparovich et al. (2025) present the Belarusian-
GLUE corpus noting that few resources were pre-
viously available, and cite UNESCO World Atlas
of Languages which lists Belarusian as ’potentially
vulnerable’. Although it is an official language in
Belarus alongside Russian, Aparovich et al. (2025)
note that it is in fact a minority language there
due to the widespread use of Russian in daily life,
education, and media.
Bashkir is part of the Turkic language family
and faces challenges as a low-resourced language
primarily due to limited parallel corpora and dig-
ital resources (Mirzakhalov et al., 2021). Despite
having more than 1.6 million speakers, Bashkir is
rated as vulnerable by UNESCO, with challenges
in NLP stemming from limited digital resources
and computing infrastructure.
Tajik is similarly characterized as low-resourced
due to insufficient linguistic data and computational
resources for building NLP applications (Mirza-
khalov et al., 2021). The Tajik National Corpus
(Tajik National Corpus, 2021) contains around 58.4
million words, which is relatively small for LLM
training compared to high-resourced languages.
The qualitative resource profiles above are cor-
roborated by a quantitative measure: Table 1 re-
ports the number of L1 speakers, UNESCO Atlas
endangerment status, and NLP resource class fol-
lowing the taxonomy of Joshi et al. (2020) for all
languages discussed in this paper. Speaker popula-
tion and NLP resource availability diverge sharply:
Tajik has roughly eight times as many speakers as
Bashkir, yet both are classified as resource class 1,

 while Belarusian, with fewer speakers than Tajik,
reaches class 3. By this quantitative measure, Be-
larusian, Bashkir, and Tajik are all lower-resourced
than Polish (class 4) and Thai (class 3) – the two
languages Wang et al. (2025) treat as low/mid-
resource stand-ins – and Bashkir and Tajik are even
lower-resourced than Yoruba (class 2) – the only
language Wang et al. (2025) treat as unambiguously
low-resourced – despite substantial differences in
speaker population and endangerment status.
As the chosen languages ( ba, be, tg)
are not available in the free unofficial
google-translator library, we used the
Google Cloud Translator API (Google, 2026) to
translate the entire dataset, including harmful
and harmless splits for training, validation, test,
and jailbreak test sets. We manually reviewed all
translations, leveraging our fluency in Russian and
background in Turkish and Farsi; see Section 8 for
a discussion of the resulting limitations.

5.2 Transfer of English Refusal Vectors
We transfer the refusal vector derived from English
to Bashkir, Belarusian, and Tajik, evaluating the
effect with WildGuard and including Yoruba as a
low-resourced reference point. All experiments use
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025) on a
single A100 GPU, accessed through HuggingFace
(Wolf et al., 2020).
Results are presented in Figures 2 and 7. The
effects of ablating the English refusal vector vary
substantially across languages. For Belarusian, ab-
lation raises the compliance rate from 0.24 to 0.99
and raises Attack Success Rate from 0.16 to 0.91,
strongly supporting the universality claim of Wang
et al. (2025). For Bashkir and Tajik, where base-
line compliance is already high (0.88 and 0.92), the
raw increase in compliance following ablation is
small in absolute terms – the model was largely
complying with harmful requests even before inter-
vention. We revisit this ceiling effect quantitatively
in Section 5.2, where we show that, once baseline
compliance is accounted for, English’s normalized

5.3 Refusal Vectors from Non-English
Languages
Wang et al. (2025) test a stronger form of the hy-
pothesis by checking whether refusal directions
derived from non-English languages can also mod-
ulate refusal across other languages. Ablating a di-
rection from any of the three safety-aligned sources
(German, Chinese, Thai) causes a near-complete
collapse of refusal across all targets, with compli-
ance approaching or exceeding 90% regardless of
source. Notably, all their non-English sources are
safety-aligned.
We repeat this experiment using our low-
resource languages as sources. Since Bashkir and
Tajik are strongly misaligned and Belarusian is
only partially aligned, this tests whether the uni-
versality holds when the source language is itself
misaligned. We extract refusal directions from En-
glish, Belarusian, Bashkir, and Tajik and ablate
each on all four target languages. In Figure 3, the
resulting compliance rates are reported; boxed cells
denote self-transfer.
The results reveal a clear asymmetry. The En-
glish direction transfers effectively to all languages,
raising compliance to near 1.0, and the Belarusian
direction transfers comparably well, reaching 0.90–
0.97 on all targets including English (0.93). In
contrast, the Bashkir and Tajik directions transfer
poorly to English (0.06 and 0.33) and to Belaru-
sian (0.44 and 0.72). Their apparent effectiveness
on Bashkir and Tajik targets (0.72–0.92) is harder
to interpret, since both languages already show
high baseline compliance (0.88 and 0.92; §5.1)
without any intervention. To disentangle genuine
transfer from this ceiling effect, we compute a
normalized effect size for each source–target pair,
∆ = (cpost −c base)/(1−c base), the fraction of re-
maining compliance headroom closed by ablation
(Figure 1). English and Belarusian close the great
majority of available headroom on most targets
(56–100%), except on Bashkir, where its effect is
markedly weaker ( ∆be→ba = 0.13 ) though still
positive. Bashkir and Tajik, however, show near-
zero or negative normalized effects even on their
own language and on each other (∆ba→ba =−1.30 ,
∆tj→tj = 0.00, ∆ba→tj =−0.91 , ∆tj→ba = 0.06):
the raw compliance rates that appeared to indicate
successful transfer are indistinguishable from – or
below – the pre-intervention baseline.
This refines the universality claim: the symmet-
ric transfer reported by Wang et al. (2025) holds
among safety-aligned languages. Belarusian, de-
spite higher baseline compliance than English, be-
haves as a safety-aligned source and target through-
out our results. Bashkir and Tajik, once ceiling
effects are accounted for, do not act as effective
refusal directions foranytarget — not only failing
on safety-aligned languages, but showing no mea-
surable effect on each other or on themselves. A
strongly misaligned language does not yield a di-
rection that transfers narrowly to other misaligned
languages; it yields a direction too weak or noisy
to reliably modulate refusal behavior anywhere.
Universality, in this stricter sense, is a property of
safety-aligned (and partially-aligned, e.g. Belaru-
sian) languages specifically, not of low-alignment
language pairs.

5.5 Exploring the Geometry of Refusal
To understand the mechanisms behind the transfer
results, we analyze the geometry of refusal repre-
sentations. Following Wang et al. (2025), we visu-
alize harmful and harmless representations at the
refusal extraction layer using PCA (Figure 5) and
quantify clustering quality using Silhouette scores
(see Table 3). This geometric analysis provides
an independent check on the behavioral alignment
classification from Section 5.1, using representa-
tion structure.
Unlike Wang et al. (2025), who report approx-
imately parallel refusal directions across all lan-
guages, we observe parallelism only for Belarusian,
whose refusal direction closely aligns with English.
For Bashkir, Tajik, and Yoruba, the directions di-
verge substantially. Separation between harmful
and harmless clusters follows the same pattern:
English shows the cleanest separation (Silhouette
0.356), Belarusian is intermediate (0.169), while
Yoruba (0.085), Tajik (0.065), and Bashkir (0.046)
are poorly separated. This ranking demonstrates
transfer effectiveness, as Belarusian responded
most strongly to both ablation and addition of the
English refusal vector. Bootstrap 95% confidence
intervals for compliance rates and silhouette scores
(English, Belarusian, Bashkir, and Tajik) are pro-
vided in Appendix D.
While the Bashkir refusal direction is near-
orthogonal to English’s at the extraction layer
(≈ −0.01), consistent with its near-zero behavioral
effect on English (∆ba→en = 0.01; §5.3), comput-
ing cosine similarity across all layers and positions
(Appendix 8) reveals that Bashkir’s refusal signal
instead peaks at substantially later layers (≈35–40)
than the layer-30 extraction point used for English
and Belarusian, with a comparable but weaker and
earlier shift for Tajik (≈20–25). This indicates that
a refusal-relevant signal exists in these languages’
representations, but is encoded at a different net-
work depth than the reference extraction point – ge-
ometric alignment that does not straightforwardly
translate into measurable behavioral transfer at the
standard extraction layer.
6 Discussion
Results partially support the universality hypothe-
sis but refine it in two ways. First, the symmetric
transferability reported by Wang et al. (2025) does
not hold when the source language is strongly mis-
aligned. Directions extracted from English and
partially aligned Belarusian transfer effectively to
almost all targets, whereas those from Bashkir
and Tajik fail to reliably modulate refusal behav-
ior on any target once baseline ceiling effects are
accounted for, including on each other and on

PAGE 7 
 themselves. Universality thus appears specific
to safety-aligned and partially-aligned source lan-
guages, rather than holding for all languages re-
gardless of resource level.
Second, geometric alignment and behavioral
transfer are dissociated. At the English extraction
layer, only Belarusian aligns with English, while
Bashkir and Tajik appear nearly orthogonal. How-
ever, ablating these directions substantially changes
raw compliance on some targets even where cosine
similarity is near zero, and cross-layer analysis
shows Bashkir’s and Tajik’s refusal signals align
with English at different layers than the extraction
point – particularly for Bashkir, whose peak align-
ment shifts to markedly later layers (≈35–40).

These findings complicate the link between
safety misalignment and non-universality proposed
for Yoruba. Our three languages differ substantially
in alignment tier and in how their refusal directions
relate to English – from partially-aligned Belaru-
sian, whose direction strongly aligns with English
both geometrically and behaviorally, to strongly
misaligned Bashkir and Tajik, whose alignment
with English is layer-shifted rather than absent.
Misalignment alone does not determine whether
a language’s refusal direction is universal; our
results instead suggest a gradient tied to align-
ment tier. Consistent with Wang et al. (2025),
low-resourcedness remains correlated with weaker
safety performance.
Finally, we point to a fairness concern in multi-
lingual safety: users of the least-resourced, most
strongly misaligned languages are systematically
exposed to weaker and less reliable safety mecha-
nisms. Addressing this requires both better multi-
lingual data coverage and safety interventions that
do not assume high-resource performance as a pre-
requisite for robust refusal.
7 Conclusion
Based on Wang et al. (2025), we expanded
PolyRefuse with three low-resourced languages
(Belarusian, Bashkir, and Tajik) and studied the
transferability and geometry of refusal directions.
Our results refine the universality hypothesis: the
English refusal direction transfers effectively to all
three languages, and the Belarusian direction trans-
fers comparably well to the other targets we test.
Directions extracted from the strongly misaligned
Bashkir and Tajik, by contrast, fail to reliably mod-
ulate refusal behavior on any target we evaluate,
including English and each other, once baseline
ceiling effects are accounted for. Universality thus
holds among safety-aligned and partially-aligned
source languages but breaks down when the source
is strongly misaligned. We further find that geo-
metric alignment and behavioral transfer are par-
tially dissociated, as Bashkir and Tajik align with
English at different network layers than English’s
own extraction point. This complicates the claim
proposed for Yoruba: although Belarusian, Bashkir,
and Tajik are all low-resourced, they differ substan-
tially in alignment tier and refusal geometry, with
Belarusian patterning closer to English than to the
other two. More broadly, our findings highlight
that users of the least-resourced, most strongly mis-
aligned languages remain exposed to weaker safety
mechanisms.
8 Limitations
Single model.Due to limited resources, all exper-
iments used a single model, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct.
While this allows a focused and consistent analy-
sis, it limits generalizability; Wang et al. (2025)
evaluated multiple model families, and our obser-
vations about asymmetric transfer and layer-shifted
alignment may be specific to Qwen.
Language range.We extend PolyRefuse with
only three low-resourced languages. Although
chosen to represent distinct families, scripts, and
resource conditions, three languages remain a
small sample from which to generalize about low-
resourced languages as a whole.
Translation quality.Our dataset relies on ma-
chine translation via the Google Cloud Translation
API, and translation quality for low-resourced lan-
guages is uncertain. Manual verification relied on
the authors’ knowledge of Russian, Turkish, and
Farsi rather than native proficiency in Belarusian,
Bashkir, or Tajik, and is therefore partial and po-
tentially biased. This bridge is not equally strong
across the three languages: Belarusian and Rus-

PAGE 8 
 sian, and Tajik and Farsi, are closely related with
substantial mutual intelligibility, whereas Bashkir’s
relationship to Russian consists primarily of loan-
word familiarity rather than structural or lexical
closeness, making our verification confidence low-
est for Bashkir specifically. The translation pipeline
may also fail to preserve subtle linguistic, harmful,
or culturally specific nuances, particularly for the
least-resourced languages where tools are weakest.
Neither automatic metrics nor our verification can
guarantee that a prompt remains equally harmful
or harmless for speakers of the target language and
culture.
9 Ethical Considerations
AI use.The authors declare the responsible use
of AI tools. Cursor and Claude were used to as-
sist with coding, with all code manually reviewed
and experiments designed and run by the authors.
Claude and ChatGPT were used for grammatical
and lexical editing of the report.
Dual-use considerations.Figure 6 presents
harmful prompts translated into Belarusian,
Bashkir, and Tajik for reproducibility. These
prompts originate from established red-teaming
benchmarks used in the original PolyRefuse
pipeline – AdvBench(Zou et al., 2023), Mali-
ciousInstruct (Huang et al., 2023),and TDC2023
(Mazeika et al., 2023) – rather than being authored
by us, and their translation is intended to substanti-
ate our claims about cross-lingual jailbreak transfer
rather than to introduce novel harmful content. We
nonetheless recognize that publishing translated,
ready-to-use harmful prompts in low-resourced lan-
guages could itself lower the barrier to misuse in
exactly the languages we argue are least protected,
and we encourage readers to treat this material as
illustrative of the transfer phenomenon rather than
as a resource for eliciting harmful behavior.
