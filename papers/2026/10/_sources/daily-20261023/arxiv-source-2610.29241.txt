Abstract
In this paper, we investigate why vision-language models (VLMs) produce errors by decomposing them into two sources: visual-representation failures , in which the visual evidence required for the correct answer is not encoded, and visual-utilization failures , in which this evidence is encoded but not successfully used. We propose to estimate the visual-utilization failure rate using lightweight probes and derive theoretical bounds on the true rate that account for probe imperfections. We introduce VELens (Visual Evidence Lens), a rank-based probe that traces visual evidence for both a target object and its ground-truth attribute, such as color, material, or shape, across VLM layers. We evaluate VELens on over 6,000 images using LLaVA-1.6-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B. Our method achieves ROC-AUC scores of 0.95 and 0.91, respectively, and outperforms alternative probes in both low-resource and unseen-attribute settings. Using it to diagnose errors, we estimate that visual-utilization failures account for 84.4% of LLaVA errors and 70.0% of Qwen errors, with conservative lower bounds of 77.5% and 61.0%, respectively. Overall, our study identifies visual utilization as the dominant bottleneck in VLMs and suggests that a central opportunity for improvement lies in developing better mechanisms to locate, route, and use the visual information they already encode.
1 Introduction
Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly answer questions requiring complex reasoning over images ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024c ; Ganz et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2025 ) . Yet they still hallucinate, i.e., produce answers inconsistent with the visual content ( Li et al., 2023 ; Tong et al., 2024 ; Wu and Xie, 2024 ; Bai et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) . Consider Figure 1 : the traffic light is red, but the model answers green . For an autonomous vehicle approaching the intersection, such a basic error could determine whether it stops safely. Understanding why it occurs is therefore as important as detecting it: did the error occur because the model never encoded that the traffic light is red, or because it encoded this information but failed to use it when generating the answer?
These possibilities correspond to two distinct failure modes. In a visual-representation failure , the internal visual representations lack the evidence required for the correct answer. In a visual-utilization failure , that evidence is encoded but not used. The failures are indistinguishable from the final answer, yet point to different limitations and solutions. Representation failures call for improving the visual encoder, whereas utilization failures call for improving how the VLM locates, routes, and incorporates encoded visual evidence during generation. Without separating them, we cannot tell whether VLMs need better visual representations or better utilization.
Existing work on VLM errors primarily defines and detects incorrect outputs ( Li et al., 2023 ; Gunjal et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024b ; Chen et al., 2026 )
or mitigates them during training and inference ( Tonmoy et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2024 ; Leng et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024a ; Yu et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) .
Complementary studies examine VLM internals using hidden-representation probes ( Alain and Bengio, 2017 ; Fu et al., 2025 ; Kogilathota et al., 2026 ) ,
vocabulary-space projections ( Neo et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025a ; Jiang et al., 2025b ) , and cross-modal attention and
information-flow analyses ( Kaduri et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2024d ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) . These approaches reveal where visual information appears and how it influences generation,
but not how often errors reflect missing rather than unused evidence. Even recent multi-stage accounts focus on utilization without showing whether the required visual evidence
was encoded ( Xiong et al., 2026 ) .
Figure 1: Diagnosing the source of errors.
We therefore shift focus from whether a VLM answers correctly to whether it possesses the necessary visual evidence to do so. We quantify the visual-utilization failure rate :
the fraction of errors for which sufficient evidence is encoded but unsuccessfully used. The rest are visual-representation failures, where the evidence was never encoded.
We estimate this unobservable rate with a probe trained to extract a target signal from representations.
However, it might guess correctly from spurious cues or fail to extract present evidence.
We account for this uncertainty by developing theoretical bounds on the true rate using answer-preserving transformations that vary evidence salience.
We introduce VELens (Visual Evidence Lens), a lightweight rank-based visual-evidence probe. We evaluate it on target-object attribute questions (“ What is the color of the traffic light? ”), which require object and attribute evidence. Because visual-token representations are not directly interpretable,
VELens projects each language-model layer’s representations into vocabulary space, providing a window into the VLM’s encoded visual information. It measures whether tokens representing the target concept (the object or correct attribute value) rank above competing tokens and combines these scores across layers to determine whether object and attribute evidence are present.
To evaluate VELens, we use six thousand object-rich images from the Visual Attributes in the Wild (VAW) dataset ( Pham et al., 2021 ) , focusing on color, material, and shape as a first step toward broader visual evidence, and two VLMs sharing the causal decoder-based design of current models, LLaVA-1.6-7B ( Liu et al., 2024c ) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B ( Bai et al., 2025 ) .
Across all attribute categories and both VLMs, VELens outperforms every baseline, and combined with the strongest object-evidence probe it reaches joint object-and-attribute ROC-AUCs of 0.95 on LLaVA and 0.91 on Qwen. We additionally test its generalization in low-resource and unseen-value settings, where it also outperforms the corresponding baselines, supporting its use for diagnosing error sources.
We then use VELens to answer the paper’s central question. On VAW, we estimate that 84.4% of LLaVA errors and 70.0% of Qwen errors are visual-utilization failures. More importantly, our conservative lower bound shows these are at least 77.5% (LLaVA) and 61.0% (Qwen) of errors overall. Thus, the main bottleneck is not representing the visual information, but utilizing it when producing the answer. We find no universal layer at which visual evidence becomes accessible. It depends heavily on the model and attribute. To test whether encoded evidence can be used more successfully, we increase attention toward the target-object region. As predicted, this achieves twice the correction rate on evidence-positive errors as on errors where VELens detects no evidence, as proof of concept that, when visual evidence is encoded, helping the model use it can resolve failures.
In summary, our contributions are: (1) we formalize the distinction between visual-representation and visual-utilization failures, introduce an estimand for their relative prevalence, and derive bounds that account for an imperfect probe; (2) we introduce VELens, a rank-based probe that traces object and attribute evidence across language-model layers, and characterize its scope and limitations (§ E ); (3) across two VLMs and three attributes, we show that visual utilization is the main bottleneck; and (4) we show that identifying whether the required visual evidence is encoded can guide error mitigation. Together, our study suggests that improving VLM reliability requires not only better encoding, but also better mechanisms to locate, route, and use what VLMs already encode.
2 Related Work
Detection and Mitigation of Errors in VLMs.
VLM responses often describe objects, attributes, or relations inconsistent with the image ( Bai et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2024b ) . Prior work has mainly focused on detecting such conflicts ( Li et al., 2023 ; Gunjal et al., 2024 ; Deng et al., 2024 ; Yin et al., 2024 ) ,
treating VLMs as black boxes ( Jiang et al., 2025a ) . Mitigation methods ( Tonmoy et al., 2024 ) reduce errors through attention-guided grounding or constrained decoding at inference ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Leng et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) , or visual-text alignment objectives during training ( Liu et al., 2024a ; Yu et al., 2024 ) .
However, they address the output without identifying its underlying cause. While Xiong et al. (2026) frame VLM errors as failures of internal processing, they analyze utilization without establishing whether the required visual evidence was encoded. We instead distinguish visual-representation failures, where evidence is not encoded, from visual-utilization failures, where encoded evidence is unsuccessfully used ( Calderon et al., 2026 ) .
Interpretability of visual information in VLM.
Motivated by evidence that LLM internal representations reveal information underlying model behavior ( Orgad et al., 2025 ; Azaria and Mitchell, 2023 ) , recent work studies how visual information is encoded and utilized within VLMs.
First, classifier-based methods, including linear probing ( Alain and Bengio, 2017 ; Fu et al., 2025 ; Kogilathota et al., 2026 ) , test whether visual concepts can be recovered from hidden states. Notably, Fu et al. (2025) shows that information easily recoverable from the intermediate vision encoder layers may fail to influence predictions.
Second, confidence-based methods project hidden states through the LM output head, following the logit lens ( nostalgebraist, 2020 ) , to identify vocabulary concepts supported by intermediate visual representations ( Jiang et al., 2025b ; Neo et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025a ) . These studies show that object semantics are encoded within visual tokens and localized to corresponding image regions, become increasingly interpretable across layers, and provide early indications of visually unsupported objects in textual responses.
Finally, cross-modality attention analysis ( Kaduri et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025b ; Liu et al., 2024d ) tracks how visual features contribute to final outputs and how attention disruptions correlate with errors. Unlike per-layer or per-concept analyses, we analyze objects and attributes jointly across layers.
3 Quantifying the Rate of Visual-Utilization Failures
Understanding the source of VLM errors requires distinguishing two failure modes: visual-representation failures , in which the required visual evidence is not encoded, and visual-utilization failures , in which it is encoded but unsuccessfully used. Although the final answer alone cannot distinguish them, they reflect different limitations and call for different interventions. We therefore formalize this distinction and propose an estimand, estimator, and bounds for the proportion of errors from each source.

We now introduce VELens, a rank-based implementation of the composite probe M = M obj ∧ M attr M=M_{\mathrm{obj}}\land M_{\mathrm{attr}} (§ 3.3 ) that projects intermediate visual-token representations into the LM’s vocabulary space and traces the resulting evidence across layers (Figure 2 ). As we show experimentally, VELens yields the most reliable probe and hence our most reliable estimate of μ ϕ \mu_{\phi} .

5 Experimental Setup
Baseline Methods.
We compare our rank-based method with three baseline categories: classifier-based , confidence-based , and attention-based . All keep the VLM frozen and use the same mapping from annotated object regions to visual tokens.
Classifier-based.
A standard linear-probing baseline ( Alain and Bengio, 2017 ) trains a separate linear classifier for each attribute value at each LM layer. Target-region visual tokens are spatially aggregated (§ B.2 ) into a full-hidden-dimension vector and used as classifier input. This approach requires knowing the attribute or object space in advance, as each value has its own trained probing classifier. Consequently, it cannot be applied to unseen attributes or used as an object probe.
Confidence-based.
Internal Confidence ( Jiang et al., 2025a ) measures whether a concept is represented at an intermediate layer by projecting the representation into vocabulary space, and examining the probability assigned to the tokens corresponding to that concept. The method takes the maximum probability across tokens and layers and passes the scalar to a binary classifier. Unlike VELens, it does not compare to scores of competing tokens and collapses layers into a single scalar. In our evaluation, we restrict the visual tokens to the annotated target-object region.
Attention-based.
High attention between a query token representing a concept and visual tokens corresponding to that concept can indicate stronger visual grounding ( Kaduri et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025b ; Liu et al., 2024d ) . In our implementation, we measure attention from the candidate attribute or target-object query tokens to visual tokens inside and outside the annotated target-object region. At each layer and head, the attention scores are normalized by region size, aggregated over heads within each layer, concatenated across layers, and passed to a binary classifier (§ C.1 ).
Pooling Configurations.
For attribute evidence, VELens and the confidence and attention baselines use two configurations: All , one probe shared across all values in a category, and Separate (Sep), one probe per value. The classifier baseline always trains one probe per value, so it has only Separate. For object evidence, the methods use one estimator shared across object categories. We select the aggregation strategy of scores (mean, max, min or median) on a validation subset (§ D.1 ), retrain on the full training set, and evaluate once on held-out test set.
Base Models.
We evaluate two VLMs: LLaVA-1.6-7B Liu et al. (2024c) , which connects a pretrained CLIP vision encoder to a Mistral-7B LM, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B Bai et al. (2025) , which pairs the Qwen2.5 LM with a natively trained dynamic-resolution vision encoder.
Dataset.
We use the Visual Attributes in the Wild (VAW) dataset ( Pham et al., 2021 ) , which provides each object with a polygon or bounding box and associated attributes. This allows us to identify the target object’s visual region and construct object-attribute questions (§ C.3 ).
VAW contains natural, object-rich scenes with over 17 objects per image on average.
We normalize attribute values to canonical forms (§ C.3 ) and sample 6,074 images.
We focus on color, material, and shape because they can be determined from the target object itself rather than its surrounding context, making them well suited for identifying visual-representation failures. VELens itself is not tied to this setting: § E discusses its scope and limitations.

Our Method Generalizes with Limited Supervision and to Unseen Attribute Values.
For M attr M_{\mathrm{attr}} , VELens uses two pooling configurations: Rank-Separate (Sep) trains one probe per value, while Rank-All shares one across values. Our main results evaluate attribute values seen during training, where Rank-Sep performs best among methods. We next ask how the probe can be extended to a new value. For example, after training on seven colors, how can it detect evidence for an eighth? We consider two options: train a value-specific probe from a few labeled examples (low-resource), or reuse probes trained on known values for out-of-distribution generalization.
In the low-resource setting, we train a value-specific Separate probe with varying numbers of labeled examples. Figure 3 (left) reports ROC-AUC averaged over five independently sampled subsets for each training size in each of the three attribute categories. VELens outperforms the baselines at every size and remains strong with only a few dozen examples per class. For the unseen-value setting, we hold out one value, train on the remaining n − 1 n-1 , and compute its evidence vector.
Separate applies the n − 1 n-1 value-specific probes to this vector and averages their predictions, while All applies one probe trained jointly on the n − 1 n-1 values. The middle and right panels show that Rank-All generalizes better to unseen values, while both configurations outperform their baselines. Thus, specialization is preferable in-domain, whereas sharing across values improves transfer: a new value can be handled with a few labeled examples using Separate, or with All when no labels are available.

C.2 Logit Lens
The logit lens ( nostalgebraist, 2020 ) provides a window into the interpretability
of intermediate representations, by applying the output head at every layer rather than only
at the last. In VLMs, it relates visual tokens that encode specific visual information to
vocabulary concepts.
We apply it to visual tokens: for the representation h l ​ ( k i ) h_{l}(k_{i}) of
visual token k i k_{i} at layer l l , the head W V ∈ ℝ | V | × d W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d} yields logits over
the vocabulary V V , and the projected rank of each v ∈ V v\in V is
rank l ⁡ ( k i , v ) := 1 + | { u ∈ V : [ W V ​ h l ​ ( k i ) ] u > [ W V ​ h l ​ ( k i ) ] v } | , \operatorname{rank}_{l}(k_{i},v):=1+\bigl|\{\,u\in V:\ [W_{V}h_{l}(k_{i})]_{u}>[W_{V}h_{l}(k_{i})]_{v}\,\}\bigr|,
(14)
running from one (highest scoring) to | V | |V| (lowest scoring). Because causal masking prevents
visual-token representations from attending to the question Q Q , these rankings depend only on
the image I I .
Figure 7 shows this projection for visual tokens sampled inside annotated
VAW object regions. The depth at which object and attribute semantics emerge varies across
objects, consistent with prior work placing semantic interpretation in the middle-to-late
layers ( Neo et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025a ; Jiang et al., 2025b ) . For the plate, for example, the sampled in-region tokens are
uninformative in the early layers and decode to plate , white and round

C.3 Dataset Preprocessing
Filtering and normalization.
We preprocess the dataset to ensure unambiguous supervision and improve compatibility with our vocabulary-based analysis. Specifically, (1) we remove object instances containing multiple present attributes within the same category, since such cases do not define a unique target attribute; (2) we filter out images containing multiple instances of the same object category with different attribute annotations, as this may introduce ambiguity regarding which instance the model refers to; and (3) we normalize fine-grained attribute values into canonical forms (e.g., light brown and dark brown → \rightarrow brown ) to reduce label sparsity and improve alignment with vocabulary-level representations, yielding 11 colors, 15 materials, and 5 shapes, with the full mapping given in Table 3 .
Question construction.
Each VAW instance provides an object name o o , a region (polygon or bounding box), and a set of present and absent attributes. From every instance we construct one question per attribute category a ∈ { color , material , shape } a\in\{\texttt{color},\texttt{material},\texttt{shape}\} for which the instance carries a ground-truth value. We use the object name exactly as it appears in the annotation. The region is not shown to the model and only serves to select the visual tokens that VELens uses for evidence detection (§ 4.1 ). All questions follow a single template, shared by both models (Figure 8 ).

Answer generation.
We keep the VLM frozen and decode greedily ( do_sample=False , num_beams=1 , max_new_tokens=10 ), so answers are reproducible across runs. The answer is generated rather than scored against the candidates, and the option list appears in the prompt only to constrain the output to a known vocabulary, so that predictions can be compared against VAW labels without a free-text matching step.
Appendix D Complementary Results
D.1 Development-Set Configuration Selection
Development Split.
To choose the configuration of our rank-based method and of each baseline, we randomly divided the
original training split into 60% for fitting the candidate estimators and 40% for development-set
evaluation. Only these training-derived subsets were used for configuration selection, leaving the
test set untouched. Tables 4
and 5 report development-set performance for every configuration
considered, on LLaVA and Qwen and separately for color, material, and shape. AUC is threshold-free,
whereas F1 and accuracy are computed at a probability threshold of 0.5 0.5 , at which a probe declares
the evidence present.
Attribute-Evidence Configurations.
Following Section 5 , we varied two design choices: the aggregation
strategy used to summarize visual-token signals (§ B.2 ), and the pooling configuration , which
determines whether one estimator is shared across all values in a category ( All ) or one is
fitted per value ( Separate ). This yields 18 configurations per model and attribute category (8 rank-based, 2 confidence-based, 2 classifier-based, and 6 attention-based).
For the rank-based method we crossed both pooling configurations with the minimum, maximum, mean,
and median aggregation strategies. The confidence-based baseline was evaluated with both pooling
configurations but requires no aggregation strategy, as it retains a single maximum confidence
value. The classifier-based baseline is inherently Separate , and we tested the mean and
maximum strategies. For the attention-based baseline we crossed both pooling configurations with
the mean, median, and maximum strategies.
Object-Evidence Configurations.
For object evidence a single estimator is shared across object categories, so the distinction between
All and Separate does not apply. We evaluated eight configurations: four rank
aggregation strategies, the confidence baseline without an aggregation strategy, and three
attention aggregation strategies. We do not evaluate the classifier baseline for objects, because
its fixed label space is incompatible with the open-ended object vocabulary.
Selected Configurations.
We selected one aggregation strategy per method, shared across attribute categories and across both
VLMs, favoring the strategy that was strongest across AUC, F1, and accuracy. For attribute evidence this gives the mean strategy for the
rank- and classifier-based methods and the maximum strategy for the attention-based baseline, and for
object evidence the median strategy for the rank- and attention-based methods. Pooling is treated
differently: we keep both All and Separate for the rank-, confidence-, and
attention-based estimators, as they are two distinct estimators, one shared across the values of a
category and one fitted per value. After fixing these
choices we refit each estimator on the complete training split, the 60% fitting and 40% development
portions combined, and evaluated it once on the held-out test set, giving the results reported in
Table 1 .
Differences Across Attribute Categories.
The rank-based results in Table 4 follow the same ordering in both
models: color and material yield comparably strong evidence, whereas shape is consistently the most
challenging. A plausible explanation is the amount of visual context each property requires, even for a
human observer. A small patch near the center of a red wooden table may be enough to identify its color,
and the grain visible on that same surface likewise reveals its material. Deciding whether the tabletop is round or rectangular, by contrast, requires seeing its outer
boundary, so no local region is sufficient. Shape is therefore the property least likely to be recoverable
from the tokens inside the annotated object region.

Appendix E VELens Beyond the Evaluated Question Set
Our experiments focus on a controlled class of visual questions: attribute questions about a known target object, with color, material, or shape as the queried category. This setting allows visual-evidence detection to be evaluated precisely, as the evidence required for answering is known and spatially grounded in an annotated object region b b . The formulation of Section 3.1 is not restricted to this class. It asks whether the VLM representations contain sufficient evidence to support the ground-truth answer, while VELens operationalizes that evidence through three inputs: a target token set 𝒯 + \mathcal{T}^{+} , a competing token set 𝒯 − \mathcal{T}^{-} , and the visual tokens 𝒦 \mathcal{K} over which evidence is measured. For object-attribute questions, this evidence is further decomposed into object and attribute components (Section 3.3 ). We characterize here which broader classes of questions admit analogous decompositions, and which require further extensions of the probe.
E.1 A Taxonomy of Visual Questions
Visual questions can be categorized along several dimensions. Linguistically, they may be polar questions (“ Is the car yellow? ”) or wh-questions requesting an object, attribute, location, or number (“ What color is the car? ”, “ Where is the dog? ”, “ How many cars are there? ”). Semantically, they may concern object existence or identity, one or several attributes, relations between objects, counting, or information requiring external knowledge. Most importantly for VELens, they differ in the structure of the evidence required for answering them: a single concept in a single region, several concepts associated with the same region, several regions and the relations between them, or global scene-level information.
This last distinction determines whether VELens applies directly. Let C ⁡ ( Q , Y ) = { c 1 , … , c m } C(Q,Y)=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\} denote the visual concepts that must hold for Y Y to be the answer to Q Q , each component c j c_{j} measured with its own inputs ( 𝒯 j + , 𝒯 j − , 𝒦 j ) (\mathcal{T}^{+}_{j},\mathcal{T}^{-}_{j},\mathcal{K}_{j}) . The composite probe then generalizes to
M ⁡ ( X , Y ) = ⋀ j = 1 m M j ​ ( X , Y ) , M(X,Y)=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}M_{j}(X,Y),
where each M j M_{j} tests whether the representations contain the corresponding required evidence, and the object-attribute probe of Section 3.3 is the case C = { object , attribute } C=\{\mathrm{object},\mathrm{attribute}\} . The central requirement is therefore not a particular question form, polar or wh-, but whether the evidence supporting an answer decomposes into identifiable grounded components.
E.2 Question Classes That Naturally Fit This Formulation
Attribute questions and verification.
Our experiments consider wh-style attribute questions such as “ What is the color of the car? ”, but can be naturally extended to verification questions such as “ Is the car yellow? ”, which require the same two evidence components, one for the target object and one for the queried value. When the queried value has a closed candidate set, all three inputs are unchanged and the composite probe applies directly. When it does not, as in “ Is the shirt striped? ”, 𝒯 − \mathcal{T}^{-} extends beyond alternatives from the same category, for example to the rest of the vocabulary as in the object probe, which changes the scale of the score and requires refitting LR attr \mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{attr}} , but not the formulation itself.
Object presence and localization.
Questions such as “ Is there a bicycle in the image? ” require evidence for an object concept rather than an object-attribute pair, with 𝒯 + \mathcal{T}^{+} representing the target object and 𝒯 − \mathcal{T}^{-} competing object concepts. For presence questions, 𝒦 \mathcal{K} covers the whole image, 𝒦 in ∪ 𝒦 out \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{in}}\cup\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{out}} , and supervision comes from image-level presence annotations rather than from the in/out contrast of Section 4.3 . Localization questions such as “ Where is the yellow car? ” combine the same two components, but retain the local scores of Eq. 1 instead of aggregating them over 𝒦 \mathcal{K} , so that contiguous high-evidence tokens can be clustered into candidate regions. The same evidence thus answers two different questions, depending on whether it is aggregated or kept per visual token.
Compositional single-object questions.
The formulation also extends when several properties of one object jointly determine the answer, as in “ Is the large metal cube red? ”. Each value contributes its own 𝒯 j + \mathcal{T}^{+}_{j} and category-specific 𝒯 j − \mathcal{T}^{-}_{j} , while all components share the object’s region 𝒦 in \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{in}} , and the composite probe is their conjunction. Syntactic complexity alone therefore poses no difficulty: questions with different surface realizations are handled identically when they reduce to the same grounded components.
E.3 Question Classes Requiring More Substantial Extensions
The setting becomes more challenging when the answer cannot be determined from evidence associated with individual concepts within a single object region. Relational and comparative questions , such as “ Is the cat to the left of the dog? ” or “ Which car is larger? ”, require evidence not only for both entities but for the relation between them, and detecting the cat and the dog separately does not establish that one is left of the other. Extending VELens would therefore require components defined over tuples of regions, replacing M j ​ ( X , Y , 𝒦 j ) M_{j}(X,Y;\mathcal{K}_{j}) with evidence functions over 𝒦 1 , … , 𝒦 r \mathcal{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{r} .
Counting and global scene questions present a related but distinct challenge. “ How many cars are there? ” requires individuating instances and aggregating them without counting the same evidence twice, whereas “ Is this a crowded street? ” depends on distributed scene-level evidence. Answering either from the local scores of Eq. 1 would require grouping them into instances and counting or summarizing the resulting groups, rather than testing a fixed set of components for their presence.
Finally, knowledge-dependent questions , such as “ What company manufactures this car? ”, require visual evidence together with knowledge stored parametrically in the language model. The distinction between visual-representation and visual-utilization failures is then less identifiable, as an incorrect answer may reflect missing visual evidence, missing knowledge, or a failure to combine the two. In such cases ϕ X \phi_{X} no longer isolates visual information, and 1 − μ ϕ 1-\mu_{\phi} no longer measures a visual-representation failure rate.
Overall, our experiments establish VELens in a deliberately controlled setting rather than claiming empirical coverage of the full space of visual questions. Nevertheless, the method is not inherently tied to color, material, and shape questions. Its natural scope is broader: questions for which the evidence supporting an answer can be represented as a structured composition of visually grounded concepts. Extending from single-region components to relations, sets of instances, and mixed visual and parametric evidence offers a natural path toward diagnosing representation and utilization failures in more general multimodal tasks.
