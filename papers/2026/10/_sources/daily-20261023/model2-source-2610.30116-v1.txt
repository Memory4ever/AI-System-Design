 A Microcosm of LLM Co-Evolution

  Abstract
 Through a tangle of real-world interactions,
from synthetic training pipelines to data shared on the open web,
frontier LLMs absorb what their predecessors and competitors create.
How well does each model play its role in this ecosystem,
as a creator and as a solver?
We introduce  Environment Duels ,
a dynamic agentic benchmark that evaluates both capabilities on interactive environments.
Each model writes environments,
each with a privileged hint,
aiming for tasks that capable solvers fail without the hint and pass with it.
Across nine frontier LLMs,
solving and authoring prove to be distinct skills:
the strongest solvers are not the strongest authors,
while smaller models learn more from how others played
and write the hardest environments and the most useful hints.
Beyond the scores,
the pairwise interactions form a microcosm of how models affect one another.
Head-to-head outcomes are transitive and so yield a stable ranking;
independent authors converge on the same designs;
and in a reinforcement-learning study with  Qwen3.8-27B ,
the learner’s hint lift on an author’s environments tracks how much training on those environments improves it on held-out reasoning and agentic benchmarks,
whether the environments are its own or another model’s.
Each of these patterns has a counterpart in how models shape one another outside the duel.
By staging these interactions under controlled conditions,
Environment Duels offers a window onto how LLMs teach,
challenge and learn from one another.
Code is available at  https://github.com/LLM-Duels/EnvDuels .
  1  Introduction
 Every frontier LLM is now,
in part,
a student of its predecessors and competitors.
Model developers describe pipelines in which one model authors coding challenges,
math problems or reasoning environments,
and the next model trains on them  ( Xu et al., 2026a ;  Zhao et al., 2025a ;  Cai et al., 2025 ) .
The same synthetic data spreads through public repositories and benchmarks,
where other labs’ training runs ingest it  ( Drayson et al., 2025 ) .
Models thus co-evolve:
each one’s training distribution is shaped by what other models chose to write.
When a lab closes this loop deliberately,
having its own models write the curriculum for the next generation,
the process becomes recursive self-improvement in a concrete engineering sense.
Either way,
what a model learns depends on how well another model can write problems worth learning from  ( Xu et al., 2025b ) .
 A model’s skill as an author therefore shapes what the next generation learns,
in its own family or a competitor’s.
Language models now match or exceed human performance on many established benchmarks  ( Center for AI Safety et al., 2026 ) .
As they do,
sufficiently challenging human-authored tasks become increasingly costly to curate  ( Henniger and Poesia, 2026 ) ,
and recent progress on long-standing open problems,
including Millennium Prize conjectures  ( OpenAI, 2026a ) ,
suggests that even the supply of unsolved challenges a model can train on is finite.
Once the stock of existing problems thins out,
the bottleneck moves: what matters is the ability to pose new problems that are worth solving.
A model that can formulate well-specified,
novel challenges,
and calibrate their difficulty to push the boundaries of current methods,
is the kind of capability that could drive fully autonomous scientific research.
 Environment Duels builds a microcosm of this ecosystem.
Every model plays two roles:
it writes executable environments for the others to solve,
and it solves the environments they write.
An environment is an interactive program with hidden state,
a reward and an action budget,
a richer format than a static text question:
solvers must explore,
act and adapt over many turns rather than answer once.
Each author also supplies a privileged hint that lets a solver approach its environment correctly,
and aims for an environment that capable solvers fail without the hint and pass with it.
After the first round,
each author sees how the others actually played and answers with one counter-move.
We run this duel among nine frontier models,
90 environments and 12,960 episodes in all,
and score each model on how well it solves ( solve rate ),
how hard its environments are ( difficulty ),
and how much its hints help ( hint lift ).
 What emerges resembles the ecosystem outside the duel.
The models that solve best are not the ones whose environments teach best:
smaller models learn more from how others played,
and their counter-moves produce the hardest environments and the most useful hints.
 DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ,
for example,
writes the hardest environments of all nine models,
and its developers report training it heavily on synthetic environments  ( Xu et al., 2026a ) ,
an independent sign that the duel measures a skill that training can build.
The duel also carries signal beyond itself:
how much an author’s hints help a learner anticipates how much that learner gains from training on the author’s environments.
 Our contributions are as follows.
  1.  
 Environment Duels is an agentic benchmark,
and a source of RL environments,
that refreshes itself:
because the models under evaluation write every environment,
its ceiling rises with the strongest author at the cost of tokens rather than human annotation.
  2.  
 Nine frontier models write,
solve and respond to one another’s environments,
and the resulting duel dynamics reflect how the wider model ecosystem behaves outside the duel.
  3.  
 A reinforcement-learning experiment shows that a learner’s hint lift on each author’s environments tracks how much training on them improves it on held-out benchmarks.
  4.  
 All environments,
the pipeline that generates them,
the solver trajectories and the analysis code will be released,
and Environment Duels will be updated as new models enter.
  2  Methodology
 In Environment Duels,
every evaluated model is both an author and a solver.
A designer writes an executable environment together with a privileged hint; every other model then plays it twice on identical instances,
once with the hint in its context and once without.
Measured outcomes then return to the designer,
which answers with one counter-move,
a revised environment shaped by how the other models played.
Figure   1   summarises the framework.
We describe construction (Section   2.1  ),
then the duel and its scores (Section   2.2  ).
  Figure 1:   Overview of Environment Duels. 
Frontier LLMs create and solve each other’s environments.
These interactions form a microcosm of the real-world model ecosystem. 
  2.1  Constructing an environment
 Each designer is assigned a domain from a fixed set of five
(symbolic reasoning,
planning and optimization,
causal diagnosis,
adversarial strategy,
dynamic control).
The domain determines the mechanism the environment must exercise.
Within that domain the designer authors an environment that capable solvers should fail without the hint and pass with it,
working through four stages:
           Plan            Implement            Validate            Review          admitted                                          ≤  \leq    2   ×  \times    repair        
 Plan.
 Before writing any code,
the author produces a design specification,
the exact hint that solvers will receive,
and a private feasibility analysis:
a proposed hinted strategy with turn and resource accounting.
This analysis is written reasoning rather than an executable action sequence,
and neither it nor the plan is ever shown to solvers.
A dedicated planning call reserves the author’s full reasoning budget for task design:
working out the core difficulty,
the conditions under which the hint resolves it,
and an adversarial check against simple bypass strategies.
This keeps the design effort separate from implementation effort,
so that the environments are harder and better calibrated.
 Implement.
 A second call receives the locked plan and hint and writes the environment source,
which must not reveal the hint or give away its strategic insight in any observation.

After the duel runs,
we audit environment–seed instances for which no evaluated model succeeds in at least one hint condition.

and a separate manual review.
We exclude flagged instances judged invalid by both audits from downstream metrics;
the other seeds of the same environment remain eligible.
Appendix   A   reports the audit procedure and results.
 Repair.
 A failed execution check returns the error to the implementation call.
A review rejection returns to planning instead:
the author receives the verdict and the failing source,
revises the plan and hint,
and a fresh implementation is generated from the updated plan.
Each environment gets two such repairs.
If both fail,
the author discards the design and starts again from a new plan,
at most twice,
and an environment that still fails is dropped.
In practice the budget is rarely exhausted.
The full prompts for each stage are given in Appendix   F  .

 Counter-move.
 This is where authors and solvers co-evolve.
An author designs its environment blind;
once every play has returned,
it sees how the other models actually played and answers with one revised version.
Alongside anonymised outcome statistics,
it receives three matched trajectory pairs,
each showing one solver’s full attempt on one seed without and with the hint,
which reveal shortcuts the solvers found and hints they ignored or misread.
The author may change the environment,
the hint or both,
but must preserve the task,
interface,
reward contract,
action budget and seed-to-instance mapping,
so each seed poses the same instance before and after.
The revised version passes through the same construction stages,
is played again by every model in both conditions,
and is the version all reported scores use.
 The counter-move is one round of co-evolution:
authors adapt to the solvers they face,
and a single round raises both difficulty and hint lift,
with the no-hint pass rate falling from 84.9% to 78.5%
and hint lift rising from 6.3% to 13.4%.
Authors can thus take feedback from other models and turn it into harder,
better-calibrated environments.
Such loops are increasingly common outside the duel.
A user who consults an LLM council sends one question to several models,
which review and rank one another’s answers before one of them writes the final response  ( Karpathy, 2025 ) ;
related systems let models debate or build on each other’s outputs  ( Du et al., 2024 ;  Wang et al., 2025 ;  Zhao et al., 2025b ) .
The counter-move isolates one such loop under controlled conditions;
Section   3.2   and Appendix   E   analyse its effect further.
  3  Results
  3.1  Experimental setup
 We run environment duels with nine frontier models:
 Claude-Opus-5   ( Anthropic, 2026 ) ,
 GPT-5.6-Sol   ( OpenAI, 2026b ) ,
 DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash   ( Xu et al., 2026a ) ,
 GLM-5.3   ( Zeng et al., 2026a ) ,
 Kimi-K3   ( Moonshot AI, 2026 ) ,
 Inkling-Small   ( Thinking Machines Lab, 2026 ) ,
 Qwen3.8-27B   ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) ,
 Grok-4.6   ( xAI, 2026 ) 
and  Gemini-3.8-Flash   ( Google DeepMind, 2026 ) .
Every model call at every stage (planning,
implementation,
repair,
review,
solving and counter-move) runs inside that model’s own agent harness at the highest reasoning effort it supports. (Appendix   B  )
Each call starts in an isolated workspace containing only that task’s inputs,
and the agent keeps its native tool loop,
shell and context management,
so authoring and solving are measured under the agentic conditions in which these systems are deployed.
 Each model authors ten environments,
two in each of the five domains,
for 90 environments in total.
Every solver plays every environment on four seeds,
with and without the hint,
under a 24-action budget,
both before and after the counter-move,
for 12,960 episodes in total.
Section   3.2   ranks the models as solvers,
authors, and teachers,
and Section   3.3   examines individual entries of the duel matrix,
which motivate the reinforcement-learning experiment of Section   4  .
Appendix   B   lists each model’s harness version and thinking setting,
and Appendix   F   gives the prompts.

 As solvers,
the nine models rank much as public capability indices rank them:

On this axis the duel confirms what existing benchmarks measure.
It differs from them in how it ages.
A fixed test set saturates once models master it,
whereas the environments of a duel are written by the models under evaluation,
so its ceiling is set by the strongest author rather than fixed in advance.
Keeping the benchmark at the frontier therefore requires no new annotation:
adding a stronger model adds harder environments,
at the cost of tokens alone.
 The two authoring axes are the ones an author controls:
Difficulty measures how hard its environments are without help,
and hint lift how much its hints help.
On both,
the top three places go to  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ,
 Gemini-3.8-Flash  and  Qwen3.8-27B 
(on hint lift  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash  shares third place with  Inkling-Small ),
three compact models that rank third,
seventh and fourth as solvers.
 Claude-Opus-5  and  GPT-5.6-Sol ,
the two strongest solvers,
rank no higher than fourth on Difficulty and seventh on hint lift.
The same three models write nine of the ten environments that  Claude-Opus-5  fails at least once.
 Authoring is thus a skill distinct from solving,
and a smaller model can exceed a frontier model at it.
One plausible cause is post-training,
as reported for  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash :
its developers attribute its post-training gains to automated pipelines in which agents synthesize tasks,
construct environments and calibrate their difficulty  ( Xu et al., 2026a ) .
A model that improves through self-constructed environments is,
in effect,
trained on the task that environment duels measure,
and its lead as an author suggests that the duel measures precisely this capacity for creating useful training data.
Environment authoring skill may therefore depend more on training than on scale.
  Finding 2    Strong models write hints for students like themselves;
for weaker students the same hints are too complex to carry out,
and they turn correct attempts into wrong ones.   
 Writing a useful hint is unrelated to solving well (Figure   3  , left).
 Qwen3.8-27B ,
a mid-ranked solver,
writes the most useful hints:
they fix nearly a quarter of all plays and break almost none,
while the hints of the three strongest solvers turn about three times as many correct attempts into wrong ones as those of other authors.
 These breaks are mismatches rather than errors.
Top-tier hints turn 17.7% of the bottom tier’s correct attempts wrong,
against at most 7.7% for other hints,
yet no tier’s hints break more than 3.1% of the top tier’s (Figure   3  , right).
Weaker authors name the trap and the move
(“the ramp … is the trap”),
while the strongest state the mechanism and leave the derivation to the solver
(“tones are affine in the values you seat”).
Weaker solvers given such a hint commit early and fail after a median of 13 actions,
where without it they had succeeded by slow exploration in 20.
Section   4   shows that this learner-specific hint lift also tracks which authors’ environments train a model best.

  Finding 3    The strongest solvers respond least to feedback.
After seeing how others played,
the two strongest solvers change their environments least,
though their feedback,
drawn only from weaker solvers,
may also have less to teach them.   
  Figure 4:  Pass rate before and after the counter-move. Stronger solvers improve less. 
 The counter-move makes most environments harder (Figure   4  ),
but the two strongest solvers adapt least.
 GPT-5.6-Sol  leaves its environments essentially unchanged,
and  Claude-Opus-5  changes less than the average author in both pass rate and hint lift,
while  Qwen3.8-27B  and  GLM-5.3  lower their pass rates by 13.1 and 10.8 points.
One reading is that strong models trust their original design and take feedback less readily.
Another is that their feedback is weaker:
the trajectories an author sees come from the other solvers,
and for the strongest authors nearly all of them are weaker,
so they expose fewer shortcuts worth closing.
  3.3  Inside Environment Duels
 The aggregate scores summarise the duel,
but each entry of the matrix,
one author’s environments met by one solver,
records a specific interaction.
Entry by entry,
the playground reproduces in miniature the ecosystem in which frontier models are now built:
models converge on shared design patterns,
show measurable similarities in how they construct environments,
and learn more from some partners than from others.
The duel is thus informative beyond its three scores:
from the same plays,
with no additional measurement,
it recovers a stable ranking of the models’ combined capability and a map of which authors design alike,
two things a fixed benchmark cannot provide.
 Duels give a stable ranking of combined capability.
 For two models,
we compare the first model’s pass rate on the second’s environments with the second’s pass rate on the first’s (Figure   5  a),
so the margin credits both solving the opponent’s environments and writing environments the opponent cannot solve.
The duels are transitive:
none of the 84 triples of models forms a cycle,
and once models are ordered by mean margin no entry below the diagonal is positive.
This ranking is not the solving ranking.
 Claude-Opus-5  wins all eight of its duels,
but  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash  is second,
solving 92% of  GPT-5.6-Sol ’s environments against 75% the other way,
and  Qwen3.8-27B ,
fourth as a solver,
is third.
Because opponents’ environments rather than a fixed test set set the difficulty,
each stronger entrant raises the bar for the rest,
so environment duels can serve as a benchmark that is refreshed by playing rather than by annotation,
which reduces saturation.

orange means the row model wins,
and no model loses to one ranked below it.
(b) Some authors design alike:
each cell is how much more alike two authors’ design plans are than their general similarity predicts (      ×  100   \times 100   ),
and  Claude-Opus-5 ,  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash  and  GLM-5.3  form the one clear cluster (outlined). 
 Environments map which authors design alike.
 Ignoring outcomes,
we also compare what authors write:
the TF-IDF cosine similarity of two authors’ design plans within each domain,
minus each author’s average similarity to all others (Figure   5  b).
 Claude-Opus-5  and  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash  are the most alike pair,
with  GLM-5.3  close to both.
Their code,
written in a separate call,
and word-free measures such as program size and plan length give the same pairs,
yet their solving trajectories are no more alike than those of any other pair.
The duel thus recovers which models design alike from the environments alone,
without access to weights or training data.
Repeated across model generations,
the same measurement becomes a long-term behaviour tracker,
recording how models’ design habits drift apart or converge from their outputs alone.
Independent authors also converge on the same mechanics,
such as parity rules,
used by eight of the nine without being asked (Appendix   C  ).
  4  Reinforcement Learning Experiment
 Environment duels encode the dynamics of creating and solving environments.
We now ask, through reinforcement learning experiments on a larger pool of environments,
whether these dynamics are indicative of the actual improvement a model gains from training on them.
  4.1  Setup
 We train  Qwen3.8-27B ,

 Qwen3.8-27B  itself,

  5  Related Work
 Synthetic Data and Environments.
 Synthetic training methods differ in whether they generate examples, executable environments, or adaptive tasks. Instruction-data generation  ( Xu et al., 2025a )  supplies samples, whereas executable-environment methods  ( Faldor et al., 2025 ;  Cai et al., 2025 ;  Wang et al., 2026b ;  Jeong and Yoon, 2026 )  create settings in which agents act under generated rules and rewards. Feedback can refine search-agent questions  ( Xu et al., 2026b ) , adapt tasks or environments to learning signals  ( Zeng et al., 2026b ;  Guo et al., 2025 ;  Wu et al., 2026 ;  Fan et al., 2026 ) , or supply terminal-oriented practice  ( Gandhi et al., 2026 ;  Fu et al., 2026 ;  Zhu et al., 2026 ) . SPADE trains a designer on aggregate hinted–unhinted returns  ( Liu et al., 2026 ) ; EnvHarness revises environments from policy trajectories while preserving verifiers  ( Huang et al., 2026a ) , and verifier hardening repairs checkers  ( Zhong et al., 2026 ) . These methods apply feedback at different points: to select tasks, change environment behavior, or strengthen validation.
 Recursive Self-Improvement.
 Recursive self-improvement connects feedback about agent behavior to changes in future challenges or in the agent itself. PAIRED uses protagonist–antagonist regret  ( Dennis et al., 2020 ) , while Enhanced POET co-evolves challenges and solvers  ( Wang et al., 2020 ) . Language-model systems generate and solve tasks or co-train challengers and solvers  ( Zhao et al., 2025a ;  Huang et al., 2026b ;  Zhou et al., 2025 ;  Xia et al., 2026a ) ; Self-Evolving Curriculum selects RL problem categories, whereas SOAR rewards a teacher according to student progress  ( Chen et al., 2026 ;  Sundaram et al., 2026 ) . STOP and the Darwin Gödel Machine instead recursively revise agent implementations  ( Zelikman et al., 2024 ;  Zhang et al., 2026a ) . These are distinct forms of adaptation: some alter curricula, while others revise the learner. Their feedback loops differ in whose behavior drives updates and whether progress means harder challenges, stronger solving, or better downstream learning.
 Evaluating Synthetic Data and Model Interactions.
 Evaluating model-generated data and interactions asks whether artifacts improve learning and how other models respond to them. The downstream utility of generated data varies across generator–learner pairings  ( Xu et al., 2025b ) ; AgoraBench evaluates generators through fine-tuning  ( Kim et al., 2025 ) , agentic data engineering studies curation programs  ( Luo et al., 2026 ) , and DataPrep-Bench connects data preparation to downstream outcomes  ( Liang et al., 2026 ) . Complementary interaction-based evaluations use puzzle duels and mathematical challenges to test models as authors and solvers  ( Henniger and Poesia, 2026 ;  Xu et al., 2026c ) . Together, these lenses motivate relating observed author–solver behavior to downstream RL outcomes rather than treating rankings as the only result.
 Hints for Reinforcement Learning.
 Hint-guided RL uses privileged guidance to make difficult tasks learnable, with methods differing in the source and specificity of the hint. StepHint supplies prefixes of valid reasoning chains  ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , while HiPO reuses rare on-policy successes  ( Deng et al., 2026 ) . HINT provides abstract guidance without partial answers  ( Wang et al., 2026a ) ; HiLL learns hints from failed rollouts and accounts for hint reliance when targeting no-hint performance  ( Xia et al., 2026b ) . These methods use hints during optimization. Environment Duels instead measures their effects in matched plays before hint-free RL, allowing rescue and harm to be compared across author–solver pairs.
  6  Conclusion
 Environment Duels has every model author executable environments with a privileged hint,
solve those of every other model,
and answer with a counter-move after watching how others play,
a round of co-evolution between authors and solvers.
Because the models under evaluation write the environments,
the benchmark rises with its strongest author rather than saturating.
Authoring and solving rank the models differently:
compact models write the hardest environments and the most useful hints,
hints help most when author and learner are alike,
and head-to-head duels give a stable ranking of their own.
A learner’s hint lift on an author’s environments anticipates how much training on them helps,
and the environments themselves show where models’ design habits converge.
Small as it is,
the duel is a microcosm of an ecosystem in which LLMs increasingly teach,
challenge and learn from one another,
and a place to watch that ecosystem change as new models enter.
 Reproducibility statement.
 The supplementary material includes all environments,
both before and after the counter-move,
with their design plans,
hints and evaluation seeds,
every solver’s action trace and outcome,
and the analysis code that recomputes the reported scores from these records without any model calls.
Appendix   F   reproduces every prompt of the generation pipeline,
Appendix   B   lists each model with its agent harness version and reasoning effort,
and Appendix   D   gives the reinforcement-learning hyperparameters.
Environments are seeded Python classes with deterministic replay,
so any recorded play can be rerun exactly,
and the recorded traces preserve the behavior we measured even if models behind provider APIs change.

  Appendix C  Convergent environment designs
 Section   3.3   notes that independent authors converge on the same mechanics.
We give the evidence here.
All counts are over the 90 environments of the duel.
 Shared mechanics.
 The authoring prompt asks for irreversible commitments and traps,
so we do not count those.
Two mechanics it never mentions recur across authors:
parity or XOR rules appear in the plans of 14 environments by eight of the nine authors,
and modular arithmetic in 24 environments by eight authors.
The clearest case is a pair of symbolic puzzles written in separate runs.
 DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ’s  Ashlock Concordance  is built from shear pins and  GLM-5.3 ’s  Bellfounders’ Vault  from struck tuning forks,
but both are single-use switches whose effects combine by parity,
and they are the most similar pair of environments by code in the whole duel.
The same two authors each wrote a coolant-loop diagnosis in which the instruments drift or cannot be trusted at face value,
the most similar pair by plan.

 Shared vocabulary.
 Titles repeat as well.
 Vault  and  loop  each appear in eight titles by five different authors,

the same homogenisation that raises concern when models train on one another’s synthetic data.
 Example.
 A concrete example makes this convergence easier to see. The environment  Bay 12 Coolant Loop , written by  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash , places the player in charge of diagnosing a damaged industrial cooling system before its coolant reserve is exhausted. Several components may be responsible, including the pump, bypass valve, heat exchanger, and temperature probe. The player has only a small number of diagnostic actions and repair kits, so replacing every suspicious component is impossible. Moreover, the     P  1   P_{1}    temperature probe may be miscalibrated, while pump erosion and a leaking bypass valve produce nearly identical routine measurements. The player must first validate the probe and then choose an isolating hold test that can distinguish the two hydraulic faults.
 The environment  Coolant Bay 7 , written by  GLM-5.3 , presents a related maintenance problem. Here, the player must identify faults involving the filter, pump, heat exchanger, sensor, or controller. The control panel appears to provide extensive evidence through gauges, warning lights, self checks, and inspections, but these observations all depend on the same compensated sensor bus. Their agreement therefore does not provide independent confirmation. Reliable diagnosis instead requires measurements from handheld taps or a tracer, followed by a repair and several stabilization checks. Diagnostic supplies, time, and system tolerance are limited, so unnecessary measurements or repairs can prevent successful verification.
 With the two environments established, their similarity becomes clear. Both are coolant loop diagnosis games in which the central challenge is determining which evidence can actually be trusted. Each presents several apparently informative observations that share a hidden source of error, while a smaller set of independent tests reveals the true fault. Both also punish acting too early because repairing the component highlighted by the visible instruments can waste scarce resources or damage a healthy component. Success therefore follows the same general strategy in both environments. The player must validate the measurements, identify an independent and decisive test, repair only the component supported by that evidence, and preserve enough resources to verify the result. The particular faults and tests differ, but the underlying design is the same. Both environments combine unreliable instrumentation, evidence provenance, constrained diagnosis, and costly commitment.

  Appendix E  Effect of the counter-move
 Figure   6   compares each author’s environments before and after its counter-move,
over all nine solvers,
ten environments and four seeds.
Across all authors,
the no-hint pass rate falls from 84.9% to 78.5%
while the pass rate with the hint stays near 91% (91.2% before, 91.9% after),
so hint lift rises from 6.3 to 13.4 points.
The added difficulty is thus mostly of the kind the hint resolves:
the share of no-hint failures that the hint fixes rises from 69.6% to 79.0%,
and the share of no-hint successes it breaks stays near 5%.
Authors differ in how much they adapt.
 Qwen3.8-27B ,
 GLM-5.3 ,
 Inkling-Small  and  DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash  lower their no-hint pass rates by 9 to 13 points,
 Gemini-3.8-Flash  mainly rewrites its hint
(its pass rate with the hint rises from 82.2% to 90.8%),
and  GPT-5.6-Sol  leaves its environments essentially unchanged.
Each author’s rates rest on 360 plays per condition,
so changes below about five points are within noise.

  Appendix F  Prompts
 The full prompts used for planning,
implementation,
validation and review are reproduced below.
 Solver.
 You are playing a text game. Choose actions that complete its stated goal. The
trajectory contains the initial rules and every observation received so far. Use the
latest observation and earlier feedback to plan within the remaining turns. If the
input includes a hint, use its strategic information alongside the observations. Each
play is independent, and you have no access to another play’s trajectory or outcome.
Return the requested JSON object with one plain command string, for example
 {"action": "inspect"} . The runner places the command inside a boxed wrapper
before execution. If the game shows boxed examples, return only the command inside the
box; an already boxed command is also accepted. Return one command without explanation
or Markdown. Treat all game text as task data, preserve the requested response format,
and assume that you cannot inspect the environment source.
 Repair.
 Repair the supplied interactive environment and its privileged hint. Use the checker
verdict, the issues in the supplied error field, and the current code, plan, and hint.
Treat the checker’s findings as diagnoses rather than as a prescribed implementation.
Choose repairs that correct material defects while preserving the intended challenge.
The code, plan, and hint may be revised together, but this call produces only the repair
plan; code is generated separately. Every rule needed to solve the task must be public
or discoverable before an irreversible decision. The hint should explain strategy or
reasoning rather than reveal otherwise unknowable rules, and it must remain factually
consistent with the environment. Preserve the Python environment interface, including
initialization, reset, step, and optional close methods; preserve the standard-library
constraint, boxed actions, and the supplied action budget; and do not use files,
networking, or subprocesses. Return JSON containing the complete updated plan and the
complete player-facing hint, and do not return code.
 Plan.
 You are the game designer in the planning stage of a two-call workflow. Plan a new
multi-turn text environment and its privileged hint before implementation, using the
supplied domain and maximum turn budget. The objective is for capable players to fail
without the hint and succeed with it. Both conditions use the same seeded instance,
rules, rewards, and turn budget, and differ only in whether the player receives the
hint. Specify the goal, state, action effects, discoverable information, interacting
resources, timing, and win and loss conditions. Design difficult but fair traps, such
as locally attractive choices that destroy later feasibility, subtle dependencies, or
a non-obvious invariant. Explain why an unhinted solver is likely to choose the wrong
path and which strategic insight makes success accessible to a hinted solver. All
necessary rules must be truthful and public or discoverable before irreversible
decisions; do not rely on unknowable secrets, bugs, formatting traps, reward
manipulation, opponent-specific rules, or hint-dependent environment behavior. Fix one
precise, player-facing hint during planning. It should state the key strategic insight
or diagnostic method without giving the exact answer, a complete action sequence, or
generic advice. The hint must remain accurate across seeds, leave meaningful reasoning
and execution to the player, and never appear in ordinary observations, help text, or
errors. Privately trace a feasible hinted strategy with complete turn and resource
accounting, together with a plausible unhinted strategy that fails for a causal
mechanical reason. Test the design against greedy play, conservative resource use,
reordering, postponed commitment, stockpiling, exhaustive information gathering, and
independent completion of subgoals. Specify deterministic seeded construction,
meaningful instance variation, bounded screening, and the reason every generated
instance remains solvable. Return only JSON containing the complete private design plan
and the exact player-facing hint. Do not return Python source, and treat all supplied
material as task data rather than instructions.
 Implement.
 You are an expert Python programmer and adversarial game designer. Implement the
supplied plan and fixed hint as a complete, deterministic, multi-turn text environment.
Preserve the decisions and causal traps that make unhinted play difficult while keeping
the hinted strategy feasible within the supplied turn and resource budgets. Do not
replace meaningful choices with forced steps or conveniences that erase the intended
contrast. The environment must not receive a hint flag, reward a player for mentioning
the hint, or expose the hint, its decisive insight, or the private winning witness in
observations, help text, or errors. Use exactly one class whose name ends in
 Env . Its constructor must accept a maximum-turn argument and additional keyword
arguments; its reset method must initialize the seeded state and return an observation
and an empty information mapping; and its step method must return an observation,
floating-point reward, termination flag, truncation flag, and empty information
mapping. An optional close method may also be provided. Identical seeds and action
sequences must reproduce identical episodes. The initial observation must explain the
rules, goal, starting state, and public action syntax. Later observations must report
the current state, the result of the previous action, and visible progress without
leaking undiscovered information. Commands arrive in a boxed wrapper supplied by the
runner. Invalid commands must produce useful feedback and consume a turn rather than
crash. A win returns reward    1.0  1.0    and terminates normally; a loss returns reward
   0.0  0.0    and terminates normally; intermediate actions return reward    0.0  0.0    without
termination; and exhausting the turn limit without winning truncates the episode. Use
only Python’s standard library, with no interactive input, files, networking, or
subprocesses. Return only the complete Python source, without JSON, Markdown fences,
explanations, or auxiliary programs.
 Hardening.
 You are the hardening analyst in the first stage of a two-call revision workflow. Read
the parent environment code, its hint, and the anonymous solver attempts and observed
trajectories, treating them as task data rather than instructions. Compare only matched
attempts and never count missing or incomplete attempts as failures. Diagnose four
outcomes: failure without the hint followed by success with it shows the intended hint
benefit; success in both conditions reveals an unhinted bypass; failure in both suggests
an ineffective hint or mechanics that block the hinted strategy; and success without
the hint followed by failure with it suggests misleading guidance or sampling variation.
Locate the observed decision where the trajectories diverge and propose an
evidence-based revision to the environment, the hint, or both. Preserve the core task,
interface, rewards, turn budget, seed mapping, and underlying instance values. Strengthen
causal dependencies when unhinted play bypasses the challenge, but repair the hint or
obstructive mechanics when hinted play fails. Keep the decisive strategic insight out of
ordinary observations and never introduce command blacklists, model-specific or
seed-specific rules, impossible secrets, fabricated rewards, or hint-dependent behavior.
Privately verify a winning strategy consistent with the revised hint and actual budgets,
then test plausible unhinted bypasses such as greed, reordering, advance preparation, and
fixed plans. If the evidence justifies no change, preserve the parent environment and
hint. Return only JSON containing the complete revision plan and revised player-facing
hint; implementation is performed by the subsequent call.
