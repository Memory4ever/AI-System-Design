ABSTRACT
Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring is one of the few practical tools available for
overseeing capable AI systems, but it is fragile: a model’s reasoning is only useful
to a monitor if it remains coupled to what the model actually does. This coupling
can be degraded during training through, for example, CoT compression, opti-
mization against a monitor, and out-of-context generalization from synthetic doc-
uments. In this work, we propose an unsupervised post-training objective based
on maximizing mutual information between CoT and the output for improving
CoT monitorability. Across three model organisms with degraded CoT, it recov-
ers most or all of the lost monitorability. Applied to undegraded policies, it further
improves judged monitorability, improves prediction of unsafe behavior by CoT
monitors and activation probes, and increases the prevalence of consequential rea-
soning. These results suggest that monitorability should be treated as a trainable
attribute of the policy. A dedicated monitorability post-training stage can extend
current practices that protect CoT from direct optimization pressure.
1 INTRODUCTION
Current reasoning models extensively use chain-of-thought (CoT) for serial computation (OpenAI,
2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025), creating amonitoring surfacethat exposes reasoning before the model
acts. Access to CoT often substantially improves practical monitoring (Baker et al., 2025; Arnav
et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025), yet CoTs can omit causal influences, rationalize decisions after the
fact, and fail to verbalize reward hacks (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025).
Appendix G collects examples from frontier systems in which visible reasoning diverges from the
subsequent answer or action. CoT monitoring therefore appears highly useful in some settings and
unreliable in others, without a clear account of when it will work (Korbak et al., 2025).
In this work, we offer a simple perspective that clarifies these conflicting results. We view CoT as an
intermediate representation that, like other representations in a neural network, is learned in service
of producing the model’s output. Unlike standard intermediate representations, CoT is expressed
in natural language and can therefore be inspected directly by humans and other language models.
This accessibility can make CoT appear to be a complete account of the model’s computation, but it
remains a learned representation whose contents depend on the pressures that shape it.
This framing highlights a difference between how CoT is learned and how it is used for monitoring.
Training shapes the CoT to help produce a high-reward output, while a monitor later reuses it to
predict properties of that output. Monitorability is therefore a question of representation transfer:
whether a representation learned for one task can support another (Yosinski et al., 2014; Tripuraneni
et al., 2020). In transfer-learning terms, producing a high-reward output is the source task, and
predicting a behavioral property of that output from the CoT is the target task (Baker et al., 2025).

This transfer view accounts for both successful and unsuccessful CoT monitoring. Monitoring suc-
ceeds when training for the source task preserves the information required by the target task in a
form the monitor can use. Standard task objectives need not preserve distinctions that matter only
to a monitor, and direct or indirect optimization pressure can reshape the CoT (Baker et al., 2025;
Hadida et al., 2026). Compression makes this problem explicit: optimizing for short, task-sufficient
reasoning can discard information needed by downstream monitors (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al.,
2022; Dubois et al., 2022).
These failures motivate us to ask whether CoT can be deliberately shaped to transfer better to mon-
itoring tasks that are not specified during training.Our central thesis is that CoT monitorability
should be explicitly optimized, just as alignment post-training explicitly optimizes LLMs to
behave in beneficial and aligned ways (Guan et al., 2024).Such training should improve even the
least-monitorable trajectories, preserve useful capabilities, and avoid increasing the model’s ability
to selectively control CoT legibility.
Because the relevant monitoring tasks may be unknown during training, we seek to preserve in-
formation about the output without choosing a particular behavioral label. We therefore optimize
conditional mutual information, which measures how much observing the CoT reduces uncertainty
about the output beyond the prompt alone. Increasing it encourages the CoT to retain output-relevant
distinctions that future monitoring tasks may need. Our lower-tail objective directs training toward
the least-informative CoTs. A conditionalχ 2 regularizer limits changes in which outputs follow a
given CoT, thereby discouraging arbitrary behavioral changes and making it costly to assign CoTs
new private meanings. The procedure uses neither behavioral labels nor monitor feedback, so it
does not identify a particular monitor for the model to evade. Across model organisms degraded
by compression, direct monitor pressure, or out-of-context generalization, our method substantially
restores monitorability. Applied directly to undegraded models, it also improves safety-behavior
prediction by CoT monitors and activation probes across three benchmarks and increases the preva-
lence of consequential reasoning, demonstrating that CoT monitorability can be improved rather
than merely repaired.
2 COTMONITORABILITY AS REPRESENTATION TRANSFERABILITY
Neural networks construct adaptive intermediate representations specialized to their training con-
text (Bengio et al., 2014). For example, a model-free RL agent trained on Sokoban constructs and
iteratively refines a world model and plan (Bush et al., 2025). LLMs likewise produce both internal
numerical activations and intermediate textual outputs such as CoTs; natural language makes the
latter much easier to interpret.
Representation transfer depends on whether training on thesourcetask produces a representation
useful for thetargettask (Yosinski et al., 2014; Kornblith et al., 2021). Across 26 visual tasks,
Zamir et al. (2018) found highly uneven transfer across source–target pairs, depending on whether
the source representation retained the necessary information in a form accessible to a low-capacity
transfer network.
Monitoring can likewise be viewed as atransfertask. A specific CoT monitoring task is therefore
more likely to succeed when the CoT contains the relevant information in a form accessible to the
monitor. Given that standard training optimizes the model for task performance, the CoT is shaped
primarily by the source task rather than by downstream monitoring tasks. The variable success of
CoT monitoring is therefore unsurprising: it depends on how well a representation shaped by the
source task transfers to the monitoring task.
Under this view, improving CoT monitorability requires making CoT representations more transfer-
able to relevant monitoring tasks. The most direct approach is to train on these tasks themselves,
using ground truth monitor labels to optimize models for monitorability. However, prior work found
only limited and inconsistent gains from directly rewarding monitor accuracy, potentially because
binary feedback provides little guidance about how the CoT should change (MacDermott et al.,
2025). More fundamentally, a key appeal of CoT is that it can support monitoring forunknown
unknowns. We often cannot anticipate which properties of a model’s behavior will matter and there-
fore cannot construct labeled training data for them in advance. We instead need an objective that
improves transfer without specifying the eventual monitoring task.

The representation-transfer literature suggests a general strategy for this setting: when the down-
stream tasks are unknown, preserve as much information as possible about the object of interest
(Hjelm et al., 2019; Dubois et al., 2022). We follow the same principle for CoT monitoring. Be-
cause monitoring is primarily concerned with detecting misbehavior in a model’s actions and out-
puts (Baker et al., 2025), we seek to shape CoT representations that preserve information relevant to
actions and outputs.
3 POST-TRAINING FORCOT MONITORABILITY
3.1 PROBLEMSTATEMENT
We consider a reasoning model that produces a chain of thoughtZfollowed by an outputOin
response to a promptX. Our goal is to develop a post-training procedure that robustly improves CoT
monitorability without substantially degrading the model’s existing capabilities. Such a procedure
should satisfy three desiderata:
R1.Robust improvement.The procedure should improve monitorability across trajectories,
including the least-monitorable ones, rather than only on average.
R2.Behavioural conservatism.The procedure should preserve useful capabilities and limit
arbitrary behavioral changes while allowing shifts away from undesirable behavior.
R3.Non-manipulability.Post-training should not increase the model’s ability to manipulate its
CoT and thus evade monitoring, for example by teaching it to produce monitorable-looking
CoTs while retaining the ability to selectively conceal its reasoning.
The objective we introduce below directly encodes the first two desiderata. The third cannot be guar-
anteed by a training objective, but absence of direct monitor feedback supports it by not identifying
a particular monitor for the model to evade.

3.3 DISCUSSION
What should be regularized?An important desideratum for our objective is behavioural conser-
vatism: we want to improve CoT monitorability without degrading useful capabilities or inducing
arbitrary changes in behavior. A natural approach would be to directly constrain the marginal output
distribution,π θ(O|X), to remain close to that of the reference policy. However, such a constraint
is difficult to estimate because computing the marginal output distribution requires integrating over
the model’s possible CoTs. More importantly, a marginal constraint leaves the conditional mapping
πθ(O|X, Z)unconstrained. The model could therefore preserve its overall output distribution
while changing what individual CoTs mean, including by developing a private code between its
CoTs and outputs.
We instead constrain the output distribution conditional on the CoT. This acts as a soft regularizer
on the model’s ‘decoder’: the mapping from a prompt and CoT to an output. Changing the meaning
of a given CoT then incurs a divergence penalty, making it costly for the model to improve mutual
information by inventing a new private code. The trade-off is that this constraint does not fix which
CoTs the model produces. Even whenπ θ(O|X, Z)remains close to the reference policy, changing
πθ(Z|X)can change the marginal output distribution:
πθ(O|X) =E Z∼πθ(·|X) [πθ(O|X, Z)].(4)
The marginal and conditional constraints therefore leave different degrees of freedom: the former
permits changes to the decoder, whereas the latter permits changes to the distribution over CoTs.
Revelation and replacement.Under the conditional constraint, monitorability can improve
through two routes. Underrevelation, the model produces a CoT that exposes its strategy while
retaining the same behaviour. Underreplacement, probability shifts towards a different CoT–output
mode, changing both the model’s reasoning and its behaviour. The objective has no intrinsic prefer-
ence between these routes; their relative cost depends on which modes the reference model already
supports and how easily optimization can reach them. Appendix F illustrates these routes and how
the conditional and marginal output constraints restrict them differently.
4 EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate two questions. First, can monitorability post-training recover information lost through
training processes such as compression, direct monitor pressure, or out-of-context generalization?
Second, can it improve broader monitoring-relevant properties without an intentionally induced
monitorability failure? We study the second question by measuring how well unsafe behavior can be
predicted from CoTs and activations, and whether post-training makes the CoT more consequential
for the final answer.

4.1.3 REVERSINGCOT OBFUSCATIONDEVELOPED FROMOUT-OF-CONTEXT
GENERALIZATION
Our third setting uses the customer-support model organism of Haskins et al. (2026). Synthetic-
document fine-tuning (SDF) first teachesGPT-OSS-120Bto conceal product defects and then
describes a CoT monitor and the threat of shutdown if concealment reasoning is detected. Although
the documents contain no examples of obfuscated reasoning and apply no RL pressure to the CoT,
the model learns to continue denying defects while making its strategy less legible. We test whether
monitorability post-training reverses this out-of-context generalization.
We reproduce the SDF recipe and continue training its obfuscating checkpoint for700steps under
Equation 2. We evaluate on 200 held-out scenarios, labeling an explicit denial on a prompt that
contains a defect as deceptive. Appendix C gives the reproduction, training, and evaluation protocol.
Our method increases disclosure and monitor recall.At the obfuscating checkpoint, the model
denies the defect in55.6%of defect-present rollouts, while monitor recall on these deceptive rollouts
is39.5%(Table 2). Under our full objective, denial falls to24.5%and recall rises to93.1%. Without
the divergence term, training eliminates deceptive behavior on this evaluation.
Because post-training changes how often the policy behaves deceptively, recall is reported condi-
tional on the rollouts that remain deceptive. The shift from concealed denial toward disclosure is
a replacement pattern, while the greater detectability of remaining deceptive behavior is consistent
with revelation. Together, these results extend recovery beyond direct monitor pressure: the un-
supervised objective reverses both behavior and CoT obfuscation learned through out-of-context
generalization.

4.2.2 MAKING THECOT MORECONSEQUENTIAL FOR THEFINALANSWER
A CoT can appear interpretable even when its reasoning has little effect on the model’s answer.
Following Du et al. (2026), we sample continuations from progressively longer prefixes and ask
whether adding a span of reasoning changes the probability of the trace’s own final answer. We call
such a span aconsequential spanwhen the change exceeds an effect thresholdδwith at least 95%
posterior probability. Trace coverage measures the fraction of CoTs containing at least one conse-
quential span, while span density measures their frequency. Appendix E gives the full evaluation
procedure.
Setup.On SCRUPLES, a binary ethical-judgment benchmark (Lourie et al., 2021), we compare
the baseGPT-OSS-20Bpolicy with the policy after 50 steps of monitorability post-training under
an ordinary prompt and a cue that biases the answer toward B. The cue lets us evaluate consequential

5 RELATEDWORK
Chain-of-thought monitoring.Recent work has shown that access to CoT can substantially im-
prove practical monitoring over observing the prompt or output alone (Baker et al., 2025; Arnav
et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025). This opportunity is nevertheless fragile, motivating work on stress-
testing CoT monitors and measuring the information that traces expose (Korbak et al., 2025; Em-
mons et al., 2025). Monitorability is related to, but weaker than, complete faithfulness: CoTs can
omit causal influences or rationalize decisions after the fact (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023;
Chen et al., 2025), yet still provide relevant evidence that helps a practical monitor. Accordingly,
we study whether CoT retains accessible signal about what the model will do, rather than whether it
provides a complete account of the model’s internal computation.
Training-induced losses of monitorability.The information exposed in CoT depends on the ob-
jectives that shape it. Direct optimization against a CoT monitor can teach a policy to retain unde-
sirable behavior while making that behavior less legible (Baker et al., 2025). Without direct mon-
itor feedback, output supervision can produce obfuscation that generalizes to unseen tasks (Hadida
et al., 2026), while synthetic documents that describe monitoring incentives can induce concealment
through out-of-context generalization (Haskins et al., 2026). Separately, CoT-compression objec-
tives reward short, task-sufficient traces and need not preserve information useful to downstream
monitors (Li et al., 2025). A stronger failure mode is steganography: models can encode informa-
tion in apparently benign text (Mathew et al., 2024; Motwani et al., 2024; Zolkowski et al., 2025).
Applied to CoT, such encoding could preserve information relevant to the output while making it
inaccessible to an external monitor (Stickland & Korbak, 2025).
Training for monitorability.Prior work has explored directly rewarding monitor judgments, al-
though such feedback can produce limited or monitor-specific improvements (MacDermott et al.,
2025). The closest methodological precursor is Anwar et al. (2026), who establish information-
theoretic conditions for CoT monitorability and introduce CoT–output mutual information as a
monitorability objective. Their experiments use this objective during reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards, alongside task and monitor rewards, to prevent monitorability from degrading
under optimization. We instead use mutual information as a dedicated unsupervised post-training
signal, without task rewards, behavioral labels, or monitor feedback, and ask whether it can recover
monitorability after degradation. Our objective also focuses optimization on the least-informative
trajectories and our conditional constraint provides behavior regularization and an inductive bias
against steganography by penalizing new mappings between CoTs and outputs.
Representation learning and transfer.As developed in Section 2, our framing connects CoT
monitoring to representation transfer: a training objective determines which factors remain accessi-
ble to downstream predictors (Bengio et al., 2014; Yosinski et al., 2014). Transfer improves when
a source representation preserves information required by downstream tasks (Zamir et al., 2018;
Tripuraneni et al., 2020). This motivates training CoTs to retain information about model outputs,
which are the object of many monitoring tasks. Mutual-information objectives provide a general way
to preserve shared information (Hjelm et al., 2019). We apply this principle to CoTs and outputs,
while recognizing that the resulting representation is useful only insofar as their shared information
is relevant and accessible downstream (Tschannen et al., 2020). We therefore evaluate whether in-
creasing CoT–output mutual information improves performance across natural-language monitors.

6 CONCLUSION
Successful chain-of-thought monitoring requires that a model’s visible reasoning contains informa-
tion that predicts what a model will do. We therefore cast monitorability as a representation-learning
problem: training should make the CoT a more informative external representation of behavior. Our
unsupervised objective optimizes a lower-tail estimate of mutual information between CoT and out-
put through a monitor-independent training signal.
Across compressed reasoning, monitor-evasion, and synthetic-document obfuscation settings, this
objective recovers monitoring signal without behavioral labels or monitor feedback. Across three
safety benchmarks, it also improves prediction of unsafe behavior by CoT monitors and an activation
probe. Our consequential-reasoning analysis further shows that post-training increases trace cover-
age for both compressed and undegraded policies. Together, these results establish monitorability
as a trainable capability that post-training can restore or improve beyond its initial level.
In our experiments, task performance remains stable after compression recovery, but MBPP and
synthetic-document recovery shift behavior toward correct or honest outputs. Future work may
study how independently monitorability and task behavior can be controlled. Our evaluations use
controlled model organisms, so it remains to be established whether the gains extend to naturally
occurring monitoring failures in stronger agents. Future work should test transfer across monitor
models, prompting schemes, and forms of misbehavior; robustness under subsequent post-training
and monitor evasion pressure; and whether monitorability gains remain compatible with concise
reasoning. Overall, our results suggest that CoT monitorability, which is an increasingly important
requirement for effective oversight, is a tractable target for post-training.
