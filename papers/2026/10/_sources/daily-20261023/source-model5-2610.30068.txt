Abstract
Recent Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) use Self-Conditioning (SC), or Loopholing, to improve their performance.
While SC is traditionally seen as an auxiliary heuristic, its empirical success raises the question: can a continuous generative model be based purely on Self-Conditioning?
We answer affirmatively with LAW (“Loopholing All the Way”), which propagates the Expected Embedding under the denoiser’s prediction across steps, decoding the tokens once, at the end.
On Sudoku, LAW outperforms previous Discrete and Continuous Diffusion Models, including Loopholing Discrete Diffusion. On Maze, LAW is the most accurate from 2 to 64 NFE. After training on TinyGSM and sampling at temperature 1 with 1024 NFE, LAW solves 42.2% of GSM8K problems against 34.5% for uniform Loopholing Discrete Diffusion. Additionally, fine-tuning a pre-trained Uniform Diffusion Model (Duo) with LAW increases its accuracy from 17.2% to 48.1%. Finally, on OpenWebText, LAW pushes the Pareto frontier and approaches Autoregressive Models (ARMs) with 64 NFE. Find our code at https://github.com/ilievanadezhda/law .

1 Introduction
Although Autoregressive Models (ARMs) dominate language modeling ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ; Kaplan et al., 2020 ; Gemini Team et al., 2023 ; Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) , their generation process is sequential and their left-to-right factorization can be limiting for tasks that need bidirectional context ( Kitouni et al., 2024 ; Papadopoulos et al., 2024 ; Nagarajan et al., 2025 ) . Discrete Diffusion Models (DDMs) ( Austin et al., 2021 ; Campbell et al., 2022 ; Lou et al., 2024 ; Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2024 ; Ou et al., 2025 ; Sahoo et al., 2025 ; Schiff et al., 2025 ; von Rütte et al., 2025 ) , the most common Diffusion Language Models (DLMs), instead use a bidirectional architecture and can refine tokens in parallel. For tractability, DDMs sample each position independently and discard the denoiser’s prediction after each step. This causes two problems. First, factorized, parallel sampling hurts the sample quality in few-step generation ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2024 ; Feng et al., 2025 ) . Second, discarding previous predictions means that the associated uncertainty is lost ( information collapse ; Jo et al., 2026 ).
Continuous DLMs ( Dieleman et al., 2022 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Gulrajani and Hashimoto, 2023 ; Jo and Hwang, 2025 ; Batzolis et al., 2026 ; Chemseddine et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026a ; Potaptchik et al., 2026 ; Roos et al., 2026 ; Shen et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026 ; Yoo et al., 2026 ) instead avoid the factorized sampling by integrating an ODE or SDE from noise to data ( Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023 ; Lipman et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2023b ) . However, on reasoning, they at most match DDMs. For example, FLM ( Lee et al., 2026a ) and 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ) remain close to MDLM and Duo on Sudoku and Maze, and 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM matches MDLM on GSM8K only with greedy generation.
In contrast, Self-Conditioning (SC; Strudel et al., 2022 ; Chen et al., 2023 ) feeds the previous predictions or hidden representations of the denoiser back as input, and improves both discrete and continuous DLMs. For example, Loopholing Discrete Diffusion Models (LDDMs; Jo et al., 2026 ) and DiffusionGemma ( Google DeepMind, 2026 ) propagate such a state across denoising steps. On hard Sudoku puzzles, LDDM-U solves 91.0%, against 58.4% for Duo and at most 45.0% for FLMs (Sec. ). So far, despite its empirical success, SC always plays an auxiliary role. We therefore ask:
“Can we find a generative model based purely on Self-Conditioning?”
Figure 2: Overview. Sampling goes from left to right, applying the backbone f θ f_{\theta} repeatedly. Boxed words are discrete tokens, colored cells SC states and white cells zero-valued states. Top: Loopholing (LDDM; Jo et al., 2026 ). At each step, discrete tokens are updated and forwarded alongside the SC state ( ). Bottom: LAW. At each step, we only propagate the SC state, computed by the state map Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} ( ). The initial tokens are drawn from the uniform prior, and we decode back to discrete tokens only at the end ( ).
We answer this question positively with “Loopholing All the Way” (LAW, Figure ):
•
We propose LAW, a continuous generative model based purely on Self-Conditioning . As in Uniform Diffusion Models (UDMs), sampling starts from a sequence of uniformly random tokens. Afterwards, LAW refines the Expected Embedding of the clean token, under the denoiser’s prediction. Thus, we decode back to tokens only once, at the last sampling step.
•
We identify the two choices that make pure SC work: the Expected Embedding as SC state (35.8% on GSM8K, against below 1.5% with the final-layer hidden representation of Jo et al., 2026 ), and temperature regularization ( ), which randomly scales the denoiser’s logits during training, doubles the GSM8K accuracy at T = 0.1 T=0.1 (24.8% to 48.8%).
•
LAW surpasses LDDM-U on hard Sudoku (96.5% vs. 91.0%, Sec. ) and is the most accurate model on Maze with up to 64 NFE (Sec. ). On GSM8K, it outperforms the diffusion and flow baselines trained with the same FLOPs, and fine-tuning a pre-trained Duo checkpoint with LAW increases the accuracy from 17.2% to 48.1% (Sec. ). On OpenWebText, at the same entropy, its Generative Perplexity (Gen. PPL) comes close to that of ARMs with 64 NFE. With 1024 NFE, LAW’s samples have lower Gen. PPL than those of the AR baseline, though the entropy reaches at most 5.1–5.2 over the swept temperatures (Sec. ).

Notation.

2.1 Uniform Discrete Diffusion Models (UDMs)
DDMs map the data distribution to a simple prior with a fixed forward process that gradually corrupts clean tokens, and learn to reverse it ( Austin et al., 2021 ; Campbell et al., 2022 ) . The prior is either a mask token, as in Masked Diffusion ( Sahoo et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2024 ; Ou et al., 2025 ) , or the uniform distribution over the vocabulary, as in UDMs ( Hoogeboom et al., 2021 ; Lou et al., 2024 ; Sahoo et al., 2025 ; Schiff et al., 2025 ) . Masked Diffusion fixes each token once it is unmasked, whereas UDMs can revise tokens at every step. Given a monotonically decreasing noise schedule α t \alpha_{t} from α 0 = 1 \alpha_{0}=1 to α 1 = 0 \alpha_{1}=0 , each token is corrupted independently, 𝐳 t ∼ ∏ ℓ = 1 q t ( ⋅ ∣ 𝐱 ) \mathbf{z}_{t}\sim\prod_{\ell=1}q_{t}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}) , with

(1)
Thus, 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} remains clean with probability α t \alpha_{t} and is otherwise drawn uniformly from the vocabulary. To reverse the forward process, the denoiser 𝐱 θ ​ ( 𝐳 t , t ) \mathbf{x}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t) learns to predict the clean tokens. It composes the embedding matrix E θ E_{\theta} , a stack of Transformer blocks f θ f_{\theta} , the Language Modeling (LM) head g θ g_{\theta} (an affine projection), and a softmax:

where 𝐡 t \mathbf{h}_{t} is the final-layer hidden representation. Generation refines the noisy state 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} from t = 1 t=1 to t = 0 t=0 . From time t t to s < t s<t , the reverse step samples from the categorical denoising posterior q s | t ​ ( 𝐳 s ∣ 𝐳 t , 𝐱 ) q_{s|t}(\mathbf{z}_{s}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{x}) , which depends on the unknown clean token 𝐱 \mathbf{x} . Recent DDMs replace 𝐱 \mathbf{x} in q s | t q_{s|t} with the prediction 𝐱 θ ​ ( 𝐳 t , t ) \mathbf{x}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t) . 2.2 Loopholing Discrete Diffusion Models (LDDMs)
The sampling step ( ) only propagates the discrete tokens 𝐳 s \mathbf{z}_{s} to the next step. Thus, the predictions 𝐱 θ ​ ( 𝐳 t , t ) \mathbf{x}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t) and their uncertainty are discarded after sampling ( information collapse ). LDDMs ( Jo et al., 2026 ) address this with SC ( Chen et al., 2023 ) , which feeds the previous predictions or hidden representations of the denoiser back as input. Alongside the sampled tokens, they propagate the SC state 𝐜 \mathbf{c} , the layer-normalized final-layer hidden representation ( ). At the next step, 𝐜 \mathbf{c} is added to the token embeddings. Starting from 𝐜 1 = 𝟎 \mathbf{c}_{1}=\mathbf{0} , one LDDM sampling step from time t t to s s maps ( 𝐳 t , 𝐜 t ) (\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{c}_{t}) to ( 𝐳 s , 𝐜 s ) (\mathbf{z}_{s},\mathbf{c}_{s}) :
( 𝐳 t , 𝐜 t ) ⏟ 𝒱 × ℝ ↦ f θ ​ ( 𝐳 t ​ E θ + 𝐜 t , t ) 𝐡 t ⏟ ℝ ↦ ( q s | t ∘ softmax ∘ g θ , Φ θ ) ( 𝐳 s , 𝐜 s ) ⏟ 𝒱 × ℝ , \underbrace{(\mathbf{z}_{t},\,\mathbf{c}_{t})}_{\mathcal{V}\times\,\mathbb{R}}\xmapsto{\;f_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t}E_{\theta}\,+\,\mathbf{c}_{t},\ t)\;}\underbrace{\mathbf{h}_{t}}_{\mathbb{R}}\xmapsto{\;\left(q_{s|t}\,\circ\,\mathrm{softmax}\,\circ\,g_{\theta},\ \,\Phi_{\theta}\right)\;}\underbrace{(\mathbf{z}_{s},\,\mathbf{c}_{s})}_{\mathcal{V}\times\,\mathbb{R}},
(5)
where 𝐳 s \mathbf{z}_{s} is sampled from the posterior q s | t ( ⋅ ∣ 𝐳 t , softmax ( g θ ( 𝐡 t ) ) ) q_{s|t}\!\left(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z}_{t},\ \mathrm{softmax}(g_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t}))\right) and the state map Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 ) = LN ​ ( 𝐡 ) \Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h})=\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{h}) computes 𝐜 s = Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 t ) \mathbf{c}_{s}=\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t}) . The masked (LDDM-M) and uniform (LDDM-U) variants build on MDLM ( Sahoo et al., 2024 ) and Duo ( Sahoo et al., 2025 ) , respectively. During training, with probability p p , the multi-pass probability ( p = 0.9 p=0.9 by default), a first forward pass on ( 𝐳 t , 𝟎 ) (\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{0}) computes 𝐜 t = sg ⁡ [ Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 t ) ] \mathbf{c}_{t}=\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t})\right] , and the NELBO ( ) is computed after a second pass on ( 𝐳 t , 𝐜 t ) (\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{c}_{t}) only. Otherwise, LDDMs execute a standard UDM training step with 𝐜 t = 𝟎 \mathbf{c}_{t}=\mathbf{0} . Thus, 𝐜 t \mathbf{c}_{t} is computed at the same time t t during training, but comes from the previous, noisier step during sampling. Despite this train–test mismatch, LDDMs outperform the pure discrete baselines ( Jo et al., 2026 ) , e.g., on hard Sudoku, LDDM-U solves 91.0% of the puzzles, against 58.4% for Duo (Sec. ).
3 Loopholing All the Way (LAW)
Although LDDMs use SC, they operate on a sequence of discrete tokens, which the SC state only complements (Sec. ). Since SC can preserve continuous information across steps, we propose LAW, a generative model based purely on SC. LAW propagates only the SC state and decodes discrete tokens at the end. As the SC state becomes the only input of the denoiser, it should resemble the token embeddings that the denoiser is trained on. We therefore use the Expected Embedding under the denoiser’s prediction, a convex combination of the token embeddings. We present the generative process first (Sec. ), since it motivates the training procedure (Sec. ).
3.1 Generative Process
Initialization.
As for a UDM at t = 1 t=1 , we obtain the initial state 𝐜 1 \mathbf{c}_{1} by embedding tokens drawn from the uniform prior:
𝐜 1 = 𝐳 1 ​ E θ , 𝐳 1 ∼ Cat ⁡ ( ⋅ , 1 | 𝒱 | ​ 𝟏 ) . \mathbf{c}_{1}=\mathbf{z}_{1}E_{\theta},\qquad\mathbf{z}_{1}\sim\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(\cdot\,;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\mathbf{1}\right).
(6)
Continuous State Updates.
For n = N , … , 2 n=N,\ldots,2 , we pass the SC state 𝐜 t n \mathbf{c}_{t_{n}} through the denoising backbone and map the resulting hidden representation into the next state:
𝐜 t n ⏟ ℝ ↦ f θ ​ ( ⋅ , t n ) 𝐡 t n ⏟ ℝ ↦ Φ θ ​ ( ⋅ , T ) 𝐜 t n − 1 ⏟ ℝ . \underbrace{\mathbf{c}_{t_{n}}}_{\mathbb{R}}\xmapsto{\;f_{\theta}(\cdot,\,t_{n})\;}\underbrace{\mathbf{h}_{t_{n}}}_{\mathbb{R}}\xmapsto{\;\Phi_{\theta}(\cdot\,;\,T)\;}\underbrace{\mathbf{c}_{t_{n-1}}}_{\mathbb{R}}.
(7)
Thus, the backbone receives the SC state alone, and no tokens are sampled between steps. The state map computes the Expected Embedding (E.E.) under the denoiser’s prediction:
Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 , τ ) ⏟ ℝ = softmax ⁡ ( g θ ​ ( 𝐡 ) τ ) ⏟ ℝ ​ E θ ⏟ ℝ , \underbrace{\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h};\tau)}_{\mathbb{R}}=\underbrace{\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{g_{\theta}(\mathbf{h})}{\tau}\right)}_{\mathbb{R}}\underbrace{E_{\theta}}_{\mathbb{R}},
(8)
where the softmax is applied over the vocabulary dimension and τ > 0 \tau>0 is a temperature. For conciseness, we write 𝐱 θ ​ ( 𝐜 , t ) \mathbf{x}_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},t) for τ = 1 \tau=1 . LDDMs add their SC state Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 ) \Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}) to the token embeddings ( ), whereas the SC state of LAW replaces them. We compare both state maps in Sec. .
At every sampling step, we set τ \tau to the sampling temperature T T ( T = 1 T=1 unless stated otherwise). Since τ \tau is randomized during training (Sec. ), the denoiser learns to process tempered SC states.
For conditional generation, we always fix the prompt positions to the clean token embeddings.
Decoding.
At the last step, a decoder η \eta maps the denoiser prediction 𝐱 ^ = 𝐱 θ ​ ( 𝐜 t 1 , t 1 , T ) \hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}_{\theta}(\mathbf{c}_{t_{1}},t_{1};T) to discrete tokens. We consider the raw and argmax decoders:
η raw ​ ( 𝐱 ^ ) ∼ Cat ⁡ ( ⋅ , 𝐱 ^ ) , η max ​ ( 𝐱 ^ ) = arg ⁡ max v ∈ 𝒱 ⁡ x ^ v . \eta_{\mathrm{raw}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\sim\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(\cdot\,;\hat{\mathbf{x}}\right),\qquad\eta_{\mathrm{max}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\,\hat{x}_{v}.
(9)

3.2 Training the Continuous Trajectory
Multi-Pass Training.
During sampling, the denoiser consumes the SC states that it produced itself ( ). Since backpropagation through the whole trajectory is costly, we adopt the multi-pass training of LDDM-U (Sec. ). We first sample a corrupted sequence 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} ( ). With probability p p , we unroll M M forward passes, and otherwise, we execute a single pass on 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} , as in standard UDM training. In the multi-pass case, the first pass receives the token embeddings of 𝐳 t \mathbf{z}_{t} , while later ones process the SC state, and we only backpropagate through the last pass. We use p = 0.9 p=0.9 and M = 2 M=2 by default, following Jo et al. (2026) . Formally,
𝐜 = 𝐳 t ​ E θ , 𝐜 = sg ⁡ [ Φ θ ​ ( f θ ​ ( 𝐜 , t ) , τ ) ] , t = t − ( i − 1 ) ​ δ , \mathbf{c}=\mathbf{z}_{t}E_{\theta},\qquad\mathbf{c}=\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\Phi_{\theta}\!\big(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},t);\,\tau\big)\right],\qquad t=t-(i-1)\,\delta,
(10)
where δ = 1 / L \delta=1/L is the step size when sampling with N = L N=L NFE. The loss is applied only to the prediction of the last pass, 𝐱 ^ = 𝐱 θ ​ ( 𝐜 , t ) \hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},t) . We minimize either the per-token NELBO ℒ tok \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}} ( ) or the Cross Entropy (CE):
ℒ LAW ​ ( θ ) = 𝔼 𝐱 , t , 𝐳 t , τ ​ [ ∑ ℓ = 1 ℒ LAW ] , ℒ LAW = { ℒ tok ​ ( 𝐱 ^ , 𝐱 , 𝐳 t , α t ) (NELBO) , − log ⁡ ⟨ 𝐱 ^ , 𝐱 ⟩ (CE) . \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LAW}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\,t,\,\mathbf{z}_{t},\,\tau}\left[\sum_{\ell=1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LAW}}\right],\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LAW}}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\,\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{z}_{t},\,\alpha_{t}\right)&\text{(NELBO)},\\[2.0pt]
-\log\langle\hat{\mathbf{x}},\,\mathbf{x}\rangle&\text{(CE)}.\end{cases}
(11)
During the multi-pass updates, we sample t ∼ 𝒰 ⁡ [ ( M − 1 ) ​ δ , 1 ] t\sim\mathcal{U}[(M-1)\delta,1] to ensure that t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 , while single-pass updates draw t ∼ 𝒰 ⁡ [ 0 , 1 ] t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1] . See Algo. for the pseudocode.
Temperature Regularization.
Sampling with T ≠ 1 T\neq 1 makes the denoiser’s logits sharper ( T < 1 T<1 ) or flatter ( T > 1 T>1 ). To expose the denoiser to SC states computed with tempered logits, we draw the temperature τ \tau at random during training, to perturbs the SC states ( ). We compute the loss with the untempered prediction 𝐱 ^ \hat{\mathbf{x}} ( ), so τ \tau only affects the SC states. We draw τ \tau as

The factor ρ ≥ 1 \rho\geq 1 makes it equally likely to sharpen ( τ < 1 \tau<1 ) or flatten ( τ > 1 \tau>1 ) the logits. We sample both sides because, with a one-sided range, the LM head g θ g_{\theta} could absorb the temperature by rescaling its weights.
Architecture.
LAW uses the same denoiser architecture as standard UDMs, a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) with pre-normalization ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ; Lou et al., 2024 ) , so it can be trained from scratch or fine-tuned from a pre-trained UDM checkpoint. We optionally add a learned mode embedding to the time conditioning, which indicates whether the denoiser receives the embeddings of noisy tokens or an SC state. We only use mode embedding on OpenWebText, where it prevents the representations from collapsing when ρ = 3 \rho=3 (Suppl. ). See Suppl. for details.
4 Experiments
We evaluate LAW on four datasets: Sudoku (Sec. ) to test constraint satisfaction with a unique solution, Maze (Sec. ) for long-range planning with possibly several optimal paths, GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) (Sec. ) for reasoning on grade-school math word problems, and OpenWebText ( Gokaslan and Cohen, 2019 ) (Sec. ) for unconditional language modeling.
Baselines and Training.
We compare to an ARM, to the DDMs MDLM ( Sahoo et al., 2024 ) , Duo ( Sahoo et al., 2025 ) , LDDM-M and LDDM-U ( Jo et al., 2026 ) , and to the continuous models CANDI ( Pynadath et al., 2026 ) , FLM ( Lee et al., 2026a ) and 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ) . Unless stated otherwise, all models are DiTs trained from scratch. Since LDDMs and LAW unroll several forward passes, we train those for fewer steps to use similar training FLOPs as the baselines (Suppl. ). Additionally, LAW always uses the raw decoder η raw \eta_{\mathrm{raw}} ( ). Full details are in Suppl. .

With 180 NFE, LAW is the most accurate model on all three difficulties (Table ). In particular, LAW outperforms LDDMs. LAW solves 96.5% of the puzzles with temperature regularization ( ρ = 2 \rho=2 , p = 0.9 p=0.9 ) and training with CE performs better than with the NELBO (Suppl. ).
4.2 Path Finding on Maze
Experimental Setup.
We evaluate shortest-path finding on mazes inspired by Wang et al. (2025) . We generate 1k training and 1k evaluation mazes on 20 × 20 20\times 20 grids ( Lehnert et al., 2024 ) and keep mazes with long-enough shortest path (71 steps). Each cell is encoded as a wall , empty , start , goal , or path token. As for Sudoku, we concatenates the unsolved grid with the solution (800 tokens).
We report the accuracy of finding any one of the shortest paths from start to goal (unlike certain work that measure exact match with a particular shortest path). The denoiser is an 8-layer DiT ( d = 256 d=256 , 8 heads, RoPE), and we train the baselines for 100k steps.
Results.
LAW is the most accurate model with few steps (Figure ). With only 4 NFE, it solves 90.9% of the mazes, more than any baseline with any number of steps besides LDDM-M, and it is ahead of every baseline up to 64 NFE. From 512 NFE, LDDM-M is slightly more accurate, as both models solve about 92% of the mazes. LDDM-U plateaus below 89%, and the continuous baselines below 82%. Temperature regularization barely changes the accuracy and training with the NELBO is generally better than with CE. Details on the ablations on the loss, M M and p p are in Suppl. .

4.3 Reasoning on GSM8K
Experimental Setup.
We train on TinyGSM ( Liu et al., 2023a ) , a dataset of 11.8M synthetic math problems, and evaluate on the GSM8K test set ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) . For each test problem, we generate and execute one Python program. We use a 12-layer DiT denoiser and the smollm tokenizer ( Allal et al., 2025 ) , with a context length of 512 and padding for shorter programs. The baselines train for 250k steps with a batch size of 512, while LDDMs and LAW train for 192k steps (Suppl. ). Unless stated otherwise, LAW uses the Expected Embedding , the NELBO, M = 2 M=2 passes with p = 0.9 p=0.9 , and temperature regularization with ρ = 3 \rho=3 . We report the GSM8K accuracy from 1 to 1024 NFE at the sampling temperatures T = 1 T=1 and T = 0.1 T=0.1 . See Suppl. for details and Suppl. for all ablations.
Results.
Trained from scratch, LAW solves 42.2% of the problems at T = 1 T=1 with 1024 NFE, against 34.5% for the strongest diffusion or flow baseline, LDDM-U, and it outperforms all of them from 32 NFE onward, with their standard samplers (Figure , Suppl. ). At T = 0.1 T=0.1 , it is the most accurate model from 4 to 512 NFE (e.g., 26.4% against 12.4% for LDDM-U with 8 NFE), and LDDMs match it only with 1024 NFE. Table shows that with T = 0.1 T=0.1 and 2 to 64 NFE, LAW is more accurate than the recent few-step approaches FMLM+ ( Agarwal et al., 2026 ) , DBTM ( Tang and Wang, 2026 ) , IDLM ( Li et al., 2026 ) and the undistilled SDM ( Deschenaux et al., 2026a ) .

Fine-Tuning from Duo.
We show that LAW can be adapted from a pre-trained Duo checkpoint. We fine-tune one for up to 50k steps, with M = 2 M=2 and p = 0.9 p=0.9 or M = 4 M=4 and p = 0.75 p=0.75 , and batch sizes from 64 to 512 (Figure ). With batch size 512 and after 50k steps, LAW solves 48.1% of the problems at T = 1 T=1 with 1024 NFE, against 17.2% for Duo and 42.2% for LAW trained from scratch (right panel of Figure ). This fine-tuning adds 26% to the training compute of Duo. With M = 2 M=2 , the accuracy peaks after 15k to 20k steps and then decreases, whereas with M = 4 M=4 it does not decrease. After 15k steps, which add 8% to the compute of Duo, LAW is more accurate than the ARM at T = 1 T=1 in 32 sampling steps (56.2% vs. 53.9% for the ARM with 512 NFE).
Comparison with AlphaDLM.
The concurrent work AlphaDLM ( Gushchin et al., 2026 ) is the most accurate method with up to 8 NFE, and is more accurate than LAW trained from scratch. Fine-tuned from Duo for 15k steps, however, LAW is more accurate than AlphaDLM from 16 NFE onward at T = 1 T=1 , for about 8% more training compute (250k Duo steps plus 15k fine-tuning steps, against 250k for AlphaDLM). Two differences might explain the higher accuracy of AlphaDLM. First, it uses a confidence-based sampler, which alone increases the accuracy of MDLM from 22.1% to 43.9% at 32 NFE. Second, it trades the many-steps performance for higher few-steps accuracy, and its hyperparameter k k , which sets this trade-off, must be chosen for a target inference budget (Table ). Distillation shows a similar trade-off, as the distilled SDM plateaus below the original checkpoint with many steps ( Deschenaux et al., 2026a ) . We therefore see AlphaDLM as complementary to LAW, and leave studying their combination for future work.
Ablations.
Three design choices are essential (Suppl. ). First, the state map Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} performs better than Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} . With Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} , LAW solves fewer than 1.5% of the problems, against 35.8% with Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} (Figure ; no regularization). Second, temperature regularization doubles the accuracy at T = 0.1 T=0.1 , from 24.8% to 48.8% with 1024 NFE (Figure ). Third, backpropagating through the M M passes decreases the accuracy from 42.2% to 20.2% at both matched training FLOPs, and at equal training steps (Figures and ). Find ablations on the number of passes and p p in Suppl. .
Pure vs. Auxiliary SC.
To isolate the choice of state map against the effect of pure Self-Conditioning, we train variants a variant of LDDM-U with Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} instead of Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} . Using Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} improves the accuracy (38.7% instead of 34.5% at T = 1 T=1 with 1024 NFE). Nevertheless, even with temperature regularization, LDDM-U with Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} remains less accurate than LAW, except at T = 0.1 T=0.1 with 256 NFE or more (Figure ; left), where LDDM slightly ouperforms LAW.

With 64 NFE, LAW outperforms the diffusion and flow baselines and comes close to the ARM that uses 1024 NFEs (Figure ). As on GSM8K, temperature regularization is essential. Without it, LAW only reaches low entropies, at a higher Gen. PPL than LDDM-U (Suppl. ). 5 Related Work
Self-Conditioning.
SC feeds the previous predictions or hidden representations of the denoiser back as input ( Strudel et al., 2022 ; Chen et al., 2023 ) . In DDMs, several methods pass such a continuous state between denoising steps. LDDMs ( Jo et al., 2026 ) and DiffusionGemma ( Google DeepMind, 2026 ) propagate a continuous state, Soft-Masked Diffusion ( Hersche et al., 2026 ) feeds masked positions a convex combination of the mask embedding and the top- k k most confident token embeddings, and Relay ( Rozonoyer et al., 2026 ) and PUMBA ( Madeira et al., 2026 ) pass the last hidden state between the steps of teacher-forced unmasking trajectories. Other methods unroll the denoiser during training. SUNDAE ( Savinov et al., 2022 ) unrolls it over discrete tokens, and FReDA ( Sun et al., 2026 ) refines the discrete drafts of the model without a forward corruption process and, like LAW, backpropagates only through the last pass. Relay and PUMBA instead backpropagate through the propagated state and find that it outperforms a stop-gradient, whereas in LAW, the stop-gradient roughly doubles the GSM8K accuracy (Suppl. ). All of these methods still sample discrete tokens between steps and use any continuous state as an auxiliary input. LAW propagates only the SC state, at all positions, and decodes once, after the final step (Suppl. ).
Continuous Language Models.
Continuous DLMs and Flow Language Models generate by integrating an ODE or SDE. They corrupt token embeddings or one-hot vectors with Gaussian noise ( Li et al., 2022 ; Gulrajani and Hashimoto, 2023 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026a ; Potaptchik et al., 2026 ; Roos et al., 2026 ; Yoo et al., 2026 ) or operate on the hypersphere ( Chemseddine et al., 2026 ; Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ) . Like these models, LAW has a continuous sampling trajectory. However, it is trained with the uniform discrete corruption of UDMs and does not use Gaussian noise nor an ODE solver.
Looped Models.
Looped DLMs reuse layers of the denoiser to vary its depth at inference ( Carballo-Castro et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026b ; Miele et al., 2026 ) . Looping changes the computation within a denoising step, whereas LAW changes what is passed between steps, so the two are complementary.
6 Conclusion and Limitations
We asked whether a generative model can be based purely on SC, and we proposed LAW. Starting from random tokens, LAW propagates only the SC state, the Expected Embedding under the denoiser’s prediction, and decodes once, after the final step. Our results suggest that pure SC can match or outperform SC as an auxiliary input. LAW is the most accurate model on Sudoku and on Maze with few steps, and on GSM8K, it outperforms the diffusion and flow baselines trained with the same FLOPs. Fine-tuning a pre-trained Duo checkpoint with LAW increases the GSM8K accuracy of Duo from 17.2% to 48.1%. On OpenWebText, it also outperforms the diffusion and flow baselines at matched entropy and comes close to the ARM with 64 NFE. The ablations identify three necessary choices: the state map, temperature regularization, and a stop-gradient between passes, as in LDDMs.
Limitations.
The NELBO is more accurate than the CE on GSM8K and Maze, whereas the CE is more accurate on Sudoku and gives more diverse samples on OpenWebText. Since the NELBO is derived for discrete diffusion processes, LAW does not completely depart from the discrete diffusion formalism. With 1024 NFE on OpenWebText, LAW’s samples cover a narrower entropy range than those of the baselines. Finally, we experiment in the standard academic setting. Whether our results hold at larger scale is left for future work.

A.3 Comparison to Loopholing Discrete Diffusion Models (LDDMs)
LAW and LDDMs both propagate an SC state across denoising steps. They differ in how the backbone uses this state, which affects both sampling and training, and in the state map that computes it ( ).
Sampling.
At each step, an LDDM samples discrete tokens from the posterior q s | t q_{s|t} and feeds the backbone the sum of their embeddings and the SC state ( ). LAW instead feeds the backbone the SC state alone ( ). It never samples tokens between steps and decodes only once, after the final step ( ).
Training.
Both methods corrupt the data once, 𝐳 t ∼ q t \mathbf{z}_{t}\sim q_{t} . With probability p p , both run several forward passes, stop the gradient through the SC state between passes, and apply the loss to the last pass only. Otherwise, both run a single pass (Sec. ). LDDMs run two passes at the same time t t : the first pass receives 𝐳 t ​ E θ \mathbf{z}_{t}E_{\theta} with a zero SC state and computes the SC state 𝐜 t \mathbf{c}_{t} , and the second pass receives 𝐳 t ​ E θ + 𝐜 t \mathbf{z}_{t}E_{\theta}+\mathbf{c}_{t} . In contrast, the later passes of LAW receive the SC state alone and run at successive times t t , and the number of passes M M can exceed two ( ).
State Map.
LDDMs propagate the layer-normalized hidden representation ( Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} ), while LAW propagates a linear combination of token embeddings, weighted by the predicted token probabilities ( Φ θ \Phi_{\theta} ) ( ).
A.4 Gradient Flow Between Passes
In ( ), a stop-gradient is applied to the SC state fed to the next pass, 𝐜 = sg ⁡ [ Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 , τ ) ] \mathbf{c}=\mathrm{sg}\big[\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h};\tau)\big] , where 𝐡 = f θ ​ ( 𝐜 , t ) \mathbf{h}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},t) is the final-layer hidden representation of the i i -th pass. As a result, the loss ( ) only updates the parameters through the last forward pass, and the earlier passes are treated as a fixed input.
Alternatively, we can remove the stop-gradient and set 𝐜 = Φ θ ​ ( 𝐡 , τ ) \mathbf{c}=\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{h};\tau) . The gradient of the loss then also flows through the SC states into the earlier passes. For M = 2 M=2 , the gradient with respect to the output of the first pass is

The stop-gradient sets this term to zero. Without it, the first pass learns to produce SC states that help the second pass. We use the stop-gradient by default and compare both variants on TinyGSM (Figure ).
A.5 Mode Embedding
The DiT conditions every block on the noise level through 𝝂 = SiLU ⁡ ( Emb ⁡ ( α t ) ) \bm{\nu}=\operatorname{SiLU}(\operatorname{Emb}(\alpha_{t})) , from which each block computes the scales, shifts, and gates of its adaLN-Zero modulation ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) . At the first pass ( ) and the first sampling step, the denoiser receives token embeddings, whereas at every later pass it receives the SC state. The mode embedding tells the denoiser which of the two it is processing. We learn two vectors, 𝐦 tok \mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{tok}} for token embeddings and 𝐦 SC \mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{SC}} for SC states, and add the matching one to the noise-level conditioning, e.g. 𝝂 = SiLU ⁡ ( Emb ⁡ ( α t ) ) + 𝐦 SC \bm{\nu}=\operatorname{SiLU}(\operatorname{Emb}(\alpha_{t}))+\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{SC}} at the later passes. We initialize 𝐦 tok = 𝐦 SC = 𝟎 \mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{tok}}=\mathbf{m}_{\mathrm{SC}}=\mathbf{0} , so a checkpoint without mode embedding, such as Duo, computes the same function after loading.
Appendix B Training Compute
We compare all methods at matched training FLOPs. Jo et al. (2026) instead train LDDMs and their base models for the same number of updates, although each LDDM update is more expensive. This section states the matching rule and lists the training steps of every LDDM and LAW run.
B.1 FLOP Matching
Let F F be the cost of one forward pass of the denoiser, and count a backward pass as 2 ​ F 2F . A standard update runs one forward and one backward pass, and costs 3 ​ F 3F . An update with M M passes and a stop-gradient between them ( ) runs M − 1 M-1 passes without gradient and a last pass with gradient, and costs ( M + 2 ) ​ F (M+2)F . Without the stop-gradient, the backward pass goes through all M M passes, and the update costs 3 ​ M ​ F 3MF (Suppl. ). LDDMs and LAW run M M passes with probability p p and a single pass otherwise, so an update costs on average
C = ( 3 + p ⁡ ( M − 1 ) ) ​ F with the stop-gradient, C = ( 3 + 3 ​ p ​ ( M − 1 ) ) ​ F without it. C=\big(3+p(M-1)\big)F\quad\text{with the stop-gradient,}\qquad C=\big(3+3p(M-1)\big)F\quad\text{without it.}
(16)
To match the FLOPs of S S baseline steps at the same batch size, a run trains for 3 ​ F ​ S / C 3FS/C steps. For example, LDDMs and LAW with M = 2 M=2 and p = 0.9 p=0.9 cost C = 3.9 ​ F C=3.9F per update and train for 77% of the steps of the baselines.
Fine-Tuning.
When we fine-tune from Duo on TinyGSM (Suppl. ), we count the FLOPs of the 250k Duo steps ( 3 ​ F 3F each) and of the fine-tuning updates, both at batch size 512. With M = 2 M=2 and p = 0.9 p=0.9 , fine-tuning adds 8% to the compute of Duo after 15k steps and 26% after 50k steps. With M = 4 M=4 and p = 0.75 p=0.75 , 50k steps add 35%.

Appendix C Experimental Setup
C.1 Shared Setup
Training Hyperparameters.
All models are trained with AdamW with a learning rate of 3 × 10 3\times 10 , ( β 1 , β 2 ) = ( 0.9 , 0.999 ) (\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999) , zero weight decay, 2.5k warmup steps followed by a constant learning rate, and gradient clipping at 1.0 1.0 . We maintain an exponential moving average of the weights with decay 0.9999 0.9999 and use it for evaluation. All DiT backbones use dropout 0.1 0.1 .
Baselines.
We compare to the following baselines, using the subset given in each experiment. Beyond the shared hyperparameters above, we use the default hyperparameters of the original implementations unless stated otherwise.
•
ARM. Causal Transformer trained with next-token prediction on the target, conditioned on the full prompt when there is one (e.g. the unsolved grid on Maze).
•
MDLM ( Sahoo et al., 2024 ) . Masked Diffusion with the SUBS parameterization, the log-linear schedule, and no time conditioning.
•
Duo ( Sahoo et al., 2025 ) . Uniform Diffusion with the log-linear schedule and time conditioning.
•
LDDM ( Jo et al., 2026 ) . We describe LDDM-M and LDDM-U in Sec. and compare them with LAW in Suppl. .
•
FLM ( Lee et al., 2026a ) . Gaussian flow on one-hot token representations, with time conditioning on t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] t\in[0,1] .
•
CANDI ( Pynadath et al., 2026 ) . Hybrid discrete–continuous diffusion with mixing coefficient 0.5 0.5 , σ min = 0.01 \sigma_{\min}=0.01 , σ max = 2 \sigma_{\max}=2 , and percentile scheduling with bounds 0.01 0.01 and 0.25 0.25 .
•
𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM and 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.) ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ) . Spherical flow trained with the CE loss and the log-linear schedule truncated at α max = α ⁡ ( 0.1 ) \alpha_{\max}=\alpha(0.1) , which depends on the vocabulary size | 𝒱 | |\mathcal{V}| and the embedding dimension d d ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 , Eq. (17)) . 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM uses the standard DiT, whereas 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.) uses the Sphere-DiT backbone with nGPT initialization.
Architecture.
All diffusion and flow models, including 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM, use the standard DiT ( Peebles and Xie, 2023 ) with RoPE ( Su et al., 2024 ) , except FLM and CANDI, which use the DiT variants of their original implementations at the same size, and 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.), which uses the Sphere-DiT. The ARM uses the same Transformer with causal attention and without time conditioning. LAW uses the same DiT as Duo, so a Duo checkpoint can be loaded into LAW without any change.
Sampling.
Unless stated otherwise, the methods use the following samplers:
•
MDLM , Duo : ancestral sampling with the posterior of each process, sampling the tokens at the last step.
•
LDDM : ancestral sampling where the SC state of each step is passed to the next one, sampling the tokens at the last step.
•
FLM : Euler integration of the flow. When there is a prompt, it is projected back to its clean value at the last step.
•
CANDI : the CANDI sampler of the original implementation.
•
𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM , 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.) : the sampling setup of the original implementation. At each step, the velocity only points toward the embedding of the most likely token (top-1 velocity), and the tokens are decoded with an argmax at the last step. 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM therefore does not depend on the sampling temperature, and appears as the same curve at both temperatures in Figure .
•
LAW : Algo. with the raw decoder η raw \eta_{\mathrm{raw}} ( ).
C.2 Sudoku
Dataset.
Following prior work ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) , we generate 48k training and 2k validation puzzles with unique solutions ( Alp, 2024 ) . The puzzles have three difficulty levels, determined by the number of visible digits: easy (40/81), medium (35/81), and hard (30/81).
Tokenization.
Each Sudoku grid is tokenized as a 1D sequence: the partial grid, a beginning-of-sequence token, then the solution. Missing digits are encoded with an empty-cell token, and rows are separated by a row-separator token. The vocabulary contains 12 tokens, and every sequence has length 180. The partial grid serves as the prompt and is never corrupted during training.
Architecture.
LDDMs and LAW use an 8-layer DiT with embedding dimension d = 512 d=512 and 8 attention heads.
Training.
LDDMs and LAW are trained from scratch on a single GPU at batch size 256, with the shared hyperparameters of Suppl. . LDDMs train for the FLOP-matched 15,385 steps, and LAW for the steps of Table . We train LAW with the NELBO and the CE, with M ∈ { 2 , 3 } M\in\{2,3\} passes, multi-pass probabilities p ∈ { 0.1 , 0.25 , 0.5 , 0.75 , 0.9 } p\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9\} , and temperature regularization with ρ ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 } \rho\in\{2,3,4,5\} (Suppl. ). The results of the other baselines in Table are taken from prior work ( Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026 ) , which trains them for 20k steps at batch size 256.
Sampling.
We sample with 180 NFE. The baselines use the samplers of Suppl. , and LAW uses Algo. with the η raw \eta_{\mathrm{raw}} decoder ( ).
Metric.
We report the exact-match accuracy, i.e., the fraction of the 2k validation puzzles for which the generated grid equals the unique solution.
C.3 TinyGSM
Dataset.
We train on TinyGSM ( Liu et al., 2023a ) , which pairs 11.8M synthetic grade-school math questions with Python programs that solve them, and evaluate on the GSM8K test set ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) .
Tokenization.
We tokenize with the HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM-135M tokenizer and discard examples longer than 512 tokens. Shorter examples are padded to 512 tokens. Each sequence is a question–program pair: the question serves as the prompt, and the loss is applied only to the program.
Architecture.
All diffusion and flow models use a 12-layer DiT with embedding dimension d = 768 d=768 and 12 attention heads.
Training.
Every model is trained from scratch on 8 GPUs at global batch size 512, with the shared hyperparameters of Suppl. . The ARM, MDLM, Duo, FLM, CANDI, and 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.) train for 250k steps, and LDDMs and LAW for the FLOP-matched steps of Table . CANDI reaches 0.2% accuracy with 1024 NFE, close to FLM, and we leave it out of the figures for readability. LDDM-U with the Expected Embedding (Sec. ) adds the SC state to the token embeddings and runs both passes at the same time t t , like the standard LDDM-U. Unlike it, it resets the SC state at the prompt positions to the embeddings of the clean prompt.
Fine-Tuning.
We fine-tune the Duo baseline (250k steps) with LAW for 50k steps, starting from its online weights, while the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the weights continues from that of Duo. The optimizer and the learning rate schedule, including the 2.5k warmup steps, start anew. We fine-tune on 4 GPUs at global batch sizes 64, 128, 256 and 512, and evaluate every 5k steps. Figure reports the run with M = 2 M=2 , p = 0.9 p=0.9 and batch size 512 after 50k steps, and Figure compares it with its checkpoint after 15k steps. Suppl. gives the training compute of fine-tuning.
Sampling.
For each GSM8K test problem, we generate and execute one program. We sweep the NFE over { 1 , 2 , 4 , … , 1024 } \{1,2,4,\dots,1024\} at the sampling temperatures T ∈ { 1 , 0.1 } T\in\{1,0.1\} , using the last checkpoint of each run trained from scratch. The baselines use the samplers of Suppl. , and LAW uses Algo. with the η raw \eta_{\mathrm{raw}} decoder ( ). LDDM-U with the Expected Embedding uses the LDDM sampler, except that the sampling temperature also applies to the state map, as in LAW. The accuracy over fine-tuning steps is measured with 256 NFE at T = 1 T=1 .
Metric.
A generated program is correct if executing it returns the reference answer. We report the accuracy over the GSM8K test set.
C.4 Maze
Dataset.
We regenerate Maze-Hard ( Wang et al., 2025 ) on 20 × 20 20\times 20 grids with an existing maze generator ( Lehnert et al., 2024 ) . For each maze, we draw a wall probability from 𝒰 ⁡ ( 0.3 , 0.5 ) \mathcal{U}(0.3,0.5) , and each cell is independently a wall with this probability. The start and the goal are placed uniformly at random on two distinct empty cells. We keep only solvable mazes whose shortest path contains at least 71 cells, including the start and the goal . The original Maze-Hard ( Wang et al., 2025 ) uses 30 × 30 30\times 30 grids and a threshold of 110 cells. To shorten the sequences from 1800 to 800 tokens, we use 20 × 20 20\times 20 grids. On our grids, a threshold of 71 cells keeps approximately the same fraction of generated mazes. We generate 1,000 training and 1,000 evaluation mazes with seed 42. The shortest paths of the evaluation mazes contain between 70 and 89 moves (71 to 90 cells), with a median of 72. The target is one shortest path, found by breadth-first search with a fixed neighbor order. We verify all 2,000 targets independently of the generator by recomputing the shortest path length with the NetworkX library ( Hagberg et al., 2008 ) , and every target passes this check.
Tokenization.
Each maze is tokenized as a 1D sequence: the unsolved grid followed by the solved grid, each flattened row by row, from top to bottom and left to right. Each cell is one of five tokens: wall , empty , start , goal , or path . With an additional padding token, the vocabulary contains 6 tokens, plus a mask token for MDLM, LDDM-M, CANDI, and the ARM. Every sequence has length 2 × 20 = 800 2\times 20=800 . The unsolved grid serves as the prompt: it is never corrupted during training, the loss is computed only on the solved grid, and the sampler resets the prompt to its clean value at every step.
Architecture.
All models use an 8-layer DiT with embedding dimension d = 256 d=256 and 8 attention heads. For 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.), | 𝒱 | = 6 |\mathcal{V}|=6 and d = 256 d=256 give α max = 0.121 \alpha_{\max}=0.121 .
Training.
Every model is trained from scratch on a single GPU at batch size 256, with the shared hyperparameters of Suppl. . The baselines train for 100k steps, and LDDMs and LAW for the FLOP-matched steps of Table . We train LAW with the NELBO and the CE, with M ∈ { 2 , 3 } M\in\{2,3\} passes, multi-pass probabilities p ∈ { 0.1 , 0.25 , 0.5 , 0.75 , 0.9 } p\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9\} , and temperature regularization with ρ ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 } \rho\in\{2,3,4,5\} (Suppl. ).
Sampling.
We evaluate on the 1,000 evaluation mazes. Unless stated otherwise, all diffusion and flow models sample with 800 NFE at sampling temperature T = 1 T=1 . The ARM decodes the 400 cells of the solution grid one at a time with a Key-Value (KV) cache, i.e., with 400 NFE. For the ARM, we report both greedy and sampled decoding. The baselines use the samplers of Suppl. , and LAW uses Algo. with the η raw \eta_{\mathrm{raw}} decoder ( ). We sweep the NFE over { 1 , 2 , 4 , … , 512 , 800 } \{1,2,4,\dots,512,800\} with all other settings fixed.
Metrics.
Prior work reports exact-match accuracy on Maze-Hard ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . Since a maze can have several shortest paths, exact match can underestimate the accuracy: a correct shortest path that differs from the target is counted as wrong (Suppl. ). We therefore report the valid-solution accuracy, i.e., the fraction of evaluation mazes for which the generated grid (i) reproduces every wall , empty , start , and goal cell of the maze, and (ii) marks a shortest path from the start to the goal . To check (ii), let n n be the number of cells on the target path (i.e., the length of a shortest path), including the start and the goal . We check that the cells labeled path lie on empty cells of the maze and, together with the start and the goal , form a connected path of exactly n n cells.

All diffusion and flow models use a 12-layer DiT with embedding dimension d = 768 d=768 and 12 attention heads. The reported LAW model also uses the mode embedding (Suppl. ).
Training.
We train on 16 GPUs at global batch size 512, with the shared hyperparameters of Suppl. . The baselines train for 400k steps, and every LDDM and LAW run, ablations included, for the FLOP-matched 308k steps (Table ). D.5 Maze: Exact Match vs. Valid Solution
Exact match counts many correct paths as wrong (Figure ). For example, 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -FLM ( 𝕊 \mathbb{S} -arch.) generates a valid solution for 77.7% of the mazes with 128 NFE, but only 58.3% of them match the target path. Since every exact match is also a valid solution, the exact-match accuracy never exceeds the valid-solution accuracy (Suppl. ). Figure shows a valid path of LAW that differs from the target.

Both paths take 70 moves, so the generated path solves the maze, but exact match counts it as wrong.
