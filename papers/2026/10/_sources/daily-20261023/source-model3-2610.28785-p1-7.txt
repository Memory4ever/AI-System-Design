ABSTRACT
Training tool-using language models requires costly interaction, yet identical
rewards within a rollout group provide no group-relative learning signal. We use
reward disagreement to guide harness evaluation and the allocation of rollouts for
policy training in Model Context Protocol (MCP) environments. We first introduce
Discord, which tests harness edits using exact paired inference and decision-
preserving early stopping: only disagreeing paired outcomes favor one harness over
another. This insight motivates prospective allocation inBRACE(Bank-calibrated
RolloutAllocation underCorrelatedEpisodes). BRACEcombines historical task
evidence, uncertainty in task success rates, and a correlated reward model to
forecast mixed-reward groups before allocating generation budget. Controlled
harness experiments favorDiscord, while live MCP experiments expose a retention–
cost trade-off. At three Qwen3-VL model scales,BRACEexceeds the strongest
plotted baseline peaks with fewer new training rollouts; historical-bank collection
is counted separately. It also leads the compared methods on BFCL and NESTFUL
without further training. Ablations probe the bank, estimator, and allocation score;
prospective evaluation checks forecast calibration. These results support forecasting
reward contrast before generation. 1
1 INTRODUCTION
Language agents use tools over multiple turns before a task’s outcome can be verified (Zhou et al.,
2024; Wang et al., 2026). The Model Context Protocol (MCP) standardizes how agent applications
discover and invoke external tools and access resources (Hou et al., 2026; Anjum et al., 2026). An
agent harnessis the model-external software that configures prompts and tools and mediates the
action–observation loop (Lee et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024). Together with the model policy, it forms
the agent: changing prompts or tool access changes the harness, whereas training model weights
changes the policy. Automated search can improve the harness (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025;
Lee et al., 2026), but deciding whether to retain an edit requires evaluating its effect across costly
agent trajectories. An apparent gain on a finite task set may reflect noise. When the candidate and
incumbent run on the same tasks, agreements cannot favor either one; only disagreements reveal the
direction of the change. This motivates DISCORD, which tests paired outcomes and stops evaluation
when the remaining tasks cannot change the retention decision.
A similar allocation problem arises when training that policy. Reinforcement learning with verifiable
rewards (RLVR) uses task outcomes from complete trajectories (Guo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026).
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) compares several rollouts of the
same task, but a group in which all rewards agree has zero centered reward advantage. Generating
such groups is especially costly when each rollout may involve repeated model inference and MCP
tool calls. DAPO discards them after generation (Yu et al., 2025); PLR prioritizes tasks by estimated
learning potential (Jiang et al., 2021), and VIP, TRACE, and DUET allocate generation prospectively
(Nguyen et al., 2026; Zou et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). For multi-turn tool-use tasks, we ask whether
outcomes from earlier runs can predict which fixed-size groups will contain mixed rewards, despite

We study two complementary routes to improving tool-using agents. DISCORDuses evaluation

informative disagreement motivates BRACEto forecast which training tasks will produce mixed-
reward groups and allocate rollouts accordingly. This directs training compute toward the policy
operating within the harness (Figure 1). Our contributions are:
• Reliable harness retention.DISCORDuses exact paired inference and decision-preserving
stopping; controlled and live experiments measure its recovery and evaluation cost.
• Prospective task selection.BRACEcombines bounded historical evidence, uncertainty over
task success, and within-group dependence to forecast informative groups. We characterize
the resulting objective and test its calibration and components.
• Training efficiency and transfer.Across three model scales, BRACEimproves held-out
tool-use performance and reaches the strongest plotted baseline peaks with fewer new
rollouts. It also transfers to BFCL (Patil et al., 2025) and NESTFUL (Basu et al., 2024)
without further training.

paired outcomes provide sufficient evidence of improvement; it does not generate proposals.

3.2 TESTING WHETHER THE CANDIDATE WINS RELIABLY
Under the paired null, a disagreement is equally likely to favor the candidate or the incumbent.
Assuming independent evaluation pairs and conditioning on the observed number of disagreements
nd =b+c , the number of candidate wins follows McNemar’s exact conditional distribution
(McNemar, 1947):
b|n d ∼Binomial(n d,1/2).(6)
DISCORDretains the candidate when the upper-tail probability is at most a prespecified α. For
example, b= 12 and c= 4 on 100 paired tasks give an 8 pp improvement and an exact one-
sided probability of 0.0384, passing at α= 0.05 . With no disagreements, retention is rejected.
Operationally, each paired task either increments b, increments c, or ties and changes neither count.
Conditioning on nd removes the unknown tie rate: the test asks whether the directions of observed
disagreements favor the candidate, independently of how often the harnesses agree. Error control
applies to this prespecified comparison, not an unrestricted adaptive search (Appendix C.2).
Stopping without changing the decision.The maximum number of paired tasks is fixed in advance.
After each block,exact curtailment(Alling, 1963) checks whether all possible remaining pairs
(candidate wins, incumbent wins, or ties) lead to the same terminal decision. If so, evaluation stops;
otherwise it continues.
For example, with b= 2 , c= 0 and two pairs remaining, the most favorable completion for the
candidate is b= 4 , c= 0 . Its one-sided probability is 1/16 = 0.0625, so it still fails an α= 0.05 test;

4 BRACE: CORRELATION-AWAREROLLOUTALLOCATION
BRACEselects tasks before generating their next rollout groups (Algorithm 1). It favors tasks pre-
dicted to produce both successes and failures while retaining uniform exploration. Main experiments
select N= 256 distinct tasks per allocation cycle, then generate one k= 5 group per task over eight
optimizer steps of 32 tasks each. Allocation changes task selection, not group size. Thus, the score
targets the chance of receiving a group-relative reward signal, rather than the highest expected solve
rate; the exploration share continues to sample eligible tasks regardless of their scores. Algorithm 1
records each selected batch and its predicted degeneracy before generation, enabling prospective
calibration checks.

Banked evidence.We approximate uncertainty in the task mean by pi ∼Beta(1 +s i,1 +f i)
using success and failure counts (si, fi); this is not the exact posterior under the correlated group
likelihood. Awarm bankprovides success and failure counts from previous runs across model scales,
capped so a large historical log cannot dominate later evidence. It is read once to initialize task-level
evidence for allocation. Subsequently, the counts are discounted and incremented with current-run
outcomes; the bank is not reread. Every rollout used for a policy update is generated by the current
policy. Without bank observations, unseen tasks all start atBeta(1,1), so allocation cannot initially
distinguish tasks that rarely solve from those that produce mixed outcomes. Bank counts make that
distinction available before the first current-run rollout; capping limits their influence as new evidence
arrives. Appendix D.2 gives the exact initialization and cost accounting.

5.2 DISCORD: HARNESSRETENTION
Inert-edit admission.Independently measured tie rates predict inert-edit admission within ±0.024
across AWM (Wang et al., 2026), BFCL (Patil et al., 2025), and two API-Bank (Li et al., 2023) tool
surfaces (Figure 2). On AWM (Wang et al., 2026), strict-improvement retention accepts43.3% of
1,440inert edits, and43.6%of the accepted edits fail to replicate (Appendices C.2 and C.1).
Controlled recovery and harmful proposals.We compare retention rules on 200 BFCL v3
(Patil et al., 2025) single-turn tasks, using four callers and eight rounds of three edits to advertised
function names. Withheld names create a recoverable handicap; a full surface makes proposals
predominantly harmful. Shared proposals and matched episode budgets isolate retention from edit
generation (Appendix C.4). The single-round gate uses a label-permutation test on disjoint task
halves; DISCORDuses discordant outcomes from the same tasks. DISCORDrecovers more of the
handicap than the single-round gate in 18 of 21 paired loops (Figure 3; one-sided paired t-test,
p= 0.00015 ). Under harmful proposals, degradation occurs in 0/17 seeds for DISCORDand 9/17
for strict-improvement retention. The seeds are matched; a two-sided exact McNemar test gives
p= 0.0039.
