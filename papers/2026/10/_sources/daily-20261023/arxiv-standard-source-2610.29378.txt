Abstract
Quantization-aware training (QAT) aims to recover reasoning capabilities in low-bit large language models (LLMs). Existing recipes typically supervise the quantized student with the original full-precision model on fixed response sequences. However, the resulting low-bit quantized models still struggle on long-horizon reasoning tasks and often fail to terminate within a token budget that suffices for the full-precision model. We identify two challenges in closing this gap. First, fixed-response supervision leaves a mismatch between training prefixes and those encountered during student generation, and this trajectory divergence grows as responses lengthen. Second, on its own prefixes, the student underpredicts some next-token candidates and overpredicts others, and this distributional misalignment becomes more frequent at high-entropy prefixes. We present OnQ , a low-bit recovery pipeline that combines on-policy distillation and candidate-wise Dynamic KL. Starting from block-wise calibration, the quantized student generates responses and receives full-precision teacher supervision on its own prefixes. Dynamic KL weights forward and reverse KL by each candidate’s teacher–student probability ratio to balance correction of under- and overprediction. Across Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B at 2- and 3-bit precision, OnQ achieves the highest nine-benchmark average, with the largest gains on long-horizon reasoning benchmarks. At 2 bits, it outperforms every baseline on every benchmark at every model size, improving the Qwen3-8B average by 10.26 points over the strongest baseline. It also produces shorter responses with fewer truncations and repetitive errors.

1 Introduction
Low-bit quantization reduces the memory footprint of large language models (LLMs), making their deployment more practical ( Frantar et al., 2022 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) . Preserving reasoning capabilities at low precision remains challenging: prior work reports substantial accuracy loss ( Liu et al., 2025 ) and inefficient generation ( Lotfi et al., 2026 ) in quantized reasoning models. Quantization-aware training (QAT) addresses this loss by adapting model parameters through quantized forward passes, often with supervision from the original full-precision model ( Hinton et al., 2015 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Du et al., 2024 ; Okoshi et al., 2026 ) . These distillation recipes typically train on fixed response sequences. Their effectiveness therefore depends on how well the training prefixes cover those encountered by the quantized student during inference ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ) .
To examine the remaining challenges in low-bit reasoning, we analyze Qwen3-1.7B at 2-bit precision ( Figure 1 ). On MATH-500, QAT baselines recover little of the full-precision model’s accuracy on questions eliciting long full-precision responses ( Figure 1 .a ). They also frequently reach the token limit on these questions, while the full-precision model terminates within the same budget ( Figure 1 .b ). Fixed-response distillation supervises only reference prefixes, but quantization changes how the student generates: the longer the response, the more the student conditions on its own outputs and the further its context drifts from those prefixes. This trajectory divergence motivates teacher supervision on the student’s own trajectories.
Training on the student’s own prefixes raises a second challenge: distributional misalignment. On these prefixes, the calibrated student exhibits two-sided probability errors: it substantially underpredicts some candidates and overpredicts others at the same prefix. These two-sided errors become more frequent at high-entropy prefixes ( Figure 1 .c ). Prior work also finds that quantized models oversample overthinking markers at high-entropy positions ( Lotfi et al., 2026 ) . Neither Kullback–Leibler (KL) direction alone corrects both sides equally well: forward KL divergence more reliably moves severely underpredicted candidates toward the teacher, while reverse KL does so for severely overpredicted candidates ( Section 3.2 ). This calls for an objective that balances correction across both sides of the distribution.
Motivated by these findings, we introduce OnQ, a low-bit recovery pipeline that combines on-policy distillation (OPD) and candidate-wise Dynamic KL ( Jung et al., 2025 ) . Starting from a block-wise calibrated checkpoint, the quantized student generates responses and receives supervision from a frozen full-precision teacher on those same prefixes ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ) . Dynamic KL weights forward and reverse KL according to each candidate’s teacher–student probability ratio, balancing the correction of under- and overpredicted probabilities along these evolving trajectories. On MATH-500, OnQ recovers more accuracy than every QAT baseline at every response length and rarely reaches the token limit ( Figure 1 .a–b ).
The key contributions of this study are as follows: 1 We identify two challenges, trajectory divergence and distributional misalignment, that limit low-bit reasoning recovery. 2 We present OnQ, a low-bit recovery pipeline combining on-policy distillation and candidate-wise Dynamic KL. 3 We show that OnQ achieves the highest nine-benchmark average in all six settings spanning Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B at 2- and 3-bit precision, improving the Qwen3-8B average at 2 bits by 10.26 points over the strongest baseline. It also improves generation behavior, producing shorter responses with fewer repetitive errors, and outperforms fixed-response distillation.

2 Related Work
Low-bit reasoning recovery.
Aggressive post-training quantization (PTQ) ( Frantar et al., 2022 ; Cheng et al., 2024 ) can impair reasoning capabilities ( Liu et al., 2025 ; Lotfi et al., 2026 ) . QAT recovers low-bit accuracy by adapting model parameters through quantized forward passes, often with supervision from a full-precision teacher ( Liu et al., 2024 ; Du et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Lv et al., 2026 ) . EfficientQAT uses block-wise optimization followed by end-to-end refinement of quantization step sizes ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , while ReasoningQAT ( Okoshi et al., 2026 ) combines mixed-domain calibration with teacher-guided fine-tuning to preserve reasoning capabilities acquired after pre-training. LLM-QAT ( Liu et al., 2024 ) , BitDistiller ( Du et al., 2024 ) , and ReasoningQAT compute teacher supervision on fixed training sequences rather than on the student’s own generations.
On-policy distillation.
Training on fixed prefixes while generating from the model’s own predictions at inference causes exposure bias, which imitation learning and sequence modeling mitigate by training on the learner’s own outputs ( Ross et al., 2011 ; Bengio et al., 2015 ; Ranzato et al., 2016 ) . OPD makes the student’s current behavior part of the training distribution: the teacher supervises prefixes generated by the student itself ( Lin et al., 2020 ; Agarwal et al., 2024 ; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025 ) . Alongside the source of training prefixes, the distillation objective determines how teacher–student discrepancies are weighted: BitDistiller mixes forward and reverse KL with a precomputed teacher-confidence coefficient ( Du et al., 2024 ) , while adaptive objectives adjust this balance using distribution discrepancies, teacher entropy, or candidate-wise probability ratios ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Jin et al., 2026 ; Jung et al., 2025 ) . Related recipes combine distillation with reinforcement learning ( Xu et al., 2025 ) or run reinforcement learning on quantized models ( Huang et al., 2026 ; Gu et al., 2026 ; Lv et al., 2026 ) . OnQ applies candidate-wise Dynamic KL on quantized student rollouts, adapting distillation feedback to teacher–student probability discrepancies.
3 Method
OnQ targets low-bit capability recovery through teacher supervision along the quantized student’s own reasoning trajectories ( Figure 2 ). Block-wise calibration provides the initialization for on-policy training. The student then samples responses through its quantized forward pass, and its full-precision teacher supplies next-token distributions conditioned on the same prefixes; this on-policy supervision addresses trajectory divergence. To address distributional misalignment, we align the student with these distributions using candidate-wise Dynamic KL, which weights forward and reverse KL (FKL and RKL) according to the teacher–student probability ratio for each candidate token.

Dynamic KL objective.
At a student-generated prefix h h , let p v = P ⁡ ( v ∣ h ) p_{v}=P(v\mid h) and q v = Q θ ​ ( v ∣ h ) q_{v}=Q_{\theta}(v\mid h) denote the teacher and student probabilities of candidate v v . The calibrated student exhibits two-sided probability errors: it underpredicts some candidates and overpredicts others at the same prefix ( Figure 3 .a ). These errors become more frequent as student entropy rises ( Figure 1 .c ).
Under local logit updates, FKL moves a larger fraction of severely underpredicted candidates ( p v / q v ≥ 20 p_{v}/q_{v}\geq 20 ) toward the teacher, whereas RKL moves a larger fraction of severely overpredicted candidates ( q v / p v ≥ 20 q_{v}/p_{v}\geq 20 ) ( Figure 3 .b ; Appendix A.4 ). Response-length schedule.
Right after calibration, the student repeats itself, mostly late in long responses, and the repetition fades within the first 100 training steps ( Figure 3 .c ; Appendix A.4 ). For Qwen3-1.7B at 2 bits, the share of repeated tokens at initialization rises from 6.7% in the first 4K response tokens to 21.1% beyond 8K; by step 100 it is about 1% at every position. Since ℒ OPD \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}} weights all response tokens equally, long early rollouts would spend supervision on this repeated text. We therefore cap responses at 4,096 tokens for steps 1–50, 8,192 for steps 51–100, and 16,384 afterwards for all model sizes and bit widths.
4 Experimental Setup
Training.
We conduct experiments on Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) at 2- and 3-bit weight precision (W2 and W3). OnQ uses uniform asymmetric weight quantization with group size 128 and BF16 activations ( Chen et al., 2025 ) . Block-wise calibration uses 4,096 windows of 2,048 tokens, with two epochs per block. OPD starts from the calibrated checkpoint and uses a multi-domain pool of 25,600 prompts spanning mathematics, code, science, and general instruction following. Each student is supervised by its original BF16 counterpart. OPD uses AdamW ( Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019 ) at a constant learning rate of 10 − 6 10^{-6} . OPD runs for 400 steps on Qwen3-1.7B and 300 steps on Qwen3-4B and 8B.
Baselines.
We compare OnQ with two groups of baselines ( Table 1 ): GPTQ ( Frantar et al., 2022 ) and AutoRound ( Cheng et al., 2024 ) for PTQ, and EfficientQAT ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , BitDistiller ( Du et al., 2024 ) , and ReasoningQAT ( Okoshi et al., 2026 ) for QAT. Their training data and configurations are detailed in Appendix A.1 and Appendix A.5 .
Evaluation.
We evaluate in thinking mode with EvalScope ( ModelScope Team, 2024 ) on nine benchmarks: GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , MATH-500 ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ) , and AIME 24–26 ( Zhang & Math-AI, 2024 ; Zhang & Math-AI, 2025 ; Zhang & Math-AI, 2026 ) for mathematics; HumanEval+, MBPP+ ( Chen et al., 2021 ; Austin et al., 2021 ; Liu et al., 2023 ) , and LiveCodeBench ( Jain et al., 2025 ) for code; MMLU-Redux ( Gema et al., 2025 ) and GPQA-Diamond ( Rein et al., 2024 ) for knowledge and science; and IFEval ( Zhou et al., 2023 ) for instruction following. We report pass@1 for code and accuracy for the other tasks. We use a response cap of 32,768 tokens, with 8,192-token exceptions for selected PTQ settings. We count responses terminated by the output-token limit as truncated. Scores are percentages; Avg. is the unweighted mean across the nine benchmarks. Full-precision, GPTQ, and QAT results average evaluation seeds 42, 43, and 44; AutoRound uses seed 42. Decoding settings and benchmark versions are given in Appendix A.6 .
5 Main Results
Figure 4: Accuracy versus mean generated tokens for Qwen3 models at 2 bits (top, a–c) and 3 bits (bottom, d–f), averaged over three evaluation seeds. Dashed lines connect model sizes within each method.
OnQ outperforms both PTQ and QAT baselines on every backbone and bit width. It achieves the highest nine-benchmark average in all six model/bit settings and the highest score on 48 of the 54 benchmark–setting pairs ( Table 1 ).
The advantage of OnQ grows as precision falls. At every model size, the margin over the strongest baseline is larger at 2 bits than at 3 bits, and at 2 bits it exceeds 10 points on Qwen3-4B and 8B. At 2 bits, the PTQ baselines perform poorly, while OnQ, trained on the trajectories that the 2-bit student actually generates, achieves the highest score on every benchmark at every model size. On Qwen3-4B at 2 bits, it achieves a higher nine-benchmark average than every 2-bit baseline on Qwen3-8B, a model twice its size. At 3 bits, OnQ comes within four points of the full-precision average on Qwen3-4B and 8B.
The largest gains occur on benchmarks that require long-horizon reasoning. In five of the six settings, the largest margin of OnQ over the strongest baseline falls on AIME or LiveCodeBench, and among the code benchmarks it falls on LiveCodeBench in every setting. HumanEval+ and MBPP+ ask for single functions from short specifications, whereas LiveCodeBench poses competitive-programming problems that require long-horizon reasoning before the final program; even at full precision, LiveCodeBench responses are about three times as long. Among the mathematics benchmarks, the largest margin likewise falls on AIME in every setting but one. For every QAT method and model size, AIME and LiveCodeBench elicit much longer responses than MATH-500 ( Figure 4 ), so the student conditions on its own outputs over thousands of tokens, where small errors compound into wrong or unfinished solutions. Fixed-response QAT supervises only reference prefixes and never trains on these long student trajectories, whereas OnQ supervises them directly.
6 Analysis
6.1 How Does OnQ Change Low-Bit Generation?
OnQ is more accurate with fewer generated tokens. Figure 4 plots accuracy against mean response length. At 2 bits, across MATH-500, AIME, and LiveCodeBench, OnQ achieves the highest accuracy at all three model sizes and shorter mean responses than the most accurate baseline in eight of the nine cases. On Qwen3-4B LiveCodeBench, accuracy rises from 28.34% for ReasoningQAT to 46.64% while the mean response shortens by more than a fifth: OnQ produces fewer and shorter incorrect responses. At 3 bits, OnQ again achieves the highest accuracy among QAT methods on all three benchmarks at every model size, with shorter mean responses than the most accurate baseline in six of the nine cases ( Figure 4 .d–f ). On Qwen3-1.7B AIME, accuracy rises from 23.33% for ReasoningQAT to 32.59% while the mean response shortens by 6%. Beyond these three benchmarks, OnQ has the lowest average truncation rate among QAT methods in all six settings ( Appendix C ).

Contribution of each stage.
To separate the two stages of OnQ, Table 2 reports each model after RTN, after calibration, and after on-policy distillation. Direct RTN severely degrades 2-bit accuracy, motivating restoration of a working policy before training on the student’s own rollouts. Calibration provides one but still struggles on long-horizon reasoning at 2 bits, as its LiveCodeBench scores show. On-policy distillation then closes about half or more of the remaining gap to full precision in every setting, with substantial gains on MATH-500 and LiveCodeBench, and lowers truncation in all six settings ( Appendix D ).

We compare on-policy objectives using a shared initialization, training set, and step budget. Table 4 reports results for OnQ, Vanilla OPD ( Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025 ) , GKD ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ) , GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , and OPD + GRPO on Qwen3-1.7B W2. Dynamic KL achieves the highest average over the three benchmarks and the highest MATH-500 and MMLU-Redux scores. Relative to FKL (GKD) and RKL (Vanilla OPD), it improves MATH-500 by 1.0 and 4.2 points, respectively. Relying solely on teacher supervision and trained only on mathematics prompts, OnQ outperforms OPD + GRPO and GRPO on all three benchmarks, showing stronger transfer to code generation and knowledge.
7 Conclusion
We present OnQ, a low-bit recovery pipeline that addresses trajectory divergence with on-policy distillation and distributional misalignment with candidate-wise Dynamic KL. On Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B at 2 and 3 bits, OnQ achieves the highest nine-benchmark average in all six settings, with the largest gains at 2 bits and on long-horizon reasoning benchmarks. The recovered models also produce shorter responses with fewer repetitive errors. On-policy training outperforms fixed-response distillation, and Dynamic KL achieves the best average among the on-policy objectives we compare. These results establish training on self-generated trajectories as an effective route to recovering reasoning under aggressive quantization.

A.2 Training Details
We quantize all linear layers in the transformer blocks, with a straight-through estimator for rounding ( Bengio et al., 2013 ) . Calibration uses batch size 2 and initializes quantization ranges at 0.7 times each group’s minimum and maximum weights. Learning rates start at 2 × 10 − 5 2\times 10^{-5} (2-bit) or 10 − 5 10^{-5} (3-bit) for latent weights and 10 − 4 10^{-4} for scales and zero points, with cosine decay to 5% of their initial values.
OPD uses verl ( Sheng et al., 2025 ) on NVIDIA H100 and H200 GPUs, with the full-precision teacher served by vLLM ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) . Each step samples one response for each of 128 prompts at temperature 1.0, without top- p p or top- k k truncation, and applies a single update. AdamW uses no warmup, weight decay 0.01, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0.

Appendix B Response Classification
Figure 5 uses seed-42 responses, with correctness determined by the benchmark scorers. Claude Sonnet 5.5 ( Anthropic, 2026 ) with high reasoning effort labels each incorrect response in two independent passes, with model identities withheld; Figure 6 gives the definition of a repetitive error. The passes agree on 93.6% of the MATH-500 responses (Cohen’s κ = 0.87 \kappa=0.87 ) and on 95.9% of the AIME responses ( κ = 0.88 \kappa=0.88 ). Disagreements and doubtful cases go to author review: the authors read them in full and set the final labels.
Definition
A response is looping if it contains a stretch where the text itself is stuck: one unit (a sentence, a line, a paragraph, a formula step, or a single token or digit) is emitted again and again, verbatim or with only trivial changes (a counter, a number, one or two words). To count, the stuck stretch must have at least about 8 consecutive repetitions of the unit, or run for at least about 1,000 characters. It may sit anywhere in the response; most often the response is still stuck when it ends.
Not looping:
- The same derivation or conclusion redone several times in different words (re-checking, second-guessing, restarting). Rewording is not a stuck text, however unproductive.
- A finite, purposeful enumeration: a table or case list that covers distinct cases and then stops (for example values for i = 2..20 followed by an answer). A list that just counts upward without end, or keeps testing values long after the answer is settled, is looping.
- Ordinary structural repetition: recurring formula scaffolding, headings, a final answer section that restates the solution once.
- A conclusion restated a handful of times ("the answer is 15") inside otherwise moving text.
