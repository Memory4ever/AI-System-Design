CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimization
for Joint RL and Distillation in Agents
Wenhao Yu 1 , Zhengcheng Wang 2 , Jiahong Liu 1 , Zexuan Qiu 1 , Minda Hu 1 ,
Shaohang Wei 3 , Jiafei Lyu 2 , Junyan Lyu 2 , Haobo Fu 2 , Irwin King 1
1 The Chinese University of Hong Kong
2 Tencent 3 Peking University

Abstract
Joint reinforcement learning and self-distillation combines task rewards
with dense teacher guidance for agent training, but their gradients can
partly cancel. A mixing coefficient controls relative scale without
selectively removing opposing components. We present CORD , a
general framework for conflict-aware optimization of two objectives.
CORD separates the choice of protected objective from the condition that
triggers projection, and combines the gradients once per optimizer
mini-batch. A nearest-point formulation yields a closed-form update and
characterizes its first-order effect on the protected objective before
optimizer transformations. Gradient diagnostics connect conflict in agent
training to lost local optimization progress and motivate selective
correction. As a gradient-combination mechanism, CORD can be added to
different joint training recipes while retaining their objectives.
Across three such recipes, it improves ALFWorld success by
1.5–15.9 percentage points. On WebShop, it improves two of the three pairs,
with SDAR rising from 60.7% to 64.6% success.
Our analyses explain why protection direction and projection gate must be
chosen together, and why the distillation objective and its mixing weight
remain consequential after projection.

1 Introduction
Language-model agents learn to complete tasks through sequences of actions,
but outcome rewards provide limited credit for individual decisions
( Feng et al., 2025 ) . Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) compares
returns across sampled attempts ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , yet supplies
little learning signal when rewards tie. On-policy distillation (OPD)
provides dense token-level feedback on student trajectories
( Agarwal et al., 2024 ) , but its usefulness depends on the teacher’s guidance.
Joint training couples this guidance with reward-based optimization,
allowing agents to learn from both task outcomes and informed action
preferences ( Lu et al., 2026 ) .
Existing approaches refine how teacher guidance enters learning.
Skill-conditioned self-distillation supplies an informed contextual view
of the policy ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , while SDAR weights token supervision
using detached teacher–student confidence gaps ( Lu et al., 2026 ) .
RLSD incorporates detached log-probability differences into token
advantages, preserving their signs without a separate distillation loss
( Yang et al., 2026a ) . Confidence gates reweight token
signals, whereas a gradient inner-product gate tests their accumulated
effect. The two operations can be composed for separate RL and OPD losses.
Joint optimization still requires a rule for combining these gradients.
Which contribution to preserve on conflict depends on the losses’ roles
and the training goal. A mixing coefficient changes scale but cannot
selectively remove opposing components.

We introduce CORD , a general framework for conflict-aware
optimization of two objectives. CORD accumulates both gradients under the
specified loss weights for each optimizer mini-batch. It retains the
selected protected gradient and minimally modifies the other before
combining them. Conflict-only projection removes opposing components,
while an always-projection variant also removes aligned components.
Either objective can be protected. Our analysis relates the resulting
first-order contribution on fixed sampled losses to finite step size and
optimizer transformations. The agent experiments evaluate protection,
triggering, and loss construction while retaining each host’s objectives.
Appendix A discusses related work.
Our contributions are threefold:
•
Conflict-aware joint optimization.
We connect measured gradient conflict to lost first-order progress and
formulate CORD to preserve a selected objective’s contribution while
correcting the conflicting component of the other.
•
Incremental gains across training methods.
CORD improves all three host recipes on ALFWorld by 1.5–15.9 percentage
points, and two of three on WebShop. These paired comparisons establish
the value of gradient combination while retaining each host’s objectives.
•
Guidance for configuring joint training.
We show why protection direction and triggering must be selected together,
and how the distillation loss, mixing weight, and aggregation determine
the gradient that projection receives. Agent cases connect these choices
to attribute completion, repeated actions, and token probability changes.

3 Method
CORD combines two objective gradients by making the protected direction and
projection gate explicit. It keeps one weighted gradient intact and makes the
smallest change to the other that satisfies the chosen geometric constraint.
The same construction accommodates either protection direction. The loss
functions and their relative weight specify the signals supplied to it.

Computational complexity.
Given the two objective gradients, CORD uses O ⁡ ( d ) O(d) arithmetic and O ⁡ ( d ) O(d)
additional gradient storage for d d trainable parameters. With K K PPO
mini-batches per training iteration, the gradient-combination cost is
O ⁡ ( K ​ d ) O(Kd) . When the objectives depend on the same student outputs, their

operations can reduce redundant work without changing the projection rule.
For a fixed workload, actor overhead differs from full-iteration overhead. Rollout, teacher
scoring, and environment interaction are shared stages, so their share of
each iteration determines how additional gradient work affects total time.
The component model in Appendix C.6 holds the
workload fixed and separates measured actor timings from estimated
iteration overhead.
4 Experiments
We test whether changing the gradient-combination rule adds value across
joint training methods, then use the analyses to guide its configuration.
The experiments answer three research questions:
(RQ1) Effectiveness: How much does CORD improve existing joint
training recipes?
(RQ2) Projection choices: How do the protected objective, projection
gate, and loss construction guide practical configuration?
(RQ3) Agent behavior: How do update choices appear in agent
interactions?
4.1 Experimental setup
Tasks and training.
We use the SDAR agent suite ( Lu et al., 2026 ) with
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct ( Yang et al., 2024 ) .
ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) evaluates household tasks over at most
50 actions. We report success across six categories and the pooled average.
WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) evaluates instruction-following shopping.
We cap episodes at 15 actions and report graded task score and strict
success rate on a 0–100 scale.
Our shared trainer uses 150 training iterations, eight rollouts per prompt,
AdamW at 10 − 6 10^{-6} , reference-KL coefficient 0.01 0.01 , and responses capped at
512 tokens on eight NVIDIA A100 40 GB GPUs. Full settings are given in
Appendix B .
Methods.
Table 1 includes Vanilla, Skill-Prompt, and OPSD values reported
by Lu et al. (2026) . GRPO and RLSD results are our own runs.
RLSD incorporates the self-distillation signal into the policy optimization
objective ( Yang et al., 2026a ) .
Following SDAR, the joint recipes share a predefined skill bank whose
contents remain fixed throughout training. Each Base–CORD pair uses the
same bank and skill-selection rule to isolate gradient combination.
Household tasks select task-specific manipulation skills, while shopping
instructions select relevant skills by keyword alongside general
strategies. This context guides teacher scoring of student-generated
actions and is absent from the student’s rollout and evaluation inputs.
We apply CORD to three joint training recipes in this shared interface:
Skill-SD ( Wang et al., 2026 ) ( k 3 k_{3} , λ = 0.001 \lambda=0.001 ), GRPO+OPSD
(host k 1 k_{1} , λ = 0.005 \lambda=0.005 ), and SDAR ( Lu et al., 2026 )
(confidence-weighted host k 1 k_{1} , λ = 0.01 \lambda=0.01 ).
Each recipe without CORD uses naive addition. + + CORD retains its coefficient,
protects the RL gradient, and projects only on conflict.
RQ2 then varies the protected objective and projection gate.
Evaluation.
We fix validation tasks to track progress on the same problems during
training and hold the final evaluation tasks constant across paired methods.
For the main comparison and full-training analyses in RQ2, including
PCGrad ( Yu et al., 2020 ) , we evaluate the final policy four times
after 150 updates at temperature 0.4 0.4 . Each run uses one training seed,
and the displayed ± \pm is the population standard deviation across
these four passes.
Our WebShop evaluation uses 500 tasks, whereas the published baseline
values in Table 1 use the 128-task protocol of
Lu et al. (2026) .
Appendix B.3 details the protocol.

Success rates do not show which attributes an agent missed or how many

5 Conclusion
Joint RL and distillation gives agents complementary feedback, but opposing
gradient components can reduce its local optimization benefit. CORD applies
a simple projection rule to this interaction, allowing either objective to
be protected and making the trigger explicit. The first-order analysis
explains the correction on fixed surrogates before optimizer transformations, and the agent
experiments establish incremental gains across three host recipes on
ALFWorld and two of three on WebShop. The analyses also yield concrete
configuration guidance: choose protection and triggering together, tune
the mixing weight for the selected distillation loss, and specify
aggregation before comparing gradient directions. CORD thus makes gradient
combination a reusable design choice.

Skill-SD.
Our shared implementation of the Skill-SD recipe uses the importance-weighted
k 3 k_{3} surrogate and a coefficient of 10 − 3 10^{-3} . It retains the SDAR skill
interface. In this implementation, the student-to-rollout importance
ratio is capped at 2 2 , and the exponential argument in the k 3 k_{3} term is
upper-clamped at 20 20 for numerical stability. These safeguards are applied
identically to the Base and + + CORD configurations. The auxiliary loss uses Equation 16 without
teacher-confidence weighting.
GRPO+OPSD.
GRPO+OPSD uses the plain teacher-minus-student log-probability term and uniform
token weights. Its coefficient in our unified implementation is
λ = 0.005 \lambda=0.005 . This matches the effective scale of the SDAR implementation’s
GRPO+OPSD configuration: the latter sets the outer coefficient to 0.01 0.01 and
the sigmoid sharpness to zero, yielding a constant weight
σ ⁡ ( 0 ) = 0.5 \sigma(0)=0.5 . Our gate-off branch uses w t = 1 w_{t}=1 , so absorbing this constant
into λ \lambda preserves the auxiliary loss and its gradient. The numerical
difference in coefficients therefore reflects the gate convention rather than
a separate hyperparameter search.
SDAR.
SDAR uses the same k 1 k_{1} token term with λ = 0.01 \lambda=0.01 and β = 5 \beta=5 .
Its detached sigmoid weight assigns greater weight to sampled tokens for which
the skill-conditioned teacher has a higher log-probability than the student.
This confidence weighting acts before differentiation. CORD subsequently
acts on the accumulated auxiliary gradient, using its alignment with the
GRPO actor gradient to decide whether to project. This separates token
weighting from the subsequent choice of gradient combination.
Additional baselines.
Our GRPO runs disable the distillation contribution. RLSD uses the
skill-conditioned self-teacher with coefficient 0.5 0.5 , a 100-step warmdown,
and clipping parameter 0.2 0.2 . We evaluate its final policies after 150 updates
in four passes using the ordered ALFWorld assignments
and the full WebShop task set described in
Appendix B.3 . The reference Vanilla,
Skill-Prompt, and OPSD entries in Table 1 are reported values from
the SDAR paper.
Fixed skill bank and teacher context.
Following SDAR, we use the predefined SkillBank adopted from SkillRL
( Lu et al., 2026 ; Xia et al., 2026 ) . The bank contains general strategies and
task-specific skills distilled from successful experience and failure
analysis. We keep its contents fixed throughout training. Thus, we use

C.6 Computational cost
Let d d be the number of trainable parameters and let a training iteration
contain K K PPO mini-batches, each partitioned into M M micro-batches.
CORD accumulates the two objective gradients over each complete PPO
mini-batch before applying the projection. Computing their inner products,
norms, and combined update requires O ⁡ ( d ) O(d) arithmetic per mini-batch,
or O ⁡ ( K ​ d ) O(Kd) per iteration. The additional gradient buffers require O ⁡ ( d ) O(d)
storage, which is distributed across workers when gradients are sharded.
The scalar inner products and norms can be communicated in a packed
reduction whose message size does not grow with d d .
For gradient evaluation, write F F for the cost of a student forward
computation and B 1 B_{1} , B 2 B_{2} for the reverse-mode computations of the two
objectives on a fixed micro-batch. If both objectives use the same student
outputs, a shared-forward schedule has actor-update cost
T CORD actor = K ​ M ​ ( F + B 1 + B 2 ) + O ⁡ ( K ​ d ) + T opt , T_{\mathrm{CORD}}^{\mathrm{actor}}=KM(F+B_{1}+B_{2})+O(Kd)+T_{\mathrm{opt}},
where T opt T_{\mathrm{opt}} includes gradient clipping and optimizer updates.
A fused-loss Base instead has cost
T Base actor = K ​ M ​ ( F + B sum ) + T opt , T_{\mathrm{Base}}^{\mathrm{actor}}=KM(F+B_{\mathrm{sum}})+T_{\mathrm{opt}},
with B sum B_{\mathrm{sum}} denoting the reverse-mode computation of the
weighted loss sum. These expressions use equal-sized micro-batches to simplify
notation. For variable workloads, the corresponding costs are summed over
micro-batches. Parameter and gradient communication belong to the gradient
evaluation costs, separately from the packed reduction for projection.
Sharing the forward computation avoids evaluating identical student
outputs twice. It does not make two objective-gradient evaluations
equivalent to one backward pass on their summed loss: the latter supplies
only the combined gradient, which generally does not determine the inner
products needed by CORD. Both schedules have the same asymptotic order
when the reverse-mode costs are comparable, while their constant factors
depend on graph reuse, gradient-buffer handling, sharding, and hardware.
Forward sharing, fused vector operations, and selective diagnostic
collection are implementation opportunities rather than a fixed
wall-clock guarantee. Teacher scores already computed for the auxiliary
objective are reused.
Cost of a complete training iteration.
Let A A denote the actor-update time of an ordinary fused-loss Base, and
let S S include rollout and environment interaction, teacher, reference,
and old-policy scoring, reward and advantage computation, and the
remaining work within a training iteration. For a fixed workload, if these
shared stages remain unchanged and the actor-update multiplier is
r actor r_{\mathrm{actor}} , then
R iter = S + r actor ​ A S + A = 1 + ( r actor − 1 ) ​ f actor , f actor = A S + A . R_{\mathrm{iter}}=\frac{S+r_{\mathrm{actor}}A}{S+A}=1+(r_{\mathrm{actor}}-1)f_{\mathrm{actor}},\qquad f_{\mathrm{actor}}=\frac{A}{S+A}.
Thus, the actor-update multiplier is weighted by its share of Base
iteration time. This comparison excludes initialization, periodic
evaluation, checkpoint saving, and time outside the training iteration.
