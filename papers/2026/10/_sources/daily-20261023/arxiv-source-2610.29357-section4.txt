4 Evaluating Memory-Augmented Agents as Continual Learners
Sections 2 and 3 specify how
experience can persist, what reusable form it can take, and how it is
admitted, consolidated, used, revised, and governed. Those design choices do
not, by themselves, establish that continual learning has occurred. A
changed store, a retrieved record, or a higher single-task score can all
arise without a durable, experience-induced change in later behavior. This
section therefore treats evaluation as an evidentiary problem, asking what
empirical observations would justify a continual-learning claim given the
architecture and lifecycle established in prior sections. We first specify
three core behavioral outcomes (retention, adaptation, and transfer) and then
establish the protocols needed to measure them without confounding memory with
expanded context windows, extra test-time computation, or transient, isolated
success.
4.1 What to Measure: Retention, Adaptation, and Transfer
Access-level metrics remain useful, but they answer a narrower question than
learning. Content correctness and retrieval scores diagnose whether
experience was written and can still be found; a single-task outcome shows
only that memory helped on the current probe
(Table 1 ). Continual learning additionally
requires that an experiential gain persist under delay and interference,
remain revisable when the world changes, and transfer beyond remembered
episodes Lopez-Paz and Ranzato [2017] , Wang et al. [2024c] . The remainder of this
subsection operationalizes those three outcomes. Component traces localize
failure; they do not substitute for the behavioral claim.

4.1.1 Retention: Learning Must Survive Delay and Interference
Section 3.3 distinguishes preservation, access,
utilization, and behavioral effect in the memory lifecycle. Retention evaluation
uses the same cascade, but changes the object of measurement from a stored
trace to a capability margin. After delay and subsequent experience, the
agent should still outperform a matched agent deprived of that experience
on the same probe. An entry can remain in a store, or a latent state can
remain decodable, while its contribution to later decisions has vanished.
Conversely, a useful procedure may persist without the originating episode.
Trace survival is therefore diagnostic; behavioral persistence is the
learning claim.
The four gates yield a testable weakest-gate hypothesis , positing that
behavioral retention is bottlenecked by the least reliable step and that
improving an earlier gate does not necessarily raise the final outcome.
Increasing capacity can raise preservation while lowering access through
denser retrieval competition. “The system remembers x x ” concerns preservation
and access; “the system retained learning from x x ” additionally requires
utilization and effect. With the base model fixed, continual insertion and
eviction in an external store can reduce performance on earlier
tasks Yu et al. [2026a] , and repeated extraction and injection of experience
can turn a single-round gain into progressive
collapse Chen et al. [2026a] . These failures are not
confined to catastrophic forgetting in weights, as stored content can survive
while remaining unaccessed, unused, or ineffective.
Time and intervening experience are not interchangeable. A retention
protocol should first measure the immediate gain Δ i 0 \Delta_{i}^{0} on capability
i i relative to a memoryless or reset condition, then remeasure after delay d d
and interference load m m . The normalized retained gain
Δ i ​ ( d , m ) / Δ i 0 \Delta_{i}(d,m)/\Delta_{i}^{0} , reported only when Δ i 0 > 0 \Delta_{i}^{0}>0 , distinguishes
failure to retain from failure to learn. Delay should be specified in
wall-clock time, turns, or token distance; interference should report the number,
semantic overlap, and conflict relation of intervening updates.
Substrate-specific traces then localize the broken gate, identifying deletion,
eviction, and rank competition in external memory, or overwriting, state
drift, and readout mismatch in activation and parametric memory
(Section 2.1 ). These outcomes are the quantities against which
capacity, interference, and cost are traded in
Section 4.3 .
4.1.2 Adaptation: Learning Must Remain Revisable under Change
When facts, preferences, tools, or rules change, retention without revision
becomes a source of error. Section 3.4 already
treats conflict resolution, persistent update, and selective forgetting as
lifecycle operations. Evaluation asks a more targeted question, examining whether
behavior faithfully follows a valid update after an environment change without
indiscriminate damage to still-useful capabilities. The relevant evaluation chain
is temporal rather than a single write (detection, revision, recovery, and
preservation), which should be quantified via detection delay, steps to
recovery, stale-error duration, and collateral degradation on unchanged probes.
Better recall does not automatically yield more
preference-aligned behavior Xiao et al. [2026] , and summary memories
can retain factual detail while losing the preference signal needed after an
environmental shift Wang et al. [2026a] .
Component diagnostics should follow the substrate. For external stores, the
question is whether the current record supersedes the stale one and whether
retrieval ranks it above obsolete evidence. Exposed maintenance operations
establish that updates are possible, but conversational QA after a long
history does not demonstrate recovery under controlled
drift Chhikara et al. [2025] . For parametric and latent memories, injection
accuracy and sequential fact updates are retention and write diagnostics,
not evidence that an acting agent detected an unannounced change or resumed
correct tool use Wang et al. [2024e] , Das et al. [2024] . Evaluated models can
verbalize that a fact has changed yet fail to propagate that judgment to
the final prediction Wallat et al. [2026] . A credible adaptation claim
therefore requires matched pre-change and post-change probes, together with
simultaneous reporting of recovery and selective preservation.
4.1.3 Transfer: Learning Must Extend beyond Remembered Episodes
Section 2 classifies reusable experience as episodic,
semantic, or procedural assets. Transfer evaluation asks whether those
assets support later behavior beyond storing an answer. Three increasingly
demanding cases should be separated: replaying the same or a near-duplicate
episode, solving an unseen variant from the same task family, and applying
shared structure to a distinct target task. Source and target partitions
must exclude duplicate instances and target answers, document the shared
structure, such as a workflow, causal rule, tool convention, or subskill, while
keeping the base model, tools, context budget, and compute matched. Otherwise
nearest-neighbour reproduction and extra context can be mistaken for
learned transfer.
Abstraction directly supports reuse, as demonstrated by the successful transfer
of insights, workflows, and executable skills across related tasks and
environments Zhao et al. [2024a] , Wang et al. [2025c] , Wang et al. [2024b] . The same
evidence shows why mean target accuracy is insufficient. Raw trajectory
memory can produce negative forward transfer where abstract insights do
not, with harm concentrated on cases that a no-memory agent could not
already solve Hu et al. [2026a] . Dedicated memory and skill-maintenance
methods are only selectively helpful and can be matched or outperformed by
ordinary in-context accumulation Asawa et al. [2026] , Guan et al. [2026] , Zhong et al. [2026] .
Transfer should therefore be reported as zero-shot target gain or forward
transfer, interactions or tokens to a target threshold, and explicit
negative-transfer rates Lopez-Paz and Ranzato [2017] , Rodríguez et al. [2018] .
For external memory, analysis should identify whether the retrieved asset
is a case, rule, workflow, or skill, and whether success depends on answer
overlap. For neural memory, it should test whether updated latent or
parametric state improves a held-out target without degrading unrelated
tasks.
4.2 How to Measure: Protocols for Experience, Controls, and Time
4.2.1 Benchmarks Must Contain Learnable Experience
A parallel distinction applies to benchmark design. The presence of
“history” does not imply the presence of learnable experience. Many
suites pre-supply dialogue logs, profiles, or knowledge bases and then test
whether related questions can be answered after the history is
given Maharana et al. [2024] , Du et al. [2024] , Wu et al. [2025a] , Zhong et al. [2024] .
Such protocols diagnose long-horizon access. They usually cannot decide
whether a new capability was formed during evaluation.
Evidence strength rises with the experience that the protocol actually
exposes (Table 2 ). Fixed history supports “remember and
use.” Continual writing of state supports “internal state accumulates.”
Feedback that improves later behavior, under the matched and sequential
controls below, approaches continual learning. Procedural streams ask
whether success and failure trajectories improve later
workflows Zheng et al. [2025] ; semantic and personalization
streams ask whether facts and preferences persist and can be
revised Maharana et al. [2024] , Wu et al. [2025a] , Tan et al. [2025a] ; dynamic-rule
streams ask whether invalidated experience is corrected in
time Hu et al. [2026b] , Asawa et al. [2026] . A benchmark may excel
on one stream while remaining silent on the others. Externally grounded
outcomes and user corrections also support stronger learning claims than
self-generated reflections, which can be stored and later retrieved as if
they were facts Shinn et al. [2023] , Park et al. [2023] .

Further baselines rule out further alternatives. A no-memory or reset
condition shows what the model can already do. A full-history context
baseline tests whether the benefit is simply more historical text; when a
memory mechanism outperforms a matched long-context condition, the evidence
favors organization or selective use rather than context expansion
alone Wang et al. [2026d] , Tan et al. [2025a] . A static RAG baseline
tests whether dynamic accumulation adds anything beyond a fixed knowledge
source. Matched computation addresses extra inference. Fine-tuning, LoRA,
and model editing absorb experience through different substrates and should
not be treated as interchangeable with all forms of agent memory, though
they remain useful comparison points.
What “reset” means depends on the substrate (Section 2.1 ).
External stores admit write-off, read-off, and clear-store interventions,
which separate encoding, use, and overall value. For activation or
parametric memory, reset cannot simply mean deleting a database; evaluation must
instead distinguish online parameter updates, the preservation of persistent state, and
subsequent readout Gutiérrez et al. [2025] . A stateful condition may also
bundle conversation history, tool state, and external memory, so a positive
Δ ​ P t \Delta P_{t} need not identify which mechanism produced the gain. A
stronger design keeps the backbone, task input, context budget, retrieval
budget, and inference compute matched while varying only
experience-related state.
4.2.3 Sequential Evaluation Separates Durable Learning from Transient Success
The retention and adaptation definitions above already require the same
capability to be measured across memory states. Chronological length does
not substitute for that invariance. Endpoint scores after a long history
show that information can be used, but not when the benefit appeared or
whether it disappeared and recovered Maharana et al. [2024] , Wu et al. [2025a] .
A short protocol that probes a capability before acquisition, immediately
afterward, and after a controlled update is stronger evidence than a
long trajectory with a single terminal score.
Four observation regimes support progressively stronger claims.
Snapshots test a final memory state;
streams report online reward on changing tasks;
reprobes repeat the same capability or a calibrated task variant; and
interventional panels combine reprobes with an event ledger and
matched stateful–reset trajectories. AgentCL and CL-Bench illustrate the
value of a stable observation target, where first-pass, frozen-memory, and
held-out probes systematically isolate acquisition, reuse, and
transfer Shu et al. [2026] , Asawa et al. [2026] . Between probes, the ledger
should record elapsed time, writes, semantic overlap, conflicts, feedback,
tool or rule changes, and retrieval or update cost. Delay plus overlapping
updates tests retention; changes in facts, preferences, or tools test
adaptation; held-out tasks that preserve latent structure test transfer.
Evidence for one evaluation setting should not be treated as validation across
all three.
A compact blueprint follows. Define capability anchors and measure them
before and immediately after acquisition; schedule checkpoints on a declared
delay–interference grid; run stateful and reset conditions with identical
backbone, tools, context, retrieval, and inference budgets; and populate a
matrix R t , i R_{t,i} that re-evaluates capability i i after update t t .
Diagonal entries measure acquisition, lower-triangular entries expose
retention and backward influence, and held-out variants test transfer.
Reporting the event ledger beside this matrix prevents apparent gains
from obscuring task drift or hidden computational overhead.
4.2.4 Fragmented Benchmarks Limit Cross-Study Conclusions
Existing suites occupy different cells of this design rather than a common
scale. Access-oriented histories, incremental ingestion, and sequential
interactive tasks are not interchangeable measurements of the same
construct, and they rarely match context budgets, retrieval depth, or
inference compute. Numerical comparison across papers is therefore
unreliable unless the protocol specifies the behavioral outcome in
Section 4.1 , the experience regime above, and the
matched and sequential controls that isolate memory. Scores should be
read within that evidentiary scope, not as a generic ranking of memory
systems.
4.3 The Memory Scaling Law: Capacity, Interference, and Learning Efficiency
4.3.1 Non-Parametric Memory Scaling Law: Experience, Capacity, and Compute
Non-parametric memory makes deployment experience a resource for continued
capability growth with fixed model weights. Agents preserve interaction
facts Chhikara et al. [2025] , distill reasoning strategies, and consolidate
experience for later tasks Ouyang et al. [2026] , Yin et al. [2025] .
The resulting capability depends on how much relevant experience is
available and how efficiently the memory system retains and uses it.
We propose a Memory Scaling Law relating attainable performance to
experience, persistent capacity, and read–write compute. Its central
prediction is a sequence of resource-limited regimes, where performance
improves as relevant experience accumulates and subsequently saturates when
capacity or read–write compute constrains its effective use. Expanding the
limiting resources shifts these saturation points. The information structure of
experience determines how quickly capacity demand grows, while reuse determines
how much computation is worth investing in writing and consolidation. This view
connects memory representations and lifecycle operations to the rate and cost
of capability growth during deployment.
