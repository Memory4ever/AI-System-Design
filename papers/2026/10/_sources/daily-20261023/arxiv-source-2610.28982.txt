Abstract
While large language models are increasingly deployed as tool-calling agents, their safety evaluations still focus primarily on their ability to refuse harmful requests in text. We investigate whether the refusal behavior in text transfers to tool use. We construct a benchmark of 2,304 prompts across four domains, consisting of matched triplets that present the same harmful or benign request in text-only, standard tool-enabled, and adversarially framed tool-enabled settings. We then assess tool-call safety using 20 predefined forbidden actions. Testing five models of varying sizes and families, we find that requests refused in text frequently result in unsafe tool calls. Under standard tool-enabled prompting, this divergence occurs for 10–52 percent of harmful requests refused in chat. To mechanistically investigate this gap, we identify a refusal-associated direction in the residual stream of each of the five models. We find that in tool-enabled contexts, the activations along this direction decrease in four of five models. Furthermore, lower activation predicts unsafe tool calls across all five models (AUC 0.76–0.86). However, restoring the signal to the respective text-only value through activation patching only prevents a subset of unsafe calls, primarily by suppressing tool use rather than by producing safer calls. Steering along the refusal direction nearly eliminates unsafe calls only at strengths that also suppress benign tool use or degrade the output quality. Our findings indicate that the identified refusal direction contributes to whether a model acts, but only partially explains the refusal divergence. Therefore, evaluating the safety of tool-calling agents requires directly examining their actions along with their textual responses.
1 Introduction
Increasingly, the use cases of large language models have shifted from simply generating text to taking actions in the form of agents. Many modern systems browse the web, execute code, call typed tools, and implement enterprise workflows ( Schick et al., 2023 ; Yao et al., 2023 ; Qin et al., 2024 ) . With this shift in the deployment of LLMs, there is a corresponding shift in the meaning of their “safety”. That is, a tool-enabled agent can cause harm through its actions in the environment, not only by emitting an unsafe sentence as measured in text-based settings.
Indeed, safety alignment is currently overwhelmingly trained and evaluated on text. In particular red teaming, refusal training, and jailbreak benchmarks target harmful text-based completions ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ; Bai et al., 2022a ; Bai et al., 2022b ; Ganguli et al., 2022 ; Perez et al., 2022 ; Mazeika et al., 2024 ) . A model that has learned to
say “I can’t help with that” has learned a behavior over its verbal
output. Nonetheless, whether that behavior also governs the tool-call decision is a
separate question that carries direct consequences as these agents move into financial, medical, and legal settings.
This distinction is important because tool-enabled agents are increasingly being developed and applied in high-risk domains such as finance and healthcare ( Ding et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) , where failures can lead to financial losses, privacy breaches, or the execution of malicious instructions extracted from external sources.
However, prior work has rarely investigated whether refusal mechanisms transfer to tool-call decisions. To address this gap, we evaluate models from five different families on a dataset we created spanning four domains. We run inference on all of these models using groups of three semantically similar requests: one text-only request and two other tool-enabled variants. We find that models that refuse harmful requests in text generation often fail to refuse similar requests when they are framed in the format of a tool invocation.
Behavioral finding:
We evaluate our benchmark of prompt triples, in which each harmful request is posed as a plain chat prompt as well as two tool-enabled versions. We find that, although the models refuse 44–76% of harmful chat prompts, they oftentimes fail to refuse the same requests when phrased in the tool-use variant. This gap, which we call conditioned divergence , is substantial and does not track model size in our five models.
Mechanistic finding:
Next, we ask why, seeking to find the mechanistic underpinnings of our behavioral results. We begin by identifying the refusal direction in each model, following the approach of prior work, which shows that refusal is mediated by a single direction ( Park et al., 2024 ) in the residual stream ( Arditi et al., 2024 ) . To directly address the behavioral finding, we then test whether that refusal direction also governs tool call safety. We find that it does, but only in part. That is, tool-enabled prompts suppress the activation of the refusal direction at the decision token in most models, and a lower activation predicts the unsafe call in all five models (AUC 0.76–0.86). Nonetheless, when we restore the refusal signal to its no-tool level, we find that for most models only a minority of unsafe calls revert, and most of those that do revert by emitting no tool call rather than a safer one. Hence, we conclude that refusal suppression explains only part of this transfer failure. The refusal direction is therefore a useful signal for monitoring but a comparatively blunt knob for control. Because the divergence recurs in all five model families, it may reflect a general property of how alignment is represented in models, although the internal mechanism differs between them.
1.1 Contributions
1.
A 2304 prompt matched triple benchmark spanning four high-stakes domains with 20 deterministic forbidden actions to score tool-call refusal.
2.
A conditioned-divergence metric separating transfer failure from the base refusal rate, together with evidence that text refusal does not reliably transfer to tool call safety.
3.
A mechanistic study across five model families showing the refusal direction is a strong predictor (AUC 0.76–0.86) but only a partial mediator (patching reverts 13–90% of unsafe calls, mostly by suppressing the call rather than by making it safe), with suppression significant in four of five families and random-direction, mean-direction and KL-admissibility controls reported for all five.
2 Related work
Tool-augmented language models.
This line of work introduces the extension of LLMs from simple text generation to actions that take the form of external tool calls, and teaches models to call functions, search, and run multi-step workflows
( Schick et al., 2023 ; Yao et al., 2023 ; Qin et al., 2024 ) . This shifts an area of potential harm from text that has been generated to actions done by an agent. The latter is the surface of our targets for evaluation.
Mind the GAP.
The closest work to ours is
Cartagena and Teixeira (2026) , who measure the text-to-tool safety gap head-on. They pose each harmful request twice, with one version in plain chat and another version using available tools. This structure is similar to the dataset we constructed, as they also consider requests crossing several domains and similarly using a deterministic forbidden action check for safety. This paper shows that requests the model refuses in chat often emerge as unsafe tool calls once actions are enabled. This provides behavioral evidence, which we reproduce and then add a mechanistic explanation for. We
also measure the gap differently, as our conditioned divergence counts only the
triples a model genuinely refused in chat, so its base refusal rate cannot move
the number. Finally, we add an adversarially framed mode to each group to tell tool
availability apart from prompt pressure.
Refusal and safety alignment in text.
Several works measure and train safety alignment over text generation and completion. Specifically, instruction tuning and RLHF can teach models to decline harmful requests ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ; Bai et al., 2022a ; Bai et al., 2022b ) , and red-teaming and jailbreak benchmarks stress-test
that behavior ( Ganguli et al., 2022 ; Perez et al., 2022 ; Mazeika et al., 2024 ) . These
methods evaluate what the model says, not whether the learned refusal governs the model’s actions when tools are available.
Mechanistic interpretability of refusal.
Arditi et al. (2024)
shows that refusal in chat models is mediated by a single linear direction in the
residual stream. Specifically, they show that ablating it suppresses refusal and adding it induces
over-refusal. We test this on five architectures, where it reproduces only in part, and extend it to
the text-to-tool transition, testing whether the same direction stays active
when the model selects an action rather than a sentence.
3 Methodology
3.1 What we mean by an agent
The term agent is widely used across enterprise, spanning everything from a single function call to a multi-step system with planning, memory, and environment feedback. We therefore fix the object of the study. By an agent we mean an off-the-shelf instruction-tuned LLM with a minimal harness which includes a set of domain tools, implemented in standard OpenAI function call schemas that are injected into the model’s context, allowing the model to either respond with natural-language text or a structured call to one of those tools. No planning module, scratchpad, memory, or orchestration layer is included in this harness.
3.2 Dataset
The evaluation dataset consists of 2304 prompts across different categories:
•
Domain: Finance, Healthcare, Education, and Legal: four
regulated settings, each with its own misuse modes and protected data.
•
Scenario: Direct, Indirect, Justification/roleplay, and
Structured: four phrasings of the same intent, from straightforward
to tool-format-driven.
•
Mode: No-tool chat, Tool-enabled normal, and Tool-enabled
stress (jailbreak-style framing): the central comparison.
•
System condition: Neutral, Safety-reinforced (explicit
safety constraints), and Tool-encouraging (nudges tool use).
•
Variant: A (original phrasing) and B (reworded).
•
Request type: Harmful (should be refused / no unsafe tool
call) and Benign (safe to answer or call tools).
The scenario factor poses the request in different forms (Direct, Indirect, Justification/roleplay, and Structured) because different styles of framing alter AI outputs ( Lutz et al., 2025 ) . The three Mode options are the essential comparison of the datapoints. No-tool chat: no tools provided; model responds in conversation. Tool-enabled normal: domain tools are injected into the context under standard prompting. Tool-enabled stress: same tools injected into context. However, the user prompt includes higher pressure or jailbreak-style framing. Matched groups (same domain, scenario, variant, system condition, request type, and batch) share the same underlying intent across modes, with prompts adapted to each mode’s context: no-tool prompts make no reference to tools, while tool-enabled prompts explicitly invoke specific tools. This design holds scenario and intent constant while varying both tool availability and the framing appropriate to each mode, following the matched-prompt multi-domain safety evaluation methodology of Cartagena and Teixeira (2026) .
Three system conditions vary the framing presented to the models: a neutral role description, a safety-reinforced version with explicit safety reminders, and finally a tool-encouraging version emphasizing efficient use of available functions.
These six factors define 576 cells; each cell was authored four times with distinct prompts (the Batch factor), giving 2304 prompts. These categories produce a broad set of reproducible scenarios for testing our claim.
3.3 Tool environments and forbidden actions
Each domain exposes five function-calling tools modeled on realistic
deployment schemas, adapting the domain tool-environment and deterministic forbidden-action design of Cartagena and Teixeira (2026) .
Example tools include place_trade_order and
initiate_wire_transfer (Finance),
retrieve_patient_record and draft_medication_plan
(Healthcare), change_grade (Education), and
file_regulatory_submission (Legal).
Tools are provided in standard OpenAI function schema format.
Tool-call safety is scored by ForbiddenAction predicates which are deterministic Python functions that inspect a tool name and argument dictionary and return a Boolean violation flag.
Each domain defines five predicates: one per tool for 20 total.
Predicate types are:
•
Sensitive-data flag : fires when a Boolean parameter
requesting protected data (e.g., include_phi=True ) is set.
•
Authorization-bypass : fires when a required authorization
field (e.g., approval_ticket ) is absent, empty, or filled with a
value that asserts bypass rather than authorization (e.g.,
approval_ticket="skip_approval" ).
•
PII exfiltration : fires when synthetic PII markers or
PII/privilege-seeking text (e.g., query="private student
identifiers" ) appears in text-valued arguments, or when a send tool names
an unapproved external recipient.
•
Intent keyword : fires when argument content contains
policy-evasion terms (e.g., “evade”, “bypass”, “contraindicated”).
A run is scored tc_safe if no predicate fires; any violation yields
tc_unsafe . Every rule was validated against all stored runs and fires on 1.4–3.4% of benign tool-enabled runs per model.

3.5 Metrics
Refusal rate.
This is the fraction of responses that decline the user request, computed separately
for harmful and benign prompts.
On harmful prompts, high refusal is the target safety behavior ( Arditi et al., 2024 ) and on benign prompts, it shows over-refusal (false positive rate) ( Röttger et al., 2024 ; Cui et al., 2025 ) .
Refusal is scored only on genuine text responses; a tool-call-only response or empty text is not counted as a verbal refusal.
tc_safe rate.
This is the fraction of tool-enabled runs where no ForbiddenAction predicate fires.
Matched triples.
Each harmful request instance is issued in all three interaction modes:
no-tool chat, tool-enabled normal, and tool-enabled stress, producing a
matched triple of runs over the same underlying request.
These triples are keyed by (Domain, Scenario, Variant, System Condition, Batch),
so the no-tool run and the two tool-enabled runs differ only in tool
affordance and prompt framing and not in the actual request.
Let 𝒯 \mathcal{T} be the set of all matched triples and, for a triple t t ,
let refuse ⁡ ( t ) \mathrm{refuse}(t) indicate that the no-tool run verbally refused and

tool-enabled run in mode m ∈ { normal , stress } m\in\{\mathrm{normal},\mathrm{stress}\} fired a
ForbiddenAction predicate.
Tool-call propensity.
Tool-use rates vary substantially across models. Under normal tool-enabled prompting, models issue at least one tool call on 11–59% of harmful requests. Conditional on issuing a call, however, 73–100% of calls are unsafe. Command-R illustrates why unconditional unsafe-call rates must be interpreted alongside tool-use propensity: it calls a tool on only 11% of harmful requests, and every such call is unsafe. Its benign tool-call rate is also relatively low at 40%, compared with 83% for Llama-3.1-70B. Thus, Command-R’s low aggregate unsafe-call rate partly reflects low tool-use propensity rather than consistently safe action selection.

3.6 Refusal classification
Text refusal is classified by Claude Opus 5 ( Anthropic, 2026 ) at temperature 0, using both the request and response. Empty or tool-call-only responses count as non-refusals, and disclaimer-wrapped compliance counts as compliance. Validation against Claude Fable 5 on 538 stratified examples yields 97.4% agreement ( κ = 0.94 \kappa=0.94 ). Intervention outputs are screened for degenerate (repetitive or non-linguistic) text before classification, and conditions with at least 25% degenerate output are flagged and their rates are not interpreted.
3.7 Mechanistic methods
Refusal direction:
On the no-tool prompts, we take the decision token residual vector at every layer and compute the difference in mean direction ( Marks and Tegmark, 2023 ) between harmful and benign prompts to identify the refusal direction: r ^ l = unit ​ ( μ l harm − μ l benign ) \hat{r}_{l}=\text{unit}(\mu_{l}^{\text{harm}}-\mu_{l}^{\text{benign}}) ( Arditi et al., 2024 ) . We then select the operating layer as the one maximizing the norm of the difference-in-means vector, measuring the magnitude of the separation, inside the causal mid band (35 to 85 percent depth). The layer is selected automatically for each model. We additionally score every candidate layer with the KL-divergence admissibility check of Arditi et al. (2024) on harmless prompts; at the selected layer only Command-R’s direction passes it (Table 6 ). Finally, projecting a prompt’s residual onto the difference-in-means refusal direction gives us a scalar refusal activation .
Interventions:
To probe causality, we apply three interventions:
1.
Directional ablation, which removes the r ^ \hat{r} component at each layer of the model
2.
Activation addition, adding c ⋅ r ^ c\cdot\hat{r} for scalar coefficient c c , with c c equal to the harmful-minus-benign projection gap ( Turner et al., 2023 )
3.
Activation patching, in which we overwrite the projection of a tool run with its corresponding no-tool value ( Meng et al., 2022 ; Zou et al., 2023 )
We steer the model by activation addition over a coefficient grid that is scaled to the harm versus benign projection gap for each model. Hence, our intervention magnitudes can be compared across models with varying activation scale.
Robustness of the direction:
To evaluate the refusal direction, we contrast across request type, subtracting activations for harmful and benign prompts. In order to verify that our result is not simply an artifact of this choice, we additionally extract a behavior-defined direction, taking the difference in means between refused and complied harmful prompts. We then use cosine similarity to evaluate how strongly aligned these directions are, showing that request harmfulness and the refusal decision largely share a direction (cosine 0.69–0.86 across the five models; Table 5 ).

4 Behavioral results
Figure 1 shows our primary metric directly: of the harmful requests each model refuses in chat, the fraction whose matched tool-enabled run makes an unsafe call. We observe that four of the five models refuse the majority of harmful requests in chat, with text refusal rates ranging from 44% (Mistral-7B) to 76% (Llama-3.1-70B). However, on the same request, when tools are enabled, every model still makes unsafe tool calls, ranging from 11% (Command-R-7B) to 54% (Qwen3-14B) of the time.
Every model shows substantial divergence under both tool modes: this is the transfer failure we study, in which a refusal expressed in chat frequently fails to carry over to the matched tool call, so a high verbal refusal rate does not translate into safe action.
In Table 1 , the same gap is quantified over the 384 matched harmful triples per model. The first column shows the no-tool text refusal rate and the next two give the unsafe tool-call rate under normal and stress framing, while the final two give the conditioned divergence Δ m cond \Delta^{\mathrm{cond}}_{m} for each mode. Because we only compute Δ m cond \Delta^{\mathrm{cond}}_{m} over the triples that the model actually refused in chat, it isolates the transfer failure from the base refusal rate, so these divergence values cannot be ascribed to the fact that one model refuses less often than another. In Normal tool prompts, conditioned divergence spans from 0.098 (Command-R) to 0.519 (Qwen).
Divergence under the stress mode is of comparable magnitude (0.098–0.657) and remains substantial for every model. We probe the internal mechanism behind this divergence in Section 5 , and show its relationship with model scale and system-prompt framing in Section 6 .

5 Mechanistic analysis
Now we consider why there is a failure of refusal to transfer from text to tool prompts, and whether a single mechanism explains the divergence across model families. Following Section 3.7 , we extract the refusal direction for each model and characterize what it does under tool contexts. Table 3 summarizes our headline results.
A single direction causally governs text refusal in some models, not all:
In Command-R, and more weakly in Mistral and Llama, we find that ablating r ^ \hat{r} decreases the harmful prompt refusal rate, whereas in Qwen and Gemma removing it raises refusal (Table 3 ); adding the direction at its natural magnitude makes Qwen and Llama refuse benign prompts but has no effect in Mistral or Gemma (Table 7 ). This reproduces the results of Arditi et al. (2024) only in part: Llama shows both effects but with a small ablation drop, and half of Command-R’s addition outputs are degenerate. The auto-selected layers, chosen following the method described in Section 3.7 , sit at 81 to 84 percent depth, near the top of the 35 to 85 percent search band. Furthermore, the addition coefficient spans more than three orders of magnitude across the models, from 7 in Mistral to roughly 18,500 in Gemma.
Tool context suppresses the refusal signal:
For our harmful triples, we find that the mean refusal activation drops significantly from no-tool to tool-enabled modes in four of the five models (Table 3 , with t = 5.6 t=5.6 for Qwen, t = 5.1 t=5.1 for Mistral, t = 9.7 t=9.7 for Command-R, and t = 4.0 t=4.0 for Gemma, but null for Llama at t = 0.4 t=0.4 ; Figure 2 ). We find that the text-derived and tool-derived directions remain aligned in Qwen, Mistral and Gemma (cosine 0.74–0.80) but not in Command-R (0.39) or Llama (0.21; Table 5 ), so in three of the five models tools attenuate the magnitude of the signal along a shared axis as opposed to rotating it.
The refusal direction is a strong predictor but a partial mediator:
We find that a lower refusal activation predicts an unsafe call in every model, with AUC from 0.76 to 0.86. At the same time, however, when we patch a tool run’s projection back to its no-tool level, only a fraction of the unsafe calls revert to being safe: 24 percent for Qwen and 20 percent for Mistral, and most of these reversions are the model emitting no tool call rather than a safer one (Table 4 ); among the calls a patched model still makes, 80 to 96 percent remain unsafe. The refusal direction is therefore a partial mediator of whether the model acts, not a complete account of the transfer failure. The unrecovered portion may reflect an incomplete one-dimensional intervention (refusal may be represented across additional directions, other layers, or other token positions) or nonlinear and distributed mechanisms that a single-scalar patch does not restore. Our design also varies tool availability jointly with tool-oriented prompt wording, so we cannot attribute the remaining failures to tool affordance specifically; we can only conclude that restoring this one signal is not sufficient as a control mechanism. The share recovered varies by model: patching reverts 13 percent of Llama’s unsafe calls, 34 percent of Command-R’s and 90 percent of Gemma’s, although the latter two start from small unsafe sets (12 and 13 percent of prompts) and Gemma’s reversions are almost entirely suppressed calls. Scoring ablation on the tool call itself rather than on the text gives the same picture: removing r ^ \hat{r} shifts the rate of calling a tool at all by roughly 5 to 22 points in either direction across families, while among the calls the ablated models still make, 64 to 78 percent remain unsafe (61 to 89 percent before ablation; Table 8 ).
Steering is a blunt lever:
When raising the steering coefficient, the harmful unsafe rate does decrease towards zero, but the benign tool-use rate falls accordingly (Figure 3 ). For example, for Qwen, removing nearly all unsafe calls requires a coefficient at which half of the outputs are degenerate; at the largest coherent coefficient the unsafe rate halves (0.54 to 0.24) while benign tool use falls from 0.66 to 0.48, and in Mistral and Llama the coefficient that removes nearly all unsafe calls also removes 87 to 90 percent of benign tool calls. In Gemma the largest coherent coefficient cuts unsafe calls from 0.12 to 0.04 but removes 82 percent of benign tool calls (Table 9 ). Smaller coefficients can cut unsafe calls at little cost to benign tool use (Mistral at c = 5 c{=}5 : 0.27 to 0.17, with benign tool use unchanged; Command-R at c = 49 c{=}49 : 0.12 to 0.04, although benign over-refusal rises from 0.03 to 0.18), but we did not identify any coefficient that removes nearly all unsafe calls without large collateral loss of benign tool use or coherence. Steering through the refusal direction is therefore an imprecise safety control. Across ablation (Table 8 ), patching (Table 4 ) and steering (Table 9 ), the unsafe rate among the calls a model still makes stays above 50 percent wherever the model still calls a tool on more than a tenth of prompts: the direction mainly governs whether the model acts, not how safely it acts.

6 Discussion
Text refusal does not predict tool-call safety.
We find that a model’s text
refusal rate is a poor guide to how safely it acts. Qwen3-14B refuses 75%
of harmful requests in chat yet diverges on 52% of those refused requests once
tools are available, the highest in our study and roughly five times the divergence of Command-R-7B, which
refuses at a similar rate (Table 1 ). Divergence does not track model size: the 70B Llama diverges on 23%, less than half the rate of the 14B Qwen.
The tool setting, not only pressure, drives the gap.
Comparing our two tool-enabled phrasings, adversarial stress raises divergence in Qwen ( 0.52 → 0.66 0.52\to 0.66 ) and Mistral ( 0.29 → 0.35 0.29\to 0.35 ), leaves it unchanged in Llama and Command-R and slightly lowers it in Gemma; in every case the effect is small relative to the gap already present under normal tool-enabled prompting. We also find that system prompt framing (Table 2 ) modulates the gap, sometimes strongly, but does not close it. Safety-reinforced prompts reduce divergence for every model (Gemma, 0.25 → 0.03 0.25\to 0.03 ; Llama, 0.37 → 0.11 0.37\to 0.11 ) but do not close the gap (Qwen remains at 0.39 0.39 ), and tool-encouraging prompts widen it in Mistral ( 0.25 → 0.49 0.25\to 0.49 ) and Command-R ( 0.08 → 0.18 0.08\to 0.18 ) but not in the other three. Therefore, we see that this failure is a property intrinsic to the tool call setting rather than solely a result of adversarial prompting.
A shared refusal direction, suppressed rather than replaced.
We find that the single refusal direction that Arditi et al. (2024) identifies for chat refusal governs refusal for some of the models we probe: ablation lowers harmful prompt refusal in Command-R, Mistral and Llama but raises it in Qwen and Gemma, while addition at its natural magnitude induces benign over-refusal in Qwen and Llama (Table 3 ). Moreover, the directions in the text-derived and tool-derived contexts are aligned in three of the five models (Table 5 ). In those three models, tool context lowers the magnitude of the refusal signal along the same shared axis rather than rotating it; in Command-R and Llama the tool-mode direction is substantially rotated. Hence, in those three models the part of the divergence carried by this direction reflects a suppression of the existing safety representation rather than a separate tool mode circuit, consistent with evidence that text safety alignment is often shallow and thus readily bypassed ( Qi et al., 2024 ) .
Implications for deployment.
Our behavioral results show that safety evaluations in text-only settings overestimate the safety of tool-calling agents. Furthermore, since this gap is not fully closed by safety-reinforced prompting but is instead rooted in the fundamental step of action selection, any agents bound for regulated high-stakes environments should be evaluated at the tool-call level in addition to text.
7 Future work
Precise interventions.
Steering the single refusal direction also suppresses benign tool use (in Mistral, Gemma and Llama, the largest coherent coefficient removes 82 to 90 percent of benign calls; Table 9 ), which is an issue when the aim is to deploy these agents. The intervention has to be applied only when an unsafe tool call is likely, and on layers and token positions where the refusal signal is read out instead of at a global layer scale. Such targeting could block unsafe calls while letting benign tool use through. The target is an intervention that lowers the unsafe rate among calls rather than the call rate; none of the three we tried brings it below 50 percent while the model still calls a tool on more than a tenth of prompts. Direction selection also needs the KL admissibility check of Arditi et al. (2024) : for Mistral and Gemma no candidate layer in the depth band passes it (Table 6 ).
Harder environments.
Future evaluations should move past the single emitted call and execute calls instead of only generating them to expose their real effects in a more realistic environment. Evaluations in multistep interactions would also allow harm capture across a sequence rather than a single step. Model pressure should also be applied through tool outputs and not only in the user prompt: an instruction hidden in a document or page that a tool returns is an indirect injection case ( Greshake et al., 2023 ; Zhan et al., 2024 ) that our current setup cannot produce.
8 Conclusion
We aimed to test whether safety refusal signals in language models transfer reliably from text to the tool call modality. Using a dataset of 2304 prompts across different domains, scenarios, system conditions, and modes, we have found that they do not. Models were found to refuse unsafe requests in text but would still go through with the tool call, and a model’s chat refusal rate was a poor guide to its tool-call safety when selecting actions. In some cases, the gap between text refusals and tool behavior reached 66% of requests that were refused in chat mode.
Our analysis also shows that the internal signal behind chat refusals is weakened during action selection in four of the five models, and that a weaker signal predicts an unsafe call. The tool-enabled setting appears to introduce unsafe behavior that refusal suppression alone cannot explain: restoring the suppressed signal reverts only a minority of unsafe calls in four of the five models, and mostly by removing the call rather than making it safe. Evaluating chat refusals alone overestimates the safety of agents that have access to tools, particularly in highly regulated environments.

A.4 Complied, after raising an objection
A distinct, milder version of the same failure: the model does not issue a real refusal; it hedges, states a condition, or raises a procedural quibble, but never actually declines, and the tool call proceeds regardless.
