Abstract
A benchmark is not merely a collection of test questions; it naturally induces an empirical measure over difficulty. In this work, we develop this measure-based view of benchmark evaluation. This perspective allows us to identify and formalize two central failure modes: measure mismatch , where the benchmark difficulty distribution differs from the intended difficulty distribution, and coverage gaps , where uncovered regions along the difficulty axis prevent benchmarks from detecting meaningful localized training progress. We propose two diagnostic metrics that capture these two failure modes and provide rigorous theoretical guarantees. Empirically, we analyze 25 popular benchmarks spanning 7 domains using carefully estimated item difficulties. Our results show that many benchmarks implicitly overweight certain difficulty regions, so their leaderboard conclusions should be interpreted with caution; others leave substantial portions of the difficulty axis uncovered, limiting their ability to monitor the model improvement process. Finally, we turn diagnosis into action by introducing BenchFill , a simple difficulty-targeted item rewriting algorithm that effectively reduces local coverage gaps.

1 Introduction
Benchmark evaluation looks simple: we run models on a set of questions and average the results. The resulting score is then used in two main ways: to compare models on leaderboards ( Alzahrani et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2018 ; Liang et al., 2022 ; Myrzakhan et al., 2024 ) , and to monitor progress during training ( Liu et al., 2025 ; Hu et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2025 ; Bercovich et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2025 ) . But this simplicity hides a basic issue: the conclusion depends not only on how well models perform, but also on how benchmark questions are distributed Siska et al. (2024) ; Zhang et al. (2026) .
Consider first the leaderboard setting in Fig. 1 (a), where we want to compare two models. Suppose they perform similarly on easy questions but differ substantially on hard ones. If the benchmark places most of its weight on easy questions, while the difficulty distribution of interest places more weight on harder questions, the two evaluations can lead to different conclusions for the same pair of models: the benchmark may suggest that the two models are nearly tied, even though under the desired difficulty distribution they are clearly separated. Now consider the training setting in Fig. 1 (b), where we want to track whether a model is improving. Suppose the model improves substantially over a local difficulty range. If the benchmark has few or no questions in that region, the benchmark score may barely change, even though the model has made real progress.
These two failure cases suggest that benchmark conclusions depend critically on how questions are distributed. To study this structure systematically, we organize benchmark questions along a difficulty axis, which provides a natural progression from easier to harder problems. The key conceptual step is to use difficulty as a way to define the distributional structure of the benchmark itself. This view recasts a benchmark from a collection of test questions into an empirical measure over difficulty, with each question contributing equal evaluation mass at its difficulty level.
This measure-theoretic formalization is useful because it turns the two failure cases above into precise and analyzable problems about the benchmark-induced difficulty measure. The first is a measure mismatch problem: the benchmark may distribute its mass differently from the target difficulty distribution that represents the intended evaluation objective, so benchmark conclusions can differ from the conclusions under the target difficulty distribution. The second is a coverage gap problem. Because the benchmark is an empirical measure supported only on finitely many difficulty points, there may be intervals of difficulty with little or no benchmark mass. A substantial performance gain concentrated in such an interval may therefore produce little or no change in the benchmark score. To our knowledge, this is the first work to identify and formalize measure mismatch and coverage gaps as two fundamental failure modes of benchmark evaluation.
These two problems naturally lead to two simple diagnostic metrics: the Wasserstein distance Villani and others (2009) between the benchmark measure and a target difficulty distribution, and the maximum gap between adjacent benchmark difficulty points. We provide theoretical results showing that these quantities directly characterize distributional mismatch and the detectability of localized improvements. Empirically, we analyze 25 popular benchmarks spanning 7 domains. We estimate question difficulty using Item Response Theory and compute the proposed metrics. Our results show that both issues are prevalent across benchmarks.
Our key contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We introduce a new measure-based perspective on benchmark evaluation, representing a benchmark as an empirical measure over difficulty. This perspective formalizes measure mismatch and coverage gaps, motivates two diagnostic metrics, and yields rigorous theoretical guarantees.
•
We conduct an empirical study across 25 benchmarks spanning 7 domains, showing that both difficulty-distribution mismatch and large coverage gaps are widespread in current benchmarks.
•
We propose BenchFill , a difficulty-targeted gap-filling algorithm. With only three inserted items per benchmark, BenchFill reduces the sum of the top three difficulty gaps by 21.09 % 21.09\% – 38.88 % 38.88\% .

2.1 Measure Mismatch
Benchmark-induced coupling.
The quantity D B ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta) implicitly couples two distinct objects: the model-dependent function Δ ⁡ ( z ) \Delta(z) and the benchmark-dependent measure ν B \nu_{B} . Thus, a leaderboard comparison is not merely determined by the difficulty-conditioned performance difference between the models; it also depends on how the benchmark allocates mass over the difficulty axis. If this allocation differs from the difficulty distribution of interest, the benchmark aggregates the same model difference under a mismatched measure. This mismatch can distort the observed model gap and, in severe cases, change the induced ordering.
Target distribution.
Formally, we introduce a target distribution
μ ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) , \mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]),
which represents the intended evaluation distribution over the difficulty space. This target distribution may encode the desired application mix. Under μ \mu , the target score of model θ \theta and the target performance difference between θ ′ \theta^{\prime} and θ \theta are defined as
S μ ​ ( θ ) := ∫ s θ ​ ( z ) ​ 𝑑 μ ​ ( z ) , D μ ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) := S μ ​ ( θ ′ ) − S μ ​ ( θ ) = ∫ Δ ⁡ ( z ) ​ 𝑑 μ ​ ( z ) . S_{\mu}(\theta):=\int s_{\theta}(z)\,d\mu(z),\hskip 20.00003ptD_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta):=S_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime})-S_{\mu}(\theta)=\int\Delta(z)\,d\mu(z).
The discrepancy between D B ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta) and D μ ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta) arises from the mismatch between the benchmark measure ν B \nu_{B} and the target distribution μ \mu . This viewpoint allows us to study distortions in leaderboard comparisons as a distribution mismatch problem.
A metric for distribution mismatch.
We measure how well the difficulty distribution of a benchmark matches a target difficulty distribution using the 1-Wasserstein distance. Specifically, for a benchmark B B with empirical difficulty measure ν B \nu_{B} and a target distribution μ \mu on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , we define its Difficulty Distribution Mismatch (DDM) as
DDM ⁡ ( B , μ ) := W 1 ​ ( ν B , μ ) = inf π ∈ Π ⁡ ( ν B , μ ) ∫ [ 0 , 1 ] 2 | u − v | ​ 𝑑 π ​ ( u , v ) , \mathrm{DDM}(B;\mu):=W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\nu_{B},\mu)}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|u-v|\,d\pi(u,v),
where Π ⁡ ( ν B , μ ) \Pi(\nu_{B},\mu) denotes the set of all couplings of ν B \nu_{B} and μ \mu . Intuitively, W 1 ​ ( ν B , μ ) W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu) measures how much probability mass must be transported, and how far, to transform the benchmark difficulty distribution into the target one. The following theorem shows that this mismatch directly controls the distortion between benchmark-based and target-based performance differences.
Theorem 2.1 (Distribution mismatch controls score distortion and rank stability) .
Suppose the performance difference function Δ \Delta is L L -Lipschitz. Then
| D B ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) − D μ ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) | ≤ L ​ W 1 ​ ( ν B , μ ) . \bigl|D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)-D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\bigr|\leq L\,W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu).

sign ⁡ ( D B ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) ) = sign ⁡ ( D μ ​ ( θ ′ , θ ) ) , \operatorname{sign}\bigl(D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\bigr)=\operatorname{sign}\bigl(D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\bigr),

A small W 1 ​ ( ν B , μ ) W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu) means that the benchmark provides a faithful approximation to the target evaluation, while a large W 1 ​ ( ν B , μ ) W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu) indicates that the benchmark may overemphasize some difficulty regions and underrepresent others, potentially distorting model comparisons and even changing their induced ranking. A principled target distribution without prior information.
When prior information is available, it is natural to choose μ \mu to reflect the intended evaluation objective over the difficulty space. In many settings, however, such prior knowledge is unavailable. In that case, choosing a non-uniform target would implicitly privilege some difficulty regions over others without evidence. To characterize a principled default choice, we consider an agnostic setting in which the location of the performance difference along the difficulty axis is completely unknown. We consider the class
ℱ ρ := { Δ : [ 0 , 1 ] → [ − 1 , 1 ] measurable : ∫ 0 1 Δ ( z ) d z = ρ } , ρ ∈ ( − 1 , 1 ) . \mathcal{F}_{\rho}:=\left\{\Delta:[0,1]\to[-1,1]\ \text{measurable}:\int_{0}^{1}\Delta(z)\,dz=\rho\right\},\hskip 20.00003pt\rho\in(-1,1).
This class encodes an uncertainty set: the signed performance difference has a fixed average level ρ \rho over the difficulty axis, but its location is otherwise unrestricted.
For any candidate target distribution μ ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) \mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]) , define its worst-case retained difference by
V ρ ​ ( μ ) := inf Δ ∈ ℱ ρ ∫ Δ ⁡ ( z ) ​ 𝑑 μ ​ ( z ) . V_{\rho}(\mu):=\inf_{\Delta\in\mathcal{F}_{\rho}}\int\Delta(z)\,d\mu(z).
The following theorem shows that, under complete uncertainty about where along the difficulty axis the performance difference occurs, the uniform distribution is the unique maximin target, and is the only choice whose worst-case retained difference remains equal to the average difference ρ \rho .
Theorem 2.2 (Uniform target as the unique maximin choice) .
For ρ ∈ ( − 1 , 1 ) \rho\in(-1,1) , we have
sup μ ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) V ρ ​ ( μ ) = ρ , \sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])}V_{\rho}(\mu)=\rho,
and the unique maximizer is the uniform distribution λ = Unif ⁡ [ 0 , 1 ] \lambda=\mathrm{Unif}[0,1] .
Theorem 2.2 does not claim that the uniform distribution is universally optimal. Rather, it justifies the uniform target as a principled default when no prior information over the difficulty space is available. In that case, adopting the uniform target avoids introducing an additional preference over difficulty regions and provides a neutrality criterion for benchmark evaluation: the evaluation does not privilege any difficulty region a priori. Full proof is provided in App. B.1 .
2.2 Coverage Gaps
Discrete benchmark measures may fail to monitor local training improvements.
Benchmarks are often used not only for final model comparison, but also to monitor training progress ( He et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2025 ) . However, training improvements are often local rather than uniform: a model may improve substantially over a particular difficulty range while improving only modestly elsewhere.
Recall that a benchmark score is an integral with respect to the benchmark empirical measure ν B \nu_{B} . Since ν B \nu_{B} is a discrete measure supported only on the finitely many benchmark difficulty points, it assigns zero mass to every interval that contains no benchmark item. Consequently, even if the model improves substantially on a nontrivial contiguous difficulty region, the benchmark score may not reflect this improvement when the region contains no benchmark item. In this sense, a benchmark can fail as a progress monitor when its local resolution is insufficient.
Maximum gap.
We first characterize the local resolution of a benchmark through its maximum gap . Let 0 ≤ z ( 1 ) ≤ ⋯ ≤ z ( n ) ≤ 1 0\leq z_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq z_{(n)}\leq 1 be the ordered benchmark difficulty points, and define z ( 0 ) := 0 z_{(0)}:=0 and z ( n + 1 ) := 1 z_{(n+1)}:=1 . The maximum gap of benchmark B B is
G ⁡ ( B ) := max 0 ≤ j ≤ n ⁡ ( z ( j + 1 ) − z ( j ) ) G(B):=\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\bigl(z_{(j+1)}-z_{(j)}\bigr)
(1)
i.e., the length of the largest interval on the difficulty axis that contains no benchmark point.
A hypothesis testing formulation.
To formalize whether a benchmark can reliably detect localized training improvements, we cast the problem as a hypothesis test. Under the null hypothesis, there is no improvement. Under the alternative, the post-improvement performance exceeds the pre-improvement performance by at least a a over some contiguous difficulty interval of width w w . The following theorem shows that the benchmark maximum gap G ⁡ ( B ) G(B) is the exact asymptotic minimax detection boundary.

Theorem 2.3 shows that the discrete geometry of the benchmark induces a sharp detectability phase transition. If the width of a local improvement does not exceed the maximum adjacent difficulty gap, then no level- α \alpha test can achieve nontrivial worst-case power, regardless of how many repeated evaluations are performed. In other words, such an improvement can be completely invisible to the benchmark in the worst case. Once the improvement width exceeds the maximum gap, repeated evaluation can eventually make the signal detectable. In this sense, G ⁡ ( B ) G(B) characterizes the intrinsic local resolution limit of benchmark B B .

3.1 Difficulty Estimation and Metrics Calculation
Method: Item Response Theory.
To estimate benchmark item difficulty, one may be tempted to use each item’s average failure rate across models ( Yu et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2026 ; Wen et al., 2025 ) . However, item difficulty is inherently relative to model ability: it should depend not only on how many models fail an item, but also on how capable the models that fail or solve it are.
We therefore use Item Response Theory (IRT), an established psychometric framework ( Lord, 1952 ; Rasch, 1993 ) that has been widely adopted for difficulty estimation in LLM benchmark analysis ( Ding et al., 2024 ; Rodriguez et al., 2021 ; Hofmann et al., 2025 ; Kipnis et al., 2024 ; Polo et al., 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2026 ) . IRT models the probability that a model solves an item as a function of both model ability and item difficulty. In the commonly used one-parameter logistic model (1PL), each model j j is assigned an ability parameter θ j \theta_{j} , and each item i i is assigned a difficulty parameter b i b_{i} . The probability that model j j correctly solves item i i is modeled as
Pr ⁡ ( Y i ​ j = 1 ) = σ ⁡ ( θ j − b i ) , \Pr(Y_{ij}=1)=\sigma(\theta_{j}-b_{i}),
where σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) is the logistic function. Intuitively, items with larger b i b_{i} require higher model ability to be solved correctly, and the estimated b i b_{i} therefore provides a natural measure of item difficulty. For multiple-choice benchmarks, the model above can be naturally extended with a guessing parameter.
In practice, difficulty estimation begins by collecting the performance of multiple models on benchmark items, forming a response matrix Y ∈ ℝ n × m Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m} , where Y i ​ j Y_{ij} denotes the empirical success rate of model j j on item i i . The latent abilities { θ j } j = 1 m \{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m} and item difficulties { b i } i = 1 n \{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n} are then estimated by fitting the likelihood induced by the IRT model.
To ensure reliable difficulty estimation, we take several practical measures, including careful model selection and rigorous IRT fitting diagnostics .
Model selection.
Accurate difficulty estimation requires models with diverse capability levels. To ensure sufficient ability coverage, we deliberately select models spanning a wide range of capabilities. For non-agentic benchmarks, we select 12 12 LLMs, ranging from 1.7 1.7 B to 397 397 B parameters, using the public leaderboard from Text Arena 1 1
1
https://arena.ai/leaderboard . Models are first ordered by their overall capability aggregated across multiple domains, then divided into several approximately equal intervals along this ranking. We perform stratified sampling by selecting one model from each interval and additionally include a few models from both the top and bottom of the ranking to better capture the extremes of the capability spectrum. Each selected model is evaluated on each benchmark item with up to 32 32 rollouts using the model-recommended temperature and top- p p . For agentic benchmarks, tasks typically require strong proprietary models. We therefore rely on the evaluation results from HAL Kapoor et al. (2026) , which reports the performance of 15 15 – 66 66 state-of-the-art closed-source LLMs on agentic task benchmarks. Detailed information about the models is provided in App. C.2 .
IRT fitting and diagnostics.
We estimate the IRT parameters using Bayesian inference via MCMC. To ensure reliable difficulty estimation, we perform a series of diagnostics both before and after IRT fitting. Before fitting, we examine the informativeness and structural consistency of the response matrix using 4 4 metrics. After fitting, we assess convergence and sampling quality using 3 3 standard Bayesian diagnostics, including the Gelman–Rubin statistic ( R ^ \hat{R} ), effective sample size (ESS), and the number of divergent transitions. All benchmarks pass all 7 7 diagnostic checks.
For non-agentic benchmarks, we further conduct a leave-one-out (LOO) robustness analysis. Results suggest that our choice of 12 12 models is sufficient for stable difficulty estimation. To support future work, we also publicly release the carefully curated difficulty estimates for the 25 25 benchmarks 2 2

Proposed metric calculation.
For each benchmark, after estimating item difficulties, we first normalize them to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] and compute their empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF). Since in general no additional prior information over the difficulty space is available, we quantify distribution mismatch by computing the 1-Wasserstein distance between the benchmark empirical measure and a same-size empirical uniform reference on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . In one dimension, this is equivalently the L 1 L_{1} distance between their ECDFs. We compute the maximum gap according to Eq. ( 1 ) and also report the sum of the five largest adjacent gaps as a reference metric. We provide posterior uncertainty analyses for both metrics in App. C.6 .
3.2 Results and Analysis
Several benchmarks exhibit large deviations from the neutral uniform target.
Tab. 1 shows that 10 out of 25 benchmarks have DDM ≥ 0.10 \mathrm{DDM}\geq 0.10 , indicating that their empirical difficulty distributions must be transported by an average distance of at least 10% of the full difficulty range to match the same-size uniform reference. These benchmarks therefore encode a strong implicit difficulty weighting: their aggregate scores are more sensitive to overrepresented difficulty regions and less sensitive to underrepresented ones. Non-uniformity is not inherently problematic when it matches the intended evaluation objective. However, when no such objective is specified, high-DDM leaderboard results should not be interpreted as difficulty-neutral comparisons. Instead, they reflect comparisons under the benchmark’s empirical difficulty weighting, so users should check whether this weighting matches the difficulty distribution relevant to their use case. App. D presents a simple benchmark calibration algorithm for moving a benchmark distribution closer to a chosen target.
Many benchmarks exhibit large local coverage gaps.
Tab. 1 shows that 8 out of 25 benchmarks have Max ​ Gap ≥ 0.10 \mathrm{Max\ Gap}\geq 0.10 . As a result, a training run may make meaningful local progress in an uncovered difficulty region, while the aggregate benchmark score remains nearly unchanged. This can cause useful training progress to be underestimated, promising runs to be prematurely discarded, and compute to be misallocated. Moreover, the problem is not limited to the single largest gap. We find that 9 out of 25 benchmarks have Top ​ - ​ 5 ​ Sum ≥ 0.30 \mathrm{Top\text{-}5\ Sum}\geq 0.30 . For example, Fig. 3 shows that AIME24 and AIME25 have top-5 gap sums close to 0.5 0.5 , leaving large portions of the difficulty axis locally unresolved despite their widespread use for monitoring RLVR training Luo et al. (2025) ; Liu et al. (2025) ; Guo et al. (2025) ; Yue et al. (2025) .
We note that although Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} intrinsically depends on benchmark size, this reflects a real coverage limitation rather than merely a nuisance effect: even if a large gap is expected for a small benchmark, it is still an uncovered difficulty interval, and by Theorem 2.3 , localized improvements inside such an interval may be undetectable in the worst case. Importantly, we find that coverage gaps are not merely a small-benchmark artifact: benchmarks with hundreds of items, such as HumanEval and BBH-5 objects, also exhibit substantial gap metrics. This motivates gap filling as a practical and necessary way to improve local benchmark resolution.

4 BenchFill : Difficulty-Targeted Gap Filling
Figure 4: Overview of BenchFill .
BenchFill iteratively rewrites parent items into child variants, annotates their difficulties, and updates the memory to guide the next round of gap filling.
4.1 Problem Setting and Method
Problem setting.
Let B = { x i } i = 1 n B=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n} be the current benchmark, where each item x i x_{i} has an estimated difficulty z i z_{i} . We sort the difficulties and consider an adjacent gap [ z L , z R ] [z_{L},z_{R}] between two benchmark items. Reducing such gaps improves a benchmark’s local resolution and its ability to capture localized model improvements. Thus, our goal is to obtain a valid new item x new x_{\mathrm{new}} whose difficulty is as close as possible to the target difficulty z ⋆ = ( z L + z R ) / 2 z^{\star}=(z_{L}+z_{R})/2 , the midpoint that most reduces the gap under a single insertion. The new item is added to the benchmark rather than replacing any existing item. By default, we apply this procedure to the largest gap of the benchmark.
One direct approach is to ask human experts to design new items at the target difficulty, but this process can be costly Rein (2024) ; Phan et al. (2025) . Motivated by recent work on LLM-assisted benchmark augmentation Cheng et al. (2026) ; Li et al. (2025) ; Ying et al. (2024) ; Xia et al. (2024) , we instead use LLMs to rewrite existing benchmark items into new variants at targeted difficulty levels. This rewriting-based approach is scalable, uses existing items as local references for controlled difficulty adjustment, and helps preserve the format and style of the original benchmark. To ensure item quality, the final selected items are further validated by human experts. We next introduce BenchFill , a rewrite-and-annotate evolutionary search algorithm ( Sharma, ; Liu et al., 2026 ) for automated difficulty-targeted item rewriting. Alg. 2 gives the pseudocode, and Fig. 4 illustrates the procedure.
BenchFill .
Given a target difficulty z ⋆ z^{\star} , our algorithm maintains a memory ℳ \mathcal{M} of annotated item–difficulty pairs. The memory is initialized with existing benchmark items whose difficulties are closest to the selected gap, and is later updated during the search. Each round follows a parent–child rewriting step. A parent item is an existing or previously rewritten item selected from ℳ \mathcal{M} as the main reference for rewriting, while a child item is a newly proposed variant derived from a parent.
At round t t , we retrieve a small set of parent items from ℳ \mathcal{M} whose annotated difficulties are closest to the target difficulty z ⋆ z^{\star} . For each parent item, the rewriter LLM is conditioned on the parent, the target difficulty, and a reference list of nearby item–difficulty pairs. The reference list provides local examples around z ⋆ z^{\star} , typically including items slightly below and above the target difficulty, e.g., items with difficulties 0.48 , 0.50 , 0.60 0.48,0.50,0.60 , and 0.62 0.62 when z ⋆ = 0.55 z^{\star}=0.55 . The rewriter then rewrites each parent into a batch of child items while preserving the format and style of the benchmark.
After rewriting, each child item is passed to the difficulty annotator module, detailed below, which returns an estimated difficulty z ^ \hat{z} . We keep only valid 3 3

This update makes the search adaptive: whenever a rewritten child is closer to z ⋆ z^{\star} than previous items, it becomes more likely to be retrieved as a parent or included in the reference list in later rounds. The rewriter is therefore conditioned on increasingly target-relevant examples as the search progresses, rather than rewriting each batch independently. After T T rounds, we select the valid child item minimizing | z ^ − z ⋆ | |\hat{z}-z^{\star}| as the proposed gap-filling item. Before insertion into the benchmark, the final item is subject to careful manual review by human experts.

4.2 Experimental Setup and Results
Experimental setup.
We perform gap filling on 8 representative benchmarks from diverse domains. For each benchmark, we target the three most pronounced coverage gaps. We use MiniMax-M2.7 Chen et al. (2026) as the rewriter LLM, with temperature set to 1.0 1.0 . The search runs for at most 8 rounds; in each round, we retrieve 4 parent items, rewrite each parent into 4 child items, and provide a reference list of at most 16 nearby item–difficulty pairs. As a baseline, we use a budget-matched best-of- N N uniform-parent rewriting variant. Additional implementation details are provided in App. E.1 .

4.3 Curated Gap-Filled Benchmarks
To provide a community resource for more reliable evaluation of future models and training methods, we further apply BenchFill to construct gap-filled variants of four widely used math benchmarks with substantial coverage gaps: AMC23, AIME24, AIME25, and AIME26. We expand these benchmarks from 40, 30, 30, and 30 items to 80, 50, 52, and 53 items, respectively. All generated items are manually reviewed, and low-quality or incorrect items are discarded. We release the resulting variants as AMC23-BenchFill , AIME24-BenchFill , AIME25-BenchFill , and AIME26-BenchFill 5 5

https://huggingface.co/datasets/Yifan99/BenchFill . As shown in Fig. 5 , these variants substantially reduce the largest original coverage gaps and bring the empirical difficulty distributions closer to a uniform distribution, providing more comprehensive coverage across the difficulty spectrum.
5 Related Work
Science of benchmarking.
A recent line of work has shifted attention from improving leaderboard scores to examining whether benchmark scores themselves provide reliable scientific evidence. Beyond data contamination, where benchmark test items appear in model training data and potentially inflate evaluation scores Oren et al. (2024) ; Sun et al. (2025) ; Zhu et al. (2024) , prior studies show that benchmark results can be sensitive to decoding, prompting, and implementation details Hochlehnert et al. (2025) , and brittle to surface wording or dataset flaws Mousavi et al. (2026) . Other work studies evaluation reliability through the lens of signal and noise Heineman et al. (2025) , or proposes broader benchmark-quality criteria and shows that widely used benchmarks vary substantially in quality Qian et al. (2026) . Our work complements these efforts by formulating a benchmark as an empirical measure over difficulty, enabling us to rigorously formalize measure mismatch and coverage gaps.
IRT and its applications.
IRT originated in psychometrics as a framework for educational and psychological measurement Embretson and Reise (2013) ; Lord (2012) . In NLP, Lalor et al. (2016) introduced IRT to system evaluation, and Rodriguez et al. (2021) extended this perspective to leaderboard analysis. IRT has since become a widely used foundation for difficulty-aware evaluation in LLM benchmarking. Easy2Hard-Bench Ding et al. (2024) uses IRT to assign standardized continuous difficulty labels to benchmark items; it also validates these labels against human pairwise judgments and reports stronger alignment than GPT-4-based difficulty annotators. Zhou et al. (2026) uses an IRT-based framework to analyze the measurement quality of existing LLM benchmarks.
6 Conclusion
In this work, we view each benchmark as an empirical measure over difficulty. This perspective enables us to formalize measure mismatch and coverage gaps, leading to theoretical results and practical diagnostics.
We believe this empirical-measure view has the potential to open new ways to rigorously study and rethink benchmark evaluation.

Appendix A Discussion
A.1 Broader Impacts
This work aims to improve the reliability and transparency of benchmark evaluation. By diagnosing distribution mismatch and coverage gaps, our methods can help researchers better understand when benchmark scores provide faithful evidence for model comparison and monitoring training progress. More reliable evaluation may support more transparent model comparison and more informed benchmark design. Since this work focuses on benchmark analysis rather than model deployment or data collection, we do not foresee direct negative societal impacts.
A.2 Limitations and Future Work
This work develops a measure-theoretic view of benchmark evaluation and uses it to characterize two central issues: measure mismatch and coverage gaps. Our goal is not to exhaust all consequences of this viewpoint, but to show that benchmark evaluation can be studied through the geometry of the empirical difficulty measure induced by the benchmark. We believe this perspective opens a broader direction for studying benchmark evaluation, where additional benchmark failures and design objectives can be formulated as properties of the induced difficulty measure.
Moreover, our current framework is primarily post-hoc: given an existing benchmark, we estimate its difficulty distribution, diagnose measure mismatch and coverage gaps, and reduce pronounced gaps through targeted item insertion. A natural next step is to move this perspective into the benchmark construction stage itself. Rather than auditing mismatch and coverage only after a benchmark has been built, benchmark developers can actively design the induced difficulty structure from the beginning, aiming to align the benchmark measure with the intended evaluation objective while maintaining high local resolution along the difficulty axis. We view this as a promising direction for difficulty-aware benchmark design.
A.3 Extended Related Work
Difficulty estimation.
Prior work often derives difficulty labels from human judgment or benchmark metadata. For example, MATH [ 18 ] annotates problems with five difficulty levels, GPQA [ 51 ] collects post-hoc expert difficulty ratings, and LingOly [ 5 ] includes five levels of human difficulty. Such labels are costly to obtain at scale, and they primarily reflect human-perceived difficulty rather than the difficulty exhibited by current LLMs on the questions. Recent work has explored alternative estimation strategies. [ 77 ] estimate LLM-perceived question difficulty from hidden representations. [ 4 ] ask an LLM to make pairwise difficulty judgments and aggregate them with Bradley–Terry scores. EIP [ 73 ] instead jointly infers question difficulty and model competency from observed successes and failures on a model–question bipartite graph. Complementarily, LoRe [ 71 ] studies intrinsic problem complexity, defined by the minimum number of computational steps required to solve a problem.
Among these approaches, IRT remains a common foundation for difficulty-aware evaluation because it provides a principled probabilistic framework for jointly modeling item difficulty and model ability directly from response data [ 9 ] .

C.3 IRT Fitting and Diagnostics
To obtain reliable difficulty estimates, we conduct several diagnostic checks on both the response matrices and the IRT fitting procedure.
Response matrix diagnostics.
We examine each response matrix using four diagnostics. The first two, model information and item information , measure whether the matrix contains enough discriminative signal along the model and item dimensions [ 3 ] .
The latter two, model inversion rate and item inversion rate , measure how well the empirical response matrix agrees with the monotone structure implied by 1PL-IRT [ 57 , 58 ] .
Formally, for each item–model pair ( i , j ) (i,j) , we aggregate the observed responses into the empirical success rate of model j j on item i i , defined as p i ​ j = n i ​ j correct / n i ​ j trials p_{ij}=n_{ij}^{\mathrm{correct}}/n_{ij}^{\mathrm{trials}} . We then aggregate these success rates along the model and item dimensions:
p j = 1 | ℐ | ​ ∑ i ∈ ℐ p i ​ j , p i = 1 | 𝒥 | ​ ∑ j ∈ 𝒥 p i ​ j , p_{j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{ij},\hskip 18.49988ptp_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}p_{ij},
where ℐ \mathcal{I} and 𝒥 \mathcal{J} denote the sets of items and models, respectively. We define model information as the average of 4 ​ p j ​ ( 1 − p j ) 4p_{j}(1-p_{j}) over models, and item information as the average of 4 ​ p i ​ ( 1 − p i ) 4p_{i}(1-p_{i}) over items. The intuition is that if many models solve nearly all items or almost none of them, then their aggregate success rates are saturated and contribute limited information for distinguishing model abilities. The same holds when many items are solved by nearly all models or by almost none. In contrast, aggregate success rates near 0.5 0.5 are more informative because they correspond to models or items that are neither saturated nor degenerate. The factor 4 4 rescales this quantity to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] .
To evaluate whether the response matrix is consistent with the ordering implied by 1PL-IRT, we first rank models by overall success rate and items by overall solve rate. We then compute two inversion rates. The model inversion rate is the fraction of ordered model pairs, evaluated item by item, for which a stronger model performs worse than a weaker one. The item inversion rate is the fraction of ordered item pairs, evaluated model by model, for which an easier item is solved less often than a harder one. Lower inversion rates indicate better agreement with the latent ordering assumed by IRT.
In our experiments, we use model information ≥ 0.3 \geq 0.3 , item information ≥ 0.3 \geq 0.3 , model inversion rate ≤ 0.3 \leq 0.3 , and item inversion rate ≤ 0.3 \leq 0.3 as basic thresholds. Passing these checks provides evidence that the matrix contains sufficient signal and is reasonably consistent with the ordering assumptions of IRT.
IRT fitting.
We fit the 1PL-IRT model using Bayesian inference. For multiple-choice benchmarks, we additionally include a guessing parameter:
Pr ⁡ ( Y i ​ j = 1 ) = g i + ( 1 − g i ) ​ σ ​ ( θ j − b i ) , \Pr(Y_{ij}=1)=g_{i}+(1-g_{i})\sigma(\theta_{j}-b_{i}),
where g i g_{i} denotes the chance-level probability of answering item i i correctly by guessing.
Model abilities θ j \theta_{j} and item difficulties b i b_{i} are assigned independent Gaussian priors,
θ j ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) , b i ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) . \theta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\hskip 18.49988ptb_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
Because each model–item pair is evaluated multiple times, we use a binomial likelihood to model the number of correct responses. Posterior inference is performed using MCMC with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) implemented in PyMC [ 1 ] . We run four chains with 1,000 warmup iterations and 1,000 posterior samples per chain. We set the target acceptance rate to 0.95.
We also explored alternative prior structures to assess robustness. In particular, we considered a hierarchical prior for model abilities,
θ j ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( μ θ , σ θ 2 ) , μ θ ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) , σ θ ∼ HalfNormal ​ ( 1 ) , \theta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}),\hskip 18.49988pt\mu_{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\hskip 9.24994pt\sigma_{\theta}\sim\text{HalfNormal}(1),
while keeping item difficulties under the same Gaussian prior as above. We additionally tested a hierarchical prior for item difficulties,
b i ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( μ b , σ b 2 ) , μ b ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) , σ b ∼ HalfNormal ​ ( 1 ) , b_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\sigma_{b}^{2}),\hskip 18.49988pt\mu_{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\hskip 9.24994pt\sigma_{b}\sim\text{HalfNormal}(1),
with model abilities using the standard Gaussian prior. Across these variants, the estimated item difficulties and model abilities were highly consistent. Therefore, we report results using the simplest specification with independent 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathcal{N}(0,1) priors.
IRT diagnostics.
To assess convergence and sampling quality, we monitor 3 3 standard MCMC diagnostics: the maximum Gelman–Rubin statistic R ^ \hat{R} across parameters, the minimum bulk effective sample size (ESS), and the number of divergent transitions. The R ^ \hat{R} statistic measures agreement across chains, with values close to 1 indicating good convergence. Bulk ESS quantifies the effective number of independent posterior samples in the main mass of the distribution, with larger values indicating more reliable estimation. Divergent transitions indicate potential numerical or geometric pathologies during NUTS sampling, and zero divergent transitions are preferred.

C.4 Metric Calculation Details
For a benchmark with n n items, we compare its normalized difficulty points (min-max normalization) to a same-size empirical uniform reference on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . Let
z ( 1 ) ≤ ⋯ ≤ z ( n ) z_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq z_{(n)} denote the sorted normalized difficulties. We define the uniform reference points as the midpoints of n n equal-width bins:
u i = i − 1 2 n , i = 1 , … , n . u_{i}=\frac{i-\frac{1}{2}}{n},\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\ldots,n.
The DDM \mathrm{DDM} is then computed as
DDM ⁡ ( B ) = 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n | z ( i ) − u i | . \mathrm{DDM}(B)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|z_{(i)}-u_{i}|.
This is the one-dimensional 1-Wasserstein distance between the benchmark empirical measure and the same-size empirical uniform reference
λ n = 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n δ u i . \lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{u_{i}}.
For the gap-based metric, let
z ( 0 ) = 0 z_{(0)}=0 and z ( n + 1 ) = 1 z_{(n+1)}=1 . The maximum gap is
G ⁡ ( B ) = max 0 ≤ i ≤ n ⁡ ( z ( i + 1 ) − z ( i ) ) . G(B)=\max_{0\leq i\leq n}\bigl(z_{(i+1)}-z_{(i)}\bigr).
C.5 Model Comparison for Different Difficulty Estimation Models

C.6 Posterior Uncertainty Analysis
We further quantify how posterior uncertainty in the fitted IRT difficulty estimates affects our calculated metrics. For each posterior draw of the fitted 1PL item difficulties, we recompute DDM \mathrm{DDM} and Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} , and summarize the posterior variability of these metrics across draws.
DDM \mathrm{DDM} is stable under IRT posterior uncertainty.
Across the 25 benchmarks, the posterior standard deviation of DDM \mathrm{DDM} is typically about 0.011, with range 0.006–0.020. This is small relative to the DDM \mathrm{DDM} point estimates, corresponding to approximately 8%–12% in typical cases. Thus, the DDM \mathrm{DDM} values reported in the main paper are not artifacts of difficulty-estimation noise.
Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} uncertainty is larger but the coverage-gap conclusion remains robust.
Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} has larger posterior uncertainty, as expected, because it is an extreme-order statistic [ 11 ] : it is defined by the largest adjacent spacing between item difficulties and is therefore more sensitive to local posterior perturbations. Its posterior standard deviation ranges from 0.017 to 0.041. However, the qualitative coverage-gap conclusion remains robust. To verify this, we compare the observed Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} against a same-size uniform-sampling baseline under posterior difficulty draws. Across benchmarks, the posterior probability that the observed Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} exceeds the same-size uniform baseline has median 0.999. The observed-to-baseline ratio has median 3.85 × 3.85\times , and is much larger for several large benchmarks, e.g., 18.8 × 18.8\times on ScienceQA, 11.3 × 11.3\times on MedQA, and 5.9 × 5.9\times on ZebraLogic. Therefore, although the exact Max ​ Gap \mathrm{Max\ Gap} magnitude carries posterior uncertainty, the conclusion that many benchmarks contain coverage gaps far beyond random uniform sampling is robust.

Appendix D Benchmark Calibration
Problem setting.
The measure-mismatch perspective suggests a natural way to redesign a benchmark: adjust the benchmark empirical measure so that it better matches a target evaluation distribution. Consider a benchmark
B = { x 1 , … , x n } , B=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\},
where each item x i x_{i} has an estimated difficulty z i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] z_{i}\in[0,1] . The benchmark induces the empirical measure
ν B = 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n δ z i \nu_{B}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{z_{i}}
over the difficulty axis. Given a target distribution μ ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) \mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]) , benchmark calibration aims to construct a new benchmark B ′ B^{\prime} whose empirical measure ν B ′ \nu_{B^{\prime}} is closer to μ \mu than ν B \nu_{B} is.
In this work, we instantiate benchmark calibration through pruning. Specifically, we retain k k items from the original benchmark by selecting a subset A ⊆ [ n ] A\subseteq[n] with | A | = k |A|=k . The selected items form a calibrated benchmark
B A = { x i : i ∈ A } , B_{A}=\{x_{i}:i\in A\},
with empirical difficulty measure
ν A = 1 k ​ ∑ i ∈ A δ z i . \nu_{A}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in A}\delta_{z_{i}}.
The goal is to choose A A so that ν A \nu_{A} better approximates the target distribution μ \mu , for example as measured by DDM ⁡ ( B A , μ ) = W 1 ​ ( ν A , μ ) \mathrm{DDM}(B_{A};\mu)=W_{1}(\nu_{A},\mu) .
A simple calibration algorithm.
We use a simple greedy quantile-matching pruning procedure, summarized in Alg. 1 . Let Q μ Q_{\mu} denote the quantile function of the target distribution μ \mu . Given a subset size k k , we first construct k k target difficulty locations by taking the midpoints of k k equal-probability intervals under μ \mu :
τ t = t − 1 2 k , q t = Q μ ( τ t ) , t = 1 , … , k . \tau_{t}=\frac{t-\frac{1}{2}}{k},\hskip 18.49988ptq_{t}=Q_{\mu}(\tau_{t}),\hskip 18.49988ptt=1,\ldots,k.
We then match these target locations to existing benchmark items. Specifically, after normalizing item difficulties to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , we iterate over q 1 , … , q k q_{1},\ldots,q_{k} and select the unused item whose difficulty is closest to the current target location:
i t ∈ arg ⁡ min i ∈ [ n ] ∖ { i 1 , … , i t − 1 } ​ | z i − q t | . i_{t}\in\arg\min_{i\in[n]\setminus\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1}\}}|z_{i}-q_{t}|.
The calibrated subset is A = { i 1 , … , i k } A=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\} .

D.1 Experimental Setup
In practice, given any target distribution over the difficulty axis, our benchmark calibration idea and Alg. 1 can be applied directly. In this experiment, we study whether such calibration can make a single benchmark produce model rankings that are more consistent with a broader in-domain benchmark family.
We consider N = 8 N=8 math benchmarks, as
shown in Tab. 3 . For each benchmark B i B_{i} , we treat it as the source benchmark to be calibrated, and use the remaining benchmarks { B j : j ≠ i } \{B_{j}:j\neq i\} only to construct an external reference ranking over models. This leave-one-out design evaluates whether the ranking induced by a calibrated version of B i B_{i} better agrees with the ranking obtained from other benchmarks in the same domain.
Concretely, let
B i = { x i ​ 1 , … , x i ​ n i } B_{i}=\{x_{i1},\ldots,x_{in_{i}}\}
be the i i -th benchmark, where item x i ​ ℓ x_{i\ell} has normalized difficulty z i ​ ℓ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] z_{i\ell}\in[0,1] . Since the leave-one-out reference ranking is aggregated from multiple benchmarks in the same domain, it is less tied to the idiosyncratic difficulty concentration of any single source benchmark. We therefore use calibration to prune items from difficulty regions where the source benchmark is overly concentrated. In the absence of an application-specific target distribution, we instantiate the calibration target as the uniform distribution
λ = Unif ⁡ [ 0 , 1 ] . \lambda=\mathrm{Unif}[0,1].
This choice should not be interpreted as assuming that the leave-one-out reference distribution is exactly uniform. Rather, uniform calibration provides a neutral debiasing strategy: it does not privilege any difficulty region a priori, and it counteracts benchmarks whose empirical mass is concentrated in a narrow portion of the difficulty axis.
We apply Alg. 1 to B i B_{i} with target distribution λ \lambda , producing a calibrated subset A i ⊆ [ n i ] A_{i}\subseteq[n_{i}] of size k i k_{i} . The calibrated benchmark is
B i , A i = { x i ​ ℓ : ℓ ∈ A i } . B_{i,A_{i}}=\{x_{i\ell}:\ell\in A_{i}\}.
Suppose all benchmarks evaluate the same M M models. Let S i ​ ( m ) S_{i}(m) denote the average score of model m m on the full source benchmark B i B_{i} , and let S i ​ ( A i , m ) S_{i}(A_{i},m) denote its average score on the calibrated subset. The uncalibrated ranking is induced by
S i ​ ( m ) , m = 1 , … , M , S_{i}(m),\hskip 18.49988ptm=1,\ldots,M,
while the calibrated ranking is induced by
S i ​ ( A i , m ) , m = 1 , … , M . S_{i}(A_{i},m),\hskip 18.49988ptm=1,\ldots,M.
For the leave-one-out reference ranking, we aggregate model scores over the remaining benchmarks:
S − i ref ​ ( m ) = 1 N − 1 ​ ∑ j ≠ i S j ​ ( m ) . S^{\mathrm{ref}}_{-i}(m)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}S_{j}(m).
The ranking induced by S − i ref ​ ( m ) S^{\mathrm{ref}}_{-i}(m) serves as the external reference ranking for source benchmark B i B_{i} .
For each source benchmark, we compute the Spearman and Kendall correlations between the source-induced ranking and the leave-one-out reference ranking, both before and after calibration. Improved correlation after calibration suggests that pruning the source benchmark toward a more balanced difficulty distribution makes its model comparison more consistent with the broader in-domain benchmark family.

D.2 Results
Experimental results are reported in Tab. 11 . The results show that our calibration algorithm improves the agreement between the source benchmark ranking and the leave-one-out reference ranking. On average, calibration improves Spearman correlation from 0.890 0.890 to 0.930 0.930 , and Kendall correlation from 0.802 0.802 to 0.848 0.848 . The improvement is especially pronounced on benchmarks with weaker initial agreement. Notably, on Minerva-Math, the full benchmark achieves only 0.601 0.601 Spearman and 0.455 0.455 Kendall correlation with the reference ranking, while calibration improves them to 0.790 0.790 and 0.667 0.667 , respectively. Similarly, on Gaokao2023en, calibration improves Spearman from 0.888 0.888 to 0.951 0.951 and Kendall from 0.788 0.788 to 0.879 0.879 . For the remaining benchmarks, the initial agreement is already high, leaving less room for improvement; nevertheless, calibration consistently preserves this high level of agreement while using the pruned benchmark.
We include a random pruning baseline that uses the same pruning size as our calibrated subset and average its results over 30 random trials. Random pruning is less effective than calibration: it achieves 0.895 0.895 average Spearman and 0.801 0.801 average Kendall correlation, both below the calibrated subset. We also report W 1 ​ ( ν , Unif ) W_{1}(\nu,\mathrm{Unif}) as a mechanism metric. Calibration consistently reduces this distance across all benchmarks, decreasing the average value from 0.096 0.096 to 0.028 0.028 . In contrast, random pruning does not reduce this distance on average, yielding an average value of 0.109 0.109 . This suggests that the improvement is not simply due to using a smaller benchmark, but comes from selecting items whose empirical difficulty measure better matches the neutral calibration target.

E.1 Implementation Details
For each benchmark, we focus on the three most pronounced gaps in its estimated difficulty distribution and rewrite one item for each gap. Since an LLM may not reliably distinguish small numerical differences on the normalized [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] difficulty scale when the target difficulty is provided in the prompt, we rescale difficulty values to [ 0 , 10 ] [0,10] for prompting while keeping all reported metrics on the original normalized scale.
For the reference list, we include nearby item–difficulty pairs on both sides of the target difficulty. Specifically, half of the reference items are selected from those with difficulty below the target and closest to it, and the other half are selected from those with difficulty above the target and closest to it. This provides the rewriter with local bidirectional context around the desired difficulty level. The full rewriter prompt template is provided below.
For difficulty annotation, we use the same set of LLMs and the same generation configuration as in the original difficulty estimation stage. The search stops early if the best valid item found so far is within tolerance τ = 0.01 \tau=0.01 of the target difficulty on the normalized [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] scale, or otherwise stops after the maximum number of rounds. We then select the valid item whose annotated difficulty is closest to the target. Before being inserted into the benchmark, each selected item undergoes a manual validity check by at least three human experts, who verify its correctness.
We additionally implement a budget-matched best-of- N N uniform-parent rewriting baseline. For each target gap g g , let T g T_{g} denote the number of rounds actually run by BenchFill before stopping. Since BenchFill rewrites 4 4 parents per round and produces 4 4 child items per parent, its proposal budget for this gap is T g × 4 × 4 = 16 ​ T g T_{g}\times 4\times 4=16T_{g} child items. To match this budget, the baseline uniformly samples 4 4 existing benchmark items as parents and asks the same rewriter LLM to rewrite 4 ​ T g 4T_{g} child items from each parent, conditioned on the parent item and the target difficulty. After difficulty annotation, it selects the valid child item whose difficulty is closest to the target among all generated candidates. Thus, the baseline uses the same total rewrite budget and the same final selection rule as BenchFill , but does not use difficulty-aware parent retrieval, nearby reference lists, or iterative memory updates.

E.2 Additional Experimental Results
Human validation.
We ensure the reliability of the generated gap-filling items using a two-stage validation process. GPT-5.4 first serves as an automatic validity judge, checking whether each candidate is well-formed and whether its reference answer is correct; candidates that fail this check are discarded and regenerated. Human experts then independently audit the final selected items. Across the 24 target gaps, one initially proposed candidate failed the GPT-5.4 check because of an incorrect reference answer and was regenerated. All 24 final selected items passed the GPT-5.4 validity check and subsequently passed human review without modification or replacement.
Does the rewriter perceive difficulty?
A natural question is whether our rewriter indeed perceives difficulty, instead of hitting the target difficulty blindly. We examine this question from two complementary angles: whether the rewriter already shows difficulty sensitivity before iterative feedback, and whether the subsequent search-and-annotate loop further improves alignment with the target difficulty.
First, to isolate the rewriter’s own difficulty perception from later IRT feedback and iterative search, we analyze only the first generation round of each gap-filling case. At this stage, the rewriter is conditioned on the specified target difficulty, but has not yet received feedback on the annotated difficulties of its generated children. Fig. 7 shows the specified target difficulty and the annotated difficulty of valid first-round children across all 8 benchmarks and 3 target gaps per benchmark. Within most benchmarks, higher specified targets produce higher mean annotated difficulties, and well-separated targets usually induce distinct child-difficulty distributions. This indicates that the rewriter already has non-trivial sensitivity to the specified difficulty scale before iterative feedback is introduced.
Second, we examine whether the search-and-annotate loop further improves target alignment. Fig. 8 shows one representative gap-filling case from each benchmark. Across these cases, the mean absolute target deviation generally decreases from the first round to the final round, and in most cases the generated children also become more concentrated around the target. This suggests that the iterative loop further refines the rewriter’s initial difficulty signal using IRT annotation and updated memory.

Detecting localized model improvements.
We further examine whether BenchFill improves a benchmark’s ability to detect localized model improvements beyond reducing difficulty gaps. We consider one target gap in each of AIME24, AIME25, and AIME26, and compare three benchmark variants: Original , Original + BenchFill , and Original + Baseline . Both augmentation methods insert exactly one item into the original benchmark. We perform mild SFT so that the model improves around the target gap region. Each item is evaluated with 32 rollouts before and after SFT, and we test whether the gain is statistically detectable using a one-sided stratified randomization test ( p < 0.05 p<0.05 ).
As shown in Tab. 12 , Original + BenchFill detects a positive post-SFT gain in all three cases (3/3), while Original and Original + Baseline detect none (0/3 each). This shows that BenchFill is not merely improving the metric it optimizes; by filling under-covered difficulty regions, it makes the benchmark more sensitive to localized model improvements that would otherwise be missed.
