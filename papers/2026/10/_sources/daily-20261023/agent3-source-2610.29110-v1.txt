Abstract
Merging different models into a single model is highly desirable for multi-task deployment,
particularly in regulated domains like healthcare. In such settings, models are often locally fine-tuned due to strict data privacy policies, necessitating that aggregation operates exclusively on the fine-tuned weights. Existing methods apply a uniform merge across all
tasks, ignoring the geometric relationships among their learned subspaces. When task updates
occupy misaligned subspaces, projecting them onto a shared low-rank basis discards
task-relevant directions, a failure mode named subspace interference. Through this
study, we show that the per-task projection error of a merged model is governed by the
principal-angle alignment between each task and the shared subspace, so that grouping
geometrically compatible tasks provably reduces this error within each group. Motivated by
this observation, we propose Group-wise Rank-Aware Model Merging (GRAM), which uses the
principal-angle geometry of task vectors to decide which tasks should be merged together
and then performs a standard SVD-based merge within each coalition; the pipeline requires
only the fine-tuned weights at merge time. With only one additional model (K{=}2),
GRAM significantly mitigates subspace interference and consistently outperforms prior
merging methods across language understanding (GLUE with Flan-T5), 8-task CLIP vision, and
medical instruction tuning (Qwen2-7B-Instruct with four English/Chinese medical LoRAs).
1 Introduction
Figure 1: How tasks are grouped on the Grassmannian governs merging quality.
Insets: three partitions of the per-task low-rank subspaces visualised on the Grassmannian—all tasks merged into a single global coalition (top, low intra-coalition similarity), three groups (middle), and the two-group GRAM partition (bottom, high intra-coalition similarity).
Curve: as intra-coalition similarity increases, merged-model accuracy rises (left axis, solid) and the within-group subspace projection error \sum_{\mathrm{pairs}\in G}(1{-}\cos^{2}\theta) falls (right axis, dashed).
Highlighted markers tie each partition to its location on the trade-off curve: GRAM places geometrically compatible tasks in the same coalition, reducing the alignment penalty that global merging incurs.
Model merging (Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023) aggregates multiple fine-tuned, task-specific models into a single deployable model without the need for additional training. This paradigm is highly attractive for multi-task deployment, as it circumvents the substantial costs of storing and serving independent models. The structured nature of weight updates—particularly in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA (Hu et al., 2022))—makes model merging highly effective. This approach is especially critical in privacy-constrained domains such as healthcare, where institutions independently fine-tune a shared backbone on local clinical data. Because data sharing across sites is strictly restricted, any unified multi-institution model must be constructed solely from the fine-tuned weights, precluding the use of the original training data.
Prior work on model merging spans parameter-space arithmetic with conflict resolution (Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023), auxiliary signal coefficient learning (Yang et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023), and structured approximations exploiting low-rank or layer-wise properties (Gargiulo et al., 2025; Stoica et al., 2024; Marczak et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025a). A pervasive limitation of these methods is their reliance on a uniform global merge: all tasks are compressed into a single shared space. Consequently, tasks whose learned parameter directions are least compatible with the majority suffer from disproportionate performance degradation—a phenomenon we term subspace interference. Furthermore, many existing techniques require access to task-specific calibration data or activations, rendering them inapplicable in strict privacy-preserving deployments where data cannot leave its source institution.
A critical signal overlooked by current methods is the inter-task geometry of the learned parameter subspaces. Existing techniques process updates individually or globally, systematically neglecting the pairwise geometric relationships between tasks. As illustrated in Figure 1, subspace similarity is highly predictive of merging compatibility: grouping tasks with high intra-coalition subspace similarity simultaneously increases merged-model accuracy and minimizes projection error. We observe distinct geometric signatures—such as cluster-outlier structures in GLUE tasks and block-diagonal affinities in vision tasks (appendix D). Crucially, the outlier tasks are precisely those that disproportionately degrade standard global SVD merging, and thus benefit the most from group-wise isolation (section 5). Subspace geometry therefore carries predictive information that current methods fail to exploit.
Motivated by this geometric insight, we propose Group-wise Rank-Aware Model Merging (GRAM). GRAM targets the task-aware deployment setting, where task identity is known at inference. We reframe the merging objective from compressing T models into a single model, to compressing T models into K\ll T models, trading a marginal increase in storage for a targeted, significant reduction in subspace interference. Crucially, GRAM is entirely data-free: the pipeline operates exclusively on the fine-tuned weights, requiring no source data, activations, or calibration sets at merge time. Our primary contributions are as follows:
1.
A Geometry-Aware Merging Algorithm: We introduce GRAM, which partitions tasks using Grassmannian k-means++ on chordal distances. Within each coalition, it dynamically merges models via a greedy adaptive rank allocator that pools singular values across members, achieving provable optimality under a global rank budget.
2.
Theoretical Guarantees: We rigorously bound the projection error of our clustering objective. We prove that the resulting partition yields a merge error within O(\log K) of the optimal partition, and its expected error is strictly bounded below that of a standard global merge.
3.
State-of-the-Art Data-Free Performance: Extensive evaluations across language understanding, vision, and medical instruction-tuning demonstrate that GRAM consistently outperforms existing data-free baselines. Notably, on the medical benchmark, GRAM surpasses even data-dependent methods (e.g., RegMean, Fisher-Merge) that rely on per-task calibration data.
2 Related Work
Model Merging.
Model merging combines independently fine-tuned parameters into a single deployable model
without retraining. Parameter-level methods average fine-tuned weights or arithmetically
combine task vectors, sometimes with sparsification or sign/magnitude-based conflict resolution
(Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024). Coefficient-learning
methods learn per-task or per-layer mixing weights from auxiliary signals or coordinate-wise
masks (Yang et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023). Structured-update methods exploit the
low-rank or layered structure of fine-tuning updates, operating in the singular-value or
per-layer domain to reduce inter-task interference
(Gargiulo et al., 2025; Stoica et al., 2024; Marczak et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Wei et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b; Sun et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025). Multi-model
approaches retain some per-task structure rather than collapsing to a single shared model
(Huang et al., 2024). A common thread across these single-model approaches is that all tasks
participate in the same global merge, implicitly assuming sufficient subspace alignment—an
assumption that fails when tasks span diverse domains. We depart from it by measuring
inter-task subspace geometry directly and merging within geometrically compatible groups.
Task Grouping in Multi-Task Learning.
Deciding which tasks should be trained together has a long history in multi-task learning.
Zamir et al. (2018) computationally map transfer relationships across 26 visual tasks, and
Standley et al. (2020) formalize the grouping problem and search over partitions to trade
accuracy against compute. Task2Vec (Achille et al., 2019) represents each task by a
Fisher-information vector, while branched networks (Vandenhende et al., 2019) and
mixture-of-experts models (Chen et al., 2023) realize grouping through the architecture
itself. Affinity-based methods avoid combinatorial search:
TAG (Fifty et al., 2021) estimates inter-task affinity from gradient signals in a single
training run, MTG-Net (Song et al., 2022) meta-learns a predictor of per-task gains across
task combinations, Grad-TAG (Li et al., 2024) scales affinity estimation via gradient
linearization, and Wang et al. (2024a) cast grouping as a mathematical program with
theoretical guarantees. Complementary work on dynamic loss weighting (Liu et al., 2022)
modulates task competition during training rather than partitioning tasks explicitly. Dynamic client cooperation (Yang et al., 2026)
mitigates interference through adaptive coalition formation rather than explicitly partitioning tasks.
All of these approaches require training-time data and gradients, making them inapplicable to
the post-hoc merging setting where only frozen weights are available. Our grouping criterion is
data-free: it uses only the left singular subspaces of the task-specific weight updates.
3 Preliminaries
Task vectors.
We consider a pre-trained model with L layers and weights
W_{0}=\{W_{0}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}. After adapting the model to task
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}, we obtain fine-tuned weights W_{i}. Following the
task-vector view of model merging (Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024),
the adaptation is summarized by the per-layer task vector
\Delta W_{i}^{(\ell)}\;=\;W_{i}^{(\ell)}-W_{0}^{(\ell)}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
(1)
i.e., the difference between the task-adapted and pre-trained weights at layer
\ell. The matrix formulation in (1) applies to linear
layers — MLP blocks and attention projection matrices — which covers all merged
parameters in our experiments: the LoRA settings adapt only linear projections by
construction, and in the fully fine-tuned vision setting SVD-based merging is
applied to all 2-D weight matrices while the remaining non-matrix parameters
(LayerNorm gains, biases) are merged by simple averaging (section D.6).
For models tuned with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022), the
task vector inherits a rank-r factorization
\tau_{i}^{(\ell)}\;=\;B_{i}^{(\ell)}A_{i}^{(\ell)},\qquad B_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\;A_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\;r\ll\min(d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}).
(2)
SVD of task updates.
The compact SVD of each update is
\Delta W_{i}^{(\ell)}=U_{i}^{(\ell)}\,\Sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}\,V_{i}^{(\ell)\top},
(3)
where U_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} contains the left singular vectors (output subspace),
\Sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r} is a diagonal matrix of singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}>0,
and V_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r} contains the right singular vectors (input subspace).
We drop the layer index (\ell) when the context is unambiguous.
Merging objective.
Given T task vectors, each of rank r across L layers,
the goal is to produce K<T merged task vectors that minimize the total
reconstruction error across all tasks subject to a per-merge rank budget:
\min_{\text{partition},\;\{\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|\Delta W_{i}-\widetilde{\Delta W}_{\pi(i)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\rank(\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k})\leq r\;\;\text{for all}\;k\in\{1,\dots,K\},
(4)
where \pi(i)\in\{1,\dots,K\} assigns task i to its group G_{\pi(i)} and
\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}} is the merged update for group G_{k}. The model
deployed for group G_{k} is, per layer,
W_{G_{k}}^{(\ell)}\;=\;W_{0}^{(\ell)}+\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}^{(\ell)},
(5)
i.e., each of the K deployed models is the pre-trained backbone plus its
group’s merged task vector, and task i is served by the model of group
G_{\pi(i)}.
Subspace interference.
Let B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be a shared output basis with orthonormal columns (B^{\top}B=I_{r}). Substituting the compact SVD \Delta W_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V_{i}^{\top}, the projection error of task i onto \mathrm{col}(B) is
\mathcal{E}_{i}\;=\;\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\Sigma_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},
(6)
We adopt \mathcal{E}_{i} as a principled measure of inter-task interference as it directly measures the task-relevant information lost when updates are forced into a shared subspace. Crucially, \mathcal{E}_{i} depends only on U_{i} and \Sigma_{i} — not on V_{i} — so partitioning by output-subspace alignment captures the interference signal a shared basis can control.
To isolate the geometric contribution of subspace misalignment from the magnitude of
the task update, we drop the singular-value weighting and analyze
\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}. Every column of U_{i} admits a unique orthogonal
decomposition into a component in \text{col}(B) and a component in \text{col}(B)^{\perp}.
Applied column-wise,
U_{i}\;=\;\underbrace{BB^{\top}U_{i}}_{\in\,\text{col}(B)}\;+\;\underbrace{(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}}_{\in\,\text{col}(B)^{\perp}}.
The two summands live in orthogonal subspaces, so by the Pythagorean theorem (in the
Frobenius inner product),
\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|BB^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}. Since B has
orthonormal columns, \left\|BB^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}, and
\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=r. Rearranging,
\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;r-\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
(7)
Let \theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r} denote the principal angles between \text{col}(U_{i})
and \text{col}(B); throughout the paper we reserve \theta for principal angles
and k for merge groups (model weights are denoted W), and index principal
angles by j. A standard
result (Golub and Van Loan, 1996) states that the
singular values of B^{\top}U_{i} are exactly \{\cos\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}, so
\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}. Substituting into
(7) and using \sin^{2}\theta_{j}+\cos^{2}\theta_{j}=1,
\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;r-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sin^{2}\theta_{j},
(8)
which is the squared chordal (projection F-) distance between the two subspaces. The
expression depends only on the relative geometry of \text{col}(U_{i}) and
\text{col}(B) and is invariant to the choice of basis. When the subspaces are
well-aligned (\theta_{j}\approx 0 for all j), the projection error vanishes. When
they are misaligned, important task directions are discarded by the merge. This motivates grouping tasks by
(singular-value-weighted) subspace compatibility before merging.
4 Method: Group-wise Rank-Aware Model Merging
Figure 2: The GRAM pipeline. Each task vector \Delta W_{i} is decomposed via SVD to extract its top-r left- and right-singular subspaces (U_{i},V_{i}) (left). The collection of U_{i}’s is fed into a subspace clustering step that partitions the T tasks into K coalitions by chordal distance (middle). Within each coalition, the selected top-k_{i} singular components from member tasks are concatenated and re-orthonormalized via thin SVD to yield a single rank-r merged update (right).
Our goal is to merge T task vectors into K\ll T per-group updates in a way that protects geometrically incompatible tasks from mutual interference.
To accomplish this, we decompose the pipeline (fig. 2) into three stages.
(1) Quantifying pairwise subspace geometry.
We compute the pairwise chordal distance between task output subspaces.
This quantity directly governs the projection error a shared basis would incur (Lemma B.3),
so it is the natural input for the clustering step rather than coordinate-wise distances on flattened parameters.
(2) Partitioning tasks into geometrically coherent coalitions.
Treating each task’s projection matrix P_{i}=U_{i}U_{i}^{\top} as a point in Euclidean space, we run Grassmannian k-means++ on the chordal-distance matrix to obtain K coalitions with low within-group and high between-group subspace distance, importing the O(\log K) approximation guarantee of k-means++ into the Grassmannian setting.
(3) Per-coalition low-rank merge.
Within each coalition we concatenate the per-task top-k_{i} singular triples under an adaptive rank allocator that distributes the rank budget to minimize the reconstruction error,
then orthonormalize via a thin SVD to produce a single rank-r merged update per coalition.
4.1 Chordal Subspace Distance
We measure the geometric compatibility between two task subspaces via the
squared chordal distance. At layer \ell, the chordal distance
between the column spaces of U_{i}^{(\ell)} and U_{j}^{(\ell)} is
d_{c}^{2}\!\bigl(U_{i}^{(\ell)},\,U_{j}^{(\ell)}\bigr)\;=\;r\,-\,\left\|U_{i}^{(\ell)\top}U_{j}^{(\ell)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{s=1}^{r}\sin^{2}\theta_{s},
(9)
where \theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r} are the principal angles between
\mathrm{col}(U_{i}^{(\ell)}) and \mathrm{col}(U_{j}^{(\ell)}) (indexed by s here
since j denotes a task). By eq. 8, this quantity directly governs the projection error
incurred when two tasks share a merged basis, making it the natural metric for
deciding which tasks to group. Furthermore, we show that the chordal distance on the Grassmannian is equivalent to the squared Frobenius distance between the orthogonal projectors P_{i}^{(\ell)}:=U_{i}^{(\ell)}U_{i}^{(\ell)\top} up to a factor of 2 (section C.4), so off-the-shelf k-means++ on \{P_{i}^{(\ell)}\} inherits its O(\log K) approximation guarantee in the chordal geometry (appendix B).
4.2 Grassmannian k-Means++ Clustering
To obtain a single distance per task pair, we average the per-layer chordal
distance across layers:
\bar{d}_{c}^{2}(i,j)\;=\;\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}d_{c}^{2}\!\bigl(U_{i}^{(\ell)},\,U_{j}^{(\ell)}\bigr).
(10)
We then run k-means++ (Arthur et al., 2007) on \bar{d}_{c}^{2} to partition
the T tasks into K groups \{G_{1},\dots,G_{K}\}.
The number of groups K is a user-specified compression budget
that trades deployment cost (K deployed models) against merging fidelity.
In our experiments (section 5), K{=}2 provides the best
cost–accuracy trade-off on all benchmarks.
4.3 Per-Group Low-Rank Merge
For each group G_{k} and layer \ell, we merge the constituent task
vectors into a single rank-r update. The procedure operates on all three
SVD components—left singular vectors U_{i}, singular values \sigma_{i},
and right singular vectors V_{i}—and allocates rank budget adaptively
rather than uniformly across tasks.
Adaptive rank allocation.
A naïve approach gives each of the |G_{k}| tasks an equal share
r/|G_{k}| of the rank budget. Since intrinsic rank varies sharply across
tasks and layers in practice (fig. 4, section D.3),
this uniform allocation wastes capacity on compact tasks and starves complex ones.
We instead formulate rank allocation as a discrete optimization problem.
Let \Delta W_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V_{i}^{\top} be the SVD of task i’s
update, and let r_{i} denote the number of singular directions allocated
to task i. The per-task reconstruction error after keeping the top
r_{i} components is
\mathcal{L}_{i}(r_{i})=\bigl\lVert(I-B_{i}^{(r_{i})}{B_{i}^{(r_{i})}}^{\top})\,\Delta W_{i}\bigr\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j>r_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{2},
(11)
where B_{i}^{(r_{i})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r_{i}} contains the top-r_{i}
left singular vectors of \Delta W_{i}, and the second equality follows
from the Eckart–Young theorem. We seek the allocation that minimizes
total reconstruction error under a global budget:
\min_{\{r_{i}\}}\;\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\sum_{j>r_{i}}\sigma_{i,j}^{2}\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i\in G_{k}}r_{i}\leq r.
(12)
The marginal gain of assigning one additional rank slot to task i is
\mathcal{L}_{i}(r_{i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(r_{i}+1)=\sigma_{i,\,r_{i}+1}^{2}.
Since singular values are sorted in descending order within each task,
the optimal strategy is greedy: pool all candidate singular
values \{\sigma_{i,j}^{2}\} across tasks in the group, select the
top-r entries, and count how many are drawn from each task. This
yields optimal per-task allocations r_{1},\dots,r_{|G_{k}|} with
\sum_{i}r_{i}=r, ensuring that rank budget flows to the directions
that most reduce reconstruction error.
Coalition merge.
Given the per-task allocations \{r_{i}\}, we follow TSV-Merge (Gargiulo et al., 2025):
concatenate the selected top-r_{i} SVD components across tasks into
\bar{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}, \bar{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}, and
\bar{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}, sort globally by singular value, replace
\bar{U} and \bar{V}^{\top} with their orthogonal polar factors \widehat{U},\widehat{V} (rather than computing an SVD of the joint operator), and reconstruct
\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}^{(\ell)}=\widehat{U}\,\diag(\bar{s})\,\widehat{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
as the task vector for group G_{k} at layer \ell.
Theoretical guarantee for grouping.
The grouping step is backed by a deterministic projection-error guarantee: with optimal per-group bases, any partition into K groups achieves total reconstruction error no larger than a single global rank-r basis fit to all tasks (Theorem B.1 in appendix B; the proof is a direct optimality argument independent of the clustering rule). The guarantee explains why the empirical gains in section 5 concentrate on tasks geometrically furthest from the bulk—exactly the tasks for which a partition-aware basis improves most over the global optimum, making the inequality strict and large in magnitude—while the alignment-penalty machinery (Lemma B.3) and the k-means++ partition error chain in appendix B quantify how much grouping helps.
5 Experiments
5.1 Setup
We evaluate GRAM on three benchmarks: GLUE (Wang et al., 2018) with Flan-T5-Base/Large (Chung et al., 2024), the 8-task vision benchmark with fully fine-tuned CLIP ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14, and medical instruction tuning on Qwen2-7B-Instruct with four LoRA experts (section 5.4). All methods operate on the same pre-existing fine-tuned models—from FusionBench (Tang et al., 2025) for GLUE and from the TSV-Merge release (Gargiulo et al., 2025) for vision—and are re-run under a unified protocol (appendix D), so cross-method differences come only from merging. We report validation accuracy on GLUE (Spearman \rho{\times}100 for STS-B), test accuracy on vision, and the metrics described in section 5.4 for the medical benchmark. All experiments use task-aware inference: task identity is known at test time and selects the group-specific adapter. This is a preference rather than a requirement (section 5.5).
For GLUE and vision, we compare against seven baselines: Weight Averaging (Wortsman et al., 2022), Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022), TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023), AdaMerging++ (Yang et al., 2023), Iso-C and Iso-CTS (Marczak et al., 2025), and TSV-Merge (Gargiulo et al., 2025). The GLUE results in table 1 additionally include Concrete TA, Concrete AM (Tang et al., 2023), and DOGE TA (Wei et al., 2025). The CLIP ViT-B/16 results in section D.5 additionally include LiNeS-Merge (Wang et al., 2024b) and LOT-Merging (Sun et al., 2025) for completeness across recent structured-update baselines. The medical benchmark (section 5.4) additionally compares against Fisher-Merge (Matena and Raffel, 2022) and RegMean (Jin et al., 2022) as data-using references. Ablation includes random-equipartition controls under the same chordal metric.

We additionally include Fisher-Merge (Matena and Raffel, 2022) and RegMean (Jin et al., 2022) as data-using references that consume a standard 64-sample-per-task calibration set.
Table 3 reports per-metric results. Table 4 evaluates the contribution of each GRAM component to overall performance. Starting from full GRAM, we remove one component at a time and report the benchmark average for both PEFT merging (Flan-T5-Base on GLUE) and full-model merging (CLIP ViT-B/32 on the 8-task vision benchmark). Removing PA-Sim replaces the chordal subspace metric with an alternative that ignores cross-task geometry, breaking the alignment-penalty signal that drives the partition. Removing Coalition collapses GRAM to K{=}1 and recovers the global TSV-Merge baseline. Removing Adapt-Rank substitutes a uniform r/|G_{k}| budget for the adaptive allocator, wasting capacity on sharp-spectrum tasks and starving long-tail ones. Each component is crucial, but their relative importance flips across modalities: on GLUE, PA-Sim drives the largest drop (-3.0), followed by Coalition (-2.1) and Adapt-Rank (-1.6); on ViT-B/32, Coalition dominates (-3.4) while PA-Sim (-0.5) and Adapt-Rank (-0.1) are small — the heterogeneous LoRA spectra make adaptive allocation vital for PEFT but largely cosmetic for the more uniform full-fine-tuned vision deltas. With all components combined, GRAM boosts TSV-Merge by +2.1 on GLUE and +3.8 on ViT-B/32, demonstrating the complementarity of these components.
Sensitivity to the number of groups K.
Table 6 evaluates GRAM for every K from 1 to T{=}8 on Flan-T5-Base and Flan-T5-Large under one unified protocol (caption). At both scales the cost–accuracy frontier has a sharp knee at K{=}2: moving from Weight Averaging—the simplest single-model baseline—to GRAM at K{=}2 gains +5.6 (Base) and +2.4 (Large) points, capturing 87\% and 73\% of the respective gaps to the no-merging oracle, for a single extra rank-16 adapter (0.7\% / 0.6\% of backbone parameters). Raising K from 2 all the way to 7 adds at most +0.1 (Base) and +0.6 (Large) on top—on Base the average is not even monotone, dipping at K{=}4. Most of the achievable gain is therefore already available at small K: GRAM’s geometry-aware clustering identifies compatible coalitions efficiently enough that a large K is not needed to capture most of the gains in this setting. The practical rule is budget-first—deploy the smallest K the serving budget allows; K{=}2, our default, captures the bulk of the gain at the tightest possible budget.

Robustness of the partition.
Table 5 compares four ways of producing the K{=}2 partition from the same subspace geometry. The partition is stable: it is identical across 10 initialization seeds at both K{=}2 and K{=}3 (10/10), and an independent hierarchy-based algorithm (agglomerative, average linkage) reproduces GRAM’s partitions exactly at K\in\{2,3,5,6,7\}, with the exact max-modularity communities coinciding for every K\geq 3. The partition structure is thus a property of the geometry, not of a particular clustering algorithm—the gain comes from the subspace signal itself. The one disagreement is informative: at K{=}2 the degree-corrected modularity objective prefers a 4/4 split that pulls MNLI into the low-similarity cluster and scores 0.9 lower (82.9), whereas the solution used by GRAM respects the raw block structure of the similarity matrix and keeps the stronger 6/2 partition.
Deployment without oracle task identity.
Task-aware inference is a preference, not a requirement: a lightweight Mahalanobis task mapper (Lee et al., 2018), as deployed in SKILL (Ge et al., 2023)—class-conditional Gaussians in the frozen base encoder’s feature space, 5 training samples per class, no gradient training, no test-time task labels—routes 100.0\% of validation examples to the correct group on Flan-T5-Base / GLUE at K{=}2, so task-agnostic GRAM equals oracle GRAM (83.8 on every task); group-routing accuracy stays {\geq}99.98\% for every K. Its only errors (QQP\leftrightarrowMRPC, 0.18\% of QQP) are absorbed by the coalition structure, since GRAM groups together precisely the similar tasks a router confuses (section D.2).

Scalability of the grouping step.
A natural concern is whether grouping becomes a bottleneck when the number of tasks is
large. Figure 3 measures the end-to-end cost of grouping on a single RTX 3080
as T grows from 8 to 1024. No benchmark offers thousands of adapters, so beyond our
real tasks we use perturbed copies of them; grouping cost depends only on adapter shapes,
not on their values, so these copies are a faithful proxy. Grouping is cheap: the real
8-task setting takes about one second, and 1024 tasks finish within a few minutes. Both
time and memory grow roughly linearly with T, because decomposing each adapter
dominates the cost and the pipeline processes one layer at a time. Llama-3-8B appears
cheaper than Flan-T5 only because its LoRA factors are used directly rather than dense
per-layer deltas.
6 Conclusion
GRAM partitions tasks by chordal subspace distance via Grassmannian k-means++ and merges within each group with a standard SVD-based procedure, avoiding the projection loss of global merging on misaligned subspaces. On GLUE with Flan-T5-Base/Large, GRAM (K{=}2) reaches 83.8%/88.7% average, matching the strongest baseline at the cost of one extra deployed model. On 8-task vision (CLIP ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14), GRAM reaches 88.7%/94.0%, the highest among compared methods. On the medical benchmark (Qwen2-7B-Instruct with four LoRA experts), GRAM attains the best Avg Rank without using any per-task calibration data. Gains concentrate on tasks whose subspaces are most dissimilar from the majority—precisely the tasks that the alignment penalty of Lemma B.3 predicts will benefit most from grouping.
Limitations.
Our results have several scope limits: (i) single-seed reporting, so small Flan-T5-Large margins should be read with caution; (ii) one model family per modality (Flan-T5 for NLU, CLIP for vision, Qwen2 for medical), and the medical evaluation is a case study on public benchmarks rather than a real federated experiment from multiple sites; (iii) the theory analyzes idealized objects (optimal basis, Grassmannian k-means objective) rather than GRAM’s exact column-stacked merge with adaptive allocation, so it should be read as geometric motivation rather than end-to-end optimality guarantee.
Future work.
Scaling the task-agnostic router of section D.2 beyond small task collections—where group routing approaches full task identification (Ge et al., 2023)—and extending the medical evaluation to a real multi-site federated setup would broaden the deployment story.

Appendix A Broader Impact
GRAM provides a way to combine independently fine-tuned task vectors into a single deployable model without retraining or pooling raw training data, and the geometry-aware grouping step is what makes this scale beyond a handful of tasks. We highlight two settings where this property carries concrete societal value.
Healthcare and other privacy-constrained domains.
Clinical AI is trained on data that cannot legally or ethically be centralized: electronic health records, radiology images, pathology slides, and clinical-note adapters live behind institutional firewalls and HIPAA/GDPR boundaries. Today, a hospital that fine-tunes a foundation model on its own patient cohort cannot easily benefit from another hospital’s adapter for a related task (a different imaging modality, a different patient population, or a different downstream label) without either sharing the underlying data or running an expensive joint training run. GRAM operates entirely on released task vectors—the parameter deltas, never the data—and merges them by clustering subspaces that are geometrically compatible. This lets a coalition of institutions pool model expertise across complementary specialties (e.g., radiology + pathology + clinical-note triage) while keeping each institution’s data on-premises, and without forcing dissimilar tasks into a single averaged model where high-stakes minority tasks would otherwise be drowned out by majority gradients. The same property applies to other privacy-sensitive verticals: legal, financial, and on-device personalization.
Decentralized and community-driven model merging.
The open-model ecosystem now hosts hundreds of thousands of community-released LoRA adapters and full fine-tunes per base model, but practitioners have no principled way to combine more than a few at a time. Naive averaging across many adapters destroys task-specific structure; per-adapter routing keeps a linear deployment cost. GRAM’s coalition structure is a middle path: a small number K of merged checkpoints that respect subspace geometry, with the compression budget K left as an explicit knob. This matches the way decentralized model development actually proceeds—asynchronous, uncoordinated, contributors who never share data—and gives downstream users a way to consume many adapters as a few well-chosen merged models, lowering the deployment cost of community fine-tuning without forcing centralization.
Risks and limitations.
Two risks are worth flagging. First, merging task vectors does not erase the biases or harmful behaviors of any constituent model; coalitions of biased adapters can yield biased merged checkpoints, and the chordal-distance grouping is geometric, not semantic, so it offers no protection against value misalignment in the inputs. Practitioners should audit each merged coalition with the same fairness and safety scrutiny they would apply to a single fine-tune. Second, GRAM lowers the cost of redistributing capabilities encoded in fine-tuned adapters, including capabilities the original fine-tuner did not intend to release. Standard adapter-licensing and provenance practices—adapter cards, licensing terms, watermarking—apply unchanged, and we encourage their use when GRAM is deployed in shared settings.
Appendix B Deferred Theoretical Analysis
We collect the geometric and complexity results that motivate GRAM but are deferred from the main text for space. Theorem B.1 below is the headline guarantee that grouping reduces total projection error (referenced from section 5.5); the results that follow extend the picture to the GRAM column-stacked basis, the partition-level error chain that ties the chordal clustering rule to a k-means++ approximation guarantee, the computational cost, and a Rademacher-complexity bound. Equation 8 in section 3 already establishes that, for any orthonormal B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}, \left\|(I-BB^{\top})U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sin^{2}\theta_{j} — the squared chordal distance between \mathrm{col}(U_{i}) and \mathrm{col}(B). All proofs are in appendix C.
Theorem B.1 (Grouping reduces total error).
Let T tasks be partitioned into K groups \{G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}\}. For each group G_{k}, let B^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be the optimal rank-r basis minimizing \sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} over orthonormal B, and let B^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be the analogous global optimum for all T tasks. Then
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{(k)}B^{(k)\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},
(13)
with strict inequality whenever some per-group optimal basis B^{(k)} improves on the global optimum B^{*} within group G_{k}. The proof (section C.1) is a direct optimality argument and does not depend on the specific clustering rule.
Theorem B.2 (Direct projection-error bound for the GRAM per-group basis).
Let G_{k}=\{1,\ldots,m\} be a coalition with rank allocation (k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}) satisfying 0\leq k_{i}\leq r and \sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\leq r (the budget enforced by the GRAM allocator of section 4.3), and let
M^{(k)}\;:=\;\bigl[\,U_{1}^{(k_{1})}\;\big|\;\cdots\;\big|\;U_{m}^{(k_{m})}\,\bigr]\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times R},\qquad R=\sum_{i}k_{i}\leq r,
be the column-wise concatenation of the per-task top-k_{i} left singular vectors of \Delta W_{i}, with the convention that U_{i}^{(0)} is an empty matrix (no contribution). Let \widehat{U}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be any orthonormal basis whose column space contains \mathrm{col}(M^{(k)}) (e.g. the GRAM merge basis, padded if R<r). Then for every task i\in G_{k},
\bigl\|(I-\widehat{U}^{(k)}\widehat{U}^{(k)\top})\,\Delta W_{i}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{\ell=k_{i}+1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2},
(14)
where the right-hand side reduces to \left\|\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} when k_{i}=0.
B.1 Grassmannian k-Means++ Error Bound
Lemma B.3 (Alignment penalty for two equally weighted subspaces).
Let U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} have orthonormal columns and principal angles 0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{r}\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}. Fix \sigma>0. Then for every matrix B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} with B^{\top}B=I_{r},
\bigl\|(I-BB^{\top})\sigma U\bigr\|_{F}^{2}+\bigl\|(I-BB^{\top})\sigma V\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\bigl(1-\cos\theta_{j}\bigr),
(15)
with equality when the columns of B are the top-r left singular vectors of [\,U\mid V\,].
Deterministic latent-group model.
There are G\geq 2 latent groups with prototype subspaces Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}, each with orthonormal columns, satisfying \theta_{j}(Q_{g},Q_{h})\geq\phi_{\min}>0 for all g\neq h and j=1,\ldots,r. There are T=mG tasks with m\geq 2 per group; task i in group g(i) has \Sigma_{i}=\sigma I_{r} and d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},Q_{g(i)})\leq\epsilon. For a partition \pi=\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}\}, define the optimal-basis merge error
\mathrm{Err}(\pi)\;:=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\min_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\,B^{\top}B=I_{r}}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\bigl\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}.
(16)
Under \Sigma_{i}=\sigma I_{r}, the optimal per-group basis is the chordal Fréchet mean on \mathrm{Gr}(r,d_{\mathrm{out}}) (Proposition C.5), and \mathrm{Err}(\pi)=\sigma^{2}\Phi(\pi) with the Grassmannian k-means cost \Phi(\pi):=\sum_{k}\min_{C\in\mathrm{Gr}(r,d_{\mathrm{out}})}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C). The bounds below compare partitions through the projection error of the optimal per-group basis (\mathrm{Err}(\pi)); they should be read as guarantees on the partition objective and how grouping shapes it, not as guarantees on GRAM’s actual column-stacked basis. Theorem B.2 provides the algorithmic-side bound on the GRAM basis itself, and section C.5 discusses when the column-stacked basis coincides with the Fréchet mean and when it merely upper-bounds it. Let \pi_{++} denote the partition from k-means++ on \{P_{i}=U_{i}U_{i}^{\top}\}, \pi_{\mathrm{rand}} a uniformly random equipartition into G groups of size m, \pi^{*}:=\argmin_{\pi}\Phi(\pi), and \pi_{\mathrm{global}} the trivial single-block partition (K=1).
Theorem B.4 (k-means++ partition error bounds).
Under the deterministic latent-group model with K=G:
1.
(k-means++ upper bound on the optimal-basis error.)
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})\bigr]\;\leq\;8(\ln K+2)\cdot\mathrm{Err}(\pi^{*})\;\leq\;8(\ln K+2)\,\sigma^{2}T\epsilon.
(17)
2.
(Random partition lower bound, valid for \epsilon\leq r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})/4.)
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}})\bigr]\;\geq\;G\!\left(1-\frac{G}{\binom{T}{m}}\right)\sigma^{2}\bigl[r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}\,\bigr].
(18)
3.
(Global merge lower bound, valid for \epsilon\leq r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})/4.)
\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{global}})\;\geq\;\lfloor T/2\rfloor\,\sigma^{2}\bigl[r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}\,\bigr].
(19)
Corollary B.5 (Projection error chain).
Under the deterministic latent-group model with K=G, in the regime \epsilon\leq c\,r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})^{2}/(m\ln K) for a small absolute constant c>0,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})\bigr]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}})\bigr]\;\leq\;\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{global}}).
(20)
The right inequality is deterministic and follows from groupwise optimality (Theorem B.1); the left inequality follows from comparing parts (1) and (2) of Theorem B.4, with the regime chosen so that the random lower bound exceeds the GRAM upper bound.
Corollary B.5 captures the headline message: refining the trivial single-block merge into K groups via k-means++ on subspace similarities is never worse than a random equipartition (in expectation, in the small-\epsilon regime), and an arbitrary equipartition is in turn never worse than not grouping at all (deterministically). A non-uniform singular-value extension is in section C.9.
B.2 Computational Complexity
Let d=\max(d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}). The GRAM pipeline incurs, once per model:
•
Per-task SVD: For LoRA deltas \Delta W_{i}=B_{i}A_{i} (Flan-T5/GLUE, Qwen medical), thin QR of B_{i} and A_{i}^{\top} followed by an r\times r SVD costs O((d_{\mathrm{out}}+d_{\mathrm{in}})r^{2}+r^{3}) per task per layer. For full-rank deltas (vision), a top-r truncated SVD costs O(d_{\mathrm{out}}d_{\mathrm{in}}r) per task per layer.
•
Pairwise chordal distance: d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},U_{j})=r-\left\|U_{i}^{\top}U_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} is O(d_{\mathrm{out}}r^{2}) per pair; the full T\times T matrix is O(T^{2}d_{\mathrm{out}}r^{2}) — no need to materialize the projection matrices P_{i}.
•
Clustering: k-means++ on the precomputed pairwise-distance matrix; negligible when T is small.
•
Per-group merge: O(KLd_{\mathrm{out}}r^{2}) for the polar-factor orthogonalization plus O(|G_{k}|r\log r) per layer for rank allocation.
The dominant term is O(TL(d_{\mathrm{out}}+d_{\mathrm{in}})r^{2}) for LoRA deltas and O(TLd_{\mathrm{out}}d_{\mathrm{in}}r) for full-rank deltas, both strictly cheaper than one epoch of fine-tuning (O(n_{\text{train}}Ld^{2}) per task). At inference, each group’s rank-r merged update costs O(dr) per layer—identical to individual or global merge on a per-query basis. GRAM stores K merged updates instead of T per-task models, a factor T/K reduction in storage with no additional per-query FLOPs.
B.3 Generalization via Rademacher Complexity
Definition 1 (Linear-update hypothesis class).
Fix W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}, a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}} with \|a\|_{2}=1, and \beta>0. Let
\mathcal{H}_{a}(\beta)=\bigl\{x\mapsto a^{\top}(W_{0}+\Delta W)\,x:\left\|\Delta W\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\beta\bigr\}.
Theorem B.6 (Rademacher bound for linear updates).
Let \{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\} be an i.i.d. sample with \|x_{i}\|_{2}\leq R. Then
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}\!\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{a}(\beta)\bigr)\;\leq\;\beta R/\sqrt{n}.
(21)
Corollary B.7 (Per-group capacity is no larger than the global class).
If the GRAM per-group merged updates \{\widetilde{\Delta W}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K} all satisfy \left\|\widetilde{\Delta W}^{(k)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\beta, each per-group update belongs to \mathcal{H}_{a}(\beta), so the per-group Rademacher complexity is bounded by \beta R/\sqrt{n}, matching the global class.
Appendix C Proof Details
C.1 Proof of Theorem B.1
Proof of Theorem B.1.
For the global merge, let B^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be the optimal rank-r basis minimizing \sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} over all tasks.
For a partition \{G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}\}, let B^{(k)} be the optimal rank-r basis for group G_{k}, i.e.,
B^{(k)}=\argmin_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\,B^{\top}B=I}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
(22)
By optimality of B^{(k)} within group G_{k}, for any orthonormal B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}:
\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{(k)}B^{(k)\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
(23)
In particular, choosing B=B^{*} (the global optimum) for each group:
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{(k)}B^{(k)\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},
(24)
which establishes that group-wise merging with per-group optimal bases always achieves error no greater than global merging.
Strictness of the inequality requires a uniqueness/non-degeneracy assumption: if some B^{(k)} strictly improves on B^{*} within group G_{k} (which generically holds when the within-group leading singular subspace differs from the global one), the inequality is strict; otherwise it can be tight.
∎
C.2 Proof of Theorem B.2
We restate the theorem for convenience and then prove it via three elementary lemmas.
Lemma C.1 (Pythagorean identity for orthogonal projectors).
For any orthogonal projector P\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}} (P^{2}=P=P^{\top}) and any matrix A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
\left\|(I-P)A\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\left\|PA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
Proof.
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|PA+(I-P)A\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|PA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+2\langle PA,(I-P)A\rangle_{F}+\left\|(I-P)A\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}. The cross term vanishes because \langle PA,(I-P)A\rangle_{F}=\tr(A^{\top}P^{\top}(I-P)A)=\tr(A^{\top}P(I-P)A)=0, using P=P^{\top} and P(I-P)=0.
∎
Lemma C.2 (Subspace monotonicity of projected energy).
Let P,Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}} be orthogonal projectors with \mathrm{col}(Q)\subseteq\mathrm{col}(P). Then P\succeq Q in the Loewner order, and for every A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
\left\|PA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq\left\|QA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
Proof.
For orthogonal projectors, \mathrm{col}(Q)\subseteq\mathrm{col}(P) is equivalent to PQ=Q (since Q projects into a subspace of \mathrm{col}(P) and P acts as the identity there). Taking transposes and using P=P^{\top}, Q=Q^{\top} also gives QP=Q, so P-Q is symmetric and idempotent: (P-Q)^{2}=P^{2}-PQ-QP+Q^{2}=P-Q-Q+Q=P-Q. Hence P-Q is itself an orthogonal projector, and for any x,
x^{\top}(P-Q)x=\|(P-Q)x\|_{2}^{2}=\|(I-Q)Px\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0,
where the last equality uses (P-Q)x=Px-Qx=Px-QPx=(I-Q)Px. Thus P\succeq Q, and applying this with x=Av gives A^{\top}(P-Q)A\succeq 0, so \tr(A^{\top}PA)\geq\tr(A^{\top}QA), i.e. \left\|PA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq\left\|QA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
∎
Lemma C.3 (Self-projection recovers the truncation energy).
For any task i with thin SVD \Delta W_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V_{i}^{\top} and any 0\leq k_{i}\leq r, letting P_{i}:=U_{i}^{(k_{i})}U_{i}^{(k_{i})\top} (with P_{i}=0 when k_{i}=0),
\left\|P_{i}\,\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}, where the sum is empty (and equals 0) when k_{i}=0.
Proof.
The case k_{i}=0 is immediate from P_{i}=0. For k_{i}\geq 1, U_{i}^{(k_{i})\top}U_{i}=[\,I_{k_{i}}\,|\,0_{k_{i}\times(r-k_{i})}\,], so
U_{i}^{(k_{i})\top}\Delta W_{i}=[\,I_{k_{i}}\,|\,0\,]\Sigma_{i}V_{i}^{\top}=[\,\Sigma_{i}^{(k_{i})}\,|\,0\,]V_{i}^{\top}=\Sigma_{i}^{(k_{i})}V_{i}^{(k_{i})\top},
where \Sigma_{i}^{(k_{i})}=\diag(\sigma_{i,1},\ldots,\sigma_{i,k_{i}}) and V_{i}^{(k_{i})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times k_{i}}. Since U_{i}^{(k_{i})} has orthonormal columns, \left\|P_{i}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|U_{i}^{(k_{i})\top}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{(k_{i})}V_{i}^{(k_{i})\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\Sigma_{i}^{(k_{i})}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2} (using \left\|XY^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|X\right\|_{\mathrm{F}} when Y has orthonormal columns).
∎
Proof of Theorem B.2.
Let P^{(k)}:=\widehat{U}^{(k)}\widehat{U}^{(k)\top} be the orthogonal projector onto \mathrm{col}(\widehat{U}^{(k)}). By the construction of \widehat{U}^{(k)}, \mathrm{col}(\widehat{U}^{(k)})=\mathrm{col}(M^{(k)}). (This holds in particular for the orthogonal polar factor of M^{(k)} used in GRAM: if M^{(k)}=u\,s\,v^{\top} is the SVD, then uv^{\top} has the same column space as u, which equals \mathrm{col}(M^{(k)}).)
Since U_{i}^{(k_{i})} is one of the column blocks of M^{(k)} by construction, every column of U_{i}^{(k_{i})} lies in \mathrm{col}(M^{(k)})=\mathrm{col}(\widehat{U}^{(k)}), so
\mathrm{col}(P_{i})\;=\;\mathrm{col}(U_{i}^{(k_{i})})\;\subseteq\;\mathrm{col}(\widehat{U}^{(k)})\;=\;\mathrm{col}(P^{(k)}).
(25)
By Lemma C.2, \left\|P^{(k)}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq\left\|P_{i}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}, and by Lemma C.3, \left\|P_{i}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}. Combining,
\left\|P^{(k)}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\geq\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}.
(26)
Applying Lemma C.1 to P=P^{(k)} and A=\Delta W_{i} gives \left\|(I-P^{(k)})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\left\|P^{(k)}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}. Substituting the lower bound and using \left\|\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2},
\left\|(I-P^{(k)})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\leq\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{\ell=k_{i}+1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2},
(27)
which is (14).
Equality case (m=1). If the coalition has a single task, M^{(k)}=U_{i}^{(k_{i})} and \mathrm{col}(P^{(k)})=\mathrm{col}(P_{i}), so the inequality in Lemma C.2 is an equality, and the bound is attained.
∎
What this proof does and does not give.
The proof bounds the projection error onto the GRAM column basis, not the reconstruction error
\|\Delta W_{i}-\widetilde{\Delta W}^{(k)}\|_{F}^{2} of the merged delta itself. The latter decomposes orthogonally as
\|\Delta W_{i}-\widetilde{\Delta W}^{(k)}\|_{F}^{2}\;=\;\|(I-P^{(k)})\Delta W_{i}\|_{F}^{2}+\|P^{(k)}\Delta W_{i}-\widetilde{\Delta W}^{(k)}\|_{F}^{2},
(28)
and Theorem B.2 controls only the first term. The second term — the in-subspace fitting error of the single merged delta against task i’s in-subspace component — is not analyzed here and would require a separate argument tracking how \widetilde{\Delta W}^{(k)}=\widehat{U}^{(k)}\,\diag(\bar{s})\,\widehat{V}^{(k)\top} is constructed from the per-task singular values and right-singular vectors. The bound in Theorem B.2 is also loose precisely when other tasks in the coalition contribute new directions that align with \mathrm{col}(U_{i})\setminus\mathrm{col}(U_{i}^{(k_{i})}) — the regime in which grouping is most useful — because the proof discards all inter-task overlap by lower-bounding \left\|P^{(k)}\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} via the smaller projector P_{i} alone.
C.3 GRAM vs. global merge under uniform allocation (statement and proof)
Corollary C.4 (Grouping reduces the projection-error upper bound for every task).
Fix a LoRA rank budget \rho per merged adapter. Compare the global merge over all T tasks under uniform allocation k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}:=\lfloor\rho/T\rfloor to a GRAM coalition G_{k} of size m<T under uniform allocation k_{i}:=\lfloor\rho/m\rfloor. Then k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}\leq k_{i}, and for every task i\in G_{k} the projection-error upper bounds from Theorem B.2 satisfy
\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=k_{i}+1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}}_{\text{GRAM bound}}\;\leq\;\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}+1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}}_{\text{global bound}},\qquad\text{improvement}\;\;\geq\;\;\sum_{\ell=k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}+1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}\;\geq\;0.
(29)
The improvement is strictly positive whenever any of the singular values \sigma_{i,k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}+1},\ldots,\sigma_{i,k_{i}} is non-zero.
Proof.
T>m implies \lfloor\rho/T\rfloor\leq\lfloor\rho/m\rfloor, hence k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}\leq k_{i}. The tail sum \sum_{\ell=k+1}^{r}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2} is non-increasing in k, so the GRAM bound is no larger than the global bound; the difference is exactly \sum_{\ell=k_{i}^{\mathrm{glob}}+1}^{k_{i}}\sigma_{i,\ell}^{2}. Applying Theorem B.2 to both bases yields (29).
∎
Remark 1 (Tightness).
Corollary C.4 states a worst-case guarantee on the gap between GRAM and global merging at the level of upper bounds, not a comparison of actual projection errors; the looseness of Theorem B.2 discussed above carries through here. The multiplicative gap between alignment-aware and random grouping is captured by Theorem B.4, not this corollary.
C.4 Equivalence of chordal and Euclidean distances on projectors
To run k-means++ off the shelf we need a Euclidean embedding of the Grassmannian. Each subspace \mathrm{col}(U_{i}^{(\ell)}) has a unique orthogonal projector P_{i}^{(\ell)}:=U_{i}^{(\ell)}U_{i}^{(\ell)\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}, which is invariant to the choice of orthonormal basis for the subspace and serves as that embedding. On these projectors,
\left\|P_{i}^{(\ell)}-P_{j}^{(\ell)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;\left\|P_{i}^{(\ell)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|P_{j}^{(\ell)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\,\langle P_{i}^{(\ell)},P_{j}^{(\ell)}\rangle\;=\;2\,d_{c}^{2}\!\bigl(U_{i}^{(\ell)},\,U_{j}^{(\ell)}\bigr),
(30)
using \left\|P_{i}^{(\ell)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=r (rank-r idempotent) and \langle P_{i}^{(\ell)},P_{j}^{(\ell)}\rangle=\left\|U_{i}^{(\ell)\top}U_{j}^{(\ell)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}. The chordal distance on the Grassmannian is therefore Euclidean (up to a factor of 2) on the projector embedding.
C.5 Chordal Fréchet mean and the GRAM merge basis
Proposition C.5 (Fréchet mean under chordal distance).
Let \{U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} have orthonormal columns and let M:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}U_{i}U_{i}^{\top}. Any minimizer of \sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C) over C\in\mathrm{Gr}(r,d_{\mathrm{out}}) is a top-r invariant subspace of M; the minimizer is unique whenever \lambda_{r}(M)>\lambda_{r+1}(M), and otherwise any choice of r-dimensional subspace within the top eigenspace is optimal.
Proof.
Write d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C)=r-\left\|C^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=r-\tr(C^{\top}U_{i}U_{i}^{\top}C) for any orthonormal C\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}. Therefore
\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C)\;=\;mr-\tr\!\bigl(C^{\top}MC\bigr).
Minimizing over orthonormal C is equivalent to maximizing \tr(C^{\top}MC) where M\succeq 0. By the Ky Fan max-trace theorem, the maximum is \sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}(M) and is achieved when C spans the top-r eigenspace of M.
∎
Relation to the GRAM column-stacked basis.
Theorem B.2 concerns the column space of M^{(k)}=[U_{1}^{(k_{1})}\!\mid\!\cdots\!\mid\!U_{m}^{(k_{m})}], which has dimension at most R=\sum_{i}k_{i} (and is set to r by the GRAM allocation rule of section 4.3, which exhausts the rank budget). The Fréchet-mean basis above is the top-r eigenspace of \sum_{i}U_{i}U_{i}^{\top}, and the two coincide only when \mathrm{col}(M^{(k)}) equals that eigenspace; in general they differ. For example, with m=r=2, \Sigma_{i}=\sigma I_{2}, U_{1}=U_{2}=[e_{1},e_{2}], and allocation k_{1}=k_{2}=1, the selected column e_{1} from each task gives \mathrm{col}(M^{(k)})=\mathrm{span}(e_{1}) while the Fréchet mean is \mathrm{span}(e_{1},e_{2}). We therefore distinguish two regimes in the analysis below:
(i) the per-task projection-error upper bound eq. 14, which holds for any orthonormal basis of \mathrm{col}(M^{(k)}) — including the GRAM output — without invoking the Fréchet-mean identification; and
(ii) the latent-group analysis (k-means++ gap, error chain), in which \mathrm{Err}(\pi) is defined via the optimal-basis projection error and the Fréchet mean is invoked as the optimum, not as GRAM’s actual output. This separation makes the theory bridge orthogonal to whether GRAM’s column-stacked basis exactly matches the Fréchet mean.
C.6 Proof of Lemma B.3
Proof of Lemma B.3.
Let P:=BB^{\top} and S:=UU^{\top}+VV^{\top}. Using \left\|(I-P)\sigma X\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\bigl(\left\|X\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\left\|B^{\top}X\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr) with \left\|U\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|V\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=r and \left\|B^{\top}X\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\tr(B^{\top}XX^{\top}B),
\left\|(I-P)\sigma U\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|(I-P)\sigma V\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;\sigma^{2}\bigl(2r-\tr(B^{\top}S\,B)\bigr).
(31)
Since S=MM^{\top} with M:=[\,U\mid V\,]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times 2r}, the nonzero eigenvalues of S equal the nonzero eigenvalues of
M^{\top}M\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&U^{\top}V\\
V^{\top}U&I_{r}\end{pmatrix}.
The singular values of U^{\top}V are \cos\theta_{1},\ldots,\cos\theta_{r} (Golub–Van Loan), so M^{\top}M is unitarily similar to \bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\cos\theta_{j}\\
\cos\theta_{j}&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr) and its eigenvalues are \{1+\cos\theta_{j},\,1-\cos\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}. Since \cos\theta_{j}\geq 0, the top-r eigenvalues of S are \{1+\cos\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}. By the Ky Fan max-trace theorem,
\tr(B^{\top}S\,B)\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1+\cos\theta_{j}),
(32)
with equality when \mathrm{col}(B) is a top-r eigenspace of S, equivalently the top-r left singular subspace of [\,U\mid V\,]. Substituting (32) into (31) gives
\left\|(I-P)\sigma U\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|(I-P)\sigma V\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\geq\;\sigma^{2}\Bigl(2r-\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1+\cos\theta_{j})\Bigr)\;=\;\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1-\cos\theta_{j}),
which is (15).
∎
C.7 Proof of Theorem B.4
We prove each part of the theorem under the deterministic latent-group model: G groups, m tasks per group (T=mG), prototype subspaces Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{G} with pairwise principal angles \geq\phi_{\min}, and within-group spread d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},Q_{g(i)})\leq\epsilon for all i.
Proof of part (1): upper bound.
Define P_{i}=U_{i}U_{i}^{\top} and recall \left\|P_{i}-P_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=2\,d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},U_{j}).
Step 1 (seeding guarantee).
k-means++ selects K seeds s_{1},\ldots,s_{K} from \{P_{i}\} via D^{2}-sampling and assigns each P_{i} to its nearest seed, yielding partition \pi_{++}. By Arthur et al. [2007],
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in G_{k}^{++}}\left\|P_{i}-s_{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right]\;\leq\;8(\ln K+2)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{E},
(33)
where \mathrm{OPT}_{E}:=\min_{\pi,\{\mu_{k}\}}\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|P_{i}-\mu_{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
Step 2 (seeds are projections).
Each seed s_{k}=P_{j_{k}} is a rank-r projection matrix, hence a valid point on \mathrm{Gr}(r,d_{\mathrm{out}}). The chordal Fréchet mean minimizes cost over rank-r projections and can only reduce cost:
\Phi(\pi_{++})\;\leq\;\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}^{++}}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},U_{j_{k}})\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}^{++}}\left\|P_{i}-s_{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.
Step 3 (Euclidean \leq Grassmannian).
For any partition \pi and group G_{k}, the Euclidean mean \bar{P}_{k} minimizes \sum_{i}\left\|P_{i}-\mu\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} over all matrices \mu, while the Fréchet mean P_{C_{k}} is constrained to rank-r projections. Therefore \sum_{i}\left\|P_{i}-\bar{P}_{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\sum_{i}\left\|P_{i}-P_{C_{k}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=2\sum_{i}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C_{k}). Minimizing over C_{k} and summing: \mathrm{OPT}_{E}\leq 2\,\Phi(\pi^{*}).
Combining. \mathbb{E}[\Phi(\pi_{++})]\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot 8(\ln K+2)\cdot 2\,\Phi(\pi^{*})=8(\ln K+2)\,\Phi(\pi^{*}). Multiplying by \sigma^{2} gives \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})]\leq 8(\ln K+2)\,\mathrm{Err}(\pi^{*}).
Absolute bound. The latent partition \pi_{\mathrm{lat}} is feasible:
\Phi(\pi^{*})\leq\Phi(\pi_{\mathrm{lat}})=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{i:g(i)=g}\!d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C_{g}^{\mathrm{lat}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{T}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},Q_{g(i)})\leq T\epsilon,
where the second inequality uses d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C_{g}^{\mathrm{lat}})\leq d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},Q_{g(i)}) since C_{g}^{\mathrm{lat}} is the cost-minimizing center.
∎
Proof of part (2): lower bound.
Call G_{k} mixed if it contains tasks i,j with g(i)\neq g(j), and pick any such pair (i,j) in each mixed G_{k}. Let C_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be the chordal Fréchet mean of G_{k}, which is the minimizer in (16) for \Sigma_{l}=\sigma I_{r} by Proposition C.5. Lemma B.3 holds for every orthonormal B, so applying it with B=C_{k} to the pair (U_{i},U_{j}) and dropping the non-negative contributions from the remaining |G_{k}|-2 tasks,
\sum_{l\in G_{k}}\bigl\|(I-C_{k}C_{k}^{\top})\Delta W_{l}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;\sigma^{2}\sum_{s=1}^{r}\bigl(1-\cos\theta_{s}(U_{i},U_{j})\bigr).
(34)
The left side is exactly the contribution of G_{k} to \mathrm{Err}(\pi) (since C_{k} achieves the minimum in (16)), so this is a per-mixed-group lower bound on \mathrm{Err}(\pi).
Procrustes metric and triangle inequality.
Define \delta_{p}(U,V):=\bigl[2\sum_{s=1}^{r}\bigl(1-\cos\theta_{s}(U,V)\bigr)\bigr]^{1/2}=\min_{R\in O(r)}\left\|U-VR\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}, the Procrustes (chordal Fubini–Study) distance, which is a metric on \mathrm{Gr}(r,d_{\mathrm{out}}) [Edelman et al., 1998]; \delta_{p}^{2} denotes its square. We use two facts:
(a) since 1-\cos t\leq\sin^{2}t for t\in[0,\pi/2], \delta_{p}^{2}(U,V)\leq 2\sum_{s}\sin^{2}\theta_{s}=2d_{c}^{2}(U,V); and
(b) since 1-\cos t\geq(1-\cos t)\cdot 1 trivially with \theta_{s}(Q_{g},Q_{h})\geq\phi_{\min}, \delta_{p}^{2}(Q_{g},Q_{h})\geq 2r(1-\cos\phi_{\min}).
Combining (a) with d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},Q_{g})\leq\epsilon gives \delta_{p}(U_{i},Q_{g})\leq\sqrt{2\epsilon}, and likewise for j. By the triangle inequality for \delta_{p},
\delta_{p}(U_{i},U_{j})\;\geq\;\delta_{p}(Q_{g},Q_{h})-\delta_{p}(U_{i},Q_{g})-\delta_{p}(U_{j},Q_{h})\;\geq\;\sqrt{2r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})}-2\sqrt{2\epsilon}.
(35)
The right side is non-negative iff \epsilon\leq r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})/4, which is the hypothesis of part (2). Squaring and dropping the non-negative cross term,
\displaystyle\delta_{p}^{2}(U_{i},U_{j})
\displaystyle\;\geq\;2r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-2\cdot 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})}\cdot\sqrt{2\epsilon}
\displaystyle\;=\;2r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-8\sqrt{r\epsilon(1-\cos\phi_{\min})}
\displaystyle\;\geq\;2r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-8\sqrt{r\epsilon},
using 1-\cos\phi_{\min}\leq 1. Halving, \sum_{s=1}^{r}(1-\cos\theta_{s}(U_{i},U_{j}))\geq r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}. Substituting into (34), the per-mixed-group contribution to \mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}}) is at least \sigma^{2}[r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}].
Expected number of mixed groups.
Under a uniformly random equipartition into G groups of size m, each group is a random m-subset; exactly G of the \binom{T}{m} such subsets are monochromatic (one per latent group). Hence \mathbb{E}[N_{\mathrm{mix}}]=G\bigl(1-G/\binom{T}{m}\bigr). Linearity of expectation across mixed groups gives (18).
∎
Proof of part (3): global merge lower bound.
Let B^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} be the global merge basis (\pi_{\mathrm{global}} has a single block containing all T=mG tasks). We construct \lfloor T/2\rfloor pairwise-disjoint inter-group pairs and apply Lemma B.3 to each.
Existence of the matching.
With G\geq 2 latent groups of size m, no group contains more than T/2 tasks (since m\leq T/2 for G\geq 2). A standard graph-theoretic result then gives a matching of size \lfloor T/2\rfloor in the inter-group graph (the complete multipartite graph K_{m,m,\ldots,m}): order tasks as \tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{T} by interleaving across groups (one task from each group, then a second from each, etc.), and pair (\tau_{2k-1},\tau_{2k}) for k=1,\ldots,\lfloor T/2\rfloor; consecutive tasks in this ordering belong to different groups whenever G\geq 2.
Per-pair lower bound.
For each disjoint inter-group pair (i,j) with g(i)\neq g(j), applying Lemma B.3 with B=B^{*} and the Procrustes argument from the proof of part (2),
\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\geq\;\sigma^{2}\bigl[r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}\bigr],
(36)
valid for \epsilon\leq r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})/4.
Summation.
Summing (36) over the \lfloor T/2\rfloor disjoint pairs and adding the non-negative contribution of any unmatched task (when T is odd),
\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{global}})\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\geq\;\lfloor T/2\rfloor\,\sigma^{2}\bigl[r(1-\cos\phi_{\min})-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}\bigr],
which is (19).
∎
C.8 Proof of Corollary B.5
Proof of Corollary B.5.
Right inequality (deterministic).
For any partition \pi with groups \{G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}\}, let B^{(k)}_{\pi} minimize \sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} over orthonormal B, so \mathrm{Err}(\pi)=\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{(k)}_{\pi}B^{(k)\top}_{\pi})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}. Let B^{*} be the analogous global minimizer for \pi_{\mathrm{global}} (a single block of all T tasks). Substituting B=B^{*} as a feasible (sub-optimal) basis inside each group G_{k},
\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}(\pi)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{(k)}_{\pi}B^{(k)\top}_{\pi})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|(I-B^{*}B^{*\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{global}}).
This is exactly Theorem B.1; it is deterministic and holds in particular for \pi=\pi_{\mathrm{rand}}, hence \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}})]\leq\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{global}}).
Left inequality (in expectation, in the small-\epsilon regime).
Let \alpha:=1-\cos\phi_{\min}\in(0,2]. From Theorem B.4 parts (1) and (2),
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})]\leq 8(\ln K+2)\,\sigma^{2}T\epsilon,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}})]\geq G\bigl(1-\tfrac{G}{\binom{T}{m}}\bigr)\sigma^{2}[r\alpha-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}],
the latter valid for \epsilon\leq r\alpha/4. Sufficient for the left inequality is \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})], i.e.
G\bigl(1-\tfrac{G}{\binom{T}{m}}\bigr)[r\alpha-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}]\;\geq\;8(\ln K+2)\,T\epsilon.
Using T=mG and 1-G/\binom{T}{m}\geq 1/2 for G\geq 2, m\geq 2, this is implied by r\alpha-4\sqrt{r\epsilon}\geq 16(\ln K+2)\,m\,\epsilon. The bracketed term is \geq r\alpha/2 whenever \epsilon\leq r\alpha^{2}/64, and the right side is \leq r\alpha/2 whenever \epsilon\leq r\alpha/(32(\ln K+2)m). Combining these (using \alpha\leq 2, K\geq 2, m\geq 2, \ln K+2\leq 4\ln K for K\geq 2), both inequalities hold whenever \epsilon\leq c\,r\alpha^{2}/(m\ln K) for a sufficiently small absolute constant c>0. Hence \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{\mathrm{rand}})].
∎
C.9 Non-uniform singular values (statement and proof)
Proposition C.6 (Non-uniform singular values).
Let \kappa:=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min} with \sigma_{\max}:=\max_{i,k}\sigma_{i,k}, \sigma_{\min}:=\min_{i,k}\sigma_{i,k}, and recall the optimal-basis merge error (16). Then for any partition \pi,
\sigma_{\min}^{2}\,\Phi(\pi)\;\leq\;\mathrm{Err}(\pi)\;\leq\;\sigma_{\max}^{2}\,\Phi(\pi),
(37)
and consequently
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})\bigr]\;\leq\;8(\ln K+2)\,\kappa^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Err}(\pi^{*}_{w}),
(38)
where \pi^{*}_{w}:=\argmin_{\pi}\mathrm{Err}(\pi).
Proof.
Fix any orthonormal B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r} and any task i. Substituting the SVD \Delta W_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V_{i}^{\top} and using V_{i}^{\top}V_{i}=I_{r},
\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\tr\!\bigl(\Delta W_{i}^{\top}(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\bigr)=\tr\!\bigl(\Sigma_{i}^{2}\,A_{i}\bigr),\qquad A_{i}:=U_{i}^{\top}(I-BB^{\top})U_{i}\;\succeq\;0,
with \tr(A_{i})=r-\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},B) by (8). Since \sigma_{\min}^{2}I_{r}\preceq\Sigma_{i}^{2}\preceq\sigma_{\max}^{2}I_{r} in the Loewner order,
\sigma_{\min}^{2}\,d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},B)\;\leq\;\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\leq\;\sigma_{\max}^{2}\,d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},B).
(39)
Now fix a partition \pi. For the lower bound, let B^{(k)}_{w}:=\argmin_{B}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2} achieve \mathrm{Err}(\pi). By the lower side of (39),
\mathrm{Err}(\pi)\;=\;\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-B^{(k)}_{w}B^{(k)\top}_{w})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\geq\;\sigma_{\min}^{2}\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},B^{(k)}_{w})\;\geq\;\sigma_{\min}^{2}\,\Phi(\pi),
where the last inequality uses that \Phi(\pi) is the minimum of \sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C^{(k)}) over orthonormal C^{(k)}.
For the upper bound, let C^{(k)}_{*} be the chordal Fréchet mean of G_{k} (achieving \Phi(\pi)). Since C^{(k)}_{*} is a feasible (sub-optimal for the weighted problem) basis,
\mathrm{Err}(\pi)\;\leq\;\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}\left\|(I-C^{(k)}_{*}C^{(k)\top}_{*})\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\leq\;\sigma_{\max}^{2}\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in G_{k}}d_{c}^{2}(U_{i},C^{(k)}_{*})\;=\;\sigma_{\max}^{2}\,\Phi(\pi),
using the upper side of (39). This is (37).
For (38), let \pi^{*}_{w}:=\argmin_{\pi}\mathrm{Err}(\pi) and let \pi^{*}:=\argmin_{\pi}\Phi(\pi). Steps 1–3 of the proof of Theorem B.4 (1) are model-independent: they show \mathbb{E}[\Phi(\pi_{++})]\leq 8(\ln K+2)\,\Phi(\pi^{*}) for any data \{U_{i}\}, using only the k-means++ guarantee [Arthur et al., 2007] and the Euclidean–Grassmannian comparison. Combining this with (37),
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Err}(\pi_{++})\bigr]\;\leq\;\sigma_{\max}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\pi_{++})]
\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sigma_{\max}^{2}\cdot 8(\ln K+2)\,\Phi(\pi^{*})
\displaystyle\;\leq\;8(\ln K+2)\,\kappa^{2}\,\sigma_{\min}^{2}\,\Phi(\pi^{*}_{w})\;\leq\;8(\ln K+2)\,\kappa^{2}\,\mathrm{Err}(\pi^{*}_{w}),
using \Phi(\pi^{*})\leq\Phi(\pi^{*}_{w}) (definition of \pi^{*}) and \sigma_{\min}^{2}\Phi(\pi^{*}_{w})\leq\mathrm{Err}(\pi^{*}_{w}) (the lower side of (37)).
∎
C.10 Proof of Theorem B.6
Proof.
Let \epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n} be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables. Splitting each h\in\mathcal{H}_{a}(\beta) as h(x)=a^{\top}W_{0}x+a^{\top}\Delta W\,x, the W_{0} part is a fixed function of x (independent of \Delta W), so
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,h(x_{i})\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,a^{\top}W_{0}x_{i}\;+\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,a^{\top}\Delta W\,x_{i},
(40)
and the first term factors out of the supremum over \Delta W. Taking expectation over \epsilon and using \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}]=0 to kill the first term,
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{a}(\beta))\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\!\left[\sup_{\left\|\Delta W\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\beta}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\,a^{\top}\Delta W\,x_{i}\right].
(41)
Step 1: rewrite as a Frobenius inner product.
For any \Delta W, a^{\top}\Delta W\,x_{i}=\tr(a^{\top}\Delta Wx_{i})=\langle\Delta W,\,ax_{i}^{\top}\rangle_{F}, where \langle A,B\rangle_{F}=\tr(A^{\top}B). Linearity gives
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\,a^{\top}\Delta W\,x_{i}\;=\;\Bigl\langle\Delta W,\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\,ax_{i}^{\top}\Bigr\rangle_{F}.
(42)
Step 2: supremum via Cauchy–Schwarz.
Let G_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\,ax_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the Frobenius inner product,
\sup_{\left\|\Delta W\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\beta}\langle\Delta W,G_{n}\rangle_{F}\;=\;\beta\,\left\|G_{n}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.
(43)
Moreover, G_{n}=a\bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\bigr) is a rank-one matrix of the form av^{\top} with v=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}, so
\left\|G_{n}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|av^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|a\|_{2}\,\|v\|_{2}=\|v\|_{2} (using \|a\|_{2}=1).
Step 3: bound \mathbb{E}\|v\|_{2} via Jensen and independence.
By Jensen’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\|v\|_{2}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\|v\|_{2}^{\,2}}\;=\;\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\,x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}\|_{2}^{\,2}}\;\leq\;\frac{R}{\sqrt{n}},
(44)
where the cross-terms vanish because \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}]=\delta_{ij}.
Step 4: combine.
Taking expectation over \epsilon,
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{a}(\beta))\;=\;\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left\|G_{n}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\;\leq\;\beta\,\frac{R}{\sqrt{n}},
(45)
which is (21).
∎
Remark on rank.
The bound makes no use of \rank(\Delta W)\leq r, so it applies uniformly to any Frobenius-bounded linear adapter, low-rank or not. A rank constraint would buy a sharper bound only if it were combined with a nuclear-norm constraint \|\Delta W\|_{*}\leq\sqrt{r}\beta via standard low-rank Rademacher arguments; we do not pursue this since for our purposes the rank-free bound already suffices to show that grouping does not enlarge the hypothesis class.

D.2 Deployment without Oracle Task Identity
GRAM targets task-aware deployments—e.g., a multi-institution medical system where each request carries its provenance, so task identity is deployment metadata rather than an inference problem. Nevertheless, GRAM extends to the task-agnostic setting at no accuracy cost via a lightweight router. We instantiate the Mahalanobis task mapper of Lee et al. [2018], as deployed for task recognition in shared-knowledge lifelong learning (SKILL) [Ge et al., 2023]: class-conditional Gaussians in the frozen base encoder’s feature space (mean-pooled hidden states; no extra model), class means estimated from only m{=}5 training samples per class (\approx 85 raw examples total across all 8 tasks), and one tied covariance (Ledoit–Wolf). A test input is routed to the group owning its nearest class centroid. The router involves no gradient training and no test-time task labels; merging itself remains fully data-free, and the router’s \approx 85-example requirement is strictly weaker than the 64-samples-per-task calibration consumed by the data-using baselines (RegMean, Fisher-Merge) in section 5.4. On Flan-T5-Base / GLUE at K{=}2, the router routes 100.0\% of validation examples of every task to the correct group, so task-agnostic GRAM equals oracle GRAM—83.8 on every task, to the decimal.

Small K is what makes routing easy. The router encodes the same formatted request the instruction-tuned model itself consumes, and it only needs to resolve groups, not tasks: at K{=}2 it faces a binary decision, and any confusion between tasks in the same coalition is harmless by construction. Table 8 reports the mapper’s group-routing accuracy under the partition chain for every K. The mapper’s only errors, at any K, are QQP\leftrightarrowMRPC confusions (72 of 40{,}430 QQP examples, 0.18\%)—two semantically adjacent paraphrase-detection tasks. These errors are invisible for K\leq 5 because GRAM’s geometry-aware clustering places QQP and MRPC in the same coalition, and only surface once K\geq 6 separates them. This reveals a structural synergy: a router’s confusions occur precisely between similar tasks, and similar tasks are precisely what GRAM groups together—the coalition structure absorbs router errors by design. A per-task deployment (K{=}8) forgoes this protection and demands full task identification, exactly the harder regime studied in SKILL (87.1\% over 102 tasks) [Ge et al., 2023].
D.3 Per-Task Intrinsic Rank Heterogeneity
Figure 4 reports the number of singular values needed to capture \geq 99\% of the per-layer Frobenius energy, computed over the 8 GLUE tasks and 72 LoRA layers of Flan-T5-Base. Uniform per-task allocation r/|G_{k}| inside a coalition either wastes slots on compact-spectrum tasks or truncates long-tail ones; the adaptive allocator of section 4.3 reads off this heterogeneity directly from the singular values and assigns rank where it actually reduces error.

D.6 Implementation Details
Flan-T5 GLUE adapters.
On both Flan-T5-Base and Flan-T5-Large we use LoRA with rank r=16 and \alpha=32 (scaling factor 2.0), applied to the query and value projections in self-attention and (in the decoder) cross-attention. Each LoRA delta has shape 768\times 768 on Base and 1024\times 1024 on Large; the number of adapted layers is L=72 on Base (12 encoder + 12 decoder blocks) and L=144 on Large (24 encoder + 24 decoder blocks). Per-task adapters are trained with AdamW (learning rate 5\times 10^{-4}, batch size 16) for 10 epochs; we report on the GLUE validation splits, with Spearman \rho{\times}100 on STS-B and accuracy on the remaining tasks.
CLIP ViT vision adapters.
We use the per-task fully fine-tuned CLIP ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14 checkpoints released by TSV-Merge [Gargiulo et al., 2025], evaluated on the standard 8-task vision benchmark (MNIST, SVHN, GTSRB, DTD, Cars, RESISC45, EuroSAT, SUN397). The ViT-B/16 results in section D.5 are produced under the same protocol on a fully fine-tuned ViT-B/16 backbone. Since the per-task checkpoints are fully fine-tuned, the per-layer task delta \Delta W_{i}^{(\ell)}=W_{i}^{(\ell)}-W_{0}^{(\ell)} is full-rank; we apply SVD truncation at merge time with rank budget r{=}16 per layer (matching the GLUE setting), which is the rank actually flowing into the chordal distance and the per-coalition merge. As stated in section 3, the matrix formulation applies to linear layers: SVD-based merging is applied to all 2-D weight matrices (MLP blocks and attention projections), while the remaining non-matrix parameters (LayerNorm gains, biases) are merged by simple averaging. All vision results report test accuracy. No additional task-specific training is performed; GRAM and all baselines operate on these checkpoints directly.
Qwen2 medical adapters.
We use four publicly released LoRA adapters on Qwen2-7B-Instruct, one per medical sub-domain: MedQA, CMExam, HealthcareMagic, and cMedQA2. Each adapter uses LoRA with rank r=8 and \alpha=16, applied to the attention (q,k,v,o) and MLP (gate, up, down) projections of every decoder block. GRAM operates directly on these adapters without further training.
Calibration set for data-using baselines.
Fisher-Merge [Matena and Raffel, 2022] and RegMean [Jin et al., 2022] require a small calibration set per task at merge time. We use a uniform 64-sample-per-task calibration set drawn from each task’s training split: Fisher-Merge uses these samples to estimate per-parameter Fisher information, and RegMean uses them to estimate input second-moment matrices for the closed-form layer-wise solver. The data-free baselines (Weight Averaging, Task Arithmetic, TIES-Merging, Iso-C, Iso-CTS, TSV-Merge) and GRAM see no calibration data.
Clustering.
For each model family we compute the T\times T pairwise chordal distance matrix (averaged across all L adapted layers) and run k-means++ with 20 random initializations on the projection-matrix embeddings. We set K=2 as the number of groups throughout.
Computational cost.
For the Flan-T5-Base/GLUE setup (T{=}8, L{=}72), SVD decomposition of all T\times L task deltas takes approximately 20 seconds on CPU; distance-matrix construction and k-means++ clustering complete in under 1 second; per-group SVD merging adds negligible overhead. Total wall-clock time for the GRAM pipeline is under 30 seconds, compared to hours of adapter training per task. The other model families exhibit the same qualitative cost structure: SVD dominates and is bounded by the per-layer cost O(d_{\mathrm{out}}d_{\mathrm{in}}r) summed over TL deltas.
D.7 Medical Benchmark Details
Task suite.
The medical benchmark covers two task families crossed with two languages:
•
Multiple-choice QA (MCQA). MedQA (English) and CMExam (Chinese): the model selects one option from a candidate set; reported metric is exact-match accuracy.
•
Doctor–patient dialogue (open-ended generation). HealthcareMagic (English) and cMedQA2 (Chinese): the model generates a free-form answer to a patient query; reported metrics are BLEU-4 and ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L (F1) against the reference response.
The 4 task families with 1 metric each (MCQA accuracy) plus 4 metrics each (BLEU-4, ROUGE-1/2/L) on the two open-ended tasks yield the 10 heterogeneous metric columns reported in table 3.
Summary metrics.
Avg Rank is the mean rank across the 10 columns, where rank 1 is the best per-column merging method (the per-task Individual oracle is excluded from ranking). Avg Impr is the per-column relative improvement over the un-adapted Pretrained baseline, averaged across the 10 columns: for each metric column j and method M, we compute (\mathrm{score}_{M,j}-\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{Pretrained},j})/\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{Pretrained},j}, then average over j. This normalizes for the wide range of metric magnitudes (MCQA accuracy in tens of points; ROUGE-2 in single digits).
