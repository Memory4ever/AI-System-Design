Abstract
A model’s failures can reveal gaps in the behavioral criteria guiding its training.
We introduce RubricOpt, a framework that lets a policy revise these criteria within preference optimization.
The policy diagnoses its own rubric-free outputs, retains requirements already covered by the rubric, and edits clauses that leave recurring failures unaddressed.
A shared semantic representation connects task demands, preference-data contrasts, and rubric clauses, while a deterministic gate checks evidence references and fixed semantic anchors.
The rubric then guides Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) during training, with preference pairs, labels, and the objective held fixed, and is removed at evaluation.
Our analysis relates the local effect on rubric-free success to semantic alignment and a residual beyond the measured factors.
Experiments on capability and open-ended generation benchmarks show that rubric conditioning can improve outcomes over ordinary DPO, with gains and regressions varying across models and datasets.
A matched continuation isolates the contribution of revision from additional training and finds mixed incremental effects for the tested edit, whose feedback overlaps the evaluation set.
RubricOpt thus brings the behavioral specification itself into the policy’s feedback loop.
 1 Introduction
Language models can supply rewards for their own iterative preference training  ( Yuan et al., 2024 ) .
They can also improve their judgment skills by assessing their own judgments  ( Wu et al., 2025a ) .
The corresponding question for semantic training rubrics is how revising their behavioral requirements affects learned behavior, including when a revision yields gains beyond continuing training with the existing rubric.
Written rubrics specify desired response properties and can supply reward signals  ( Gunjal et al., 2025 ) or guide the construction of synthetic preference data  ( Gallego, 2025 ) .
EvoRubrics jointly trains a policy and a rubric generator to adapt rubric-based rewards as learning proceeds  ( Ding et al., 2026 ) .
SERPO co-evolves response evidence, query-specific rubrics, and policy parameters, using rubric scores as rewards for test-time reinforcement learning  ( Wang et al., 2026a ) .
We study rubric revision in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) , holding the pairs, labels, and objective fixed while changing the rubric context.
A matched continuation with the existing rubric measures the edit’s incremental contribution.

We introduce RubricOpt to study how a model revises its own training rubric across preference-training rounds ( Fig.   1 ).
Fine-grained evaluation describes response quality through explicit skills  ( Ye et al., 2024 ) .
We use a fixed eight-factor ontology to connect the model’s task failures, the contrasts in its preference data, and the rubric’s Prefer/Avoid clauses.
Diagnosis-guided self-evolution uses failure histories to direct subsequent learning  ( Wei et al., 2026 ) .
RubricOpt applies this diagnostic idea to rubric revision, with the current policy reading task outcomes, self-judged factor scores, and judge feedback on its own outputs to attribute recurring failures to the eight factors.
It retains explicit requirements for further learning and proposes clause edits where the wording leaves recurring failures uncovered.
A deterministic gate checks evidence references, factor anchors, and edit size before carrying the rubric into the next round.
Training-only Semantic Rubric Steering uses the resulting rubric to condition both responses of a preference pair during DPO  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) , with the pairs, labels, and objective held fixed.
Its exposure decreases during training, and evaluation uses the resulting policy without the rubric.
This use of training-only information has a precedent in privileged-information learning  ( Vapnik & Vashist, 2009 ) .
Our analysis relates the extra update induced by a rubric to rubric-free success through its alignment with task demand ( Sec.   3.3 ).
The experiments evaluate how changes to the training specification affect comprehensive capability and open-ended generation.
Fixed-state probes and criterion comparisons show that rubric text changes the learning update and can alter rubric-free outcomes ( Sec.   4.1 ).
Across model families and preference corpora, rubric conditioning improves some capability and generation evaluations over ordinary DPO, with gains and regressions varying by setting ( Tabs.   1 and  2 ).
The dataset comparison relates these effect profiles to the behavioral contrasts available in each corpus ( Sec.   4.2 ).
A matched continuation separates revision from further optimization and finds mixed incremental results from the tested edit ( Tab.   3 ).
RubricOpt makes the behavioral specification itself an object of policy-driven revision within preference learning.
Shared semantic coordinates connect failure diagnosis and preference-data contrasts to editable rubric clauses, while training-only steering connects those clauses to subsequent parameter updates.
This closes the loop from learned behavior to the specification of further learning, with the effect of revision evaluated independently of additional training.
 2 Related Work
Preference Optimization and Credit Assignment.
The family descends from RLHF, in which a reward model is fit to pairwise human judgements  ( Stiennon et al., 2020 ) and a policy is then optimised against it at assistant scale  ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ; Bai et al., 2022 ) .
DPO instead fits chosen–rejected pairs with a classification objective  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) , whose implicit reward is the log density ratio between the policy and the reference  ( Sugiyama et al., 2012 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) .
Later objectives change one component of that recipe: IPO replaces it with a squared loss on the pairwise margin  ( Azar et al., 2024 ) , KTO drops the pairing requirement by scoring each response against a reference point  ( Ethayarajh et al., 2024 ) , and SimPO removes the reference model and normalises the margin by length  ( Meng et al., 2024 ) .
Two analyses place the family in a wider frame: one connects these objectives to imitation learning  ( Xiao et al., 2025 ) , and one disentangles how their update suppresses the rejected response and preserves the chosen one  ( Chen et al., 2026 ) .
A complementary line assigns credit inside a response rather than to the response as a whole: TDPO reweights tokens by a forward-KL penalty  ( Zeng et al., 2024 ) , TIS-DPO reweights them by importance sampling against an estimated weight  ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , SEPO selects tokens using a token-level reward estimate  ( Yang et al., 2025b ) , T-REG uses a token-level reward as a regulariser  ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) , and TIDPO guides the update by token importance  ( Yang et al., 2026 ) .
RubricOpt operates at a different level: a semantic factor names a behavioral distinction that can span several tokens or reasoning steps, while the pairwise loss itself is left unchanged.
Criteria and Behavioral Diagnosis.
Written criteria already enter learning through several interfaces.
As reward signals, Rubrics as Rewards scores on-policy responses against a rubric  ( Gunjal et al., 2025 ) .
As data, Configurable Preference Tuning builds rubric-conditioned synthetic preference pairs  ( Gallego, 2025 ) .
And as weights, CoRT contrasts rubric-conditioned with rubric-free replays to form token-level weights  ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) .
Criterion-conditioned training also has close relatives outside preference optimisation: learning using privileged information trains on a signal that is absent at test time  ( Vapnik & Vashist, 2009 ) .
Context distillation trains on a context and then evaluates without it, introduced as an alignment technique  ( Askell et al., 2021 ) and developed as a standalone method  ( Snell et al., 2022 ) .
And classifier-free guidance interpolates between conditioned and unconditioned predictions  ( Ho & Salimans, 2022 ) .
On the diagnostic side, fine-grained evaluation describes response quality through interpretable skill sets and error taxonomies—FLASK by grading along skill dimensions  ( Ye et al., 2024 ) , EvalTree by profiling weaknesses as a hierarchical capability tree  ( Zeng et al., 2025 ) , and ErrorMap and ErrorAtlas by charting the failure landscape into error categories   ( Ashury-Tahan et al., 2026 ) —and diagnosis-driven post-training then acts on what those tools find, with DiagEvo feeding diagnosed errors back into self-evolution through a hierarchical error memory  ( Wei et al., 2026 ) and APTER turning expert-grounded rubrics into post-training supervision   ( Wang et al., 2026b ) .
RubricOpt uses shared semantic coordinates to connect task diagnosis, preference-data contrasts, and edits to the training rubric.
Self-Improvement and Recursive Feedback.
Self-improvement methods differ in the component they revise and how that revision enters subsequent behavior.
Self-Refine edits outputs from self-feedback  ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) , and automatic prompt optimisation edits inference-time text from feedback  ( Pryzant et al., 2023 ) .
In parameter learning, Self-Rewarding uses the model to generate its own reward signals  ( Yuan et al., 2024 ) , Meta-Rewarding trains judgment skills through a meta-judge  ( Wu et al., 2025a ) , and self-play preference optimisation regenerates preferences  ( Wu et al., 2025b ) .
Rubric evolution also enters the reward interface through joint policy–generator learning  ( Ding et al., 2026 ) and co-evolving query-specific rubrics during test-time reinforcement learning  ( Wang et al., 2026a ) .
RubricOpt places self-revision in the semantic rubric that conditions preference training.
The current policy proposes clause edits from feedback about its own behavior, then learns under the resulting rubric in the next round.
The initial clause bank, factor ontology, task outcomes, and fixed scoring prompt provide the reference points for revision.
 3 RubricOpt: Method and Analysis
RubricOpt alternates preference learning under a written rubric with revision of that rubric by the resulting policy.
Problem Setup.
We study preference optimization on a fixed dataset \mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{+},{\bm{y}}^{-})\} of chosen–rejected pairs, with a trainable policy \pi_{\theta} and a frozen reference \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} trained by DPO  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) ,
 \mathcal{L}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\left[\bar{\ell}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}^{+}\mid{\bm{x}})-\bar{\ell}_{\mathrm{ref}}({\bm{y}}^{+}\mid{\bm{x}})-\bar{\ell}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}^{-}\mid{\bm{x}})+\bar{\ell}_{\mathrm{ref}}({\bm{y}}^{-}\mid{\bm{x}})\right]\right)\right], 
 (1) 
with \bar{\ell}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}})=L^{-1}\log\pi({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}) averaged over the L response tokens.
The pairs, labels, and objective are held fixed; only the text that conditions a pair may change, which is the degree of freedom RubricOpt introduces.
Behavior is described by a fixed eight-factor ontology \mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{8}\} (appendix  A.2 ), on which a blind judge returns one ordinal score per factor, z_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\{0,\ldots,4\} (appendix  A.3 ).
Every behavioral quantity here is a contrast between two groups of these scores, and only two contrasts are used.
Responses to a benchmark B are grouped by the task outcome o\in\{0,1\} , and semantic demand is the mean score gap between the groups, measured for one model on one task,
 D_{m,B,k}=\mathbb{E}[z_{k}\mid o=1,m,B]-\mathbb{E}[z_{k}\mid o=0,m,B]. 
 (2) 
Inside a preference pair the two responses are contrasted by \Delta z_{k}=z_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{+})-z_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{-}) , and semantic supply summarizes its distribution,
 \mu_{k}=\mathbb{E}[\Delta z_{k}],\qquad C_{k}=\Pr(\Delta z_{k}\neq 0),\qquad P_{k}=\Pr(\Delta z_{k}>0), 
 (3) 
as the mean signed contrast, the contrast coverage, and the share of pairs whose contrast agrees with the label.
Demand is a property of the model–task pair and supply a property of the dataset, and both live on the same eight axes.
The artifact RubricOpt edits is a rubric R_{r} , a short natural-language specification whose clauses are keyed to factors, with r indexing rounds and a frozen clause bank supplying the initial R_{0} .
For factor group \mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F} the training context is
 c_{r}=\operatorname{render}(R_{r},\mathcal{G}), 
 (4) 
where the renderer concatenates the current Prefer/Avoid clauses in the fixed factor order and \varnothing denotes the absent criterion.
Here \mathcal{G}=\mathcal{F} except in the factor-set ablation of Sec.   4.2 .
A conditioning context is therefore u\in\{c_{r},\varnothing\} , \pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},u) is the policy under that context, the main evaluations use \pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}) , and Sec.   4.2 tests rubric reinsertion at inference time.
Appendix  A.1 collects the symbols used throughout.
 3.1 Diagnosis and Conditioning
Shared Behavioral Coordinates.
With the coordinates of Sec.   3 fixed, the diagnosis is a measurement on one model and one dataset: we score responses from Qwen3-4B on each benchmark and each HelpSteer3 pair, then read out demand D_{m,B,k} ( Eq.   2 ) and supply \mu_{k} , C_{k} , P_{k} ( Eq.   3 ).
We mark demand gaps of at least 0.15 on the 0 – 4 scale as meaningful and contrast coverage below 0.25 as sparse.
 Tab.   7 reports the complete Qwen3-4B demand matrix. Judge checks and length controls appear in appendices  A.3 and  D.1 .
The measurement separates the factors into Common ( f_{2} reasoning validity, f_{5} completeness), Specific ( f_{4} factual grounding, f_{7} clarity, f_{8} efficiency), and Low-demand ( f_{1} comprehension, f_{3} verification, f_{6} instruction adherence).
The shared factor ontology links task diagnosis, preference-data contrasts, and clause-level feedback.
All eight factors enter the initial rubric from the frozen clause bank.
The demand and supply measurements characterize the learning setting; recurring failures whose requirements are incompletely expressed determine subsequent wording edits.
Training-Only Conditioning.
Each round fixes R_{r} and its full-factor context c_{r}=\operatorname{render}(R_{r},\mathcal{F}) .
At step t of N , let \rho_{t}=t/N and independently sample u_{t}=c_{r} with probability q(\rho_{t}) and u_{t}=\varnothing otherwise for each pair, sharing it across both responses, the policy, and the frozen reference.
Exposure is q(\rho)=1 for \rho\leq 0.1 , \{1+\cos[\pi(\rho-0.1)/0.8]\}/2 for 0.1<\rho\leq 0.9 , and 0 thereafter.
With response-token mean log-probability \bar{\ell}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},u)=L({\bm{y}})^{-1}\log\pi({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},u) , training uses the unchanged DPO objective
 \displaystyle M_{u} 
 \displaystyle=\bar{\ell}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}^{+}\mid{\bm{x}},u)-\bar{\ell}_{\rm ref}({\bm{y}}^{+}\mid{\bm{x}},u)-\bar{\ell}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}^{-}\mid{\bm{x}},u)+\bar{\ell}_{\rm ref}({\bm{y}}^{-}\mid{\bm{x}},u), 
 (5) 
 \displaystyle\mathcal{L} 
 \displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[\log\sigma(\beta M_{u_{t}})]. 
Rubric tokens are masked from the loss and excluded from response-length normalization.
Evaluation uses \pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}) without a rubric.
 3.2 Evidence-Grounded Recursive Refinement
Given rubric R_{r} , the policy trained with its rendered context c_{r} , and item-level benchmark feedback \mathcal{B}_{r+1} , one iteration is
 R_{r}\longrightarrow c_{r}\longrightarrow\pi_{r+1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{r+1}\longrightarrow R_{r+1}. 
 (6) 
Five prompt stages extract benchmark failures, synthesize recurring patterns, attribute them to the eight factors, propose conservative clause updates, and audit the candidate rubric.
The policy \pi_{r+1} performs this analysis and proposes the edits itself, using task outcomes, self-judged factor scores, and fixed-judge feedback on its own rubric-free outputs.
Attribution compares each recurring failure with the current clauses. An explicit requirement is retained for further learning, while an incompletely expressed requirement is eligible for refinement.
A separate deterministic gate checks evidence references, factor anchors, and edit size before accepting a change.
A covered requirement or a failed check returns keep .
The accepted rubric is rendered as c_{r+1}=\operatorname{render}(R_{r+1},\mathcal{G}) for the next training round; keep retains the corresponding clauses from R_{r} .
Full prompts, stage contracts, and the deterministic acceptance checks are in appendix  C.2 .

The audit is advisory; step 6 decides acceptance. Covered requirements and rejected edits retain the original clause. The excerpt shows the factual-grounding edit.  3.3 Analysis of the Steering Effect
The Update and Its Rubric-Free Effect.
Fix one round, with rubric context c=c_{r} and absent context 0=\varnothing .
For s_{u}({\bm{y}})=L^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},u) , the per-pair loss gradient is
 g_{u}=\nabla_{\theta}[-\log\sigma(\beta M_{u})]=-\kappa_{u}[s_{u}({\bm{y}}^{+})-s_{u}({\bm{y}}^{-})],\qquad\kappa_{u}=\beta\sigma(-\beta M_{u}), 
 (7) 
Let d_{u}=s_{u}({\bm{y}}^{+})-s_{u}({\bm{y}}^{-}) and v_{u}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\kappa_{u}d_{u}] be the mean descent direction.
Independent pair-level context sampling gives v_{q}=v_{0}+q\Delta v , where \Delta v=v_{c}-v_{0} is the rubric’s extra update direction.
Fix the evaluation prompt law \nu , binary success rule o_{B} , and factor scores z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{8})^{\top} .
Assume finite prompt and response spaces and a positive, continuously differentiable policy.
Rubric-free deployment uses P_{\theta}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\nu({\bm{x}})\pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},0) , with success probability p=p_{B}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\theta}}[o_{B}]\in(0,1) .
The extra direction changes deployment log-probabilities at rate
 h({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},0),\Delta v\right\rangle. 
 (8) 
Let \bm{D}=\mathbb{E}[z\mid o_{B}=1]-\mathbb{E}[z\mid o_{B}=0] be population demand under P_{\theta} , and a the least-squares coefficients of h on centered factor scores.
Write \epsilon_{h},\epsilon_{o} for the root-mean-square projection residuals of h and o_{B}-p , respectively; the full construction is in appendix  B.2 .
 Proposition 1 (Local success from semantic alignment) 
 At a shared parameter state and reference, with the update directions held fixed, 
 \left\langle\nabla_{\theta}p_{B},v_{q}-v_{0}\right\rangle=q\,p(1-p)\bigl(\bm{D}^{\top}a+\mathcal{R}\bigr), 
 (9) 
 where |\mathcal{R}|\leq\epsilon_{o}\epsilon_{h}/[p(1-p)] .
For q>0 , if \bm{D}^{\top}a>\epsilon_{o}\epsilon_{h}/[p(1-p)] , then
 p_{B}(\theta+\eta v_{q})>p_{B}(\theta+\eta v_{0}) for all sufficiently small \eta>0 .
The comparison reverses when \bm{D}^{\top}a<-\epsilon_{o}\epsilon_{h}/[p(1-p)] . 
Demand \bm{D} describes the behaviors associated with success; a expresses the rubric-induced change in deployment log-probabilities in those semantic coordinates.
Their alignment sets the sign of the local success effect when it dominates the residual contribution outside the measured factors.
Changing the preference corpus can change the update directions and hence this alignment, even under the same rubric.
For two rendered rubrics c_{0},c_{1} at a shared policy and reference, replacing \Delta v by v_{c_{1}}-v_{c_{0}} gives the local edit comparison at a common exposure rate.
This comparison motivates the matched keep / refine continuation in Sec.   4.3 , which measures the edit’s incremental effect over further training with the existing rubric.
 4 Experiments
The analysis in Sec.   3.3 organizes three empirical questions: how rubric text changes the training update, how conditioning affects rubric-free outcomes across datasets, and what an edit adds beyond continuing with the existing rubric.
We examine them through fixed-state probes and a criterion-identity pilot, dataset-matched ordinary-DPO controls, and matched keep / refine continuations, respectively.
Models and Datasets.
We cover two families and three sizes: Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B-Instruct from the Qwen3 family  ( Yang et al., 2025a ) , and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 from the Mistral family  ( Jiang et al., 2023 ) .
Formal runs train a rank- 64 LoRA adapter ( \alpha=128 , dropout 0.05 ) at learning rate 10^{-6} , global batch 64 , DPO \beta=1 .
We use two public preference datasets, HelpSteer3  ( Wang et al., 2025 ) and Tulu3  ( Lambert et al., 2025 ) .
Benchmark Classes.
Evaluation covers two benchmark classes, summarized separately.
The comprehensive-capability suite is seven accuracy benchmarks—GSM8K  ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) , MATH-Hard  ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) , BBH  ( Suzgun et al., 2023 ) , MuSR  ( Sprague et al., 2024 ) , ARC  ( Clark et al., 2018 ) , TruthfulQA  ( Lin et al., 2022 ) , and MMLU-Pro  ( Wang et al., 2024 ) —each with a task check defining the binary success outcome o_{B} in Prop.   1 .
The open-ended generative suite is three protocols—MT-Bench  ( Zheng et al., 2023 ) , Arena-Hard  ( Li et al., 2024 ) , and WildBench  ( Lin et al., 2024 ) —on which a judge scores free-form responses and there is no task check.
Generative protocols are scored on matched item sets under one judge.
Feedback and Evaluation.
The refinement engine reads item-level outputs from first-round rubric-free benchmark evaluations: judge rationales on Arena-Hard, MT-Bench, and WildBench, and correctness feedback on GSM8K, MATH-Hard, and MMLU-Pro.
The keep and refine continuations are then compared on matched evaluation items under the same benchmark protocols.
This evaluates a rubric update informed by benchmark feedback, with the unchanged-rubric branch controlling for additional training.
Feedback items overlap the final evaluation set, so the continuation comparison measures adaptation to the observed benchmark feedback.
The diagnostic/held-out split in Sec.   3.1 assesses measurement stability.
Generation, judging, extraction, paired tests, and all absolute scores are in appendix  D.1 .
 4.1 How Rubric Text Changes Learning
Context-Dependent Updates.
In Eq.   7 , context enters the update through both the margin-dependent weight \kappa_{u} and the pair direction d_{u} .
A fixed-state probe over 7{,}253 preference pairs gives conditioned–unconditioned gradient cosines of 0.74 – 0.79 and relative gradient changes of 0.65 – 0.72 .
The likelihood probe measures the semantic sensitivity of the pairwise margin: the f_{5} completeness criterion’s margin-change slope is 0.0226 nats per response token per unit of \Delta z_{5} , compared with 0.0116 when read against reasoning contrast \Delta z_{2} .
Criterion Identity at Fixed Exposure.
Changing the criterion text can change v_{c} in Sec.   3.3 while preserving the conditioning interface.
A 150 -step pilot compares reasoning ( f_{2} ) and clarity ( f_{7} ) criteria of nearly equal length, using the same pairs and conditioned rows at q=0.2 .
MuSR pass@4 is 70.8\% under the reasoning criterion, 68.6\% under the clarity criterion, and 70.8\% under ordinary DPO.
The paired f_{2} – f_{7} comparison has 17 prompts passing only under f_{2} and 6 only under f_{7} (appendix  C.1 ).
The two criterion texts produce different rubric-free outcomes on MuSR pass@4, with the reasoning criterion matching ordinary DPO there.
 4.2 Dataset Contrasts and Rubric-Free Outcomes
The Two Datasets Contrast Different Factors.
The shared coordinates of Sec.   3 are what make the two datasets comparable.
 Fig.   3 a reports contrast coverage C_{k} on both, and Tab.   8 gives the HelpSteer3 statistics behind it.
HelpSteer3 leaves three factors sparse against the 0.25 sparsity rule: f_{2} reasoning validity ( 0.146 ), f_{4} factual and evidential grounding ( 0.149 ), and f_{3} verification and calibration ( 0.220 ).
Reasoning validity ( f_{2} ) is meaningful on five of the seven benchmarks but is contrasted in only 14.6\% of HelpSteer3 pairs, while completeness ( f_{5} ) is meaningful on four and contrasted in 47.4\% .
Tulu3 contrasts seven of the eight factors more often than HelpSteer3 does, and no factor of its eight falls below that rule; its lowest coverage is 0.294 .
Its largest advantages over HelpSteer3 are on f_{2} ( +0.268 ), f_{8} ( +0.199 ), f_{4} ( +0.161 ), and f_{7} ( +0.135 ).
These profiles describe the frequency of behavioral contrasts on the same axes used for task demand in Sec.   3.1 .
The full demand matrix is Tab.   7 , and across diagnostic and held-out subsets its largest change is 0.045 , with every gap keeping its sign.
 Fig.   3 b groups the same gaps by task family: verification and calibration separates success from failure about six times more strongly on the knowledge benchmarks than on the reasoning benchmarks ( 0.131 against 0.020 ), and clarity and organization runs the other way ( 0.051 against 0.105 ).
Demand profiles differ between the two Qwen3 models in a task-dependent way ( Fig.   4 ).
On BBH, the eight-factor profiles of Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B are negatively correlated ( r=-0.751 ): completeness and clarity change from positive to negative gaps, while verification and calibration becomes more strongly associated with success.
TruthfulQA retains a similar relative profile across the two models ( r=0.953 ), although the gap magnitudes change.
These measurements support diagnosing semantic priorities for each model and task.

Variation Across Datasets and Models.

The edit comparison in Sec.   3.3 fixes the starting policy and varies only the rubric-induced update direction; it is carried through a matched continuation, with keep controlling for additional training. The trained Qwen3-4B policy reviews its first-round feedback and finds a recurring f_{4} gap in its training rubric.
The frozen clause prohibited unsupported assertions but left the evidence requirement for “common knowledge” ambiguous.
The revised Prefer/Avoid pair reads:
 Prefer: factual claims are grounded in the given information or explicitly sourced common knowledge.
 Avoid: invented facts, unsupported assertions, or inferences from vague common knowledge .
Round 1 refines only f_{4} ; the other seven factors return keep because no recurring uncovered requirement is identified.
The edit adds seven words across the Prefer/Avoid pair in a 234 -token rubric.

 5 Conclusion
RubricOpt connects policy self-evaluation to revision of the behavioral specification guiding preference learning.
Its semantic rubric translates failure feedback into training-time criteria while preference pairs, labels, and the optimization objective remain fixed.
Experiments across comprehensive capability and open-ended generation show that rubric semantics can shape rubric-free behavior, with benefits depending on the model and preference corpus.
The tested revision yields mixed incremental results over unchanged-rubric continuation.
This makes the behavioral specification itself adaptable through the policy’s feedback loop.
Scope and Future Work.
The study uses an associative demand measure and a manually specified ontology.
Evaluating successive revisions on feedback-disjoint tasks and comparing proposals from initial and updated policies under a shared training budget would test the scope for sustained self-improvement.
Reproducibility Statement
Every trained method in this paper is a rank- 64 LoRA adapter over a released checkpoint, and the appendices specify the dataset, optimizer, schedule, seed, conditioning rate, reference convention, evaluation protocols, and judge configuration for every reported run.
The two preference datasets are public, and the seven accuracy benchmarks and three open-ended protocols are used through their released forms.
The initial formal rubrics can be reconstructed from the canonical bank in appendix  A.2 and the template in appendix  C.1 .
The accepted HelpSteer3 R_{1} edit is quoted in Sec.   4.3 .
The refinement operator is specified as five stages with their inputs, outputs, and the deterministic acceptance checks (appendix  C.2 ).
Broader Impact Statement
RubricOpt exposes behavioral criteria as short, inspectable training artifacts, with an evidence trail for each accepted edit.
The policy under diagnosis proposes the edits, so its interpretation of failures can reinforce its own biases.
Deterministic checks enforce evidence references and textual constraints; rubric-free evaluation measures the resulting behavior.
Deployments should review proposed rubric changes against their intended behaviors and evaluate each edit with a matched continuation control.

 C.2 Stages, Contracts and the Deterministic Gate





Evidence Set and Engine.


 

 Fig.   2 in the main text gives the six-step workflow.
The diagnostic stage reads item-level judge rationales for the all-eight-factor method against ordinary DPO and the Instruct model on Arena-Hard, MT-Bench (including a dedicated second-turn batch), and WildBench, together with generated correctness items carrying a gold answer on GSM8K, MATH-Hard, and MMLU-Pro.
That evidence is the first-round rubric-free benchmark evaluation output, read item by item, as described in Sec.   4 .
The analysis and proposal engine is the current policy itself, the all-eight-factor merged checkpoint, so the model that reads the benchmark feedback is the model the feedback is about.
The external anchors are the gold answers and benchmark outcomes, the fixed judge’s rationales and checklists, and the frozen [math] text.

 
 

Five prompt stages process feedback from policy [math] , trained with the frozen round- [math] rubric [math] , in the order 1 (benchmark diagnostic), 2 (cross-benchmark synthesis), 3 (factor attribution), 4 (conservative update), and 5 (rubric audit), followed by step 6 (deterministic gate).
Each stage is a separate call with its own schema, and no stage sees the next stage’s output.

 
 

Stage 1 sees the factor schema, the criterion currently in force, and one benchmark’s evidence, and returns a four-key YAML document:

 
 
 

 preserve:
  - behavior: / factor: / evidence:
 improve:
  - failure_mode: / factor: / evidence:
 criterion_gaps:
  - factor: / current_problem: / suggested_direction:
 overall_diagnosis: 
 

 
 
 

Stage 1 uses only [math] – [math] and separates behaviors to preserve from those to improve.
An isolated example requires strong evidence, and attributing a benchmark change to the criterion requires behavioral evidence.
The output must be concrete and concise.

 
 

Stage 2 aggregates every stage-1 report for one policy.
It may not rewrite the criterion or propose wording.
It organises evidence and records disagreement, adding the benchmarks that support each pattern, an evidence-strength level (strong, moderate, weak), and an explicit record of contradictions.
A benchmark where the policy did not change still counts as evidence about what is stable.

 
 

Stage 3 attributes each recurring behaviour onto the ontology and decides whether the current wording already covers it.
The coverage decision follows this rule:

 
 
 

If the current Prefer/Avoid wording states this failure mode in full, the model simply did not learn it: the decision is keep .
If the wording does not state the failure mode, or states only part of it, that is the only case in which refine may be considered .

 
 
 

Attribution disambiguates reasoning validity, verification and calibration, completeness, and instruction adherence, naming the primary factor when several are involved.
It attributes only what the evidence supports.

 
 

Stage 4 proposes the smallest clause edits that address high-confidence recurring gaps while preserving factor identity and behaviors marked for preservation.
Edits may clarify wording, narrow an overly broad reading, make an implicit requirement explicit, or refine a matched Avoid clause.
Stage 4 prohibits examples, task-specific tricks, benchmark or dataset names, scores, numerical weights, and intensifiers.
Unsupported changes return keep .
The output is a candidate [math] and a per-factor ledger with the action, reason, and old and new clauses.

 
 

Stage 5 audits semantic identity, benchmark and dataset leakage, evidential support, specificity, Prefer/Avoid matching, preserved behaviors, and edit magnitude.
It emits a justification per axis and one PASS or FAIL .
The audit is advisory rather than decisive: in one executed round it returned a clean eight-of-eight PASS on a candidate that the deterministic gate then rejected.

 





Step 6: The Deterministic Gate.


 

The binding checks constrain eligibility, semantic identity, and procedural consistency.

 
 

Acceptance is decided by eight checks in round 1 and ten in round 2.
Round 2 adds that only gate-accepted factors may differ, and that the recorded decisions are consistent with the rendered rubric.

 
 
 
 

 1. 
 

 All eight factors must occur exactly once, each with a keep / refine decision.
 

 


 2. 
 

 The input clauses of every refinement must match the frozen canonical wording byte-for-byte.
 

 


 3. 
 

 Each new clause must retain at least [math] of the old clause’s content tokens, with its length change inside [math] words.
 

 


 4. 
 

 Every factor must retain one matched Prefer/Avoid pair.
 

 


 5. 
 

 Clauses must contain no benchmark or dataset names, digits, intensifiers, or example markers.
 

 


 6. 
 

 Every refinement must be traceable to an attribution judging the failure not fully covered.
 

 


 7. 
 

 Edit magnitude must remain within the specified budget.
 

 


 8. 
 

 Cross-factor vocabulary imports raise a warning rather than a rejection.
 

 


 9. 
 

 In round 2, only gate-accepted factors may differ.
 

 


 10. 
 

 In round 2, the recorded decisions must agree with the rendered rubric.
 

 

 
 
 
 

Failures of the binding checks reject the affected edit, so a factor that violates a check is reverted to keep .
