S-EDL: Eliciting Self-Evidence from Sequence Likelihoods for Semantic Calibration of LLMs
Yawei Li Jiazheng Li David Rügamer Bernd Bischl Yulan He Mina Rezaei
Affiliation: LMU Munich
Affiliation: King’s College London
Affiliation: Munich Center for Machine Learning (MCML)
Abstract
Calibrating large language models (LLMs) in open-ended generation is uniquely challenging because many distinct token sequences can express the same underlying meaning. Prior calibration techniques target either fixed-set classification confidence or the binary correctness of individual strings, making them inherently ill-suited for the dynamic, multi-string nature of open-ended semantic outcomes. To bridge this gap, we introduce S-EDL, a novel evidential method that natively optimizes LLMs for semantic calibration. By eliciting evidence from the sequence likelihoods of the model itself, S-EDL constructs a differentiable Dirichlet prior over prompt-specific semantic classes. Optimizing the evidential loss under this prior yields a calibration-aware training signal over variable semantic classes, completely bypassing the need for fixed multiple-choice options. Evaluations across four open-ended benchmarks and three models demonstrate that S-EDL substantially reduces semantic calibration error while improving accuracy. By aligning generative likelihoods with semantic correctness, this work establishes a framework for deploying trustworthy LLMs in complex, open-ended applications. Code is available at: https://github.com/sandylaker/s-edl .
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for open-ended reasoning tasks, where reliable confidence estimates are important for deciding when model outputs can be trusted [ 28 , 32 , 56 , 24 ] . Early research has primarily focused on multiple-choice question answering (MCQA), where calibration can be defined over a small set of answer labels [ 48 , 53 , 37 , 25 ] . Open-ended generation is more subtle: many distinct token sequences can express the same answer, and the probability assigned to a surface form does not necessarily reflect confidence in its semantic content.
Prior work has studied open-ended uncertainty and calibration through several related targets. Semantic uncertainty metrics [ 20 , 12 ] sample multiple generations and group equivalent responses to evaluate the predictive distribution over semantic classes. Building on this perspective, semantic calibration [ 33 ] translates this grouping concept into an evaluation metric for open-ended generation. In parallel, verbal confidence calibration methods [ 18 , 43 , 52 , 30 , 3 , 9 , 55 ] teach models to report a confidence score of correctness. However, this calibrates the binary correctness of a single response rather than the underlying distribution over alternative semantic answers. Thus, a gap remains: how to fine-tune open-ended LLMs under a semantic calibration objective.
Evidential deep learning (EDL) natively bridges this gap. By placing a Dirichlet prior over categorical probabilities, it explicitly models the strength of evidence for multiple competing meanings, allowing calibration losses to act on distributions over semantic meanings. Building on this, we propose S-EDL, a self-evidenced EDL method tailored for semantic calibration. Unlike conventional EDL methods that require a fixed set of answer labels [ 39 , 7 ] , S-EDL accommodates the dynamic nature of open-ended generation by transforming the sequence likelihoods of the model into a differentiable evidence vector. This formulation allocates greater evidential support to semantic classes backed by highly probable strings. The evidence vector then parametrizes a Dirichlet prior over the semantic outcomes, providing a calibration-aware training signal. Our contributions are three-fold:
•
We extend EDL from fixed-choice calibration to open-ended semantic calibration, allowing LLMs to optimize their confidence over diverse semantic meanings.
•
We introduce a novel parametrization that derives a differentiable evidence vector from the model’s sequence likelihoods. This formulation also theoretically unifies post-hoc metrics such as Semantic Entropy [ 20 , 12 ] under a common evidential framework.
•
Across three models and four open-ended benchmarks, S-EDL jointly improves average accuracy and calibration over supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reducing average calibration error by up to roughly 1/3.
2 Related work
From MCQA to semantic calibration. Early LLM calibration focused on MCQA, where a fixed label set enables standard classification-style methods, including Bayesian low-rank adaptation [ 48 , 53 , 37 , 42 ] and evidential approaches [ 25 ] . Open-ended generation introduces a level mismatch: many distinct sequences can express the same answer. Semantic uncertainty metrics [ 20 , 12 , 11 , 34 , 36 , 6 ] sample generations and group by meaning, and semantic calibration [ 33 ] turns this view into a calibration target for open-ended generation. Our work follows this semantic line.
Verbal and token-level calibration. A separate line of work asks models to emit a scalar correctness score [ 18 , 43 , 52 , 30 , 3 , 9 , 55 ] , which calibrates a Bernoulli pushforward (correct vs. incorrect) of the output distribution rather than a categorical distribution over semantic outcomes. Meanwhile, SelfReflect [ 19 ] captures the categorical distribution by a verbalized string. Token-level methods such as label smoothing [ 15 , 44 ] , temperature scaling [ 41 , 22 ] , RCFT [ 50 ] , and CATTO [ 35 ] , regularize per-token distributions, supervising a different granularity than the semantic marginal targeted here.
Evidential deep learning. EDL [ 31 , 39 , 10 , 7 , 17 , 5 , 4 , 54 ] places a Dirichlet prior over class probabilities through an evidence vector. Although previous work has applied EDL to calibrate LLMs in MCQA [ 25 ] and detect misbehavior in vision-language models [ 16 ] , S-EDL is, to the best of our knowledge, the first instantiation of EDL for calibrating LLMs in open-ended generation.
3 Background
3.1 Semantic calibration in open-ended LLM generation
Our work focuses on calibration in open-ended generation, for which we adopt the framework of B-calibration [ 33 ] . Let 𝒟 \mathcal{D} be a distribution over prompt-completion pairs ( x , y ) ∈ 𝒱 ∗ × 𝒱 L (x,y)\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}^{L} , where x x is a prompt over vocabulary 𝒱 ∗ \mathcal{V}^{*} and y ∈ 𝒱 L y\in\mathcal{V}^{L} is a ground-truth completion of length L L . We write s := B ⁡ ( x , z ) s:=B(x,z) for a generation’s class and s ∗ := B ⁡ ( x , y ) s^{*}:=B(x,y) for the ground-truth class of ( x , y ) (x,y) . The collapsing function B B can be implemented by a verification tool or an LLM-as-judge that clusters responses by meaning.

π x ( s ) := ∑ z : B ⁡ ( x , z ) = s p 𝜽 ( z | x ) , s ∈ ℕ , z ∈ 𝒱 L . \pi_{x}(s):=\textstyle\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x),\quad s\in\mathbb{N},z\in\mathcal{V}^{L}.

Semantic calibration requires that the confidence aligns with the true probability of correctness:
𝔼 ( x , y ) ∼ 𝒟 [ 1 { s x top = s ∗ } | conf ( x ) ] = conf ( x ) almost surely. \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\,\mathbbm{1}\{s_{x}^{\text{top}}=s^{*}\}\,\big|\,\mathrm{conf}(x)\,\right]=\mathrm{conf}(x)\quad\text{almost surely.}

The calibration objective above asks the model to report not just which semantic class is most likely but also how confident it is in that belief. Previous work IB-EDL [ 25 ] has shown that EDL methods are very effective in calibrating LLMs in the MCQA setting, but their approach is limited to a fixed set of options such as A/B/C/D. 4 Methodology
4.1 Motivation
Given EDL’s effectiveness for calibrating LLMs in MCQA, we now discuss the adaptations required to apply it in open-ended generation.
Instantiating the EDL framework in this setting hinges on one design choice: how to parametrize the evidence vector 𝒆 \bm{e} . Conventional EDL learns 𝒆 \bm{e} through a dedicated prediction head whose output dimension equals the number of classes. This recipe does not transfer to our setting for two reasons. First, the relevant semantic class inventory is prompt-dependent and not known in advance, so there is no canonical fixed output space. Second, and most fundamentally, the distribution whose calibration we ultimately care about is p 𝜽 ( ⋅ | x ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x) ; routing evidence through an independent head decouples it from the very object we aim to calibrate. We therefore look for a parametrization that derives 𝒆 \bm{e} from p 𝜽 ( ⋅ | x ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x) .
A natural choice for constructing 𝒆 \bm{e} is utilizing π x \pi_{x} itself, which already encodes how p 𝜽 ( ⋅ | x ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x) distributes mass over semantic classes. Since 𝒱 L \mathcal{V}^{L} is typically vast, Nakkiran et al. [33] estimate π x \pi_{x} from N N i.i.d. Monte-Carlo samples z ( i ) ∼ p 𝜽 ( ⋅ | x ) z^{(i)}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x) with semantic classes s ( i ) = B ⁡ ( x , z ( i ) ) s^{(i)}=B(x,z^{(i)}) , which induce the prompt-specific semantic class set 𝒮 x := { s ( i ) : i ∈ [ N ] } ⊂ ℕ \mathcal{S}_{x}:=\{s^{(i)}:i\in[N]\}\subset\mathbb{N} . Further details regarding the empirical construction of 𝒮 x \mathcal{S}_{x} in edge cases are in Section B.1 . The plug-in estimator of π x \pi_{x} is then
π ^ x ( s ) = 1 N ∑ i = 1 N 𝟙 [ s ( i ) = s ] , s ∈ 𝒮 x . \hat{\pi}_{x}(s)=\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}[s^{(i)}=s],\quad s\in\mathcal{S}_{x}.
(3)
However, this histogram is not a suitable evidence vector for EDL training. Once the samples are drawn, the class indicators are constants with respect to 𝜽 \bm{\theta} , so an evidence vector proportional to π ^ x \hat{\pi}_{x} provides no differentiable path from the EDL loss back to the model parameters. In addition, the histogram treats all samples equally, regardless of their likelihood under p 𝜽 ( ⋅ ∣ x ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid x) . This is misaligned with the evidential interpretation of 𝒆 \bm{e} : a semantic class supported by high-likelihood generations should contribute more evidence than one represented only by low-likelihood samples.
Desiderata. These observations lead to two desiderata for parametrizing evidence 𝒆 \bm{e} :
(a)
The parametrization should assign more evidence to semantic classes supported by high-likelihood completions, so that likely completions contribute more to evidence.
(b)
The parametrization should remain differentiable through the sample log-likelihoods, so that the EDL loss can update the likelihoods underlying the sampled semantic estimate.
Next, we instantiate these desiderata through a Monte-Carlo estimate of a tempered semantic distribution, and use this estimate to define a Dirichlet prior over the sampled semantic classes.
4.2 Formulation of self-evidence
Throughout this subsection, we fix the input prompt x x and drop the subscript from prompt-specific quantities (e.g., π x \pi_{x} to π \pi , 𝒮 x \mathcal{S}_{x} to 𝒮 \mathcal{S} ) to lighten notation; all such objects remain implicitly conditional on x x . We work directly with semantic labels s ∈ 𝒮 s\in\mathcal{S} , without introducing a separate local class index. In addition, we use the term self-evidence to refer to evidential support constructed from the LLM’s own Monte-Carlo samples. At the sample level, each generation z ( i ) z^{(i)} receives a scalar self-evidence weight u ( i ) u^{(i)} ; at the class level, these weights aggregate into the self-evidence vector 𝒆 \bm{e} .
Tempered semantic distribution. For a scalar β ≥ 1 \beta\geq 1 , we define the tempered semantic distribution over classes s ∈ 𝒮 s\in\mathcal{S} and z , z ′ ∈ 𝒱 L z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L} as
π β ( s ) := ∑ z : B ⁡ ( x , z ) = s p 𝜽 ( z | x ) β ∑ z ′ ∈ 𝒱 L p 𝜽 ​ ( z ′ | x ) β . \pi_{\beta}(s):=\frac{\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)^{\beta}}{\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{\prime}|x)^{\beta}}.
(4)
For β = 1 \beta=1 , Eq. 4 recovers the standard semantic distribution π \pi in Eq. 1 .
For β > 1 \beta>1 , raising per-sequence likelihoods to a higher power shifts mass toward classes that the model supports with high-confidence sequences, formalizing desideratum (a).
Self-evidence estimator. Direct evaluation of Eq. 4 is intractable because both the classwise numerator and the global normalizer require sums over 𝒱 L \mathcal{V}^{L} . We therefore estimate π β \pi_{\beta} from the same N N Monte-Carlo samples introduced in Section 4.1 . Writing ℓ ( i ) := log ⁡ p 𝜽 ​ ( z ( i ) | x ) \ell^{(i)}:=\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x) for teacher-forcing log-likelihood for sample i i , we assign to each sample the self-evidence weight
u ( i ) := exp ⁡ ( ( β − 1 ) ​ ℓ ( i ) ) = p 𝜽 ​ ( z ( i ) | x ) β − 1 . u^{(i)}:=\exp\!\left((\beta-1)\,\ell^{(i)}\right)=p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1}.

The exponent is β − 1 \beta-1 rather than β \beta because the samples z ( i ) z^{(i)} are themselves drawn from p 𝜽 ( ⋅ | x ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x) , so multiplying by p 𝜽 ​ ( z ( i ) | x ) β − 1 p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1} recovers the p 𝜽 β p_{\bm{\theta}}^{\beta} terms of π β \pi_{\beta} in Eq. 4 (made precise in Proposition 1 ). Because u ( i ) u^{(i)} is a smooth function of ℓ ( i ) \ell^{(i)} , gradients can pass through the sample log-likelihoods to 𝜽 \bm{\theta} and satisfy desideratum (b).
We aggregate the weights within each semantic class and normalize:
U s := ∑ i ∈ [ N ] : s ( i ) = s u ( i ) , π ^ β ( s ) := U s ∑ r ∈ 𝒮 U r . U_{s}:=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{i\in[N]\,:\,s^{(i)}=s}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!u^{(i)},\qquad\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s):=\frac{U_{s}}{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}U_{r}}.
(6)
A natural question is whether this estimator π ^ β \hat{\pi}_{\beta} recovers the true tempered distribution π β \pi_{\beta} as the number of samples grows. The next result confirms this (see the proof in Section A.1 ).
Proposition 1 .
As N → ∞ N\to\infty , π ^ β ​ ( s ) → a.s. π β ​ ( s ) \hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\pi_{\beta}(s) for every s ∈ 𝒮 s\in\mathcal{S} .
Connection to Semantic Entropy and the practical choice of β \beta . Semantic Entropy (SE) [ 20 , 12 ] also aggregates samples by their sequence probabilities p 𝜽 ​ ( z ( i ) | x ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x) , which under Eq. 5 corresponds to the special case β = 2 \beta=2 . Self-evidence vector and Dirichlet prior. We finally convert π ^ β \hat{\pi}_{\beta} into an evidential prior by distributing the sample budget N N over the semantic classes according to the estimated tempered distribution:

The SFT loss retains the next-token learning signal, while the evidential term provides the semantic-level calibration signal. where λ MSE \lambda_{\text{MSE}} is the weight for the evidential loss. The next subsection analyzes the gradient of ℒ MSE \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} .

5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental setup
Datasets. We conduct training and evaluation on four open-ended STEM datasets: GSM8K [ 8 ] , MATH [ 13 ] , ChemistryQA [ 49 ] , and FormulaReasoning [ 23 ] , a physics QA dataset. These benchmarks share two properties that make them well suited to our study. First, final answers admit canonical numerical or symbolic forms, so the collapsing function that groups sampled responses into semantic classes (a symbolic verifier or an LLM-as-judge) incurs comparatively low evaluation noise. Second, they often require multi-step reasoning, so calibration over complete semantic outcomes is more relevant than calibration over individual tokens. We train on the training split of each dataset and evaluate on the corresponding test split, with the exception of MATH, where we follow standard practice [ 13 , 26 ] and evaluate on MATH-500, a subset of the MATH test split widely used in other literature. Dataset construction is detailed in Section B.3 .
Models and training. Following Nakkiran et al. [33] , we report two semantic-level metrics computed from N eval = 50 N_{\text{eval}}=50 generations per question: Semantic Accuracy (S-Acc.), the accuracy of majority voting over semantically clustered responses, and Smooth ECE (smECE), a kernel-smoothed calibration error metric. As a single-sequence-level metric, we additionally report Pass@1 accuracy.
Baselines. Besides standard SFT, we compare S-EDL against baselines spanning three categories: (i) token-level calibration , including Label Smoothing (LS) [ 15 ] and RCFT [ 50 ] , which regularize token-wise output distributions; (ii) Bayesian ensembling , represented by BLoB [ 48 ] ; and (iii) sequence-level correctness optimization , represented by Dr.GRPO [ 29 ] with a binary 0 / 1 0/1 correctness reward. We include Dr.GRPO as a control that isolates the effect of sequence-level supervision on calibration: under the binary reward, it maximizes the probability of the correct semantic class without an explicit calibration term, which lets us assess whether operating at the sequence level alone accounts for the gains of S-EDL or whether the Brier-style calibration objective like Eq. 2 is required. The details about the baselines are outlined in Section B.5 .

Dr.GRPO also outperforms every token-level baseline in average smECE on Qwen3-8B-Base and MiMo-7B-Base, but its 11.4 on Ministral3-8B-Base does not improve over RCFT’s 10.9, suggesting that operating at the sequence level alone is not sufficient and that the choice of training signal also matters.
The underperformance of token-level methods may reflect a level mismatch between supervision and evaluation: LS and RCFT calibrate per-token conditional distributions, whereas sequence-level objectives act on complete responses; S-EDL further applies its loss to a sampled estimate of the semantic distribution, matching the granularity of the semantic metrics.
This mismatch may be amplified in chain-of-thought generation, where a small subset of decisive tokens (e.g., a branching choice indicated by “alternatively”) largely determines the semantic outcome [ 47 ] , while most tokens are routine continuations; per-token regularization applies to both groups, potentially diluting the signal on the tokens that actually matter.
Furthermore, we observe that several baselines improve over SFT on one metric at the cost of the other.
LS provides a representative example: on MiMo-7B-Base, it lowers average Pass@1 from 56.2 56.2 to 53.5 53.5 while improving smECE only marginally, from 12.1 12.1 to 11.9 11.9 .
Averaged across the four datasets, S-EDL improves over SFT on all three metrics for every model, e.g., with + 9.6 +9.6 Pass@1, + 5.7 +5.7 S-Acc, and − 6.0 -6.0 smECE on Qwen3-8B-Base. Fig. 6 in Section C.2 visualizes the averaged S-Acc × \times smECE plane. S-EDL consistently lies on the Pareto frontier of the plane.
The structure of the evidential loss may help explain this behavior. Eq. 2 is a Brier-style proper scoring rule, which penalizes both classification error and miscalibration simultaneously. Dr.GRPO with a binary 0 / 1 0/1 reward, maximizes the probability of the correct semantic class without an explicit calibration term, and may improve accuracy at the cost of miscalibration.
5.3 Cross-domain evaluation
To test the transferability of each method, we train on MATH and evaluate on three other cross-domain datasets as shown in Table 2 . This setting tests not only whether accuracy transfers, but also whether semantic confidence remains aligned with correctness.
The grouping into sequence-level (Dr.GRPO, S-EDL) and token-level/Bayesian (LS, RCFT, BLoB) baselines from Section 5.2 separates more sharply in average Pass@1 in cross-domain evaluation than in-domain.
The average Pass@1 gap between the better-performing sequence-level method and the strongest token-level/Bayesian baseline widens from 5.6 / 9.5 / 10.8 5.6/9.5/10.8 points in-domain to 13.8 / 16.5 / 13.6 13.8/16.5/13.6 points cross-domain on Qwen3-8B-Base, Ministral3-8B-Base, and MiMo-7B-Base, respectively.
A consistent diagnostic appears against the SFT reference: on Qwen3-8B-Base and MiMo-7B-Base, none of LS, RCFT, or BLoB improves average cross-domain smECE over plain SFT.
One possible interpretation is that per-token regularization and Bayesian ensembling shape conditional distributions tied to the lexical statistics of the training distribution, so the resulting effect on the implied semantic distribution need not be preserved when input statistics change.
In contrast, objectives defined over complete responses are less tied to token-level statistics and better aligned with the semantic nature.
The two sequence-level methods themselves, however, do not behave identically under shift.
In cross-domain evaluation, the average Pass@1 of S-EDL exceeds that of Dr.GRPO by 7.3 / 6.3 / 3.3 7.3/6.3/3.3 points across the three models.
Its average cross-domain smECE is lower by 4.7 / 3.0 / 0.1 4.7/3.0/0.1 points.
While Dr.GRPO is effective at optimizing correctness, its binary reward does not explicitly regularize the semantic confidence.
The performance gap between the two methods suggests that coupling sequence-level training with an explicit evidential calibration objective could contribute to more generalizable improvements in accuracy and calibration.

5.4 Token probability profiles of generated solutions
Figure 1 : Violin plots of per-token probability profiles on MATH-500 generated answers. We record the probabilities of the sampled tokens. Solid and dotted black ticks mark the mean and median, respectively. Logarithm is applied to the violin widths to improve readability.
To investigate the source of calibration improvements in S-EDL, we analyze the sampled-token probability profiles of generated solutions. For each generated solution z z and decoded position t t , we record p 𝜽 ​ ( z t ∣ x , z < t ) p_{\bm{\theta}}(z_{t}\mid x,z_{<t}) and pool these values across all token positions t t and samples z z .
Fig. 1 and Fig. 7 (in Section C.3 ) illustrate three distinct mechanisms for altering token confidence on MATH-500. In Fig. 1 , LS globally suppresses token probability, yielding the lowest mean p ⁡ ( sampled token ) p(\text{sampled token}) values of 0.413, 0.507, and 0.411 across the three models. Conversely, Dr.GRPO systematically sharpens distributions, reaching high-end means of 0.904, 0.954, and 0.939.
Unlike LS and Dr.GRPO, S-EDL preserves the SFT token probability profile. Across all twelve model-dataset pairs, the absolute difference between the S-EDL mean and the SFT mean remains below 0.06, and the sign of this minor difference varies across settings. Furthermore, S-EDL maintains a pooled token profile statistically indistinguishable from those of RCFT and BLoB, with means differing by at most 0.06, yet achieves average smECE values at least 2 points lower than all three. The calibration gain therefore cannot be explained by a coarse shift in token concentration.
This empirical behavior directly corroborates the semantic-level reallocation described by Corollary 2 . S-EDL achieves semantic calibration by operating on the semantic-class marginal rather than uniformly on token distributions, routing probability mass between semantic outcomes without imposing a fixed direction of change on token probabilities. Pooled statistics cannot rule out position- or class-specific shifts, but the absence of global drift is informative: it isolates the calibration gain to a structural property of evidential loss rather than a side effect of token-level regularization.

5.6 Accelerating semantic clustering by a find-first algorithm
The number of sampled sequences N train N_{\text{train}} also affects training speed, since computing the EDL loss requires clustering these sequences into semantic classes.
To simplify the notation in the following analysis, we will use N N instead of N train N_{\text{train}} . A naive approach, which compares every pair of sequences, requires O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) oracle calls. By contrast, Dr.GRPO uses the verifier or LLM judge to score each sequence individually, requiring only O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) calls.
To reduce this gap, we introduce a find-first clustering algorithm, detailed in Algorithm 1 in Section B.2 .
Instead of exhaustive pairwise checks, the algorithm maintains a growing list of cluster representatives and compares each new sequence against them until it finds an equivalent one. If no match is found, it then creates a new cluster.
As illustrated in Fig. 4 , clustering N = 50 N=50 sampled sequences with the naive pairwise approach requires N ⁡ ( N − 1 ) / 2 = 1225 N(N-1)/2=1225 verifier calls per prompt.
Across the evaluated models, find-first clustering reduces the average to approximately 65 calls on GSM8K and 165 calls on MATH, corresponding to roughly 1.3 ​ N 1.3N and 3.3 ​ N 3.3N calls, respectively.
This cuts the computational burden to a small fraction of the O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) worst case, bringing the practical cost much closer to O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) .
For general tasks that rely on an LLM judge, each sequence can instead be matched against all current cluster representatives in a single classification call.
This implementation requires O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) judge calls per question prompt, matching the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) calls of Dr.GRPO.
Decision models such as Jev [ 46 ] could be explored to further accelerate this classification step.
Across both verifier-based and judge-based settings, these implementations substantially narrows the gap in oracle calls between S-EDL and other sequence-level methods such as Dr.GRPO.

These hint-augmented prompts are applied exclusively during the cross-domain evaluation. Furthermore, these hint-augmented prompts are used across evaluation of all fine-tuning methods to ensure fairness.

B.5 Hyperparameters of baselines
BLoB
During training, we set bayes_samples to 1, bayes_epsilon to 0.05, bayes_beta to 0.2, and bayes_kllr to 0.1, with bayes_kl_reweighting enabled. During evaluation, we use 5 Bayesian samples. We refer readers to the official BLoB codebase for the precise definitions of these hyperparameters.
RCFT
During training, we configure the hyperparameters by setting rcft_alpha to 0.5, rcft_calibration_scale to 0.001, rcft_num_bins to 10, and rcft_eps to 0.000001. Please refer to the official RCFT codebase for detailed explanations of these variables.
LS
During training, we apply a label smoothing coefficient of 0.1 0.1 .
Dr.GRPO
We start the fine tuning process from the supervised checkpoint at epoch 1. To ensure a fair comparison, we set the total number of training steps for Dr.GRPO to exactly match the number of steps where the evidential loss is active during S-EDL training. We use a group size of 8. Following Liu et al. [29] , we do not use the KL divergence penalty w.r.t. the reference model.

In the following, we provide examples of the training prompts used for fine tuning Qwen3-8B-Base. We intentionally do not enclose the chain of thought reasoning within <think> and </think> blocks. Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed.
•
While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example
(a)
If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.
(b)
If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully.
(c)
If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset).
(d)
We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
Answer: [No]
Justification: The main calibration and accuracy results in Table 1 and Table 2 report point estimates from a single training run per (model, dataset, method) configuration without error bars, due to compute constraints associated with fine-tuning three 7–8B parameter models across four datasets and six methods. Guidelines:
•
The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
•
The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.
•

•
If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.

Broader impacts
Question: Does the paper discuss both potential positive societal impacts and negative societal impacts of the work performed?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Improving semantic calibration helps users and downstream systems decide when to trust an LLM’s open-ended output, which is broadly beneficial in high-stakes settings such as scientific reasoning and decision support. Potential negative impacts are limited: S-EDL is a fine-tuning objective rather than a capability-extending technique, and a better-calibrated model could in principle make over-trusted but plausible, looking incorrect outputs less obvious, we mitigate this concern by reporting both accuracy and calibration error so practitioners can audit both dimensions.
Guidelines:
•
The answer [N/A] means that there is no societal impact of the work performed.
•
If the authors answer [N/A] or [No] , they should explain why their work has no societal impact or why the paper does not address societal impact.
•
Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.
•
The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.
•
The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.
•
If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
