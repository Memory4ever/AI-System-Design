Abstract
Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have seen broad adoption, but their biases are still far less examined than those of standard LLMs, creating possible social harms when deployed in real-world settings.
Prior efforts to extend bias analyses from LLMs into the multi-modal space either cover only a few bias categories or do not ensure that the groups being compared are clearly addressed by visual cues in the images.
To overcome these limitations, we introduce Melange , a wide-ranging synthetic visual benchmark covering seven categories for assessing social biases in MLLMs.
It is grounded in the widely used language-only BBQ dataset (Bias Benchmark for Question Answering) from BigBench, carefully extending its constructed contexts via controlled generation of visual scenarios so the target attributes appear visually and clearly.
To maximize reliability, our generated images pass through two stages of validation: automated screening using visual question answering (VQA) and subsequent manual inspection by human annotators.
By systematically evaluating 23 open-source MLLMs across three matched evaluation settings—text-only, image-only, and image-with-context—Melange isolates the fairness effect of the input modality.
Switching from text to vision reduces fairness by 0.291 on average, a gap that varies by model family rather than by scale alone.
A perception control confirms that this gap is not an artifact of visual comprehension: models identify the depicted attributes at 90–95% accuracy yet still fail to abstain.
When models do commit to a biased answer, polarity-aware stereotype alignment shows they choose the human-stereotypical response roughly three-quarters of the time.

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) excel in image captioning  ( Sarto et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2024a ; Nguyen et al., 2023 ) , visual question answering  ( Yue et al., 2024 ; Fu et al., 2024 ) , and multimodal reasoning  ( Zhang et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Yu et al., 2024 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) . Their increasing adoption in sensitive domains such as healthcare  ( Tran et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2023 ; Xia et al., 2024 ) makes bias and fairness critical considerations. While several bias benchmarks exist for LLMs  ( Nadeem et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2020 ; Parrish et al., 2022 ; Gallegos et al., 2024 ) , comparable resources for MLLMs  ( Ruggeri and Nozza, 2023 ; Xiao et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2025b ; Howard et al., 2025 ) are lacking in scale, diversity, and comprehensiveness. The absence of large, diverse, and visually-grounded bias datasets limits meaningful comparison and reproducibility of bias studies in vision, underscoring the urgent need for robust, multimodal benchmarks capable of evaluating bias across both text and image inputs.
To address these challenges, we introduce Melange , a vision-language bias benchmark created by extending the Bias Benchmark for Question Answering (BBQ)  ( Parrish et al., 2022 ) from BigBench  ( Srivastava et al., 2022 ) to the visual domain.
Melange, named for its varied mixture of humans with demographic diversity, provides a scalable and diverse dataset for a comprehensive MLLM bias evaluation. We select seven key categories from BBQ: age , gender , appearance , socioeconomic status , race , religion , and disability . We then map them to the visual domain by generating images that depict these bias scenarios. This allows us to gain deeper insights into how MLLMs encode and manifest biases across modalities.
Melange’s construction (Figure  2 ) involves a multi-stage pipeline to ensure data quality, relevance, and diversity. First, we filter BBQ prompts to select scenarios with explicit visual cues and then generate corresponding images using diffusion models. This is followed by two quality-filtering phases: an automatic VQA filtering to ensure prompt-image alignment, and a human filtering phase to validate semantic consistency and eliminate artifacts in the generated images.
Furthermore, human validation eliminates biases that could propagate from the text-to-image model into the dataset, thereby ensuring the objectivity of Melange. Finally, we curate ten visually diverse but semantically equivalent images for each unique prompt, creating a robust dataset with varied visual realizations. By evaluating these images alongside their text-only BBQ counterparts in three matched settings, Melange enables direct comparison of model fairness across modalities and helps determine whether biases stem from textual or visual inputs.
Using Melange, we evaluate 23 open-source MLLMs spanning nine model families with parameter counts from 1B to 72B. Three key findings emerge. First, switching from text to vision reduces fairness by a mean of 0.291 (FaiR), but this modality gap varies by model family rather than by scale alone: within Qwen2.5-VL larger models are consistently fairer, yet InternVL3.5-38B incurs the largest gap measured despite strong text-only performance. Second, a perception control shows that models identify the depicted demographic attributes at 90–95% accuracy, ruling out visual comprehension failure as the source of the gap. Third, when models do commit to a biased answer, polarity-aware stereotype alignment reveals they choose the human-stereotypical response roughly three-quarters of the time—a finding obscured in earlier analyses by a metric that ignored question polarity.
Our main contributions are: 1) Melange, the first large-scale, visually grounded bias dataset and benchmark designed for MLLMs, covering 12,640 bias scenarios across seven categories totaling 126,400 evaluation images—the per-character and per-scene image pools support generating substantially more compositions than the ten per scenario we release, so this figure represents a deliberate sample rather than the pipeline’s ceiling; 2) three matched evaluation settings (text-only, image-only, image-with-context) that isolate the fairness effect of the input modality, together with a perception control that rules out visual comprehension failure; and 3) a comprehensive evaluation of 23 open-source MLLMs across nine model families, revealing a modality gap that is family-bound rather than scale-bound.

 2 Dataset Generation
 2.1 Background: The BBQ Dataset
The Bias Benchmark for QA (BBQ) ( Parrish et al., 2022 ) is a dataset for measuring social biases in LLMs. BBQ includes question sets that target social biases against people belonging to protected classes across nine social dimensions: age, disability status, gender identity, nationality, physical appearance, race/ethnicity, religion, socio-economic status (SES), and sexual orientation, along with two intersectional categories. The dataset consists of over 58,000 prompts, with templates created by the authors and validated by experts and crowdworkers. Each example provides both an ambiguous context, where the correct answer is “Unknown” , and a disambiguated context, where a specific individual is the correct answer, allowing us to evaluate when biases lead to stereotypical responses.
 2.2 Textual Bias Scenarios: The BBQ dataset
 Category Selection. 
Our Melange builds upon the Bias Benchmark for Question Answering (BBQ) dataset, focusing on seven categories: age, gender, appearance, socio-economic status (SES), race, religion, and disability. We exclude two categories of sexual orientation and nationality, as individuals with these attributes often lack distinct visual cues.
We only focus on BBQ contexts that offer clear visual differentiation, ensuring that the attributes are meaningfully represented in imagery.
 Context Selection. 
Each category contains multiple contexts, with every unique context appearing four times, i.e. twice with reordered character positions and twice with polarity-controlled questions, refer to Table  1 . A central strength of our dataset lies in its careful manual curation : we include only scenarios where distinguishing attributes are visually discernible, excluding cases such as mentally conditioned vs. healthy individuals in disability category or transgender vs. cisgender vs. straight individuals in the gender identity category where no reliable visual cue exists (full table of contexts in Appendix F). This filtering ensures that all evaluated examples provide a valid testbed for assessing visual bias in MLLMs. More details in dataset statistics in the supplementary.
 Component Generation. As shown in Figure  3 , each context is decomposed into three components: the scene, character 1, and character 2. Scenes are manually written locations that reflect the environment that best narrates the context, while character descriptions are extracted from BBQ answer options. Each component is rendered as a separate image using Flux ( Labs, 2024 ) and then composed into a complete visual scenario.

 3 Dataset Sanity Checks
As shown in Figure  1 , we decompose each textual context into three independently generated visual components: a scene and two characters . Scenes are manually authored location descriptions that convey the environmental context, while character prompts are derived from the corresponding BBQ answer options. We render each component with Flux  ( Labs, 2024 ) and then compose them into a single photorealistic visual scenario. The following are the steps to do sanity checks on the quality of the data, as shown in Figure  2 .
 Filtering. 
For each character, we generate 50 candidate renderings (25 per seed across two random seeds). We then apply a structured visual quality-control pipeline using VQA prompts evaluated by Qwen3-VL  ( Bai et al., 2025 ) . We discard any image that (i) contains more than one person, (ii) is cartoon-like or non-photorealistic, (iii) substantially occludes the face, or (iv) contains nudity. If fewer than 25 valid images remain, a human annotator iteratively refines the diffusion prompt by adding semantically consistent descriptors that preserve the intended identity while improving realism and clarity (see Appendix H). The final output of this stage is a curated pool of at least 25 and up to 50 high-quality depictions per character (Figure  2 ).
 Tagging. 
Given a filtered pool with at least 25 valid images per character, we annotate each image with coarse demographic attributes to control for visual confounds. Concretely, we tag the three most visually salient social dimensions: age, gender, and race/ethnicity. These tags enable controlled comparisons where, for a target bias dimension (e.g., socioeconomic status), the paired characters are matched on non-target attributes (age, gender, race/ethnicity), thereby isolating the factor under evaluation. Tagging is performed via an MLLM in a VQA setting: we classify age into four bins ( child , teenager , adult , elderly ), gender into two categories ( male , female ), and race/ethnicity into five coarse groups ( White , Black , Asian , Hispanic , Other ). When the bias category under study coincides with one of these attributes, we do not enforce matching on that attribute, meaning, for instance, if the bias category is age, we take the two tags of gender and race into consideration, but not the age itself. Please refer to Appendix A for more elaboration.
 Sampling. 
Finally, we sample composite scenarios from three pools: one per character and one for background scenes. Scene images are generated analogously to characters, with additional constraints that they contain no people and align with the textual context of BBQ. For each context, we sample 10 composites by placing both characters into a sampled scene. Character images are drawn randomly, subject to tag-consistent matching : the two characters are matched on all three attributes (age, gender, race) so that differences between them are restricted to the bias category under investigation. In cases where one of the age, gender, or race is a target bias itself, that tag is not going to be considered for character matching process.
 Human Evaluation. 
To validate that the automated pipeline yields faithful and usable stimuli, we conduct human checks on the filtered and tagged pools. Annotators verify that (i) each image is photorealistic and depicts exactly one person, (ii) the character is visually consistent with the intended description (e.g., profession- and role-consistent cues), and (iii) the tag-based matches do not introduce unintended differences between paired characters beyond the target attribute. Images failing these criteria are removed, and prompts are refined when necessary; the remaining images constitute the final vetted pools used for composition and evaluation.
 3.1 Binary VQA via Token Probabilities
In all stages of our pipeline involving visual question answering (VQA), we adopt a unified probability-based evaluation strategy. For filtering, the model is prompted with four binary questions and asked to respond with “yes” or “no”; we then compute the probability of the next token corresponding to each option to determine the model’s confidence. A similar approach is applied in the tagging stage, where we assess age, gender, and race by prompting the model with categorical yes/no questions (e.g., “Is this character best described as an adult?”). Finally, during bias analysis, models are prompted to select an answer choice labeled A, B, or C, and we compute the likelihood of each token to determine the predicted response. Further implementation and scoring details are provided in Appendix A.

 Evaluation Settings. 
We evaluate each model under three settings that progressively introduce visual information (Figure  3 ):
 • 
 bbq_llm (text-only): the original BBQ benchmark format. The model receives the full context naming both characters, the question, and three answer options identified by the character names.
 • 
 melange (image-only): the model receives the generated scenario image, the question, and spatial answer options (“Image on the left” / “Image on the right” / “Unknown”).
 • 
 melange_with_context : identical to melange but with a neutral scene description prepended that names neither character, providing a textual scaffold without demographic cues.
Two derived quantities capture the modality effect: the text→vision gap ( melange - bbq_llm ), measuring how much fairness a model loses when the social signal shifts from text to pixels; and the context recovery ( melange_with_context - melange ), measuring how much of that loss neutral text can compensate.
 Evaluation Metric. 
To assess fairness in MLLMs during multiple-choice VQA tasks, we propose FaiR (Fairness Ratio) , the fraction of model responses that select the neutral answer:
 S_{\text{Fair}}=\frac{N_{\text{unbiased}}}{N_{\text{total}}} 
where N_{\text{unbiased}} counts answers such as unknown or cannot tell , reflecting neutrality in ambiguous scenarios. A higher S_{\text{Fair}} indicates fairer, less biased model behavior.
 Stereotype alignment and question polarity. 
Each BBQ context appears twice: once with a negatively framed question and once with a positively framed question (exactly 50/50 split). Crucially, the BBQ stereotype label marks the target group rather than the answer slot , so the stereotypical answer flips between the two polarities. A naïve (polarity-blind) alignment rate averages a high match on one framing with an artificially low match on the other, yielding {\sim}50\% by construction even for a strongly stereotype-aligned model. We therefore report polarity-aware stereotype alignment : for each biased response we identify the stereotypical answer under the question’s actual polarity before computing the match rate.
 Option-order invariance. 
Models show a mild letter preference (mean FaiR 0.667 when the neutral option is C vs. 0.609 on A and 0.616 on B, mean within-cell spread 0.113). Because BBQ places the unknown option near-uniformly across positions (A 32.6%, B 34.3%, C 33.1%), this preference cancels in aggregate. We confirm this directly for Qwen3-VL-8B by rotating the option→letter mapping: the resulting FaiR spread is only 0.007 on bbq_llm and 0.010 on melange (see Appendix for per-position results). No rotation averaging is applied to the reported numbers.
 Interpreting FaiR: an important caveat. 
A high FaiR score may reflect genuine absence of social bias, a general tendency to abstain, or poor visual comprehension that makes the model default to “unknown.” We address this ambiguity with two complementary analyses: a perception control (Section  4.2 ) that directly tests whether models can identify the depicted attributes, and the stereotype-alignment breakdown in Section  5 .

 4.1 Results
Table  2 presents FaiR scores for all 23 models under the three settings, sorted by the text→vision gap.
 The modality gap is the central finding. 
Every model is less fair in melange than in bbq_llm (mean gap - 0.291, range - 0.525 to - 0.098). Text-only fairness saturates near 0.91–0.96 for models above {\sim} 27B, while vision-setting performance varies enormously. What differentiates models is how much of their text-side fairness survives the switch to pixels.
 The gap is family-bound, not scale-bound. 
Scale effects are real within some families—Qwen2.5-VL is cleanly monotone (7B - 0.252 \rightarrow 32B - 0.129 \rightarrow 72B - 0.098)—but they do not generalize. Every dense InternVL3.5 model sits at the bottom of the table regardless of size: the 38B variant incurs the largest gap measured ( - 0.525) despite the second-best text-only score in its family. Sparsity is not uniformly penalised either: Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B drops - 0.287 while InternVL3.5-30B-A3B drops only - 0.132 and is the best InternVL3.5 at any size.
 Dense vs. MoE within the same release. 
The Qwen3.6 family provides a controlled comparison: same vendor, same release, two architectures. The dense 27B variant incurs a modest gap ( - 0.137) and ranks sixth overall, while the MoE 35B-A3B variant—despite recording the best text-only FaiR of any model tested (0.956)—drops - 0.414 and ranks among the worst. This three-fold gap difference, with vendor and release held constant, isolates architecture density as a variable: the MoE model’s sparse activation pattern may limit the capacity available for vision–language alignment during the modality switch.
 Context recovery is compensatory. 
Adding a neutral text description recovers an average of + 0.047 FaiR points. The pattern is systematic: models that lose most to vision recover most from text (InternVL3.5-14B + 0.157, InternVL3.5-1B + 0.145, InternVL3.5-38B + 0.141), while modality-robust models are flat or slightly negative. Text scaffolding patches a weak visual pathway rather than improving a strong one.

 4.2 Perception Control
A high FaiR score in melange might simply reflect the model’s inability to perceive the depicted characters. To test this, we design a perception probe : for each scenario image, the model is asked which depicted person carries the manipulated demographic attribute (e.g., “Which person looks wealthier?”). Ground truth comes from BBQ’s group tags, giving 100% coverage of all 12,640 scenarios. The probe uses the same images, the same left/right answer format, and the same greedy decoding as the bias question, but is a forced two-way choice with no unknown option (chance =0.5 ). Each scenario is probed from both sides, so a model that always answers one side scores 0.5.

Table  3 shows the results for seven representative models under both probe types. Attribute-probe perception spans only 2.6 percentage points (0.905–0.931) and detection-probe perception spans 2.9 points (0.924–0.953), while FaiR spans 82 points (0.080–0.898). Restricting FaiR to items the model provably perceives changes it by at most 0.011, always slightly downward.
The decisive case is LLaVA-OneVision-7B: attribute perception 0.926 , detection perception 0.944 —statistically indistinguishable from the 72B Qwen—yet FaiR is 0.080 . The model identifies the two characters and their attributes almost perfectly but essentially never selects the neutral response. This demonstrates that the text→vision fairness gap is not a perception failure: models identify the manipulated attribute at 90–95% regardless of size or family, and conditioning on demonstrated perception leaves FaiR unchanged.
Per-category perception accuracy, averaged over the seven models: gender 0.997, age 0.987, race 0.938, disability 0.907, appearance 0.894, socioeconomic status 0.862, and religion 0.848. The relatively high religion score (0.848) warrants disclosure: religious affiliation is not visually determinable in principle, yet models achieve well above chance, indicating that the image generator encoded visible religious markers (e.g., clothing or headwear). The religion scenarios therefore partly test response to visual cues the benchmark’s framing treats as absent. Per-category results are reported in the Appendix.

 Figure 4 : FaiR scores as a function of model size within each family. Scale effects are visible within Qwen2.5-VL but do not generalise: InternVL3.5-38B incurs the largest modality gap measured despite the second-best text-only score in its family. 
 Scale effects are family-dependent, not universal. Within the Qwen2.5-VL family, larger models are consistently fairer: the text→vision gap shrinks monotonically from - 0.345 (3B) to - 0.098 (72B). However, this pattern does not generalise across families (Figure  4 ). InternVL3.5 shows no clear scale trend: the 38B variant incurs the largest gap of any model measured ( - 0.525), while the sparse 30B-A3B variant incurs only - 0.132 and is the best InternVL3.5 at any size. Among Qwen3-VL models, the 32B is far more robust than the 30B-A3B ( - 0.121 vs. - 0.287), indicating that architecture and training decisions dominate over raw parameter count. These results caution against blanket claims that scale reduces bias; the relationship is mediated by family-specific vision–language alignment.

 Figure 6 : Polarity-blind stereotype alignment rates. These rates hover near 50%, but this is an artifact of ignoring question polarity (see text). Polarity-aware alignment is 62–99% across all 23 models. 
 When MLLMs commit to a biased answer, it overwhelmingly aligns with human stereotypes. The polarity-blind stereotype-alignment rate (Figure  6 ) averages 0.496 across all 69 model–setting cells, suggesting at first glance that biased outputs are randomly distributed relative to human stereotypes. However, this figure is an artifact of the metric: because each context appears with both a negatively and a positively framed question, and the stereotypical answer flips between them (Section  4 ), a polarity-blind rate yields {\sim} 0.50 by construction for any stereotype-aligned model.
Correcting for polarity reveals the true picture. Across all 23 models and three settings, polarity-aware stereotype alignment averages 0.770 (range 0.591–0.985). On negatively framed questions the rate reaches 0.90 on average, while on positively framed questions it is 0.74. Alignment is consistently higher in bbq_llm (mean 0.853) than in melange (mean 0.725) or melange_with_context (mean 0.733), indicating that the visual pathway introduces noise but does not reverse the stereotypical tendency. These results demonstrate that when MLLMs commit to a non-neutral answer, they overwhelmingly reproduce human social stereotypes rather than exhibiting novel bias patterns.
This analysis covers six of the seven demographic categories for which stereotype annotations are available (age, gender, appearance, disability, religion, and race). The socioeconomic status (SES) category is excluded because group membership cannot be reliably inferred from appearance alone, so no stereotype labels were annotated for SES. Among the covered categories, gender has near-complete annotation (96% of scenarios) while race has partial coverage (27%), because stereotype labels were assigned only when the relevant group attributes are visually apparent.
 6 Related Work
 Bias benchmarks for language models. 
The Bias Benchmark for Question Answering (BBQ)  ( Parrish et al., 2022 ) , included in BIG-bench  ( Srivastava et al., 2022 ) , assesses the extent to which LLMs rely on social stereotypes when responding to situations that lack sufficient contextual information. BBQ serves as the primary foundation for Melange, which preserves the benchmark’s ambiguous scenario formulation and coverage of seven bias categories while adapting its evaluation framework to visual inputs. Among the earlier approaches developed exclusively for textual evaluation, UNQOVER  ( Li et al., 2020 ) examines stereotypical tendencies through questions with insufficient contextual detail, whereas StereoSet  ( Nadeem et al., 2021 ) investigates the stereotypical associations learned by pretrained language models. A broader perspective is presented by Gallegos et al. (2024) , who systematically review research on bias and fairness in LLMs, encompassing existing evaluation benchmarks, measurement techniques, and bias mitigation methods. Nevertheless, these approaches operate entirely within the textual modality and therefore cannot establish whether model behaviour changes when socially relevant information is communicated through images instead of language.
 Bias in vision and text-to-image generation. 
Research on visual generative systems has also revealed substantial demographic biases. Bianchi et al. (2023) show that text-to-image generation systems reinforce demographic stereotypes across large-scale outputs, while Cheong et al. (2024) identify biases associated with gender and race in images produced by DALL-E Mini. Considering the role of data preparation, Paullada et al. (2021) examine how decisions made during dataset construction can introduce and preserve social biases, whereas hanna2019towards advocate incorporating critical-race methodologies into research on algorithmic fairness. However, the emphasis of these studies lies primarily on the content of generated imagery or the characteristics of underlying datasets, rather than on decisions made by multimodal models. Consequently, they explain the representations produced by image generators without investigating how a downstream MLLM interprets and responds to visually presented social situations that remain ambiguous.
 Evaluating bias in multimodal models. 
Existing efforts to assess social bias in MLLMs have introduced several evaluation benchmarks, although their coverage remains relatively constrained. Ruggeri and Nozza (2023) investigate correlations related to gender by analysing the next-token probabilities assigned by MLLMs when answering questions designed to expose biased associations. Using artificially constructed counterfactual images, xiao2024genderbias and howard2024uncovering examine biases concerning gender and occupations. Similarly, Sathe et al. (2024) and Fraser and Kiritchenko (2024) assess gender- and race-related biases in occupational contexts. The work of wu2024evaluating instead concentrates on identifying demographic characteristics within natural images, addressing a different objective from our investigation of harmful stereotypical associations. The approaches most directly comparable to Melange are those proposed by Narnaware et al. (2025) and wang2024vlbiasbench , both of which transfer BBQ scenarios into the visual modality. However, their evaluation designs do not guarantee that paired individuals are identical in demographic characteristics other than the attribute being examined. Furthermore, their use of written character names introduces a possible interaction between visually conveyed information and linguistic bias. Melange addresses these limitations by identifying individuals exclusively through spatial references and ensuring that paired characters are aligned across every demographic characteristic except the intended target attribute.
 Synthetic and counterfactual evaluation data. 
The use of controlled image synthesis to construct evaluation datasets has become increasingly established. For instance, yeganeh2025latent employ latent drifting within diffusion models to generate counterfactual medical images, while Chang et al. (2024) review methodologies for evaluating large language models, including approaches based on synthetic datasets. Despite these developments, none of the aforementioned studies construct corresponding text-only and vision-only versions of the same socially ambiguous situations. This paired evaluation structure constitutes the central contribution of Melange, comprising 12,640 scenarios derived from BBQ and assessed under both bbq_llm and melange conditions. In addition, a perception control is incorporated to demonstrate that the observed performance difference between modalities cannot be attributed to limitations in visual competence.
 7 Limitations
Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.
First, all 12,640 scenarios the benchmark contains no disambiguated items. Melange therefore measures abstention under ambiguity and cannot detect bias in cases where a correct answer exists. Extending to disambiguated contexts would test whether models apply stereotypes even when sufficient information is available to answer correctly.
Second, stereotype annotations are deliberately absent where group membership is not reliably inferable from appearance. SES has 0% coverage, race 27%, and gender 96%; the remaining four categories (age, appearance, disability, religion) have 100% coverage. The stereotype-alignment analysis in Section  5 therefore covers six categories and excludes SES entirely.
Third, all images are synthetic, generated by Flux  ( flux ) . The perception probe reveals that the generator encodes visible religious markers (models score 0.848 on religion despite religious affiliation being visually indeterminate in principle), meaning the religion scenarios partly test response to visual cues the benchmark’s framing treats as absent. More broadly, synthetic images may introduce distributional differences from natural photographs that affect model behaviour in ways our evaluation cannot isolate.
Fourth, the original experimental design included four evaluation settings; two of these (side-by-side character crops without a scene background) are not reproducible from the public release because the per-character renders are no longer available. All results in this paper use the three reproducible settings: bbq_llm , melange , and melange_with_context .
Finally, our methodology does not explicitly control for latent visual confounds in generated images, such as facial expressions or clothing colour. Prior work has shown that MLLMs associate bright clothing with positive attributes; if the diffusion model systematically renders one demographic group with different expressions or attire, this could introduce a bias pathway orthogonal to the intended variable of analysis. Controlling for these factors is an important direction for future work.
 8 Conclusion
We introduce Melange, a vision-language bias benchmark containing 126,400 images spanning 12,640 scenarios across seven demographic categories. By evaluating the same BBQ-derived scenarios in matched text-only and vision-only settings, we isolate the fairness effect of the input modality. Across 23 open-source MLLMs, switching from text to vision reduces fairness by 0.291 on average—a gap that varies by model family rather than by scale alone. A perception control confirms that this gap is not an artifact of visual comprehension: models identify the depicted demographic attributes at 90–95% accuracy yet still fail to abstain. When models do commit to a biased answer, polarity-aware stereotype alignment shows they choose the human-stereotypical response roughly three-quarters of the time. Melange, its evaluation code, and all generated images are publicly available to support continued research on mitigating bias in multimodal AI systems.
