Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) based on PPO-style clipping has become an effective approach to improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs).
However, standard token-level clipping measures policy change only through the conditional probability of the current token, while the prefix conditioning context is itself generated by the policy.
This mismatch becomes especially consequential in long-response and off-policy training.
We reformulate autoregressive generation as a finite-horizon Markov decision process and derive its performance-difference identity under behavior-policy sampling.
The resulting change of measure identifies the cumulative likelihood ratio of the generated prefix as the exact importance weight for each stepwise policy-improvement term.
Starting from the monotonic-improvement principle of trust-region policy optimization, we then construct a prefix-level trust region using the exact reverse-KL coordinate r − 1 − log ⁡ r r-1-\log r , whose behavior-policy expectation equals the corresponding prefix-distribution KL divergence.
This yields EPIC, a PPO-style sequence-clipping method that directly regulates prefix-level policy drift without relying on a local quadratic approximation or expert-routing replay.
Experiments on off-policy reinforcement learning with a sparse mixture-of-experts language model show that EPIC consistently outperforms standard and sequence-level clipping baselines across mathematical-reasoning benchmarks, while remaining competitive with routing-replay-based alternatives.
Code is available at https://github.com/LiChengxi666/EPIC .

1 Introduction
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a central component of
large language model (LLM) post-training, driving substantial advances in reasoning and coding capabilities [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . In throughput-oriented modern LLM RL pipelines such as slime [ 5 ] and verl [ 6 ] , fully asynchronous rollout generation and policy optimization [ 7 ] , together with differences in kernel implementations between inference and training engines [ 8 ] , inevitably introduce distributional mismatch that precludes strictly on-policy training. PPO-style clipping [ 9 ] is widely adopted to stabilize RL training by discarding gradient contributions associated with large deviations in current-to-behavior probability ratios, which serve as a proxy for policy divergence.
However, even among recently released large-scale models, the appropriate clipping mechanism and granularity for autoregressive LLMs remain unsettled: For example, Ling-2.6 [ 10 ] , Qwen-AgentWorld [ 11 ] , and Harvey Tenet [ 12 ] adopt sequence-level clipping through GSPO,
whereas DeepSeek-V4 [ 13 ] and GLM-5 [ 14 ] continue to use token-level clipping through GRPO.
This unresolved problem is particularly consequential in modern high-throughput LLM training pipelines, where decoupling rollout generation from optimization improves efficiency but introduces policy lag, causing training to rely on samples generated by outdated behavior policies [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] . Existing clipping-based methods remain susceptible to training instability in these off-policy settings [ 3 , 8 ] .
To mitigate this instability, current mixture-of-experts (MoE) training pipelines often rely on auxiliary routing-alignment mechanisms, such as Vanilla Routing Replay (R2) [ 8 ] and Rollout Routing Replay (R3) [ 19 ] , to explicitly suppress the distribution mismatch. However, R2 and R3 limit the freedom of learning and incur additional memory overhead to store expert routing indices, while leaving the more fundamental challenge unresolved: how to stably train LLMs in substantially off-policy settings?
To address this challenge, we revisit the original design and motivation of PPO-clip and reformulate this technique in the autoregressive LLM RL setting.
TRPO seeks conservative policy improvement by constraining the KL divergence from the behavior policy, while PPO-clip replaces this update with a first-order clipped surrogate [ 20 , 9 ] .
We formulate autoregressive generation as a finite-horizon MDP and derive its performance-difference identity under behavior-policy sampling.
The resulting change of measure identifies the cumulative prefix ratio as the exact importance weight for each stepwise improvement term [ 21 ] .
Applying the monotonic-improvement principle of TRPO then leads to a trust region over the behavior- and updated-policy-induced prefix distributions.
Thus, the prefix ratio both measures each autoregressive improvement term and provides the ratio coordinate for its KL constraint.
Motivated by this distinction, we propose EPIC, a prefix-aware clipped objective for off-policy LLM reinforcement learning.
Based on the prefix policy ratio r pre r^{\mathrm{pre}} , we use the exact reverse-KL coordinate r pre − 1 − log ⁡ r pre r^{\mathrm{pre}}-1-\log r^{\mathrm{pre}} , whose expectation under the behavior policy equals the KL divergence between the behavior- and updated-policy-induced prefix distributions.
Combining this exact KL characterization with the monotonic-improvement framework of TRPO, we translate the prefix-level trust region into a practical PPO-style clipped objective.
This construction stabilizes strongly off-policy MoE training without expert-routing replay, thereby avoiding the additional storage and system complexity required to record and replay expert assignments. Figure 1 illustrates how EPIC differs from token- and response-level clipping and highlights its memory efficiency relative to routing replay.
In conclusion, our contributions are threefold:
•
We reformulate trust-region policy optimization for autoregressive generation.
By deriving the performance-difference identity under behavior-policy sampling, we show that the cumulative prefix ratio is the exact change-of-measure factor for each stepwise improvement term, and extend the TRPO monotonic-improvement principle to policy-induced prefix distributions.
•
Based on this formulation, we develop EPIC, a practical PPO-style clipped objective that constructs prefix-ratio bounds from
the exact reverse-KL coordinate
r pre − 1 − log ⁡ r pre r^{\mathrm{pre}}-1-\log r^{\mathrm{pre}} and implements the resulting
prefix-level trust region through efficient token-level computation.
•
In strongly off-policy MoE training, EPIC outperforms GSPO by 6.50 6.50 percentage points.
Compared with routing-replay baselines, EPIC achieves competitive four-benchmark performance while reducing median driver-side process memory by 77.5 % 77.5\% and 93.0 % 93.0\% relative to R2 and R3.

We compare EPIC with GRPO with higher upper clip combined with R2 and R3 under the same protocol.
Unlike these replay-based approaches, EPIC does not store or replay expert-routing decisions and modifies only the clipped policy objective.
As shown in Figure 4(a) , both R2 and R3 substantially improve GRPO Clip-Higher in this off-policy setting.
EPIC slightly outperforms both replay-based variants on the four-benchmark average without replaying expert-routing decisions.
Figure 4(b) shows that EPIC maintains a substantially lower and more stable process-memory usage than GRPO Clip-Higher with R2 or R3 throughout the recorded training period.
As these methods have similar response lengths ( 8,557 8{,}557 , 9,476 9{,}476 , and 9,001 9{,}001 tokens for R2, R3, and EPIC), these measurements suggest that EPIC avoids substantial driver-side memory overhead associated with carrying routing-replay state.
Together with its competitive benchmark performance, these observations suggest that EPIC offers a memory-efficient alternative to routing replay in the evaluated setting.
The reported memory metric is driver-process resident CPU memory, rather than GPU memory or total cluster memory. It characterizes the observed system-level cost, not an isolated measurement of routing-state storage.
5 Conclusion
We revisit PPO-style clipping through a finite-horizon MDP formulation of autoregressive generation, identifying the prefix ratio as the exact stepwise importance weight.
Building on TRPO’s monotonic-improvement framework, we derive clipping bounds from the exact reverse-KL coordinate to obtain EPIC.
Experiments show improved reasoning performance over clipping baselines and competitive results with routing replay at lower process-memory cost.
This work connects trust-region theory with practical optimization, advancing principled and efficient reinforcement learning for language models.

6 Related work
Importance Sampling in Off-Policy Reinforcement Learning.
In off-policy reinforcement learning, importance sampling provides a standard mechanism for estimating expectations under a target policy using samples collected by a different behavior policy [ 31 ] .
In sequential decision problems,
a full-trajectory likelihood ratio gives an unbiased correction
but can suffer from rapidly increasing variance
as the horizon grows [ 21 , 32 , 33 ] .
Per-decision importance sampling alleviates this problem by applying
only the likelihood ratios associated with the trajectory
prefix relevant to each decision [ 21 ] .
Another widely used approach is to truncate or clip importance ratios.
Retrace truncates importance weights in multistep off-policy corrections to reduce variance [ 34 ] , while PPO clips the likelihood ratio in its surrogate objective to prevent excessively large policy updates [ 9 ] .
Although their specific objectives differ, both methods limit the influence of extreme likelihood ratios on learning.
Ratio truncation therefore provides a practical trade-off: it introduces controlled bias in exchange for reduced variance and a more stable optimization signal.
ACER further combines truncated importance sampling with explicit bias correction and an efficient trust-region update to support stable off-policy actor–critic learning with experience replay [ 35 ] .
Policy Optimization for Language Models.
Reinforcement learning studies how an agent learns a policy that maximizes its expected cumulative reward through interaction with an environment [ 36 ] .
Policy optimization directly parameterizes this policy and improves it using gradients estimated from sampled trajectories [ 37 ] .
Such methods have become a standard component of reinforcement-learning-based post-training for large language models (LLMs) [ 38 , 29 ] .
Trust Region Policy Optimization constrains policy updates through a KL-divergence trust region but requires an expensive constrained optimization procedure [ 20 ] .
PPO replaces this procedure with a clipped importance-ratio objective that supports multiple minibatch updates on the same rollout data [ 9 ] .
GRPO adapts this objective to LLM reinforcement learning by replacing the learned critic with group-relative advantages [ 29 ] , while DAPO introduces asymmetric clipping and other training techniques to improve exploration and large-scale optimization [ 2 ] .
More recent methods reconsider the granularity and geometry of the clipping ratio. GSPO applies clipping to the geometric mean of the full-response likelihood ratio, improving the numerical stability of MoE reinforcement learning but assigning the same sequence-level statistic to every token [ 3 ] .
CTPO instead uses a cumulative prefix ratio and position-dependent log-space bounds, aligning the importance correction more closely with the causal structure of token-level gradients [ 30 ] .
RIPO approaches clipping from a complementary geometric perspective and adapts the ratio bounds according to the behavior-policy probability, addressing the non-uniform policy divergence induced by fixed ratio intervals [ 26 ] .
Our work combines prefix-level importance correction with a trust region constructed from the reverse-KL coordinate in its non-approximated nonlinear form, together with position-dependent budgets and behavior-probability calibration.

9.4 Evaluation and Checkpoint Selection
Evaluation schedule and metric.
We evaluate the initial model before training and repeat evaluation every five global steps.
Each evaluation samples eight responses per prompt, producing 1,040 responses across the four benchmarks.
The evaluation temperature is 1.0 and top- p p is 0.7.
For each benchmark, mean ​ @ ​ 8 \mathrm{mean@8} is the average binary correctness over its prompts and their eight sampled responses.
Checkpoint selection and seed aggregation.
We first compute mean ​ @ ​ 8 \mathrm{mean@8} within each benchmark and then take the equal-weight arithmetic mean of the four benchmark scores.
The four benchmarks receive equal weight regardless of their numbers of prompts.
For each run, we select the checkpoint that maximizes this four-benchmark average.
All benchmark scores reported for that run therefore come from the same selected checkpoint, rather than independently selected benchmark-specific checkpoints.
The main six-method comparison is repeated with three random seeds.
We apply checkpoint selection separately to each run and then average the selected-checkpoint scores across the three seeds.

10.2 Analysis of Training Diagnostics
Figure 6 provides additional diagnostics for the optimization dynamics. The clip-fraction curves in Figure 6 6(a) show that EPIC maintains a persistently active clipping regime throughout the later stages of training, with a clip fraction on the same order as GSPO. In contrast, GRPO, DAPO, and RIPO operate with substantially smaller late-stage clip fractions. This indicates that the prefix-level trust region continues to influence the update rather than becoming effectively inactive.
The entropy trajectories in Figure 6 6(b) show different degrees of policy concentration across methods.
GRPO and RIPO gradually reach entropy values on the order of 10 − 2 10^{-2} , indicating a strong reduction in output diversity.
DAPO instead enters a higher and more variable entropy regime during the later stages of training. EPIC remains in an intermediate entropy regime, suggesting that its improved performance is not obtained by either excessive entropy collapse or uncontrolled entropy growth.
The gradient-norm curves in Figure 6 6(c) indicate that all methods experience a rapid reduction in update magnitude during the initial stage.
After this transient, EPIC maintains a comparatively controlled gradient scale and does not exhibit the sustained late-stage growth observed for some baselines.
Thus, its active clipping regime is not accompanied by excessively large parameter updates.

10.4 Ablation of Prefix-Probability Calibration
We disentangle two design choices in EPIC:
the behavior-policy probability used to calibrate the trust-region
budget and the scale used to represent the prefix ratio in the

in the budget calibration with the unnormalized cumulative probability
μ ( o i , 1 : t ∣ x ) \mu(o_{i,1:t}\mid x) .
The Cumulative-ratio ablation keeps the geometric-mean probability
calibration but uses the cumulative prefix ratio directly in the
optimization objective.
The final row is the default EPIC configuration, which applies
geometric averaging to both the behavior-policy probability and the
prefix optimization ratio.
The validation trajectories in Figure 8

compared with approximately 31.3 % 31.3\% for Cumulative-ratio and
33.3 % 33.3\% for EPIC.

reaching a maximum gradient norm of approximately 3.98 3.98 ,
compared with approximately 2.00 2.00 for Cumulative-ratio and
approximately 1.73 1.73 for EPIC.

of the unnormalized cumulative prefix probability.
The position-wise profiles show the mechanism behind this difference.
With Raw-prob, clipping is concentrated almost entirely in the first
relative-position bin and becomes negligible afterward.
In contrast, Cumulative-ratio and EPIC remain close to their

10.5 Memory Cost of Routing-Replay State
Routing replay introduces additional state beyond the token IDs,
log-probabilities, and rewards required by the policy objective.
For each retained token, it records the selected experts at every
MoE layer so that the routing decisions can be reused during
subsequent policy evaluations.
In the historical implementation associated with our experiments,
R2 converts the recorded expert indices to uint8 before
transferring them to CPU memory.
The vLLM backport used for R3 instead stores the rollout routing
indices as int32 , including its host shared-memory buffer.
The subsequent agent-loop processing preserves this dtype when
constructing padded routing tensors.
Storage estimate.
For a batch containing N N responses, each stored at length T T ,
the memory required by one dense tensor of routing indices is
M indices = N ​ T ​ L MoE ​ K ​ b , M_{\mathrm{indices}}=NTL_{\mathrm{MoE}}Kb,
(40)
where L MoE L_{\mathrm{MoE}} is the number of MoE layers, K K is the number of selected experts per token, and b b is the number of bytes per index. Qwen3-30B-A3B has 48 MoE layers and selects eight experts per token. Our rollout batch contains 64 × 8 = 512 64\times 8=512 responses, and the configured maximum prompt-plus-response length is 2,048 + 16,384 = 18,432 2{,}048+16{,}384=18{,}432 tokens. Table 4 reports the corresponding single-copy storage estimates. For the same tensor shape, the R3 rollout representation requires four times as much storage as the compressed R2 representation.
Relation to observed process memory.
Beyond the single-tensor storage estimated in Table 4 , the process-memory differences in the main-text figure also reflect shared-memory mappings, temporary storage during routing-index collection and concatenation, and larger routing tensors produced during validation.
The R3 rollout backend copies routing indices out of shared memory; the agent loop then constructs padded tensors and concatenates them within workers, followed by batch aggregation in the training coordinator.
R2 likewise performs gathering, CPU transfer, and concatenation of recorded indices.
Inputs and newly allocated outputs may coexist during these operations, increasing transient memory usage.
R3 also allocates a host shared-memory buffer according to the configured KV-token capacity, whose size need not equal that of the current response batch.
These storage and data-movement mechanisms provide a concrete explanation for why routing replay can incur substantial CPU-memory overhead, consistent with the process-memory differences in Figure 4(b) .
