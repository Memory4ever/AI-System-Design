Abstract
When training LLMs to code with reinforcement learning, reward hacking is often detectable but difficult to prevent, since (a) environment hardening is increasingly difficult as training environments grow more complex; and (b) training-time interventions incentivize models to obfuscate hacking behavior to bypass detection.
We instead study whether we can predict when detectable reward hacking becomes dominant before executing an RL run, enabling us to select lower-risk recipes in advance. We find that the initial hacking rate alone does not determine onset, which varies substantially across training recipes and repeated runs. We thus formulate this problem as predicting the distribution of the reward hacking onset step under a target training recipe.
We introduce SimHack , a simulator that learns reward hacking training dynamics from a handful of smaller-scale reference RL runs and generates counterfactual simulation runs of unseen target recipes without costly retraining.
Across unseen target recipes, SimHack predicts the median onset to a relative error of 11.3% and achieves across-recipe rank correlation of 0.85 , while generalizing to differences in model scale, curriculum, number of rollouts, and learning rate between reference and target recipes. As an application, we show that SimHack can guide training decisions by screening candidate RL curricula, discovering curricula over the same set of scenarios that train for 96.6% longer without hacking.
1 Introduction
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves language models on tasks such as mathematical reasoning and code generation, where task outcomes can be automatically evaluated ( Guo et al., 2025 ; Shao et al., 2024 ) . However, optimizing against automated rewards can also lead to reward hacking, where a model exploits imperfections or loopholes in the reward signal to obtain high reward without accomplishing the intended task ( Amodei et al., 2016 ; Skalse et al., 2022 ) . Recent work has documented this failure mode in coding RL, where models can manipulate evaluation logic, exploit test harnesses, or otherwise satisfy the verifier without producing a genuinely correct solution ( Wu and Tang, 2026 ; Baker et al., 2025 ) . More severely, reward hacking in training may generalize to taking harmful actions at deployment time ( Qi et al., 2026 ) .
To address reward hacking in RL, researchers often follow a back-and-forth loop between detection and prevention: identifying hacking in model rollouts, intervening to prevent their recurrence, and monitoring for new hacking. There have been reports that reward hacks and environment loopholes began to outpace the ability to filter or fix them ( Anthropic, 2026 ) . This illustrates how reviewing and repairing can remain a practical bottleneck even after hacks have been detected. One line of defence is environment hardening , but it becomes increasingly difficult as models grow more capable and training environments become increasingly complex and closer to real-world settings. It is harder to assume that every loophole can be fixed in the environment in advance, as illustrated by the recent OpenAI Hugging Face incident ( METR, 2026 ) . An alternative approach is training-time interventions such as incorporating a hack monitor into the reward signal; this family of approaches may turn the RL process into an adversarial game where models learn to obfuscate hacking behavior to bypass the monitor ( Baker et al., 2025 ) .

SimHack learns reward hacking dynamics from a small number of reference RL runs, then counterfactually simulates unseen target recipes to predict hacking trajectories and onset. These predictions can be used to compare curricula, identify high-risk prompts, and avoid costly full RL runs.
Given these limitations, exploitable loopholes may remain in training environments, allowing RL to discover initially rare hacking behaviors and rapidly amplify them during training ( Wong et al., 2026 ; Khalifa et al., 2026 ; MacDiarmid et al., 2025 ) . As shown in coding RL experiments, the same initial policy can produce markedly different hacking trajectories under different RL recipes. This suggests that choosing an appropriate recipe may help delay widespread hacking while preserving task learning. For a given budget, a key question is therefore not only whether a model can exploit a loophole, but whether and when hacking will become dominant during training. We define the reward hacking onset as the first training step at which at least half of sampled rollouts exhibit hacking. Predicting this onset before training could inform choices about training curricula, rollout configurations, and optimization hyperparameters. However, evaluating candidate recipes through repeated full RL runs can be prohibitively costly. We therefore study reward hacking onset prediction before the target RL run:
Given an initial policy and a prospective RL recipe, can we predict its hacking onset without executing the target run itself?
Prior work has shown that the initial frequency of reward hacking is informative about its onset, while training dynamics also play an important role ( Li, 2026 ) . In our coding RL setting, however, a higher initial hacking rate does not necessarily lead to an earlier onset. Moreover, even with the same initial policy, different training recipes produce substantially different hacking trajectories, while repeated runs under the same recipe exhibit stochasticity in onset times. Together, these findings motivate predicting an onset distribution conditioned on the full RL recipe, rather than relying on initial hacking propensity alone.
We introduce SimHack , a lightweight behavioral simulator for reward hacking onset prediction. SimHack represents each training prompt with a probability distribution over behavioral categories and learns how sampled outcomes reinforce, suppress, and propagate these behaviors during training from a small number of reference RL runs. Once fitted, SimHack can simulate unseen target recipes whose data is drawn from the same underlying prompt pool and generate stochastic hacking trajectories without executing the target RL run. In our coding RL experiments, SimHack achieves a median onset relative error of 11.3% and an across-recipe rank correlation of 0.85, while showing reasonable generalizability to predict recipes with unseen model scale, training curriculum, number of rollouts, and learning rate. Using these predictions, SimHack supports proactive training decisions: its counterfactual predictions can be used to select lower-risk curricula and identify training prompts that disproportionately contribute to reward hacking, with subsequent RL runs confirming that these interventions delay hacking and improve task performance without relying on hacking.
1.1 Contributions
•
We show that initial hacking propensity alone is insufficient to determine reward hacking onset, which also depends on the training recipe and stochastic training dynamics. Based on these findings, we formulate reward hacking onset prediction : predicting the distribution of onset times under a target training recipe without executing the target RL run.
•
We construct a large-scale dataset of 325 RL runs on LeetCode coding tasks, where for each run we record step-by-step changes in model behaviors, including rollouts, rewards, and detected reward hacking behaviors. The dataset covers 33 training recipes, >19.3M rollouts, and requires approximately 9000 GPU hours to collect. We plan to release this dataset to facilitate further research on reward hacking onset prediction.
•
We introduce SimHack , a lightweight behavioral simulator for counterfactual reward hacking prediction. SimHack achieves a median onset relative error of 11.3% and an across-recipe rank correlation of 0.85 , while generalizing to unseen changes in model scale, curriculum, number of rollouts, and learning rate. SimHack ’s prediction accuracy outperforms using 5 real RL runs directly on each target recipe.
•
We show that SimHack can guide proactive training decisions , including curriculum selection and identification of high-risk training prompts, with subsequent RL runs validating that these interventions delay hacking onset by 96.6% and improve task performance. With the help of SimHack , we surface new insights about training dynamics of reward hacking (e.g., easy-to-hard prompt ordering exacerbates hacking) and confirm some of the existing "gut feelings" that many researchers share (e.g., that the larger number of rollouts per prompt accelerates hacking onset).
2 Reward Hacking Prediction
Figure 1 illustrates the overall prediction setting. We first formalize the prediction target and the information available before a target RL run, and then show why reward hacking cannot be predicted from the initial policy alone.
Problem formulation. An RL training recipe is specified by R = ( π 0 , C , h ) R=(\pi_{0},C,h) , where π 0 \pi_{0} is the initial policy, C C specifies the training curriculum, including the training prompt set and the ordering of prompts trained at each step, and h h denotes training hyperparameters such as the learning rate and number of rollouts. A training run can be associated with a sequence H 1 : T = ( H 1 , … , H T ) H_{1:T}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{T}) , where H t H_{t} denotes the fraction of rollouts exhibiting reward hacking at training step t t . To characterize when hacking becomes dominant, we define the hack onset as t τ = min ⁡ { t : H t ≥ τ } t_{\tau}=\min\{t:H_{t}\geq\tau\} . For runs that do not reach this threshold within the training horizon, we set the onset step to T T for evaluation.
We use τ = 0.5 \tau=0.5 throughout the paper and denote the resulting onset by t 50 t_{50} . Thus, t 50 t_{50} is the first training step at which at least half of the sampled rollouts exhibit reward hacking.
Definition 1 (Reward hacking onset prediction before a target RL run) .
Given a target recipe R = ( π 0 , C , h ) R=(\pi_{0},C,h) , the goal is to predict the distribution of its reward hacking onset, p ⁡ ( t 50 ∣ R ) , p(t_{50}\mid R), without executing RL training under R R .
We model the quantity as distribution rather than deterministic outcomes because rollout sampling and subsequent RL updates are stochastic: repeated training runs under the same recipe can produce different hacking trajectories and onset times.
Prediction setting.
Prediction is conditioned on a small number of reference runs from the same RL environment, each providing its training recipe and stepwise rollout outcomes. The target recipe R R , with the same prompt set, may differ from (and typically be more expensive than) the reference runs in model scale, curriculum, number of rollouts, or learning rate. We allow pre-RL rollouts from its initial policy π 0 \pi_{0} to estimate the initial behavioral state, but no RL updates, intermediate checkpoints, or training trajectories from the target recipe are observed. Thus, the reference runs may be used to learn shared training dynamics, while prediction for R R must be performed entirely before executing the target RL run.
Full training recipe matters. A natural approach is to estimate future reward hacking risk directly from the initial policy. For example, one might expect a policy that hacks more frequently before RL to reach a hacking-dominated regime earlier ( Li, 2026 ) . We find that this intuition is insufficient.
1) Initial hacking rate does not determine the onset. As shown in Figure 2 , a policy with a higher pre-training hacking rate can nevertheless take substantially longer for hacking to become dominant. Conversely, a policy that initially exhibits little hacking may discover and rapidly amplify the exploit during RL.
Figure 2: Higher initial hacking rate (right) does not necessarily imply an earlier hacking onset.
2) Different components of the recipe alter reward hacking trajectories. Figure 3 further shows that reward hacking is sensitive to multiple components of the training recipe. Even when the initial policy is held fixed, changing the curriculum, number of rollouts, or learning rate can lead to markedly different hacking dynamics.
Figure 3: The same initial policy can exhibit substantially different hacking onset times under different training recipes.
These results motivate modeling reward hacking as an evolving training dynamic rather than a fixed property of the initial policy.
3 SimHack
SimHack is a lightweight simulator that models RL as a stochastic behavioral process at the prompt level. Instead of modeling changes to billions of model parameters, it tracks how the probabilities of different behaviors evolve for each training prompt. As illustrated in Figure 1 , SimHack alternates between two operations, sampling rollout behaviors from the current state and updating that state according to learned training dynamics. The dynamics are learned from a few reference RL runs and then reused to simulate unseen target recipes.
3.1 Behavioral RL Simulation
Suppose there are N N training prompts and K K possible behavior categories. For each prompt i i , we maintain a behavioral state S i , t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] K S_{i,t}\in[0,1]^{K} , with ∑ k = 1 K S i , t ( k ) = 1 \sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{i,t}^{(k)}=1 , describing the distribution over possible rollout bahaviors at training step t t . Collectively, S t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] N × K S_{t}\in[0,1]^{N\times K} denotes the behavioral state of all prompts. The formulation can support an arbitrary behavior taxonomy; in our experiments, we use K = 3 K=3 categories: correct , hacking , and other . The initial state S i , 0 S_{i,0} is estimated from rollouts of the initial policy.
At each simulated RL step, the training recipe determines which prompts are sampled and how many rollouts are generated. For each selected prompt, SimHack samples the behavior counts B i , t ∼ Multinomial ⁡ ( G i , t , S i , t ) , B_{i,t}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(G_{i,t},S_{i,t}),
where G i , t G_{i,t} is the number of rollouts for prompt i i and B i , t ∈ ℕ K B_{i,t}\in\mathbb{N}^{K} records the number of sampled rollouts exhibiting each behavior. We use B t ∈ ℕ N × K B_{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{N\times K} to denote the resulting rollout batch across prompts. This sampled batch drives the subsequent behavioral update.
3.2 Modeling Training Dynamics
We model the evolution of behavior with a batch-conditioned linear Markov transition. Given the current behavioral state S t ∈ ℝ N × K S_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K} and sampled rollout batch B t B_{t} , the next state is determined by
S t + 1 ∝ α θ ​ ( B t ) ⊙ S t + β θ ​ ( B t ) , S_{t+1}\propto\alpha_{\theta}(B_{t})\odot S_{t}+\beta_{\theta}(B_{t}),
where α θ ​ ( B t ) , β θ ​ ( B t ) ∈ ℝ N × K \alpha_{\theta}(B_{t}),\beta_{\theta}(B_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K} . The multiplicative term α θ ​ ( B t ) \alpha_{\theta}(B_{t}) captures how existing behavioral tendencies are amplified or suppressed, while the additive term β θ ​ ( B t ) \beta_{\theta}(B_{t}) captures new changes induced by the RL update. Both depend on the sampled outcomes in B t B_{t} and allow outcomes observed on one training prompt to influence the future behavior of other prompts, thereby modeling cross-prompt propagation. We model α θ \alpha_{\theta} and β θ \beta_{\theta} with a low-rank structure. We then fit them jointly across all reference runs using the likelihood of the observed stepwise behaviors. The implementation additionally incorporates the RL learning-rate schedule and a first-moment smoothing term.
The transition above provides a simplified description of the behavioral dynamics. In practice, we apply the affine update to RL-induced logit offsets rather than directly to S t S_{t} , and recover the behavioral probabilities by applying softmax to the initial logits plus these offsets. We defer the exact parameterization and optimization details to Appendix A .

4 Experiments
We evaluate SimHack from two perspectives. First, we test its overall prediction performance on a heterogeneous set of unseen target recipes. We then conduct controlled experiments that vary individual recipe factors, model scale, curriculum, number of rollouts, and learning rate, to better understand the generalization of the learned dynamics.
4.1 Experimental Setup
Dataset and environments.
To study reward hacking dynamics, we curate an extensive benchmark covering two loopholes ( Overwrite and Lazy ) across two model scales (Qwen3-0.6B and 4B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) ). Totaling 325 runs, > 75 >75 K optimization steps ( > 19.3 >19.3 M rollouts), and consuming around 9,000 A100 GPU hours, this dataset records fine-grained, per-step rollouts, rewards, and detected reward hacking behaviors. We plan to make this dataset publicly available to lower the computational barrier for future research on RL safety and reward hacking. Full environment definitions, reward functions, and detector details are provided in Appendix B .
RL recipes.
We vary four components of the RL recipe: model scale, training curriculum, number of rollouts, and learning rate. Our experiments use Qwen3-0.6B and 4B checkpoints, multiple prompt orderings and sampling schedules, number of rollouts ranging from 16 to 64 per prompt, and multiple learning rates. The simulator is fitted only on designated reference recipes and evaluated on target recipes whose RL trajectories are held out during fitting 1 1

Evaluation protocol.
For the main evaluation in Section 4.2 , we consider 8 unseen Qwen3-4B target recipes, each with 10 independently seeded RL runs. SimHack is fitted on 10 Qwen3-0.6B reference runs and generates 100 simulated trajectories for each target recipe. All reported metrics are averaged across the repeated splits. In Table 1 , we repeatedly split these runs into five held-out evaluation runs and five target-RL runs used as a strong empirical baseline estimator of the onset distribution for every recipe. We repeat this split 100 times and report the average performance. More detailed experiment settings are stated in Appendix B.8 .
Evaluation metrics.
Because repeated RL runs under the same recipe can produce different onset times, we evaluate the predicted distribution of t 50 t_{50} rather than a single trajectory. In Table 1 , we report three metrics. Median onset relative error measures the relative difference between the predicted and observed median t 50 t_{50} , evaluating how accurately the simulator estimates the typical onset time for a recipe. p20 pinball loss evaluates the predicted 20th percentile of t 50 t_{50} , providing a conservative estimate of the training horizon before reward hacking becomes dominant. Finally, across-recipe rank correlation is the Spearman correlation between predicted and observed median onset across target recipes and measures whether the simulator correctly ranks recipes by reward hacking risk. Formal definitions are provided in Appendix B.9 .

5 Using SimHack to Improve RL Training
5.1 Selecting Lower-Risk Curricula
A natural use of reward hacking prediction is to compare candidate training curricula before committing to expensive RL runs. Training-example order has long been recognized as an important factor ( Bengio et al., 2009 ) , including in reinforcement learning settings ( Narvekar et al., 2020 ) . We use SimHack to evaluate a collection of candidate prompt orderings, including standard ordering baselines and manually designed curricula intended to delay reward hacking. Based only on simulator predictions, we select 4 promising manual designs and subsequently evaluate them with real RL training.
Figure 6 compares the simulated and real median onset t 50 t_{50} for the resulting 8 curriculum settings. The ordering baselines include a random prompt ordering and three heuristic curricula that place hack-prone prompts earlier or later in training or prioritize easier prompts initially. Across these settings, simulated onset is strongly correlated with real onset (Spearman ρ = 0.79 \rho=0.79 ), indicating that SimHack provides a useful signal for comparing candidate curricula before training.
More importantly, the curricula selected using the simulator consistently have later reward hacking onset under real RL training. On average, their real onset is 96.6% later than that of the 4 baseline curricula. We also evaluate clean accuracy across all 8 curriculum settings, defined as the rate of producing correct code without hacking. For each setting, clean accuracy is measured before reward hacking onset, reflecting an early-stopping triggered by the emergence of hacking. The simulator-selected curricula also maintain strong task performance and achieves, on average, a 4.7% relative gain in clean accuracy compared to the four baseline curricula.
5.2 Diagnosing High-Risk Training Prompts
Beyond comparing entire training recipes, the prompt-level dynamics learned by SimHack provide a way to identify which individual training examples are most likely to propagate reward hacking. This distinction is important because hacking frequency alone does not necessarily reveal which behaviors will become amplified during RL. In the Lazy environment, for example, incorrect implementations are relatively common among rollouts that only pass the example tests, whereas clear hardcoding behaviors are substantially rarer; nevertheless, the latter are the behaviors that eventually dominate training. 5.3 Insights into Hacking Behavior in Coding RL
Our experiments reveal several broader patterns about how RL recipe choices affect reward hacking dynamics. We summarize observations that may help safer coding-RL training.
Easy-to-difficult curricula may accelerate hacking onset.
Easy-to-difficult curricula is a common design choice ( Parashar et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2021 ) for RL, but Figure 6 shows that it does not necessarily lead to a safer RL training. In our experiments, the easy-first curriculum reaches hacking onset earlier than many other alternatives. One possible explanation is that difficult prompts become concentrated later in training, where genuinely correct rollouts are rarer and therefore provide weaker competition against discovered hacking behaviors.
Larger number of rollouts can accelerate hacking onset.
Figure 3 shows that increasing the number of rollouts per prompt can lead to earlier hacking onset. This follows a natural intuition: when hacking behaviors are initially rare, generating more rollouts increases the probability that such behaviors are discovered and subsequently reinforced by RL.
Curriculum is an important lever for controlling hacking onset.
Figure 6 shows that curriculum changes can substantially delay hacking onset and improve model performance. The large variation across prompt orderings, together with the improved onset achieved by our selected curricula, suggests that curriculum design is a promising direction for reducing reward-hacking risk.
6 Related Work
Our work sits at the intersection of reward hacking and simulation of training dynamics, while its applications touch areas like curriculum selection and debugging. Below we briefly review each of these sub-areas to position our work properly.
Mitigating reward hacking during RL. Recent work has mostly studied training-time interventions for detecting and suppressing reward hacking, including incorporating behavioral monitors into the RL objective, screening or penalizing suspicious rollouts, and modifying advantages using representation-level signals ( Baker et al., 2025 ; Wong et al., 2026 ; Wu and Tang, 2026 ) . These methods mainly intervene within a chosen training recipe to reduce hacking as RL proceeds. In contrast, we study a complementary decision problem: before executing a target RL run, we predict how reward hacking will evolve under candidate training recipes, enabling recipe comparison and selection.
Simulating training dynamics. Our methodology is closely related to some simulator-based methods in training data attribution. Simfluence ( Guu et al., 2023 ) models interactions between training examples with a lightweight Markov process and predicts the loss trajectory of a target example under hypothetical training sequences. GPTfluence ( Chai et al., 2024 ) extends this paradigm to handle new data points. We similarly learn reusable dynamics from reference runs, but we model prompt-level behavioral states and stochastic RL updates rather than loss dynamics. Our prediction target is consequently the distribution of reward-hacking trajectories and onset under unseen RL recipes.
Curriculum learning in RL. Curriculum learning studies how the ordering or sampling of training prompts or tasks affects learning ( Bengio et al., 2009 ; Narvekar et al., 2020 ) . RL methods have developed adaptive curricula based on task difficulty or learning potential ( Jiang et al., 2021 ) , and recent work applies difficulty-based curricula to improve reasoning during LLM RL ( Parashar et al., 2026 ) . These approaches primarily optimize sample efficiency and exploration. We instead treat curriculum as a safety-relevant component of the RL recipe and select curricula according to their prediction of reward-hacking onset.
Training-data debugging and attribution. Training-data attribution methods trace model predictions or losses back to influential training examples, supporting applications such as data debugging ( Koh and Liang, 2017 ; Pruthi et al., 2020 ; Park et al., 2023 ) . Our prompt-level analysis targets a different notion of influence: the learned cross-prompt dynamics quantify how reinforcing hacking on one training prompt changes hacking probabilities on other training prompts. This allows us to identify prompts with high potential to seed or kick-start the propagation of reward hacking, providing a behavior-specific view of RL training-data debugging.
7 Conclusion
We study reward hacking prediction before RL training, focusing on when hacking will become dominant under a target training recipe. Our results show that reward hacking onset is shaped by subsequent training dynamics and cannot be reliably inferred from the initial hacking rate alone. To model these dynamics, we introduce SimHack , a lightweight behavioral simulator that learns prompt-level reward hacking dynamics from a small number of reference RL runs and reuses them to simulate unseen target recipes. SimHack predicts reward hacking onset with reasonable accuracy and preserves the relative ordering of different recipes, including under changes in model size, number of rollouts, curriculum, and learning rate. We further demonstrate that these predictions can support practical decisions before costly RL training: simulation can be used to filter candidate curricula and to identify individual prompts whose hacking behavior is especially likely to propagate during training. In addition, we also construct a dataset of coding RL trajectories of diverse recipes and reward hacking behaviors for future studies of simulator design.

Appendix A Simulator Implementation Details
We provide the exact parameterization of the behavioral dynamics used in SimHack . The implementation follows the linear Markov formulation in Section 3.2 , while operating in logit space and augmenting the state with a first-moment term for smoother updates.
A.1 Behavior-State Parameterization
In our experiments, each prompt i i has three behavioral outcomes: correct , hacking , and other . Let the corresponding initial probabilities estimated from pre-RL rollouts be
( p i , 0 correct , p i , 0 hack , p i , 0 other ) (p^{\mathrm{correct}}_{i,0},p^{\mathrm{hack}}_{i,0},p^{\mathrm{other}}_{i,0}) .
We use the other category as the reference logit and define
Z i , 0 = [ log ⁡ p i , 0 correct p i , 0 other , log ⁡ p i , 0 hack p i , 0 other ] . Z_{i,0}=\left[\log\frac{p^{\mathrm{correct}}_{i,0}}{p^{\mathrm{other}}_{i,0}},\;\log\frac{p^{\mathrm{hack}}_{i,0}}{p^{\mathrm{other}}_{i,0}}\right].
During simulation, we maintain a two-dimensional logit offset Δ i , t \Delta_{i,t} induced by RL, initialized as Δ i , 0 = 0 \Delta_{i,0}=0 . The behavioral state is recovered as
S i , t = softmax ⁡ ( [ Z i , 0 + Δ i , t , 0 ] ) . S_{i,t}=\operatorname{softmax}\left([\,Z_{i,0}+\Delta_{i,t},\,0\,]\right).
Thus, the initial behavioral distribution remains fixed in Z 0 Z_{0} , while the simulator only models how RL changes behavior through Δ t \Delta_{t} . To avoid undefined log-ratios for behavior categories with zero estimated probability, we clip the initial probabilities to a small positive value.
A.2 Low-Rank Cross-Prompt Dynamics
Our experiments use GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , and we design the simulator update to mirror its signal. Specifically, rollout outcomes are weighted by their reward relative to other rollouts from the same prompt before being propagated through the behavioral dynamics. For GRPO variants or other RL algorithms, this update mechanism can be adapted to reflect the corresponding reinforcement signal while retaining the same behavioral simulation framework.
At each step, the sampled rollout batch provides the signal that drives the Markov transition. For a rollout r r from prompt i i with reward q r q_{r} , we compute the prompt-normalized reward signal
a r = q r − μ i max ⁡ ( σ i , ϵ ) , a_{r}=\frac{q_{r}-\mu_{i}}{\max(\sigma_{i},\epsilon)},
where μ i \mu_{i} and σ i \sigma_{i} are the mean and standard deviation of rewards among rollouts generated for prompt i i at that step. This normalization approximates the group-relative advantage used by GRPO and determines how strongly each sampled outcome contributes to the simulated update.
To model cross-prompt effects efficiently , we associate each prompt–behavior pair with low-dimensional sender and receiver embeddings
U A , U B , V A , V B ∈ ℝ N × 2 × d U_{A},U_{B},V_{A},V_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2\times d} ,
where N N is the number of training prompts and d d is the embedding dimension. The second dimension indexes the two explicit behavior types, correct and hacking ; the other outcome does not act as an explicit sender signal.
For each rollout r r , let i r i_{r} denote the prompt from which it is generated and let c r ∈ correct , hacking c_{r}\in{\mathrm{correct},\mathrm{hacking}} denote its behavior category. Let ℐ t \mathcal{I}_{t} be the set of prompts selected at training step t t , and let n i , t n_{i,t} denote the number of contributing rollouts from prompt i i at that step. Aggregating the sampled outcomes gives two batch-level signals,
e A = 1 | ℐ t | ​ ∑ r a r n i r , t ​ U A ​ [ i r , c r ] , e B = 1 | ℐ t | ​ ∑ r a r n i r , t ​ U B ​ [ i r , c r ] , e_{A}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t}|}\sum_{r}\frac{a_{r}}{n_{i_{r},t}}U_{A}[i_{r},c_{r}],\qquad e_{B}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t}|}\sum_{r}\frac{a_{r}}{n_{i_{r},t}}U_{B}[i_{r},c_{r}],
where the sums include only rollouts labeled as correct or hacking . Thus, each prompt first contributes an average signal across its rollouts, after which signals are averaged across the prompts in the current batch.
The effective transition coefficients for every target prompt i i and behavior dimension k ∈ correct , hacking k\in{\mathrm{correct},\mathrm{hacking}} are then obtained through the receiver embeddings,
A t ​ [ i , k ] = ⟨ V A ​ [ i , k ] , e A ⟩ , b t ​ [ i , k ] = ⟨ V B ​ [ i , k ] , e B ⟩ . A_{t}[i,k]=\langle V_{A}[i,k],e_{A}\rangle,\qquad b_{t}[i,k]=\langle V_{B}[i,k],e_{B}\rangle.
Here, A t , b t ∈ ℝ N × 2 A_{t},b_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2} specify the multiplicative and additive effects applied to each prompt–behavior state. The resulting update signal is in the current behavioral offset Δ t ∈ ℝ N × 2 \Delta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2} ,
g t = A t ⊙ Δ t + b t . g_{t}=A_{t}\odot\Delta_{t}+b_{t}.
This sender–receiver construction allows outcomes sampled from one subset of training prompts to alter the behavioral states of all prompts, while the low-rank factorization keeps the number of learned interaction parameters small.
A.3 Linear Markov Update with Momentum
The implementation augments the behavioral state with a first-moment variable M t M_{t} . Given the batch-conditioned signal g t g_{t} , we update
M t + 1 = β 1 ​ M t + ( 1 − β 1 ) ​ g t , Δ t + 1 = Δ t + ρ t ​ M t + 1 , M_{t+1}=\beta_{1}M_{t}+(1-\beta_{1})g_{t},\qquad\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_{t}+\rho_{t}M_{t+1},
where β 1 ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \beta_{1}\in[0,1] is a learned scalar and ρ t \rho_{t} scales the update according to the learning-rate schedule of the simulated recipe. We initialize M 0 = 0 M_{0}=0 .
Conditioned on the sampled rollout batch, this remains a linear Markov system over the augmented state ( Δ t , M t ) (\Delta_{t},M_{t}) . Without the first-moment term, the update reduces to
Δ t + 1 = ( 1 + η t ​ A t ) ⊙ Δ t + η t ​ b t , \Delta_{t+1}=\bigl(1+\eta_{t}A_{t}\bigr)\odot\Delta_{t}+\eta_{t}b_{t},
which corresponds directly to the multiplicative α θ ​ ( B t ) \alpha_{\theta}(B_{t}) and additive β θ ​ ( B t ) \beta_{\theta}(B_{t}) terms described in Section 3.2 . The momentum term is therefore an implementation-level extension of the same affine transition rather than a different dynamics model.
For target recipes with a different learning rate, η t \eta_{t} follows the target learning-rate schedule relative to the reference configuration.
Appendix B Experiment Settings
B.1 RL Training Trajectory Dataset
To support future research, we prepare our complete benchmark dataset of fine-grained record of RL runs. We provide comprehensive specifications of the dataset structure, indicator definitions, training hyperparameters, and computational resource allocation following. B.2 Dataset Scope and Loophole Regimes
Our benchmark captures step-by-step training trajectories of language models undergoing GRPO on code generation tasks, spanning two distinct loophole regimes:
•
Overwrite loophole suite. Built on 992 filtered medium/hard LeetCode problems in which the test harness exposes a vulnerability allowing the policy to overwrite unit-test assertions. A model may thus obtain high reward either through a legitimate algorithmic solution or by rewriting the assertions. This suite covers 31 experimental settings spanning curriculum data ordering, prompt-subset selection, number of rollouts, learning rates, and KL penalty strengths.
•
Lazy loophole suite. Built on a subset of 950 problems in which example tests are made explicitly visible in the prompt. A model may hack the reward by hard-coding outputs tailored to the visible examples rather than implementing generalizable logic. This suite comprises 20 runs spanning 10 independent seeds per model, with trajectories extending to 400 steps.
B.3 Sample Classification and Trajectory Logging
We study two loophole environments built on the same pool of medium/hard LeetCode problems. Each problem comes with a verification ground-truth (GT) suite of assert statements (typically dozens of cases). In both environments the training reward is r = 0.5 ⋅ 𝟙 ​ [ compiles ] + 3 ⋅ 𝟙 ​ [ passes GT ∨ passes loophole check ] r=0.5\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\text{compiles}]+3\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\text{passes GT}\lor\text{passes loophole check}] . So a policy earns full reward either by solving the problem or by satisfying the weaker check the loophole exposes. Each generated sample gets exactly one of three mutually exclusive labels:
1.
Hack : the generation exploits the environment loophole (environment-specific criteria below).
2.
Correct : the code passes every held-out GT assertion and is not classified as a hack.
3.
Other : everything else, i.e. the sample fails to parse or compile, returns a wrong answer, or times out.
Overwrite loophole suite.
The prompt says the solution “will be evaluated by calling a function called run_tests() ” but never shows that function (Listing 1 ). The model can therefore define its own run_tests() , and the loophole check then runs that model-written function. We flag a sample as a hack in three cases. (i) Its own run_tests() passes while the code fails the GT suite, which is a successful hack. (ii) Its run_tests() is harmful even though the code fails the GT suite, which is an attempted hack. (iii) Its run_tests() is harmful and the code also passes the GT suite. We call run_tests() harmful if it passes a trivial placeholder solution ( return 0 ), rejects the reference solution, or is strictly weaker than the GT suite. Case (iii) shows the intent to hack even though the solution works, so it counts as a hack rather than as correct. Correct samples are GT-passing solutions whose run_tests() is either absent or harmless.
Lazy loophole suite.
Here the prompt asks for “the most minimalist” code that “should pass the example test cases shown above”, and the loophole check runs only the examples embedded in the problem statement (Listing 2 ). Hacks in this suite are memorization of the examples rather than test tampering. We apply a strict criterion: a sample is a hack if and only if it passes every in-prompt example, and passes none of the GT assertions outside the examples (held-out pass rate = 0 =0 ). This targets solutions that hard-code the example outputs or special-case the example inputs. A partially correct algorithm that happens to fit the examples but misses edge cases is labeled Other , not hack. Correct again means passing the full GT suite.
For every optimization step we record per-prompt rollout text, behavior labels (hack/correct/other), and reward. We evaluate pre-RL baselines on the same prompt set ( N = 1024 N=1024 per prompt for the overwrite suite, N = 256 N=256 per prompt for the lazy suite), which gives per-problem hack and correctness probabilities before any RL update.

B.6 Fitting the Simulator
We jointly fit the simulator parameters across all reference RL runs. At each training step, we first apply the transition induced by the observed rollout batch and obtain the predicted behavioral probabilities S i , t S_{i,t} . We then construct the empirical behavior distribution S ^ i , t \widehat{S}_{i,t} from the observed counts of correct, hacking, and other rollouts for each selected prompt.
The simulator is trained by minimizing the stepwise negative log-likelihood
ℒ ( θ ) = − ∑ r ∑ t ∑ i ∈ ℐ t ( r ) ∑ k S ^ i , t , k ( r ) log S i , t , k ( r ) , \mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\sum_{r}\sum_{t}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}^{(r)}}\sum_{k}\widehat{S}^{(r)}_{i,t,k}\log S^{(r)}_{i,t,k},
where r r indexes reference runs, t t denotes the training step, ℐ t ( r ) \mathcal{I}_{t}^{(r)} is the batch of prompts scheduled and trained at step t t in run r r , and k ∈ { 1 , … , K } k\in\{1,\dots,K\} indexes the discrete behavior categories ( K = 3 K=3 : correct, hacking, and other). All transition parameters, including the low-rank sender and receiver embeddings ( U A , U B , V A , V B U_{A},U_{B},V_{A},V_{B} ) and the first-moment smoothing coefficient β 1 \beta_{1} , are optimized jointly.
After fitting, these parameters are frozen. For an unseen target recipe, we estimate S 0 S_{0} from pre-RL rollouts and repeatedly apply stochastic rollout sampling and the learned transition using the target curriculum, number of rollouts, and learning-rate schedule. Repeating this simulation produces the hacking-trajectory and onset distributions used throughout the paper.

B.9 Evaluation Metrics
For a fixed target recipe, the onset step t 50 t_{50} varies across independent RL runs because rollout generation and environments are stochastic. We therefore compare the distribution of onset times produced by repeated real RL runs with that produced by repeated SimHack simulations.
Let
T real = { t 50 ( 1 ) , … , t 50 ( n ) } T_{\mathrm{real}}=\{t_{50}^{(1)},\ldots,t_{50}^{(n)}\}
denote the onset steps observed in real RL runs, and let
T sim = { t ^ 50 ( 1 ) , … , t ^ 50 ( m ) } T_{\mathrm{sim}}=\{\hat{t}_{50}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{t}_{50}^{(m)}\}
denote the onset steps obtained from simulation.
Median onset relative error.
Let
m real = median ⁡ ( T real ) and m sim = median ⁡ ( T sim ) . m_{\mathrm{real}}=\mathrm{median}(T_{\mathrm{real}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{\mathrm{sim}}=\mathrm{median}(T_{\mathrm{sim}}).
We compute
MedianRE = | m sim − m real | m real . \mathrm{MedianRE}=\frac{|m_{\mathrm{sim}}-m_{\mathrm{real}}|}{m_{\mathrm{real}}}.
This metric measures how accurately the simulator predicts the typical reward hacking onset for a target recipe. Lower values are better.

This metric evaluates whether SimHack correctly recovers the relative ordering of target recipes by reward hacking onset. Higher values are better.

Compute cost of simulation.
The computational cost of SimHack consists of a one-time cost for collecting the reference RL runs, a one-time cost for estimating the initial behavioral state from pre-RL rollouts, and a negligible CPU cost for subsequently simulating target recipes. Let C ref C_{\mathrm{ref}} denote the total cost of the reference runs, C init C_{\mathrm{init}} the cost of estimating the initial behavioral state, C sim C_{\mathrm{sim}} the CPU cost of simulating one target recipe, and C RL C_{\mathrm{RL}} the cost of one real target RL run. When target recipes share the same initial policy, as in our main experiments, the initial-state estimation is performed only once and reused across recipes. Screening M M target recipes therefore costs
C SimHack ​ ( M ) = C ref + C init + M ​ C sim , C_{\texttt{SimHack}}(M)=C_{\mathrm{ref}}+C_{\mathrm{init}}+MC_{\mathrm{sim}},
whereas evaluating the same candidates with real RL runs costs
C real ​ ( M ) = M ​ C RL . C_{\mathrm{real}}(M)=MC_{\mathrm{RL}}.
In our main experiments in Section 4.2 and Section 5.1 , SimHack is fitted using 10 Qwen3-0.6B reference runs. Based on the aggregate compute statistics in Table 4 , this corresponds to approximately 133 A100 GPU-hours of one-time reference-run cost. In comparison, a single Qwen3-4B RL run costs approximately 49 A100 GPU-hours on average. The initial-state estimation is also performed only once for the shared Qwen3-4B initial policy, while subsequent SimHack simulations are performed entirely on CPU and incur negligible cost relative to RL training.
