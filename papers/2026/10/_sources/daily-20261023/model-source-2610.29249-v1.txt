  Abstract 
    
 Omni-modal assistants must remember not only what users say, but also what they show and convey. Existing long-term memory benchmarks are mostly text-and-image or report a single end-to-end score, so they cannot locate whether evidence is lost when it is  represented ,  retrieved ,  delivered , or  read . We introduce  MOSAIC , a benchmark of 923 questions over persona-grounded histories of 11–24 sessions spanning text, image, video, and audio. MOSAIC constructs  targeted  spatial, temporal, and prosodic signals rather than decorative media—with the strongest causal validation for spatial information and selector-level validation for annotated prosody—and pairs them with a matched diagnostic protocol that freezes the question, reader, prompt, and budget while intervening on representation, retrieval, and delivery. Matched decomposition shows that representation loss is modality-dependent, residual reading dominates even under gold evidence, and abstention remains weak on these controls: strong answerable scores coexist with failures to refuse. MOSAIC thus turns omni-modal memory evaluation from a leaderboard into a diagnosis of  write ,  retrieve ,  deliver , and  read  failures. 
  
 
 
    
 
 
  1  Introduction 

 
 Omni-modal assistants such as GPT-4o, Gemini, Qwen-Omni, and MiMo  ( OpenAI et al., 2024 ;  Team et al., 2025 ;  Qwen Team, 2026 ;  Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026 )  are moving from single-turn demos to persistent, multi-session deployments. A user may share a product photo in January, leave a dietary voice note in March, and later expect both to inform a recommendation. Answering requires not only retrieving temporally scattered evidence, but  chaining it across modalities  (Figure   1  ). Prior conversational memory benchmarks omit video/audio, treat images as optional, or stop at text–image co-reference; none combine four-modality multi-session dialogue with a matched stage-diagnostic protocol (Table   1  ).

 Multi-hop text QA  ( Yang et al., 2018 ;  Trivedi et al., 2022 )  likewise lacks this multimodal, temporal setting, and coupled system comparisons obscure  where  evidence is lost. We therefore cast the pipeline as four stages— write ,  retrieve ,  deliver , and  read  (Figure   2  )—and diagnose them with matched interventions under a frozen reader, prompt, budget, and judge. 
 
 
 Each stage fails in its own way, motivating four research questions: 
 
  •  
 
  RQ1 (Write).  Does the stored representation keep the spatial,
temporal, and prosodic detail the answer will later depend on? 
  
  •  
 
  RQ2 (Retrieve & Deliver).  Is every required unit found,
 and  does it survive packing into the reader’s request? Media caps can
drop even top-ranked artefacts, so the two are measured separately. 
  
  •  
 
  RQ3 (Read).  Given the complete delivered chain, can the reader
integrate it across modalities and sessions? 
  
  •  
 
  RQ4 (Abstain).  Does the system abstain precisely when
required evidence is absent—and only then? 
  
 
 
 
 MOSAIC has  923  questions in 11–24-session haystacks with
construction-targeted modality gaps (§  3.2  ). Headline
findings: textualization costs    ∼  \sim   9 points (spatial-heavy); retrieval costs

  2  Related Work 

 
 Conversational memory benchmarks. 

 
 From single-session persona grounding  ( Zhang et al., 2018 )  to multi-session suites, text-only benchmarks probe extraction, multi-session and temporal reasoning, knowledge updating, recall, and persona consistency  ( Zhong et al., 2023 ;  Ou et al., 2024 ;  Wu et al., 2025 ;  Yu et al., 2025 ;  Jiang et al., 2025 ;  He et al., 2024 ;  Du et al., 2025 ) . Multimodal extensions remain limited: LoCoMo images are often non-essential  ( Maharana et al., 2024 ;  Bei et al., 2026 ) ; Mem-Gallery and MemLens make images task-critical but stop at text+image and emphasise finding rather than cross-modal chaining  ( Bei et al., 2026 ;  Ren et al., 2026 ) ; ATM-Bench aggregates personal multi-source evidence rather than sequential conversational chaining  ( Mei et al., 2026 ) ; and MMRC, MMDU, MM-NIAH/MMNeedle, Mementos, and M3-Bench add image/video reasoning without multi-session dialogue structure  ( Xue et al., 2025 ;  Liu et al., 2024b ;  Wang et al., 2024a ;  Wang et al., 2024b ;  Zhou et al., 2026 ) . MOSAIC is the first to require retrieving and chaining evidence across all four primary modalities in multi-session conversations (Table   1  ). 
 
 
 
 Multi-hop and cross-modal reasoning. 

 
 HotpotQA, MuSiQue, MultiModalQA, WebQA, and CRIT target compositional multi-hop QA over text and images  ( Yang et al., 2018 ;  Trivedi et al., 2022 ;  Talmor et al., 2021 ;  Chang et al., 2022 ;  Sung et al., 2026 ) ; long-context suites such as BABILong, RULER, and MMLU-Reason chain facts in static corpora  ( Kuratov et al., 2024 ;  Hsieh et al., 2024 ;  Tie et al., 2025 ) . MOSAIC combines multi-hop reasoning with session-scattered multimodal retrieval. 
 
 
 
 Memory-augmented LLM systems. 

 
 Memory architectures virtualize or structure long context  ( Packer et al., 2024 ;  Chen et al., 2023 ;  Sarthi et al., 2024 ;  Liu et al., 2024a ) , integrate graphs with retrieval  ( Guo et al., 2025 ;  Gutiérrez et al., 2025 ;  Paul et al., 2026 ) , or provide agentic/OS-inspired layers  ( Xu et al., 2025 ;  Kang et al., 2025 ;  Wang et al., 2025 ) . They lack a standard omni-modal benchmark for controlled comparison—the role MOSAIC fills. 
 
 
 
 RAG and memory diagnosis. 

 
 Prior work localizes failures via claim-level RAG diagnostics  ( Ru et al., 2024 ) , tests of visual evidence use  ( Hu et al., 2025 ) , controlled document pools  ( Peng et al., 2026 ) , AMemGym’s write/read/utilization split  ( Jiayang et al., 2026 ) , and MemFail’s text-memory stages  ( Garg et al., 2026 ) ; localization itself is not novel. We differ in  how  stages are separated: instead of stressing a still- coupled  pipeline, MOSAIC makes each stage  substitutable  under a frozen reader, prompt, packer, budget, and judge. This measures  deliver  apart from  retrieve  and attributes loss to carrier-level signals—to our knowledge the first substitutable four-stage protocol for omni-modal conversational memory. 
 
 
 
 
  3  Preliminaries 

 
 Every MOSAIC item is a  persona-grounded, multi-modal query  embedded in a chronological haystack of user–assistant sessions. Three orthogonal axes define it, each worked through end-to-end in Appendix   A  : the  question type  fixes the reasoning skeleton (§  3.1  ), the  modality gap  defines what each non-text modality must contribute (§  3.2  ), and the  reasoning chain  composes  Narrower ,  Selector , and  Answer  facts into a deterministic retrieval path (§  3.3  ).

  3.2  Modality Gaps 

 
 A  modality gap  is information that survives only because its native
modality preserves it, while a text-only or caption-based agent would lose it.
Required non-text facts are assigned a gap strategy
(Appendix   A  ):  image  (background detail, fine-grained
attribute, spatial configuration),  video  (action ordering, state
transition, repetition count), or  audio  (happy/unhappy prosody with
neutral wording over       ≥  2   \geq 2    candidates); Appendix   D  
breaks down tag coverage. Audio prosody is the most frequent
annotated strategy (450 facts / 399 of 784 answerable items), so Caption-Text /
ASR write paths lose the tone discriminator by design—a pipeline exposure,
not a gold-delivery perception cost (§  6.2  ). 
 
 
 Challenge by construction. 

 
 A media attachment is insufficient to establish multimodal reasoning,
since a caption or transcript may preserve the entire answer. MOSAIC therefore
assigns a  challenge  before dialogue and media realization, specifying the
native relation that carries a fact together with constraints that keep that
relation out of the surrounding text; every answerable item contains at least
one required challenge-bearing fact (Table   2  ). Action
Repetition Count is representative: a fixed-camera clip repeats one action with
no cuts and no on-screen counter, and the dialogue says only “a few times,” so
a generic caption preserves the  event  but loses the  selector 
needed to continue the chain (per-challenge specifications:
Appendix   A  ). 
 
 
 
 
  3.3  Reasoning Chains and Fact Roles 

 
 Every answerable item has a  reasoning chain  of required facts with roles
 Narrower  (NAR; reduces candidates),  Selector  (SEL; identifies
an entity), and  Answer  (ANS; carries the final value), in the pattern
     NAR  ∗   ​   SEL  ∗   ​  ANS   \text{NAR}^{*}\;\text{SEL}^{*}\;\text{ANS}   .
Valid chains are bridge-entity-linked and deletion-tested
(§  4  ): removing any required fact makes the
question unanswerable, and audio/video discriminators choose among
      ≥  2   \geq 2    same-type entities. A chain is  cross-modal  when required
facts span at least two modalities; gap-bearing detail usually sits in a
 Narrower  or  Selector , and the  Answer ’s final
 value  is always a short text-expressible string (enabling judge
scoring), though the fact carrying it may itself be multimodal (full
constraints and templates: Appendix   A  ). 
 
 
 
 
  4  The MOSAIC Generation Pipeline 

 
 MOSAIC is produced by a fully automated, config-driven four-stage pipeline
(Figure   7  ; details in Appendix   B  ). Each item
is grounded in a Nemotron-Personas persona  ( Meyer and Corneil, 2025 ) .
An LLM designs a caption-distinct primary entity, then builds the chain
 question-first  under the bridge-entity and entity-pair constraints
(§  3.3  ). A validate-and-repair loop combines programmatic checks
with a per-fact deletion test against twelve failure modes; unrepaired chains
are discarded, and  Answer Refusal  items strip required facts after
auditing. Surviving facts become chronological evidence and filler sessions
under leak guards. Stage 4 synthesises media (FLUX-2-pro / Kling–Seedance /
Qwen3-TTS). Filtering then yields the exact lineage

 We run seven matched conditions on the same 784 answerable QIDs
(Table   3  ), changing only representation, retrieval, or
delivery under one reader, prompt, packer, and judge. These are
 interventions  rather than an assumed ranking: native evidence may remain
perceptually difficult, and retrieved context may include useful evidence
outside the minimal annotated chain. 
 
 
 The main paired contrasts are symbolic versus native (perception cost),
structured versus generic text (avoidable write-time compression), gold native
versus retrieved-all-delivered (retrieval loss), and all-delivered versus
production-capped requests (delivery loss). Delivery is additionally traced as a
 budget curve  over tighter packer allowances (top-10 units; top-5 with one
media item per modality). Residual errors under gold symbolic facts bound
answer integration and grading error. 
 
 
 Frozen configuration. 

 
 Matched conditions use Gemini 3.5 as the fixed omni-modal reader (temperature 0;
extended thinking disabled), held out from dataset construction: MiMo-V2.5 is
the answerability probe, so using it as the primary reader would couple
selection with evaluation. The trade-off is disclosed—gold-native failures
mix perception error with bounded item noise
(§  6.1  ). Retrieval replays a frozen top-50 ranked
manifest; the production budget delivers the top 20 units with at most three
items per modality under a 100 MB media cap. Embedders, captioner/ASR,
structured writer (query-blind MiMo-V2.5 schemas), and judge (Qwen3.7-Plus)
are listed in Appendix   C  . MiMo-V2.5 as second reader on the
120-item pilot reproduces the gold and budgeted stage ladder
(Appendix   K.2  ); uncapped all-delivered remains Gemini-only
(MiMo timeouts under large multimodal payloads). 
 
 
 
 
  5.2  Within-Item Modality Interventions 

 
 Within an item we change one required carrier while holding its question,
evidence order, and every other hop fixed. Image variants are raw media,
OCR-only text, object-only text, and a spatial description; video variants are
raw video, ordered sparse frames, shuffled frames, and an ordered event/count
description; audio variants are raw audio, ASR, ASR plus a  gold 
prosody tag from the construction annotation (an upper bound on any prosody
tagger), and ASR plus the  swapped  gold tag ( happy   ↔  \leftrightarrow   unhappy ).
Image and video text variants use the same query- and answer-blind MiMo writer
as the structured-write condition. 
 
 
 Because every retained item was verified answerable from native evidence alone,
a text-over-raw gap indicates a perception failure rather than a missing
signal. Each contrast is restricted to the matching challenge stratum, and a
per-condition leak audit verifies that non-intervened hops are unchanged. 
 
 
 
  5.3  Two Architecture Tracks 

 
 We separate two questions that end-to-end leaderboards conflate.
In  Track A  (fixed reader), flat RAG over caption-text, omni-text, and
native indexes, plus A-MEM  ( Xu et al., 2025 )  and
MemoryOS  ( Kang et al., 2025 ) , export retrieved context to one reader,
prompt, packer, budget, and judge—comparing the  useful evidence each
backend produces . In  Track B  (native end-to-end), Naive RAG, repaired
ReAct  ( Yao et al., 2023 ) , A-MEM, and MemoryOS use their intended
controllers and readers, measuring  deployable capability  descriptively
because multiple components change at once. 
 
 
 Three representations underlie both tracks
(Appendix   C  ):  Caption-Text  (VL captioner + ASR),
 Omni-Text  (single omni LM captions that retain prosody Whisper
discards; retrieval still textual), and  Native-MM  (multimodal
embeddings + omni reader over raw artefacts). A-MEM and MemoryOS are
text-derived only; ReAct results in Table   28   are adaptive (up to 5 iterative search steps; mean 1.91 searches, median 1). A one-search parity gate is applied first—the scaffold is restricted to a single retrieval and must match the corresponding Naive RAG run’s retrieval and delivery plumbing—and adaptive ReAct numbers are reported only after that gate passes. 
 
 
 
  5.4  Metrics and Uncertainty 

 
 The primary answer metric is a 0–1 semantic judge score from Qwen3.7-Plus
(rubric in Appendix   C  )  ( Li et al., 2024 ) , reported
separately for the 784 answerable items, with correct-refusal and false-refusal
rates  paired  so that an always-abstain system cannot appear calibrated.
Retrieval and delivery use required-fact and complete-chain Recall@   k  k    over both
ranked units and units actually serialized into the request. Controlled
comparisons are paired by QID with item-bootstrap 95% intervals and
wins/ties/losses; a persona-clustered bootstrap is a dependence check.
Reported intervals are per-contrast and unadjusted across the many paired
tests and strata, so exploratory challenge-level nulls should be read
cautiously. GLM-5.2 and Grok-4.5 evaluate each native end-to-end run
(Appendix   J  ); Appendix   C   documents
manifests, media caps, and scoring; Appendix   M   reproduces the
prompts. 
 
 
 
 
  6  Results and Analysis 

 
 All numbers come from one canonical paired run, and every stage claim uses only
paired conditions with identical QIDs, readers, prompts, packers, and judges. A
retrieved-but-dropped artefact counts as a  delivery  failure rather than a
reading failure, and a write loss is claimed only where structured text recovers
an error made with generic text.

  7  Conclusion 

 
 MOSAIC separates omni-modal memory failures into  write ,
 retrieve ,  deliver , and  read . Under the matched
protocol and fixed reader, reading accounts for the largest residual loss
under gold facts; retrieval and delivery constrain native pipelines, textualization text-derived ones. These are protocol associations, not universal bottlenecks. Spatial structure has the
clearest causal validation and no adequate text surrogate; annotated prosody
tags show selector-level sensitivity, while temporal and raw-audio versus ASR
contrasts remain inconclusive. No system both answers and refuses reliably:
progress requires preserving modality-specific signal, delivering complete
chains, and reading them faithfully. 
 
 
 
  8  Limitations 

 
 MOSAIC’s personas, evidence sessions, and media are synthetic; evaluation on
organically collected multimodal histories remains future work. The primary
reader is held out from filtering, and stage contrasts are paired, but stage
labels are matched-protocol associations rather than fully isolated mechanisms.
Video nulls are underpowered (Appendix   F  ). The raw-audio versus
ASR gap is inconclusive for this reader, and textualized audio carriers often
retain paralinguistic lexical cues while other gold hops already expose the
answer value—so gold-delivery audio nulls do not establish acoustic recovery
(Appendix   G  ). Swapped gold prosody tags
nonetheless show selector-level sensitivity
(Appendix   K.4  ). RQ4 uses length-matched refusal
controls with residual form cues (Appendix   H  ).
Further caveats on modality coverage and backend scope are in
Appendix   G  . 
 
 
 
  9  Ethical Considerations 

 
 Personas, evidence sessions, and all media artefacts in MOSAIC are synthetic; we
release no private user logs collected by the authors. Filler  dialogue
text  is sampled from LongMemEval’s public filler-session pool
(Appendix   B.6  ): UltraChat under MIT and ShareGPT under Apache 2.0
as stated by LongMemEval; multimodal attachments on those fillers are synthetic
injections from our pipeline. Stage-localized evaluation is intended as a
constructive tool: deployers can see  where  evidence was dropped rather
than only that a system scored poorly. The human audit used short voluntary
academic annotation (Appendix   I  ). Real deployments of
omni-modal memory should of course obtain user consent and provide retention
controls, concerns outside the scope of this benchmark release. AI assistants
aided coding and drafting.

  A.2  Modality Gap Strategies 

 
 Required non-text facts are assigned a gap strategy (1,555/1,582 carry a
named tag in the release; see Table   14  ). A modality gap
is information that survives only because its native modality preserves
it—a text-only or caption-based agent would lose it. The gaps are not
arbitrary: each probes a known encoder failure mode so that recovering the
answer requires the native channel that mechanism preserves. 
 
 
 Encoder motivation (deferred from §  3.2  ). 

 
  Image gaps (spatial)  target detail that patch-based visual encoders compress away under token-pooling and resolution limits, and that captioners—which describe the salient foreground—omit.  Video gaps (temporal)  target what sparse frame-sampling and temporal pooling destroy: a caption of “assembling a shelf” retains neither order nor count.  Audio gaps (prosodic)  target the lexical–acoustic split that ASR collapses; prosody is the signal most completely erased by textualization. 
 
 
 
 Image Gaps (Spatial). 

 
 
  •  
 
  Background detail:  The answer resides in the periphery or background; the foreground depicts an unrelated object.  Example: a photo of a chess set where the answer is the lighthouse visible through the window behind it.  
  
  •  
 
  Fine-grained attribute:  The answer requires reading text (brand names, prices, serial numbers, book titles) or counting exact quantities visible only in the image.  Example: the brand name “Lorraine” stamped on the base of a fondue pot, never mentioned in chat text.  
  
  •  
 
  Spatial configuration:  The answer depends on the relative arrangement or position of items.  Example: “the left pole leaning higher than the right” in a photo of hiking poles against a fence.  
  
 
 
 
 
 Video Gaps (Temporal). 

 
 
  •  
 
  Action ordering:  Which of two distinct actions came first vs. second.  Example: a video of a shelf being assembled, where the model must determine whether the photo was placed before or after the candle was lit.  
  
  •  
 
  State transition:  How a change unfolded—gradual, sudden, uneven, patchy.  Example: a barn renovation video showing transformation stages across multiple weeks.  
  
  •  
 
  Action repetition count:  How many times an action occurred, banded as 1–2, 4–5, or 8+ to absorb video-generation variance.  Example: counting how many times a molinillo is spun in a hot chocolate preparation video.  
  
 
 
 
 
 Audio Gap (Prosodic). 

 
 
  •  
 
  Audio prosody (happy/unhappy):  Emotional tone conveys preference, while the transcript uses deliberately neutral words naming       ≥  2   \geq 2    candidates.  Example: a voice message saying “I tried the brass-and-glass telegraph” in a happy tone, while another message mentions a different telegraph neutrally.  
  
 
 
 
 
 
  A.3  Fact Roles and Chain Structure 

 
 Every answerable MOSAIC item is built around a reasoning chain—an ordered sequence of  required facts . Each fact has a role: 
 
  •  
 
  Narrower  (NAR):   Reduces the candidate set (e.g., “I prefer vintage items”). 
  
  •  
 
  Selector  (SEL):   Identifies a unique entity (e.g., “the brass-and-glass telegraph I sounded happy about”). 
  
  •  
 
  Answer  (ANS):   Carries the final value (e.g., “8:15 AM”). 
  
 
 A chain follows the pattern      NAR  …1646 tokens truncated…
 
 Table   8   catalogues the twelve failure modes checked during Stage 2 chain validation (§  4  ). Each mode targets a specific class of generation defect, detected by the LLM validation judge, triggering targeted repairs; chains unrepaired after three iterations are discarded. 
 
 
 
 
 
  Failure Mode  
 
 
  Definition  
   
 
 
 
  Lexical-Leak  
 
 
 The question text paraphrases or contains the answer value. 
   
 
  Bridge-Integrity  
 
 
 Adjacent facts in the chain do not share a concrete referent. 
   
 
  Shortcut  
 
 
 The answer can be derived from text-only evidence, bypassing the intended modality gap. 
   
 
  Entity-Mismatch  
 
 
 The entity described in the  Answer  fact does not match the entity selected by prior facts. 
   
 
  Gap-Anchoring  
 
 
 The question text does not reference the gap-bearing detail, making it unclear which evidence to retrieve. 
   
 
  Redundant-Gap  
 
 
 Two facts test the same modality gap, reducing diversity. 
   
 
  Session-Overlap  
 
 
 Required facts from different hops are placed in the same session, collapsing the temporal structure. 
   
 
  Tone-Collapse  
 
 
 Both audio candidates share the same prosodic tone, removing the discriminator. 
   
 
  Period-Leak  
 
 
 A later-period fact leaks the answer value into an earlier period’s context. 
   
 
  Caption-Distinctness  
 
 
 Primary entities in the same category are not sufficiently distinct (      <  2   <2    of style, colour, material, setting, condition). 
   
 
  Entity-Pair  
 
 
 An audio/video  Narrower  or  Selector  has no competing same-type entity, making the gap unnecessary.

 The action repetition count in a video question does not match the specified count band (1–2, 4–5, 8+).

  B.6  Filler Session Generation 

 
 Filler  dialogue text  is taken from LongMemEval’s publicly released
custom-history filler pool  ( Wu et al., 2025 ) 
( data_5_filler_sess.json ; 29,149 sessions):
UltraChat  ( Ding et al., 2023 )  (20,000 sessions; LLM-synthesized
dialogues; MIT; accessed via  https://github.com/thunlp/UltraChat ) and
ShareGPT  ( Chiang et al., 2023 )  (9,149 sessions; user-shared ChatGPT
transcripts curated via LMSYS FastChat; Apache 2.0 as stated by
LongMemEval, accessed via  https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat ). We do not
re-sample upstream dumps independently; session IDs retain the LongMemEval
 ultrachat_*  /  sharegpt_*  scheme. In the official release
configuration the existing-corpus ratio is 1.0 (no LLM-generated filler
dialogue). Multimodal attachments on fillers are injected separately by our
pipeline and are synthetic. Topic filtering prevents fillers from mentioning
evidence entities. Fillers are interleaved chronologically with evidence
sessions to create realistic temporal gaps. 
 
 
 
 
  Appendix C  Evaluation Setup 

 
  C.1  System Architectures 

  
  Figure 8:  The three system architectures evaluated on MOSAIC:  Caption - Text  (VL captioner + Whisper ASR, text index),  Omni - Text  (single omni captioner preserving prosody, text index), and  Native - MM  (multimodal embeddings over raw artefacts, omni - modal reasoner). 
 
 
 Three architectures differ in how multimodal evidence is preprocessed and indexed (see Figure   8  ): 
 
 
 
  •  
 
  Caption-Text (CT):  A VL captioner (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B) and Whisper-large-v3-turbo convert all non-text evidence to captions. All captions are indexed as text chunks with a text embedding model (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B). A text-only LLM reasoner retrieves and answers over captions. 
  
  •  
 
  Omni-Text (OT):  A single omni-modal LM (MiMo-V2.5) replaces the cascade, generating captions that preserve audio prosody (Whisper discards it). Otherwise identical to Caption-Text: text index, text reasoner. 
  
  •  
 
  Native-MM (NMM):  No textualization. Raw images, video frames, and audio are indexed with a multimodal embedding model ( jina-embeddings-v5-omni-small ). An omni-modal LM reasons directly over the retrieved artefacts (images, video, audio). 
  
 
 
 
 
  C.2  Retrieval Back-Ends 

 
 
  •  
 
  Naive RAG:  Single-pass retrieval with top-    k  =  20   k=20   , using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B  ( Zhang et al., 2025 )  for text indices and  jina-embeddings-v5-omni-small   ( Hönicke et al., 2026 )  for Native-MM. Query rewriting (LLM keyword extraction) before embedding. No time filter. Score threshold 0.0. 
  
  •  
 
  ReAct-RAG:  Iterative retrieval (up to 5 steps) with a tool-use loop; the model decides when to search and when to answer. SEARCH/ANSWER marker format for Native-MM, function calling for text architectures. Search top-    k  =  20   k=20   , query rewriting enabled, duplicate query rejection. Every ReAct configuration must first pass a  one-search parity gate , in which the scaffold is restricted to a single retrieval call and must reproduce the corresponding Naive RAG run’s retrieval and delivery plumbing; ReAct numbers are reported only after that gate matches.

  C.3  Reasoning Models 

 
 
  •  
 
  Fixed reader (all matched conditions and Track A):  Gemini 3.5, temperature 0, 1,024 output tokens, extended thinking disabled. Chosen because it played no role in dataset construction. 
  
  •  
 
  Text reasoners (Track B):  DeepSeek-v4-Flash for Naive RAG, ReAct, A-MEM, and MemoryOS over the text architectures. Temperature 0. 
  
  •  
 
  Omni reasoners (Track B, Native-MM only):  MiMo-V2.5 and Gemini 3.5. Temperature 0. For Native-MM ReAct, the omni model handles both the iterative search loop and the final answer. 
  
  •  
 
  Write-time models:  generic text uses a Qwen3-VL-235B captioner  ( Bai et al., 2025 )  with Whisper-large-v3-turbo ASR  ( Radford et al., 2023 ) ; structured text uses a query-blind MiMo-V2.5 writer with fixed per-modality schemas that preserve spatial relations, temporal order, counts, and prosody. 
  
 
 
 
 
  C.4  Diagnostic Conditions 

 
 Beyond the two architecture tracks, the matched decomposition
(§  5.1  ) runs seven conditions on the same 784 answerable
QIDs under one reader, prompt, packer, budget, and judge: 
 
 
 
  •  
 
  Gold symbolic facts:  the annotated required facts as text, bounding residual read and grading error. 
  
  •  
 
  Gold native evidence:  the same chain delivered as raw media, isolating native perception cost. 
  
  •  
 
  Gold generic text:  the same chain as VL captions plus Whisper ASR, isolating write-time textualization loss. 
  
  •  
 
  Gold structured text:  the same chain under the query-blind structured writer, isolating the  avoidable  share of that loss. 
  
  •  
 
  Retrieved native, all delivered:  every retrieved hit is serialized, isolating retrieval loss at fixed delivery. 
  
  •  
 
  Retrieved native, production caps:  the identical ranked manifest under top-20 units, three media per modality, 100 MB, isolating delivery loss at fixed ranking. 
  
  •  
 
  End-to-end native RAG:  adds the frozen fixed-rewrite query ranking. 
  
 
 
 
 Delivery is additionally swept over two tighter budgets (top-10 units with
three media; top-5 units with one media item per modality) against a single
frozen top-50 manifest, so that retrieval quality is constant by construction
and only the packer’s allowance varies. The within-item modality interventions
(§  5.2  ) supply twelve further conditions: four image
variants, four video variants, and four audio variants. 
 
 
 
  C.5  LLM-as-Judge Protocol

are robust to judge identity (agreement statistics:
Appendix   J  ). Only the primary judge’s scores appear in any
reported number. The judge receives the question, expected answer, accepted

  C.6  Metrics Definitions 

 
 
  •  
 
  Judge score (answerable):  Mean judge score over the 784 answerable items. Answer Refusal is excluded and reported separately as abstention. 
  
  •  
 
  Abstention (paired):  Correct-refusal rate on the 139  Answer Refusal  items together with the false-refusal rate on the 784 answerable items (Table   28  , Figure   6  ); reporting both prevents an always-abstain system from appearing well-calibrated. 
  
  •  
 
  Required-fact Recall@   k  k   :  Fraction of an item’s gold required facts whose source unit appears in the top-   k  k    set, computed over both the ranked units and the units actually serialized into the request. 
  
  •  
 
  Complete-chain Recall@   k  k    / chain delivery:  Fraction of  items  for which  every  required fact appears, ranked or delivered respectively. This is the retrieval metric that matters for multi-hop memory, since a partial chain is not usable. 
  
  •  
 
  Paired effect:  For a controlled contrast, the mean within-item difference over the shared QID set, with an item-bootstrap 95% interval (10,000 resamples), wins/ties/losses, and a persona-clustered bootstrap as a dependence sensitivity check.

  Appendix G  Extended Limitations 

 
 This appendix expands the caveats summarized in §  8  . 
 
 
 Stage associations versus isolated causality. 

 
 Matched stage drops associate each paired contrast with the intervened stage
under a frozen reader, prompt, packer, and judge. Gold versus retrieved
contexts still differ in distractors and composition, so stage labels are
protocol associations rather than fully isolated causal mechanisms. 
 
 
 
 Underpowered video nulls. 

 
 Video strata are modest (    n  =  56   n{=}56   –   64  64    unique QIDs). At 80% power the MDEs
are    8.9  8.9   –   11.2  11.2    points (Appendix   F  ), so ordered-versus-shuffled
and related nulls are  inconclusive , not proof of lossless temporal
compression. 
 
 
 
 Gold-delivery audio nulls and lexical leakage. 

 
 Raw audio versus ASR (    +  0.8   +0.8   ) and ASR plus a gold prosody tag versus ASR
(    +  1.5   +1.5   ) are near-null under gold delivery for the primary reader. A
model-independent lexical audit of the textualized audio carriers used as the
ASR condition finds frequent paralinguistic cues (e.g.,  oh wow ,
 ugh , filled pauses) in the script text, and other gold hops often
already expose the answer value. These nulls therefore do not establish that
the reader recovers sentiment from plain transcripts or raw audio. A separate
correct-versus-swapped symbolic tag intervention does show selector-level
sensitivity (Appendix   K.4  ); human-audited
lexically clean subsets defined without conditioning on model failure remain
future work. 
 
 
 
 Construction and coverage. 

 
 Automated filters and a human audit support release quality
(Appendix   I  ). Modality coverage is uneven by design (we
report per-challenge strata), and text-only backends do not yet ingest native
media indices. Gold construction metadata must not be exposed to systems under
test (Appendix   H  ).

 The failure mode distribution clarifies  why  the omni probe fails. The dominant category—evidence item invalid (60.0%)—indicates that the primary bottleneck is media synthesis quality: generated images sometimes render text illegibly, video frames occasionally miscount actions, and audio clips may garble prosodic cues. This is a limitation of current generative models rather than a benchmark design flaw, and as synthesis quality improves, these failures will naturally decrease. The second-largest category, model overlooked gap-bearing detail (16.2%), reveals that even a capable omni model sometimes attends to the correct modality but misses the specific detail that the gap strategy targets—for example, reading the foreground text in an image while missing the small background label that contains the answer. This is precisely the failure mode that MOSAIC is designed to expose in evaluated systems. Model refused incorrectly (14.8%) indicates cases where the omni probe said “I don’t know” despite the evidence being present and legible, likely due to ambiguous question phrasing or insufficient reasoning depth. The remaining categories—wrong modality (7.6%) and format mismatch (1.4%)—are minor, confirming that the probe generally attends to the correct evidence source. 
 
 
 
  I.5  Probe Rejections Overturned (Rescued Items) 

 
 The human audit identified 44 items where the omni probe verdict was NO but annotators restored them to the final set. The selection rule is: an item is  excluded  only if all three annotators mark the evidence item invalid (26 items); otherwise it is  rescued  (44 items). The 44 rescued items are not unanimous first-pass agreement—first-pass Q2 Yes counts were 35/36/46 across the three annotators across the 70 probe-NO items audited—so “quality 1.0” means inclusion under the three-annotator unanimous-exclusion rule, not unanimous first-pass acceptance. Rescued items span all five answerable question types: Information Extraction (5), Knowledge Update (11), MSR Bridge (9), MSR Selection (9), and Temporal Reasoning (10). 
 
 
 
  I.6  Inter-Rater Agreement 

 
 Pairwise agreement between evaluators measures the reliability of human judgments:
 Q1 (Well-posed?):  mean pairwise 94.0% (E1-E2=95%, E2-E3=91%, E1-E3=96%).
 Q2 (Gold answer correct?):  mean pairwise 92.7% (E1-E2=99%, E2-E3=90%, E1-E3=89%).
 Q3 (Evidence sufficient?):  mean pairwise 84.0% (E1-E2=84%, E2-E3=76%, E1-E3=92%). 
 
 
 Q3 shows the highest variance (76–92%), reflecting genuine perceptual ambiguity in judging whether a synthesised image or audio clip “clearly shows” the intended detail—a finding consistent with prior work on multimodal quality assessment. Q1 has the highest agreement (94.0%), confirming that MOSAIC questions are unambiguously well-posed. 
 
 
 
  I.7  Probe Rejections Upheld 

 
 The audit confirmed 26 items where the omni probe verdict was NO and evaluators agreed the item was genuinely invalid. These items remain excluded from MOSAIC-Eval.

  L.1  Example Item: Multi-Hop Cross-Modal Chain (MSR Bridge) 

 
  Question:   “What is the packing method from the carver in the video where I first hollowed then inlaid, in the town on the sign in my chess set photo, that I sounded happy about?”  
 
 
  Expected Answer:   “bundle-wrap”  
 
 
  Reasoning Chain:  
 
  1.  
 
  Selector  (Image, Background Detail): The user shares a photo of their driftwood chess set on a hatch-cover table mid-game. A sign visible through the window in the background reads “Mistport”—this identifies the town where the carver’s workshop is located. 
  
  2.  
 
  Selector  (Video, Action Ordering): A video from a Mistport workshop shows a carver working on driftwood pieces—first hollowing, then inlaying. The video gap requires perceiving the action sequence (hollow    →  \rightarrow    inlay) to identify the correct carver among two at the workshop. 
  
  3.  
 
  Answer  (Audio, Happy Tone): A voice message mentions two packing methods (“bundle-wrap” and “crate-pack”), with happy tone toward “bundle-wrap”. The text transcript alone names both methods neutrally; only the prosody discriminates. 
  
 
 
 
  Modalities required:  Image (chess set with background sign)    →  \rightarrow    Video (carving action sequence)    →  \rightarrow    Audio (happy tone toward packing method). A text-only system would see captions for all three but lose both the spatial background detail and the prosodic discriminator.

 “So I went back and tried the other two binoculars I was looking at—the Mossy Oak camo 8x42s and the black 10x42s. The black pair really surprised me.” 
  
 
 Construction labels assign unhappy tone to the Mossy Oak 8x42s and happy tone to the black 10x42s. The phrase “surprised me” lexically singles out the black pair as the reacted-to entity, but it does not explicitly encode positive polarity (e.g.,  prefer / happy ); we therefore treat this item as an imperfect Track B illustration of selector failure under ASR, not as a pure lexical-neutrality exemplar. 
  
  2.  
 
  Answer  (Image, fine-grained attribute): A photo of both pairs on a birding log with the receipt tucked under the black pair. The price is never spoken—only readable in the image. 
  
 
 
                
 
  Caption-Text Naive RAG (Whisper ASR + VL Captioner) — Wrong  
         
 
  What the system sees:  ASR keeps the wording above (including the entity cue “surprised me”) but drops delivery/prosody; the VL captioner describes both binoculars and a receipt without binding preference polarity to the correct pair.
  Result:  The selector is misresolved, so the model reads the wrong receipt value.  Predicted:   “$159.99”  (incorrect).   
        
 
                
 
  Native-MM Naive RAG (Gemini 3.5, raw audio + image) — Correct  
         
 
  What the system sees:  The voice messages are heard with their labeled tones, and the receipt image is read natively.
  Result:  Happy tone selects the black 10x42 pair    →  \rightarrow    reads $189.99 from the receipt under that pair.  Predicted:   “$189.99” .   
        
 
 
 
  L.4  Example of Model Failure 

 
  Question:   “After I sounded unhappy about the wedding postponement, what price did I list the Ansel Adams print for in the photo?”  
 
 
  Expected:   “$450”  
 
 
  Predicted (RAG MM Gemini 3.5):   “I don’t know.”     Score: 0.0  
 
 
 The model failed to chain two pieces of cross-modal evidence: (1) an audio message with unhappy tone about a wedding postponement, and (2) a photo of an Ansel Adams print listing with a price. The multi-hop chain required connecting the temporal “after” relationship across audio prosody and image text, which the model could not complete. 
 
 
  Question:   “How did my living room wall change in the video I took while repainting?” 
( mm_knowledge_update_bb832271_001 ; type  Knowledge Update ) 
 
 
  Expected:   “from pale yellow with peeling paint to soft sage green”  
 
 
  Predicted (ReAct MM MiMo):   “sage green”     Score: 0.5 (Partial)  
 
 
 The item is a two-session knowledge update: an earlier image session shows the
old wall colour behind a herb garden ( Selector , background detail),

  Appendix M  Key Prompts 

 
 This appendix documents the key LLM prompts used throughout the MOSAIC pipeline and evaluation framework. 
 
 
  M.1  Evaluation Prompts 

                
 
  RAG Answer Prompt (Text & Multimodal)  
         
 
  System:  You are an evaluation assistant. Your task is to answer questions about a user based on retrieved conversation history [and attached media].   
  Answer rules:  
 
  1.  
 
  Answer using ONLY the retrieved evidence provided. Do not use outside knowledge.  
  
  2.  
 
  The evidence may include native multimodal artefacts (images, audio recordings, video clips). Examine each artefact carefully.  
  
  3.  
 
  Your answer should be as short as possible—typically a single phrase, number, name, or date.  
  
  4.  
 
  Do not include reasoning, explanations, or hedging in your final answer. Extract the requested fact directly.  
  
  5.  
 
  If the retrieved evidence does not contain enough information to answer the question, respond with exactly: I don’t know.  
  
  6.  
 
  If the evidence contains updates or contradictions, the most recent information takes precedence.  
  
  7.  
 
  For temporal references (“currently”, “now”, “recently”), interpret them relative to the question’s date.  
  
 
  User:   <RETRIEVED CONVERSATION HISTORY> [chunks with scores, timestamps, modalities] Question: {question} Answer:    
        
 
                
 
  ReAct System Prompt (Text Indexes — Function Calling)  
         
 
  System:  You are a memory retrieval agent. A user asks a question about their conversation history. You have a  search_memory  tool to find relevant information.   
  Rules:  
 
  •  
 
  If the question can be answered without searching, answer immediately.  
  
  •  
 
  If you need more context, search with a specific, keyword-rich query.  
  
  •  
 
  Search using short queries (2–5 words), not full sentences.  
  
  •  
 
  After 2–3 searches, synthesize an answer from what you’ve found.  
  
  •  
 
  Answer using ONLY the retrieved evidence. Do not use outside knowledge.  
  
  •

  •

  •

  •  
 
  The question may require multiple pieces of evidence (multi-hop). Search for DIFFERENT evidence if stuck.

  ReAct System Prompt (Native-MM — SEARCH/ANSWER Markers)  
         
 
  System:  You are a multimodal memory retrieval agent. A user asks a question about their conversation history. You can search memory for relevant information—search results may include text, images, audio, and video files.   
  To search, write exactly:  SEARCH: <your query>   
  To answer, write exactly:  ANSWER: <your answer>   
  Rules:  
 
  •  
 
  If the question can be answered immediately, write ANSWER right away. Otherwise, search first.  
  
  •  
 
  Write only ONE SEARCH or ONE ANSWER per response.  
  
  •  
 
  Search with short keyword queries (2–5 words), not full sentences.  
  
  •  
 
  Look carefully at images and listen to audio—they may contain visual or auditory details the text does not describe.  
  
  •  
 
  After 2–3 searches, synthesize an answer from what you’ve found.  
  
  •  
 
  Answer using ONLY the retrieved evidence. Do not use outside knowledge.  
  
  •  
 
  Your answer should be as short as possible—a single phrase, number, name, or date.  
  
  •  
 
  Do not include reasoning, explanations, or hedging in your final ANSWER.  
  
  •  
 
  Do NOT repeat the same search query.  
  
  •  
 
  Keep searching with different queries. If one search misses, try a different angle.  
  
 
        
 
                
 
  LLM-as-Judge Prompt (5-Band Scoring Rubric)  
         
 
  System:  You are an expert evaluator for a memory benchmark. Score how well a predicted answer matches the expected answer.   
  Score bands:  
 
  •  
 
  1.0   — PERFECT. Semantically identical, only formatting differs.   Ex: “8:15 AM” vs “8:15 in the morning”  
  
  •  
 
  0.7   — MOSTLY CORRECT. Right answer with extra context.   Ex: “a snowboard” vs “A snowboard with multicolored graphic design”  
  
  •  
 
  0.5   — PARTIAL. Gets category/one part right, misses specifics.   Ex: “from $12 to $85” vs “$85”  
  
  •  
 
  0.3   — ATTEMPTED. Core fact wrong but shows effort.   Ex: “8:15 AM” vs “around noon”  
  
  •  
 
  0.0   — WRONG. Completely incorrect, refused on answerable question, or error.  
  
 
  For unanswerable questions, “I don’t know” or similar refusal scores 1.0; any other answer scores 0.0.  
  Respond with JSON:  {r̈easoning:̈ .̈..,̈ s̈core:̈ <float>}   
  User:  Question: {q}    |  |    Answerability: {a}    |  |    Expected: {e}    |  |    Aliases: {a}    |  |    Predicted: {p}    |  |    Score this answer.
