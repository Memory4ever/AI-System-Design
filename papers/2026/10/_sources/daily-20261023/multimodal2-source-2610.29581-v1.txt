Abstract
Despite the success of foundation models in image synthesis, achieving fine-grained spatial control remains a significant challenge, typically requiring auxiliary structural adapters or complex inference-time steering. While modern vision-language (VLM) text encoders possess the latent capacity to represent spatial coordinates, generative models often fail to leverage this information. We demonstrate that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) distribution is a critical determinant of spatial grounding in flow-matching models. Using a novel synthetic benchmark of geometric primitives, we isolate structural mechanics from textural complexity inherent in natural imagery and introduce a rigorous color-aware layout metric to quantify spatial fidelity. We show that training on a centered SNR distribution results in poor layout adherence (25.9%). Instead, we identify high-noise regimes as a ”critical learning phase” for spatial grounding. Shifting the training focus toward these stages increases layout adherence to 80.8%, effectively reaching the 83.6% reconstruction ceiling of the underlying variational autoencoder. We find that the necessity for this shift scales with resolution and aspect ratio variance, and that the conservative noise schedules employed by models like FLUX.1 are insufficient for precise layout adherence. Finally, we show that these findings translate to real-world imagery via the COCO dataset (1.0 vs. 57.3 Average Precision), where state-of-the-art layout adherence is unlocked through distribution tuning alone, without architectural modifications.

 1 Introduction
The rapid evolution of diffusion [ Ho et al.(2020)Ho, Jain, and Abbeel , Song et al.(2021)Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon, and Poole ] and flow-matching [ Lipman et al.(2023)Lipman, Chen, Ben-Hamu, Nickel, and Le , Liu et al.(2023)Liu, Gong, and qiang liu ] models is outpacing the adoption of these models for specialized use cases. In particular, while new state-of-the-art image synthesis models [ Wu et al.(2025a)Wu, Li, Zhou, Lin, Gao, Yan, ming Yin, Bai, Xu, Chen, Chen, Tang, Zhang, Wang, Yang, Yu, Cheng, Liu, Li, Zhang, Meng, Wei, Ni, Chen, Cao, Peng, Qu, Wu, Wang, Yu, Wen, Feng, Xu, Wang, Zhang, Zhu, Wu, Cai, and Liu , Team(2025) , Labs(2025) , Cao et al.(2025)Cao, Chen, Chen, Cheng, Cui, Deng, Dong, Gong, Gu, Gu, et al. ] are frequently released, achieving fine-grained spatial control over their outputs typically requires adopting one of two paradigms. The first involves training-based methods, which utilize auxiliary structural adapters such as ControlNet [ Zhang et al.(2023)Zhang, Rao, and Agrawala ] , GLIGEN [ Li et al.(2023)Li, Liu, Wu, Mu, Yang, Gao, Li, and Lee ] , or CreatiLayout [ Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Hong, Wang, Shao, Wu, Wu, and Jiang ] . The second relies on training-free methods, which steer the generation process of frozen backbones during inference to follow a target layout. However, adapters are often tightly coupled to specific model architectures, making them costly to retrain for each new release, while training-free methods often struggle with complex layouts or specific architectural constraints. This creates a critical need to understand why foundation models struggle to generate images that follow layouts from text alone.
Standard pre-trained models typically fail to interpret 2D coordinates embedded in text prompts, often treating them as non-spatial semantic tokens rather than geometric constraints. In contrast, recent advancements in vision-language models (VLMs), such as the Qwen-VL series [ Bai et al.(2025b)Bai, Chen, Liu, Wang, Ge, Song, Dang, Wang, Wang, Tang, Zhong, Zhu, Yang, Li, Wan, Wang, Ding, Fu, Xu, Ye, Zhang, Xie, Cheng, Zhang, Yang, Xu, and Lin , Bai et al.(2025a)Bai, Cai, Chen, Chen, Chen, Cheng, Deng, Ding, Gao, Ge, Ge, Guo, Huang, Huang, Huang, Hui, Jiang, Li, Li, Li, Li, Lin, Lin, Liu, Liu, Liu, Liu, Liu, Liu, Lu, Luo, Lv, Men, Meng, Ren, Ren, Song, Sun, Tang, Tu, Wan, Wang, Wang, Wang, Wang, Xie, Xu, Xu, Xu, Yang, Yang, Yang, Yang, Yu, Zhang, Zhang, Zhang, Zheng, Zhong, Zhou, Zhou, Zhou, Zhu, and Zhu ] , demonstrate that multimodal embeddings can successfully encode spatial relationships and discrete coordinates for tasks like object detection and grounding. This suggests that their multimodal embedding space already possesses the latent capacity to represent spatial information. As state-of-the-art text-to-image models increasingly adopt VLM-based text encoders, it remains an open question why the generative process fails to leverage this innate grounding capability. Therefore, we investigate whether, and under what conditions, text-to-image models can learn to follow explicit 2D layout specifications encoded solely in text prompts.
To this end, we examine the grounding capabilities of flow-matching models by fine-tuning them to interpret 2D coordinates directly from text prompts without introducing structural modifications. To isolate the mechanisms of spatial grounding, we first construct a controlled synthetic dataset of geometric primitives, enabling exact, pixel-level assessment of layout fidelity (Fig.  1 ). Our analysis reveals that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) distribution is the critical determinant of spatial grounding. We find that training on a balanced SNR distribution actually hinders the model from accurately learning coordinate-based constraints. Instead, biasing the training toward high-noise regimes, where global structure is established, allows the model to internalize the relationship between text tokens and spatial coordinates. Notably, we find that this distribution shift is necessary even at lower resolutions ( 256^{2} ), and that the conservative shifts employed by recent models like FLUX.1  [ Labs et al.(2025)Labs, Batifol, Blattmann, Boesel, Consul, Diagne, Dockhorn, English, English, Esser, Kulal, Lacey, Levi, Li, Lorenz, Müller, Podell, Rombach, Saini, Sauer, and Smith ] are insufficient for precise layout adherence. Finally, we demonstrate that these principles successfully translate to real-world imagery via the COCO dataset  [ Lin et al.(2014)Lin, Maire, Belongie, Hays, Perona, Ramanan, Dollár, and Zitnick ] (Fig.  1 ), showing that state-of-the-art layout adherence can be unlocked in standard flow-matching models through distribution tuning alone, without the need for specialized architectural extensions.
Taken together, our results demonstrate that high-fidelity spatial control is a latent capability in state-of-the-art flow-matching models that can be unlocked by strategically shifting the SNR distribution during training, without requiring any architectural modifications. We show that the high-noise regime serves as a ”critical learning phase” for spatial grounding, forcing the model to internalize geometric constraints when global structure is not yet observable. Our main contributions are as follows:
 • 
 Identification of SNR as a Key Facilitator: We provide a systematic study showing that biasing the SNR distribution toward high-noise regimes is essential for teaching models to interpret 2D coordinates embedded in text.
 • 
 A Synthetic Benchmark for Spatial Precision: We introduce a benchmark consisting of geometric primitives that enables the exact, pixel-level quantification of layout adherence, isolating grounding mechanics from the textural complexities of real-world images.
 • 
 Transfer to Real-World Imagery: We provide empirical evidence that these insights translate to complex real-world imagery via the COCO dataset, achieving state-of-the-art layout adherence in standard flow-matching models through distribution tuning alone, bypassing the need for specialized structural adapters.

 Training Dynamics and SNR Schedules. 
The performance of flow-matching and diffusion models heavily depends on the timestep sampling distribution p(t) , which determines the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) levels encountered during training [ Kingma and Gao(2023) , Esser et al.(2024)Esser, Kulal, Blattmann, Entezari, Müller, Saini, Levi, Lorenz, Sauer, Boesel, Podell, Dockhorn, English, and Rombach , Black Forest Labs(2025) ] . While logit-normal distributions are typically tuned to improve visual fidelity based on resolution [ Hoogeboom et al.(2023)Hoogeboom, Heek, and Salimans , Labs et al.(2025)Labs, Batifol, Blattmann, Boesel, Consul, Diagne, Dockhorn, English, English, Esser, Kulal, Lacey, Levi, Li, Lorenz, Müller, Podell, Rombach, Saini, Sauer, and Smith , Esser et al.(2024)Esser, Kulal, Blattmann, Entezari, Müller, Saini, Levi, Lorenz, Sauer, Boesel, Podell, Dockhorn, English, and Rombach ] or dimensionality [ Zheng et al.(2026)Zheng, Ma, Tong, and Xie ] , their impact on spatial grounding is only beginning to be examined. Recent work by Wu et al   [ Wu et al.(2025b)Wu, Chen, Liang, Wang, Li, Zhang, Wang, and Yuan ] and Zhang et al   [ Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Hong, Wang, Shao, Wu, Wu, and Jiang ] provides evidence that noise schedules influence layout adherence. However, their findings are coupled with specialized regional or siamese architectures. In contrast, our work demonstrates that the SNR distribution alone serves as the primary lever for internalizing spatial-semantic mappings in standard, structurally unmodified flow-matching models. We show that the acquisition of spatial grounding from text prompts alone is highly sensitive to p(t) , revealing that high-noise regimes represent the essential window for anchoring textual coordinates to global structural composition.
 Layout-to-Image (L2I) Generation. Layout-to-image approaches address the limitations of native spatial control by augmenting pre-trained synthesis models with explicit geometric conditioning (e.g., bounding boxes or masks). This is increasingly viewed as a multi-stage pipeline, beginning with upstream planning where Large Language Models (LLMs) or autoregressive VLMs translate linguistic descriptions into structural layouts [ Wang et al.(2025)Wang, Cao, Ding, Xie, Gu, and Chen , He et al.(2025)He, Cheng, Ma, Jia, Liu, Ma, Wu, Wu, Leng, and Yin , Feng et al.(2024b)Feng, Gong, Chen, Shen, Liu, and Zhou ] . Other approaches bridge the gap between text and pixels by synthesizing intermediate representations, such as depth maps, to guide the final generative process [ Zhou et al.(2025b)Zhou, Xie, Yang, and Yang , Zhou et al.(2025a)Zhou, Xie, Yang, and Yang , Eldesokey and Wonka(2025) ] . Once a spatial structure is established, the literature diverges into training-based and training-free strategies. Training-based L2I methods adapt model architectures to interpret spatial signals via fine-tuning or dedicated adapters [ Li et al.(2023)Li, Liu, Wu, Mu, Yang, Gao, Li, and Lee , Zheng et al.(2023)Zheng, Zhou, Li, Qi, Shan, and Li , Yang et al.(2023)Yang, Luo, Chen, Wang, Liang, and Lin ] . These works have evolved to handle multi-instance scenarios by using spatial attention disentanglement [ Zhou et al.(2024)Zhou, Li, Ma, Zhang, and Yang , Wang et al.(2024)Wang, Darrell, Rambhatla, Girdhar, and Misra ] or hierarchical composition [ Cheng et al.(2024)Cheng, Ma, Wu, Liu, Ma, Wu, Leng, and Yin ] to minimize attribute leakage. Beyond image quality, these models often serve as data engines to synthesize paired training data for perception tasks [ Feng et al.(2024a)Feng, Zhong, Jie, Xie, and Ma , Zhu et al.(2024)Zhu, Li, Liu, Yuan, Huang, Shan, and Ma ] . Recently, MMDiT architectures have been enhanced with siamese attention to isolate layout signals [ Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Hong, Wang, Shao, Wu, Wu, and Jiang ] and hybrid control frameworks to achieve data-efficient grounding [ Wu et al.(2025b)Wu, Chen, Liang, Wang, Li, Zhang, Wang, and Yuan ] . To avoid the high cost of retraining, training-free L2I methods intervene in the sampling process of frozen backbones. These strategies primarily manipulate internal cross-attention maps to ensure semantic isolation of subjects [ Xue et al.(2023)Xue, Huang, Sun, Song, and Zhang , Dahary et al.(2025)Dahary, Patashnik, Aberman, and Cohen-Or , Zhan and Liu(2025) ] or employ optimization-based guidance to steer the denoising trajectory toward specified coordinates [ Chen et al.(2024)Chen, Laina, and Vedaldi , Phung et al.(2024)Phung, Ge, and Huang , Xiao et al.(2024)Xiao, Lv, Li, Wang, and Huang , Liu et al.(2025)Liu, Huang, and Xu , Li et al.(2025)Li, Hu, Liu, and Wang ] . Beyond attention manipulation, compositional strategies decentralize the process by merging localized per-object noise estimates or synchronizing global and instance-specific denoising branches [ Shirakawa and Uchida(2024) , Lee et al.(2024)Lee, Yoon, and Sung ] . Finally, probabilistic approaches like Psi-Sampler [ Yoon et al.(2026)Yoon, Min, Yeo, and Sung ] reinterpret layout adherence as an inference-time reward alignment problem, using Sequential Monte Carlo sampling to filter trajectories.
While existing L2I methods typically rely on external modules to bypass spatial reasoning limitations, this study investigates a more fundamental question: can flow-matching models acquire spatial grounding from text prompts alone, without any structural modifications?
 3 Method
 3.1 Synthetic Benchmark for Text-Layout Image Generation
To facilitate training and provide a controlled environment for evaluation, we construct a synthetic dataset comprising 100,000 training pairs. For rigorous quantitative evaluation, we hold out a curated benchmark set of 1,000 text-image pairs. Images are rendered at resolution W\times H and contain n\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,10\} rectangles. In our standard benchmark, we set W=H=256 . Notably, our generation pipeline is agnostic to resolution and aspect ratio, enabling us to evaluate scalability across higher resolutions (e.g., 1024^{2} ) and varying aspect ratios, as detailed in Section  4.2 . By utilizing simple geometric primitives, this approach mitigates the textural complexity and photometric noise present in real-world images. This simplification enables us to disentangle spatial control from semantic rendering, allowing for a systematic and quantitative evaluation of a model’s geometric layout compliance.
 Layout Synthesis. We adopt rectangles as spatial primitives to mirror standard bounding-box formats and enable precise evaluation. To facilitate a systematic study of spatial grounding, we sample geometric properties from an extensive distribution that spans diverse scales and aspect ratios. Specifically, we define aspect ratio r and area A through log-uniform sampling: \ln(r)\sim\mathcal{U}(\ln\frac{1}{20},\ln 20) and \ln(A)\sim\mathcal{U}(\ln A_{min},\ln A_{max}) , where A_{min}=0.05W\times 0.05H and A_{max}=0.9W\times 0.9H . Dimensions are then derived as w=\sqrt{A\cdot r} and h=\sqrt{A/r} . This diverse geometric sampling ensures the benchmark provides a robust evaluation of spatial control, testing the model’s ability to generalize across a wide range of object shapes.
 Scene Composition. We sample centers (c_{x},c_{y}) uniformly and clip the resulting rectangles to the image frame [0,W]\times[0,H] . As rectangles are rendered sequentially, we remove fully obscured rectangles from the metadata and prompt to ensure semantic consistency. This guarantees that all described objects are at least partially visible, preventing a mismatch between the textual description and visual ground truth.
 Semantic Mapping. Each rectangle is assigned a unique color from a fixed palette of ten distinct values. To ensure maximum discriminability, which is important for our layout adherence metric introduced in Section  3.2 , these colors are selected to be maximally distant from one another in the CIELAB color space. Each color is mapped to its corresponding linguistic label using the XKCD color naming convention [ Munroe(2010) ] (e.g., fluro green).
 Layout Encoding. Following Bai et al   [ Bai et al.(2025a)Bai, Cai, Chen, Chen, Chen, Cheng, Deng, Ding, Gao, Ge, Ge, Guo, Huang, Huang, Huang, Hui, Jiang, Li, Li, Li, Li, Lin, Lin, Liu, Liu, Liu, Liu, Liu, Liu, Lu, Luo, Lv, Men, Meng, Ren, Ren, Song, Sun, Tang, Tu, Wan, Wang, Wang, Wang, Wang, Xie, Xu, Xu, Xu, Yang, Yang, Yang, Yang, Yu, Zhang, Zhang, Zhang, Zheng, Zhong, Zhou, Zhou, Zhou, Zhu, and Zhu ] , we normalize coordinates to [0,1000] . A typical object is formatted as: [x_{min},y_{min},x_{max},y_{max},\text{color}] . Crucially, rectangles are listed in the prompt in their exact rendering order, establishing a spatial hierarchy where the textual sequence dictates occlusion priority. To succeed, the model must not only solve the challenging problem of spatial grounding but also master the compositional reasoning required to render overlapping geometries where objects are frequently partially obscured.  3.2 Layout Adherence Metric
To assess the spatial fidelity of generated images relative to specified text layouts, we introduce a Layout Adherence Metric based on color-coded segmentation masks. This metric quantifies the alignment between the ground-truth image I_{\text{gt}} and synthesized output I_{\text{gen}} by first transforming both into the perceptually uniform CIELAB color space. We then identify the set of unique foreground colors \mathcal{C} present in I_{\text{gt}} , where |\mathcal{C}|\leq 10 corresponds to the number of layout rectangles. For each color c\in\mathcal{C} , we derive binary masks for both the ground-truth and generated images. Specifically, binary masks M_{c}(I;\tau) are derived for both images by thresholding the perceptual color distance between each pixel \mathbf{p} and the target color c :
 M_{c}(I;\tau)=\left\{\mathbf{p}\in\Omega\mid\|I(\mathbf{p})-c\|_{2}\leq\tau\right\}, 
 (1) 
where \Omega denotes the image spatial domain and \tau is a predefined color-matching threshold. This masking process accounts for minor color deviations in the generative process while isolating the spatial extent of each layout element. The adherence for a specific color c is then calculated using the Intersection over Union ( \mathrm{IoU} ) between the respective masks:
 \mathrm{IoU}_{c}(I_{\mathrm{gt}},I_{\mathrm{gen}};\tau)=\frac{|M_{c}(I_{\mathrm{gt}};\tau)\cap M_{c}(I_{\mathrm{gen}};\tau)|}{|M_{c}(I_{\mathrm{gt}};\tau)\cup M_{c}(I_{\mathrm{gen}};\tau)|}. 
 (2) 
Finally, we compute the mean IoU ( \mathrm{mIoU}_{\tau} ) across all unique colors in the image:
 \mathrm{mIoU}_{\tau}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{IoU}_{c}(I_{\mathrm{gt}},I_{\mathrm{gen}};\tau). 
 (3) 
As illustrated in Fig.  3 , this metric effectively penalizes both spatial misalignments and the omission of required layout elements. To ensure the metric is not overly sensitive to the choice of the threshold \tau (Fig.  3 ), we report the mean performance over a discrete set of thresholds \mathcal{T}=\{2,3,\dots,25\} , defined as:
 \text{mIoU}_{2:25}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{mIoU}_{\tau}. 
 (4) 
This allows us to observe the trade-off between strict color matching and spatial alignment. Furthermore, we establish an empirical upper bound for this metric by calculating the adherence of the VAE-reconstructed ground truth ( I_{\text{vae}}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}(I_{\text{gt}})) ). This serves as a calibration point to isolate generative errors from the autoencoder’s reconstruction limitations.

 3.3 Implementation Details
 Fine-Tuning. While our primary experiments utilize FLUX.2-4B-base, we also scale our approach to larger models including FLUX.2-9B-base [ Labs(2025) ] , FLUX.1-dev (12B) [ Labs et al.(2025)Labs, Batifol, Blattmann, Boesel, Consul, Diagne, Dockhorn, English, English, Esser, Kulal, Lacey, Levi, Li, Lorenz, Müller, Podell, Rombach, Saini, Sauer, and Smith , Labs(2024) ] , and Qwen-Image (20B) [ Wu et al.(2025a)Wu, Li, Zhou, Lin, Gao, Yan, ming Yin, Bai, Xu, Chen, Chen, Tang, Zhang, Wang, Yang, Yu, Cheng, Liu, Li, Zhang, Meng, Wei, Ni, Chen, Cao, Peng, Qu, Wu, Wang, Yu, Wen, Feng, Xu, Wang, Zhang, Zhu, Wu, Cai, and Liu ] . To enable fine-tuning on a single 80GB A100 GPU, we utilize the Adafactor optimizer [ Shazeer and Stern(2018) ] (with constant scale, disabling relative step and parameter scaling) alongside a fused backward pass and gradient checkpointing. This configuration, combined with BFloat16 precision, reduces the peak memory footprint of the 20B Qwen-Image model from 160GB to 44GB at 256\times 256 resolution. We fine-tune for 100,000 steps using a unit batch size, as layer-wise fused updates are mathematically incompatible with gradient accumulation. This approach leverages recent findings that small-batch training maintains competitive convergence and generalization for large generative models [ Marek et al.(2026)Marek, Lotfi, Somasundaram, Wilson, and Goldblum ] . To ensure training stability and preserve generation fidelity, we employ an EMA model (0.9995 decay, \gamma=10 Karras schedule [ Karras et al.(2024)Karras, Aittala, Lehtinen, Hellsten, Aila, and Laine ] ) and a cosine learning rate schedule ( 1\times 10^{-5} to 5\times 10^{-6} ) following a 5,000-step warmup. Finally, 10% of prompts are nullified for classifier-free guidance (CFG).
 Inference. We generate images using 25 steps with a CFG scale of 3.0. We utilize a shifted logit-normal timestep scheduler ( \mu=1.0 ), which provided the best empirical trade-off between speed and fidelity.
 4 Experimental Results
Building on our setup, we systematically evaluate how SNR distribution, image resolution and aspect ratio, object properties, and model architecture influence the layout adherence of flow-matching models.
 4.1 Signal-to-Noise Ratio Distribution
The SNR distribution during fine-tuning determines which noise levels the model encounters most frequently, directly influencing what it learns to denoise. This makes the timestep sampling distribution t\sim p(t) a hyperparameter with significant impact on final model performance  [ Kingma and Gao(2023) , Black Forest Labs(2025) ] . In the context of rectified flow models [ Liu et al.(2023)Liu, Gong, and qiang liu ] , the noised latent x_{t} at timestep t\in[0,1] is defined by the interpolation:
 x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+t\epsilon,\quad x_{0}\sim p_{\text{data}},\;\;\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\;\;t\sim p(t). 
 (5) 
We therefore investigate the effect of p(t) on layout adherence and hypothesize that the model primarily learns the mapping between text coordinates and image locations during the high-noise stages, where it establishes global structure. Following recent advancements in flow-matching scaling  [ Esser et al.(2024)Esser, Kulal, Blattmann, Entezari, Müller, Saini, Levi, Lorenz, Sauer, Boesel, Podell, Dockhorn, English, and Rombach , Hoogeboom et al.(2023)Hoogeboom, Heek, and Salimans , Labs et al.(2025)Labs, Batifol, Blattmann, Boesel, Consul, Diagne, Dockhorn, English, English, Esser, Kulal, Lacey, Levi, Li, Lorenz, Müller, Podell, Rombach, Saini, Sauer, and Smith , Black Forest Labs(2025) ] , we shift the logit-normal distribution:
 p_{ln}(t;\mu,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\exp\left(-\frac{(\mathrm{logit}(t)-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right), 
 (6) 
where \mathrm{logit}(t)=\log\frac{t}{1-t} . Following Batifol et al   [ Labs et al.(2025)Labs, Batifol, Blattmann, Boesel, Consul, Diagne, Dockhorn, English, English, Esser, Kulal, Lacey, Levi, Li, Lorenz, Müller, Podell, Rombach, Saini, Sauer, and Smith , Black Forest Labs(2025) ] , we fix \sigma=1 to isolate the effect of the mean SNR ( \mu ) shift on layout adherence. By adjusting \mu , we can systematically shift the fine-tuning focus toward the high-noise regime ( \mu>0 ), as illustrated in Fig.  4 . For our experiment, we investigate \mu\in\left\{0.0,0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5,3.0\right\} .

 Results. We find that the location parameter \mu strongly influences spatial grounding (Tab.  2 ). Fig.  6 shows that lower values ( \mu\leq 0.5 ) result in perceivable structural misalignments. While increasing \mu to 3.0 yields visually stable layouts, we observe a quantitative performance degradation in \mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} (Tab.  2 , Fig.  5 ). Our analysis (see Appendix  A ) reveals that this drop stems not from structural failure, but from a loss of pixel-level color precision. At this extreme, the model’s color assignments frequently fall just outside color-matching threshold \tau , despite correct spatial placement. This observation highlights a fundamental trade-off in the \mu -shifting schedule: while a high \mu prioritizes global structural grounding, it may limit the model’s capacity to resolve high-frequency components. In the context of natural images, this would manifest as a loss of fine-grained textural details. Importantly, moderate shifts ( \mu\in[1.0,2.5] ) nearly reach the VAE reconstruction upper bound of 83.6. These results suggest that vanilla flow-matching architectures possess the latent capacity to internalize coordinate-based constraints directly from text prompts. Within our controlled synthetic environment, the model achieves near-optimal layout adherence through distribution shifting alone, demonstrating the architectural feasibility of precise spatial grounding without auxiliary modules or inference-time interventions.
 4.2 Resolution & Aspect Ratio
To evaluate if this architectural feasibility generalizes beyond fixed square layouts, we extend our approach along two axes: resolution and aspect ratio, which we investigate progressively ( 256^{2}\rightarrow 512^{2}\rightarrow 1024^{2} and r_{\mathrm{max}}:1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow 3 ). For resolution experiments, we re-render our image data at 512^{2} and 1024^{2} while keeping all geometric and textual properties fixed, isolating the effect of resolution from layout complexity. For aspect ratio experiments, we derive resolution buckets (w,h) from a fixed area A=256^{2} such that w\cdot h\approx A , where w and h are multiples of 16. We include all pairs satisfying \frac{\max(w,h)}{\min(w,h)}\leq r_{\mathrm{max}} , ensuring portrait-landscape symmetry. Following prior work [ Hoogeboom et al.(2023)Hoogeboom, Heek, and Salimans , Esser et al.(2024)Esser, Kulal, Blattmann, Entezari, Müller, Saini, Levi, Lorenz, Sauer, Boesel, Podell, Dockhorn, English, and Rombach , Labs et al.(2025)Labs, Batifol, Blattmann, Boesel, Consul, Diagne, Dockhorn, English, English, Esser, Kulal, Lacey, Levi, Li, Lorenz, Müller, Podell, Rombach, Saini, Sauer, and Smith ] suggesting that higher resolutions require increased timestep shifting for optimal image quality, we use the optimal \mu from the previous configuration as a lower bound for subsequent searches. For instance, having found \mu=1 optimal for 256^{2} , we limit our search to \mu\in[1,3] for the 512^{2} scale. By applying this strategy, we can substantially reduce the search space compared to a full \mu -sweep for each setting. Crucially, as bounding box coordinates remain normalized to [0,1000] , the textual prompts are identical across all configurations. This ensures that layout, object count, and color assignments remain constant, allowing for a controlled comparison of spatial grounding performance across varying scales and geometries.
 Resolution. Table  2 summarizes the impact of the logit-normal shift \mu on layout adherence as resolution increases. We observe a clear trend: the optimal \mu value is positively correlated with the image resolution. For the 256^{2} base resolution, a moderate shift of \mu=1.0 yields the highest performance ( 80.8~\mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} ), nearly saturating the VAE reconstruction bound of 83.6 . However, this configuration fails to generalize to higher resolutions. At 512^{2} , \mu=1.0 results in a significant drop in layout adherence ( 48.1~\mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} ). Instead, higher resolutions require a more aggressive shift toward earlier timesteps (higher noise levels) to maintain spatial control. Specifically, peak performance shifts to \mu=2.0 for 512^{2} and \mu=2.5 for 1024^{2} . Notably, with the appropriately tuned \mu , our model maintains high layout adherence across all scales, reaching 82.7~\mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} at 1024^{2} , approaching the VAE’s empirical ceiling. This suggests that as the resolution increases, a stronger prior on the early denoising phase is essential for the acquisition of spatial grounding from text prompts alone.
 Aspect Ratio. We further evaluate the impact of \mu across varying aspect ratios ( r_{\mathrm{max}} ), as shown in Tab.  2 . Consistent with the resolution experiments, we find that the base configuration ( \mu=1.0 ) is insufficient for handling non-square geometries, with performance dropping from 80.8 to 67.8~\mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} when moving from r_{\mathrm{max}}=1 to r_{\mathrm{max}}=2 . By increasing \mu to the 2.0\text{--}2.5 range, the model recovers strong layout adherence even for extreme aspect ratios ( r_{\mathrm{max}}=3 ). While there is a slight decrease in absolute \mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} as the aspect ratio becomes more extreme (likely due to the increased difficulty of placing objects in narrow or highly elongated canvases), the shift in \mu effectively mitigates the drop in spatial control seen with centered timestep distributions. These results demonstrate that the relationship between the timestep distribution p(t) and spatial grounding is not static, but rather highly sensitive to the geometric complexity of the output space. Collectively, these findings indicate that spatial grounding is heavily dependent on the noise levels encountered during training, necessitating a higher noise prior as image resolution and aspect ratios increase.
We further find that layout adherence is strongly dependent on object size (tiny vs. large) but largely density-independent, remaining stable for n\in[3,10] objects per image. For tiny objects ( <16^{2} px), median \mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} is approximately 0.41, versus 0.85-0.95 for large ( 64-128^{2} px) and huge ( >128^{2} px) objects. However, a similar degradation appears in the VAE reconstruction ceiling itself, suggesting this size-dependence is a property of the underlying latent space rather than a model-specific limitation. Full results are in Appendix  B .

This effect remains consistent regardless of the specific transformer backbone or text-conditioning strategy. While our primary experiments use the FLUX.2-4B-base architecture, the ability to recover layout adherence in 9B, 12B, and 20B models by tuning the SNR shift suggests a fundamental property of how flow-matching models interpret spatial instructions across scales.
 Impact of Text Conditioning. 
We observe a clear correlation between the choice of text encoder and the model’s sensitivity to the shifting parameter \mu . At \mu=1.0 , models using Vision-Language Model (VLM) encoders, such as Qwen-Image and FLUX.2, demonstrate high layout adherence ( \geq 80.8~\mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} ). This suggests that VLM-based conditioning provides a more integrated spatial-semantic representation that is inherently more ”accessible” to the denoising process than the separately trained embeddings provided by T5 and CLIP encoders.

While all four models achieve high \mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} scores, their ”generative gaps” ( \Delta ) reveal a fundamental trade-off between VAE reconstruction fidelity and latent-space learnability. The FLUX.2 VAE is specifically optimized for representation learnability  [ Black Forest Labs(2025) ] , which is reflected in the remarkably small gaps of 2.8 (FLUX.2-4B-base) and 2.1 (FLUX.2-9B-base), indicating that the FLUX.2 models can almost fully exploit the empirical VAE reconstruction limit. However, this optimization for learnability entails a trade-off in reconstruction precision (e.g., color fidelity), leading to a lower VAE upper bound compared to Qwen-Image or FLUX.1-dev. Conversely, while the latter models possess higher reconstruction ceilings, their latent spaces appear significantly harder to navigate for precise spatial grounding, as evidenced by their larger generative gaps. This suggests that recent advancements in improving VAE learnability  [ Zheng et al.(2026)Zheng, Ma, Tong, and Xie , Black Forest Labs(2025) ] not only benefit image quality but also play a critical role in a model’s ability to adhere to complex layout instructions from text prompts.

 Implementation Details. We select FLUX.2-9B-base for our real-world experiments, as it achieved the strongest layout adherence among the architectures evaluated in Tab.  3 ( 81.5 \mathrm{mIoU}_{2:25} at \mu=1.0 ). To preserve the base model’s generative priors amidst complex real-world textures, we use a conservative learning rate of 1\times 10^{-6} , which we found yields more stable convergence than the higher rates used for geometric primitives. For real-world images, we adopt the standard FLUX.2 sampling configuration (50 steps, 4.0 guidance). While more efficient settings sufficed in our synthetic study, these default parameters are better suited to resolving complex real-world textures. Bounding boxes are normalized to [0,1000] , sequenced by appearance in the COCO annotations, and formatted as: [x_{min},y_{min},x_{max},y_{max},\text{category name}] .

 Results. Table  4 demonstrates that spatial grounding via SNR distribution shifting ( \mu=3.0 ) robustly transfers from our controlled synthetic environment to complex real-world imagery. Despite using only text prompts and a lightweight 5-epoch fine-tuning on MS-COCO, our model achieves state-of-the-art layout adherence compared to specialized layout-to-image models (Table  5 ). Specifically, our Average Recall ( \mathrm{AR} ) of 65.2 significantly outperforms established models like GLIGEN ( 37.2 ), InstanceDiff ( 43.7 ), or Hybrid Layout ( 51.8 ), indicating that our model is remarkably effective at populating the canvas with all requested objects. Furthermore, our \mathrm{AP}^{50} of 81.8 exceeds the YOLO-v11-x detector’s empirical ceiling on ground-truth COCO images ( 77.2 ). Since ground-truth represents a theoretical upper bound, we hypothesize this reflects known limitations in the COCO dataset itself (e.g., bounding box noise, motion blur, and greater occlusion and background clutter than in our generated images) that lower the detector’s measured ceiling on real-world images. Overall, this shows that our model’s spatial precision is effectively saturating the empirical ceiling. While FID ( 32.38 ) and CLIP scores ( 28.01 ) trail CreatiLayout  [ Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Hong, Wang, Shao, Wu, Wu, and Jiang ] , which utilizes a more complex multi-stream architecture, our model achieves the highest Inception Score ( 30.32 ) among all compared methods, including CreatiLayout ( 30.26 ) and the ground-truth COCO images themselves ( 29.06 ), indicating that the rich generative priors of the FLUX.2-9B-base model are preserved during fine-tuning. To assess whether extended training yields further gains, we fine-tune FLUX.2-9B-base at \mu=3.0 for 10 epochs while evaluating after each epoch, which further improves layout adherence across all metrics ( \mathrm{AP} : 61.8 , \mathrm{AP}^{50} : 84.7 , \mathrm{AR} : 68.8 ; Tab.  5 ). The complete epoch-ablation is reported in Appendix  C . Qualitative results (Fig.  7 ) of the FLUX.2-9B-base model after 10 epochs of fine-tuning show faithful spatial generation across diverse scenes, challenging compositions, and densities (rows 1–3). Observed failure modes (row 4) include compositional artifacts (e.g., merged or duplicated anatomical features) and high-frequency imprecision (e.g., clock face details). Preliminary experiments applying layout-conditioned denoising only during the first 40–50% of steps improved fine-grained details with only a marginal \mathrm{AP} drop ( 1.5 – 5.1 points). While some compositional artifacts persist, also occasionally seen in the pre-trained FLUX.2-9B-base model prior to any fine-tuning (Appendix  D ), unifying these dynamics into a single, potentially scaled, foundation model remains future work. Ultimately, these findings validate the insights from our synthetic benchmark: the lack of native spatial control in foundation flow-matching models is not an architectural limitation. By shifting the SNR distribution to prioritize global structural dependencies, a standard flow-matching transformer can parse spatial coordinates directly from the text stream and compete with specialized architectures.
 6 Conclusion
In this work, we investigated the latent spatial grounding capabilities of flow-matching models, addressing the discrepancy between the rich spatial embeddings of modern vision-language encoders and the poor native layout adherence of the generative models that leverage them. Unlike the prevailing paradigm of introducing auxiliary structural adapters or complex inference-time optimizations, we show that precise spatial control can be ”unlocked” by shifting the timestep sampling distribution. Our systematic evaluation using a novel synthetic benchmark of geometric primitives allowed us to isolate the mechanics of spatial grounding from textural complexities. We identified the high-noise regime as a ”critical learning phase”, where the model is forced to internalize geometric constraints to establish global structure. By biasing the fine-tuning process toward these regimes, we enabled models to interpret 2D coordinates directly from text prompts with strong spatial accuracy. Furthermore, we demonstrated that these insights generalize to real-world imagery via the COCO dataset, achieving state-of-the-art layout adherence without any architectural modifications. The implications of our findings are twofold: first, they suggest that the perceived lack of spatial control in current models is not a limitation of architectural capacity. Second, our approach offers a highly efficient, architecture-agnostic path for enhancing spatial controllability, providing a recipe for integrating native layout adherence into future foundation models.
 Limitations and future work. While our approach provides a lightweight and architecture-agnostic path to spatial grounding, it reveals a trade-off: a strong bias toward high-noise regimes during fine-tuning, while essential for layout adherence, can compromise high-frequency textural synthesis. This suggests that the global structural alignment facilitated by high-noise training may compete with local generative refinement required for high-fidelity synthesis. Future research should investigate multi-stage or scheduled sampling distributions that transition from a high-noise bias (to lock in grounding) to a more uniform distribution (to recover textural quality). Furthermore, exploring Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)  [ Liu et al.(2026c)Liu, Liu, Liang, Yuan, Liu, Zheng, Wu, Wang, Xia, Wang, et al. ] or other reinforcement learning techniques  [ Liu et al.(2026b)Liu, Liu, Liang, Li, Liu, Wang, Wan, Zhang, and Ouyang ] presents a promising avenue for aligning model outputs with both layout constraints and human-perceived image quality.
