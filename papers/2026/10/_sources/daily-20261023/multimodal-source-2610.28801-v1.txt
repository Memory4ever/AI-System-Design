Abstract
Most unified multimodal models treat images and text asymmetrically: a pretrained language model generates text, an attached diffusion head generates images, and outputs that contain both are produced in a fixed order, so the first output cannot adapt to the second. We ask whether both modalities can instead be generated by one process that is trained on them jointly from scratch. Pretrained encoder spaces hold both modalities in one continuous form and carry knowledge learned at scale, and recent work finds them effective as generative targets for vision and language. We therefore model the joint distribution directly in these spaces. We introduce JUICE (Joint Unified multimodal modeling In Continuous Encoder spaces), a single flow matching Transformer over frozen image and text representations, in which each modality has its own noise level. Which modalities are clean and which are generated then decides the task: image generation, captioning, visual question answering, or joint generation. Controlled pretraining runs test each design choice. At equal guidance, generating text as continuous states leaves image generation intact (48.41 GenEval, against 47.86 for a model trained only for text-to-image generation), while captions lose some exact wording. Training on joint and conditional denoising together beats either alone, and semantic encoder spaces beat VAE latents and raw pixels as targets. At 2.1B parameters, with no language model initialization and at 224{\times}224 , JUICE reaches up to 0.88 on GenEval and 73.1 on VQAv2. By sampling both modalities from the joint distribution (coupled sampling), it can also draw a route on a map while writing out the matching moves. On mazes with several valid solutions, joint success rises from 54.6% with independent sampling to 93.7%, and no modality has to go first. On visual spatial planning, JUICE solves 99.8% of maps at its training sizes.

1  Introduction
Unified multimodal models aim to understand and generate both images and text within a single model. Most current systems, however, treat the two modalities asymmetrically. In the dominant design, a pretrained autoregressive language model generates text and drives a diffusion or flow matching head that generates images  [ 1 , 2 ] ; other models quantize images and generate both modalities as discrete tokens  [ 3 , 4 ] . Either way, the two modalities are generated by different processes or through a quantizer, and the joint distribution is factorized in a fixed order: an output that contains both an image and text is produced one modality after the other, and the first cannot be revised in light of the second. Vision is also usually added to a model that was first pretrained on language, although controlled studies of multimodal pretraining find that late integration makes models lean on language priors and that training the modalities jointly from the start works better  [ 5 ] . This suggests a different design: a model that treats both modalities alike, generates them with one process, and learns them together from the start.
Such a design needs two things: a space in which one process can generate both modalities, and a source of knowledge for a backbone trained without a language model. Frozen pretrained encoders provide both. Their spaces are continuous for images and text alike and carry visual and linguistic knowledge learned at scale, and recent studies show that they are good generative targets for vision  [ 6 , 7 , 8 ] and for language  [ 9 ] , although they were never built for generation. In these spaces, one flow matching process generates both modalities with the same objective, the same features serve as targets and as context, and a model of the joint distribution also contains its conditionals. Give each modality its own noise level and the two levels span a square (Figure  2 ). Text-to-image generation, captioning, and question answering hold one modality clean and move along an edge. Joint generation cuts through the middle, where image and text take shape together. To build a working model on this idea, we had to settle four questions that existing unified models leave open: whether text should be generated as continuous states or kept as tokens, which denoising tasks to train on, which encoder spaces to read and write, and whether these answers still hold as the model grows.
We introduce JUICE ( J oint U nified multimodal modeling I n C ontinuous E ncoder spaces; Figure  1 ), a unified model that works directly in the continuous spaces of frozen pretrained image and text encoders. A single flow matching backbone, MM-RiT, trained from scratch, connects the image and text streams through bidirectional joint attention  [ 10 ] , and each stream has its own noise scale and schedule. We train MM-RiT on both the edges and the interior of the square, with conditional and joint denoising. At inference, a task is a path through the square: keeping some streams clean ( t=1 ) and generating the others gives text-to-image generation, captioning, visual question answering, or coupled joint generation (Section  3 ).

(b) Conditional tasks (text-to-image, visual question answering) and joint reasoning (maze solving) use the same interface by assigning clean context ( t{=}1 ) and generation targets ( t{<}1 ).
Our pretraining studies (Section  4 ) change one design choice at a time and keep the rest of the recipe fixed.
 (i) Generative frameworks. At a shared guidance scale, JUICE scores 48.41 GenEval and a model trained only for text-to-image generation scores 47.86, while all three frameworks that write text as tokens fall below that model. Captions generated as continuous states lose some exact wording but match the images as well as autoregressive captions do.
 (ii) Training task mixtures. Training should cover the regions that sampling visits: joint and conditional denoising together outperform either one alone, beyond the effect of supervising more targets.
 (iii) Representation spaces. The VAE reconstructs better than the semantic encoders, yet generation and captioning both work better in the semantic spaces, and Flan-T5 states beat larger text embedding models.
 (iv) Scaling. From 179M to 2.1B backbone parameters, SigLIP2  [ 11 ] , which is aligned with language, writes better captions at every size, while the early lead of WebSSL-DINO  [ 12 ] (DINO) in image generation disappears.
With these answers in hand, we train two 2.1B models that pair Flan-T5 with SigLIP2 or with DINO (Section  5 ). Working at 224{\times}224 and starting from no language model, each uses one set of weights for compositional image generation, generation that needs world knowledge, and visual question answering. The two reach 0.87 and 0.88 GenEval, and the SigLIP2 model reaches 85.22 on DPG-Bench, 0.45 on WISE  [ 13 ] , and 73.1 on VQAv2.
Because JUICE models the joint distribution, it can also reason jointly across modalities: with coupled sampling, the image and its text are generated together, each conditioning on the other’s evolving state. We adapt JUICE to two planning tasks, mazes with multiple solutions  [ 14 ] and visual spatial planning  [ 15 ] , in which it draws a route on the map and writes the matching moves. Sampled independently, the two outputs often disagree on mazes: the model draws one valid route and writes out a different one, and joint success is only 54.6%. Generating one modality first repairs this, but the result then depends on the order (95.2% with the image first, 85.3% with the text first), and nothing says in advance which order to use. Coupled sampling reaches 93.7% without making that choice. In the two cases we trace, whichever output settles later rewrites itself to match the other. On visual spatial planning, JUICE solves 99.8% of maps at the training sizes, and 94% and 69% at two larger sizes it never saw in training.
Our main contributions are:
 1. 
 Joint modeling in pretrained representation spaces. We propose JUICE, which models the joint distribution of images and text in the continuous spaces of frozen pretrained encoders with one flow matching Transformer trained from scratch. With a noise level for each modality, every task is a path through one square, with no fixed generation order.
 2. 
 Systematic design studies. Section  4 reports controlled pretraining runs on how text is generated, which tasks are trained, and which encoder spaces are used, plus a scaling study from 179M to 2.1B parameters. We summarize what they show as four takeaways for continuous joint modeling.
 3. 
 Scaling and joint reasoning. A single set of 2.1B weights handles compositional image generation, generation that needs world knowledge, and visual question answering (Section  5 ). On two planning tasks, coupled sampling gets the drawn route and the written moves to agree without a fixed generation order (Section  5.4 ).

Unified multimodal models.
Most unified models are hybrids that combine different representation spaces and generative processes (Table  1 ).
Chameleon  [ 16 ] models discrete text tokens and quantized image codes autoregressively.
Transfusion  [ 17 ] pairs autoregressive text with diffusion over continuous image latents, and Show-o  [ 20 ] pairs autoregressive text with diffusion over discrete image tokens.
MMaDA  [ 3 ] and Muddit  [ 4 ] use discrete masked diffusion for both modalities, while D-DiT  [ 18 ] combines discrete masked diffusion for text with continuous flow for images.
D-DiT and Muddit start from pretrained image generators, Show-o and MMaDA from pretrained language models, and Chameleon and Transfusion are trained from scratch.
In most of these models, the two modalities are generated by different processes, and outputs that contain both are produced in a fixed order. JUICE instead generates both with one flow matching process in frozen representation spaces and is trained from scratch; Section  4.2 compares it with representative formulations under the same recipe.
Generation in pretrained representation spaces.
Representation autoencoders (RAEs)  [ 6 , 7 ] show that diffusion works well on frozen pretrained visual features, whose semantic structure makes them good targets.
Beyond Language Modeling (BLM)  [ 8 ] takes up this idea and trains a unified backbone from scratch with flow matching over frozen visual features, while language is still predicted autoregressively as discrete tokens.
Other unified models pick different visual targets. Show-o2  [ 1 ] still generates VAE latents, which it processes semantically. TUNA  [ 21 ] aligns unified features built from VAE latents, and Tuna-2  [ 2 ] removes the visual encoders altogether and generates pixels.
For text, ELF  [ 9 ] applies continuous flow matching directly in pretrained text representation spaces, though only to generate text on its own.
Each of these works generates only one modality in a continuous representation space: BLM still predicts text as tokens, and ELF generates no images. JUICE generates both modalities in pretrained spaces with one process, so the same representations serve as generation targets and as cross-modal context, and Sections  4.4 and  4.5 study which spaces suit this joint use and how the answer changes with scale.
Joint and conditional multimodal modeling.
Separate noise schedules for each modality  [ 19 , 22 ] provide a mathematical framework for a single model to capture the marginal, conditional, and joint distributions of images and text.
Existing implementations, however, need specialized components. UniDiffuser  [ 19 ] diffuses CLIP embeddings and needs a separate autoregressive GPT-2 to decode text; OneFlow  [ 23 ] models text with discrete edit flows.
OmniFlow  [ 24 ] uses rectified flow for generation from any modality to any other. Its MMDiT backbone starts from Stable Diffusion 3, and its text latent comes from a VAE built on Flan-T5 and TinyLlama.
JUICE keeps both modalities as continuous encoder states with one state per token, decodes text with a lightweight parallel decoder, and trains one backbone on them from scratch. In this setting, joint denoising improves conditional generation by more than its extra targets explain (Section  4.3 ), and coupled sampling lets the two streams settle on the same one of several valid solutions (Section  5.4 ).
Continuous diffusion for language.
Early work diffused learned word embeddings and rounded them back to tokens (Diffusion-LM  [ 25 ] , DiffuSeq  [ 26 ] ); later work improved the likelihood objectives (CDCD  [ 27 ] , Plaid  [ 28 ] ). Flow matching over categorical distributions, which places each token on the probability simplex, offers another continuous route to discrete data such as text  [ 29 , 30 ] .
A second line of work diffuses directly in the representation spaces of pretrained language models, either after compressing sequences into fixed latents (LD4LG  [ 31 ] ) or over contextual encoder states (TEncDM  [ 32 ] , ELF  [ 9 ] ).
JUICE follows the second line for its text stream and diffuses contextual encoder states. Unlike these text only models, it generates the states jointly with images, and Section  4.2 compares this choice with discrete text under one recipe.
3  Method
As shown in Figure  1 , JUICE operates directly in the continuous representation spaces of frozen pretrained encoders, using a single flow matching Transformer (MM-RiT) trained from scratch.
The same model supports generation, understanding, and joint tasks over images and text simply by varying which representations are provided as clean inputs and which are generated from noise.
Section  3.1 reviews flow matching, Section  3.2 presents the formulation, and Sections  3.3 to  3.5 describe the architecture, training procedure, and inference; Appendix  A.6 provides implementation details.
3.1  Preliminaries
Flow matching  [ 33 ] trains a network to predict a velocity field that, over time, transports a noise distribution to the data distribution.
Given a clean representation z and Gaussian noise \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}) , we use the linear interpolation
 z^{t}=(1-t)\,\epsilon+t\,z,\qquad t\in[0,1], 
 (1) 
where t{=}0 is pure noise, t{=}1 is clean data, and the target velocity is v^{*}=z-\epsilon .
Following JiT  [ 34 ] , MM-RiT predicts the clean state  \widehat{z}_{\theta} rather than velocity.
This places the network output directly in the representation space used by the decoders. At any noisy state z^{t} , the predicted clean data \widehat{z}_{\theta} defines the velocity field via
 \widehat{v}_{\theta}(z^{t};\,t)=\frac{\widehat{z}_{\theta}-z^{t}}{d(t)}, 
where d(t)=\max(1-t,0.05) floors the denominator near t{=}1 for numerical stability. For the linear path, velocity matching is therefore a denoising loss on \widehat{z}_{\theta} weighted by 1/d(t)^{2} .
3.2  Formulation
JUICE models the joint distribution of an image representation z_{\mathrm{i}} and a text representation z_{\mathrm{t}} , both extracted from frozen pretrained encoders (Section  3.3 ). Each modality has its own time t_{m}\in[0,1] , and a single network predicts both clean states from the pair of noisy states:
 (\widehat{z}_{\mathrm{i}},\,\widehat{z}_{\mathrm{t}})=f_{\theta}\!\bigl(z_{\mathrm{i}}^{t_{\mathrm{i}}},\,z_{\mathrm{t}}^{t_{\mathrm{t}}};\;t_{\mathrm{i}},t_{\mathrm{t}}\bigr). 
 (2) 
The pair of times lives in a unit square, the 2D time domain of Figure  2 . Setting t_{m}{=}1 makes modality  m a clean condition, and any t_{m}{<}1 means it is still being generated. A task is thus just a path through the square. Text-to-image generation follows the edge t_{\mathrm{t}}{=}1 and captioning the edge t_{\mathrm{i}}{=}1 , while joint generation cuts through the interior. Question answering sets times per token, so the image and the question stay at t{=}1 while the answer is generated (Appendix  A.6.2 ). We therefore train on the edges with conditional denoising and on the interior with joint denoising, so that every region sampling passes through is covered.
The formulation introduces three requirements, each addressed by one design component. First, because the two representation spaces differ in dimensionality and feature statistics, each modality has its own projections, noise scale, and noise schedule (Sections  3.3 and  3.4 ). Second, because any token can be either a condition or a generation target, time tokens in the sequence and a time embedding for each token tell the network the role of each position (Section  3.3 ). Third, because one set of weights must serve both conditional directions and joint generation, MM-RiT is trained on a mixture of conditional and joint denoising (Section  3.4 ).

3.3  Architecture
 Representation Encoders. 
Given a paired image  x_{\mathrm{i}} and text  x_{\mathrm{t}} , frozen pretrained encoders extract features that are normalized to align their scales with the diffusion noise  [ 35 ] :
 h_{m}=E_{m}(x_{m}),\qquad z_{m}=N_{m}(h_{m}),\qquad m\in\{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{t}\}. 
 (3) 
SigLIP2  [ 11 ] and Flan-T5-Small  [ 36 ] are our default image and text encoders, and Section  4.4 looks at other choices. We do not finetune the encoders. Their spaces already encode visual and linguistic knowledge learned at scale, and if they stay fixed, the same features can be used both as targets to generate and as context to condition on.
 Multimodal Representation Diffusion Transformer (MM-RiT). 
MM-RiT implements f_{\theta} in Equation  2 : it receives both streams at independently specified noise levels (t_{\mathrm{i}},t_{\mathrm{t}})   [ 19 ] and predicts both denoised clean states, which we convert to velocities for training (velocity matching loss) and inference (ODE solver).
To accommodate the distinct image and text representation spaces, each MM-RiT block uses separate Q/K/V projections for each modality, concatenates the resulting sequences for one bidirectional joint attention operation, and then applies separate output projections and feedforward networks  [ 7 , 8 , 37 ] . Time tokens in the sequence and additive time embeddings for each token expose the noise level of each modality and each token, so the network can distinguish clean conditioning positions ( t{=}1 ) from generation targets ( t{<}1 ).
 Representation Decoders. 
Fixed decoders map the generated representations back to pixels and tokens at inference:
 \widehat{x}_{\mathrm{i}}=D_{\mathrm{i}}(z_{\mathrm{i}}),\qquad\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{t}}=D_{\mathrm{t}}(z_{\mathrm{t}}). 
 (4) 
For images we use the Scale-RAE decoder that belongs to the image encoder  [ 7 ] . For text, a lightweight parallel decoder maps all generated states to tokens in one forward pass, which is much faster than autoregressive decoding; the pretraining study and the final models each use one decoder of this design. All decoders are trained separately and stay fixed during MM-RiT training. Appendix  A.4 describes them and compares parallel with autoregressive decoding.
3.4  Training
 Noise and Schedules for Each Modality. 
Image and text representations differ in dimensionality, scale, and geometry, and noise settings that work for one may not work for the other, so each modality gets its own.
First, the Gaussian noise scale is set separately ( \sigma_{\mathrm{i}}{=}1 , \sigma_{\mathrm{t}}{=}2 by default) and matched to the statistics of each space after normalization.
Second, the noise levels follow a separate distribution for each modality: for modality  m , we draw a base time  u_{m} from a logit normal distribution and transform it by
 t_{m}=S_{\alpha_{m}}(u_{m}),\qquad S_{\alpha}(u)=\frac{u}{\alpha+(1-\alpha)\,u}. 
 (5) 
For images, we set the shift to \alpha_{\mathrm{i}}=\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{i}}\,d_{\mathrm{i}}/4096} , where n_{\mathrm{i}} is the number of image tokens and d_{\mathrm{i}} their dimension. This moves probability mass toward noisier states. Text is not shifted ( \alpha_{\mathrm{t}}{=}1 ). Features of high dimension can keep much of their information at moderate noise levels, so the shift makes the model see states dominated by noise more often  [ 10 , 6 ] .
 Joint and Conditional Training. 
With these schedules, MM-RiT is trained on a mixture of three denoising tasks (Algorithm  1 ).
For each pair of image and text from a training set  \mathcal{D} , we draw a task with the mixture weights (\pi_{\mathrm{J}},\pi_{\mathrm{T2I}},\pi_{\mathrm{I2T}}) :
 joint denoising draws the noise levels of both modalities independently, t_{m}\sim p_{m}(t) with p_{m} the distribution of Equation  5 , and corrupts both streams;
conditional T2I keeps the text clean at t_{\mathrm{t}}{=}1 and corrupts only the image;
and conditional I2T does the opposite, keeping the image clean at t_{\mathrm{i}}{=}1 .
In the time domain of Section  3.2 , joint denoising samples the interior ( t_{\mathrm{i}}{<}1,\,t_{\mathrm{t}}{<}1 ) and the conditional tasks lie on its edges  [ 19 ] .
The conditional tasks match the clean conditions used at inference. Joint denoising is what teaches the model the joint distribution, which coupled sampling and the guidance reference for image conditions need (Section  3.5 ). In pretraining, the velocity matching loss covers only corrupted target positions, so clean conditioning positions and text padding are left out; \lambda_{\mathrm{i}} and \lambda_{\mathrm{t}} balance the two modalities.

3.5  Inference
 Conditional and Joint Generation. 
At inference, conditioning inputs stay at t{=}1 , and generation targets are integrated from Gaussian noise up to t{=}1 .
Text-to-image, image-to-text, and joint generation differ only in the path they take through the 2D time domain  [ 19 ] (Figure  2 ):
 (t_{\mathrm{i}}(s),\,t_{\mathrm{t}}(s))=\begin{cases}(S_{\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}}(s),\;1),&\text{T2I},\\[2.0pt]
(1,\;S_{\alpha_{\mathrm{t}}}(s)),&\text{I2T},\\[2.0pt]
(S_{\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}}(s),\;S_{\alpha_{\mathrm{t}}}(s)),&\text{joint},\end{cases}\quad s\in[0,1]. 
 (6) 
 Classifier-Free Guidance. 
For conditional tasks we apply classifier-free guidance  [ 38 ] in velocity space: the prediction with the condition is pushed away from a reference prediction without it, which strengthens what the condition adds. No training stage drops the condition, so JUICE has no learned null token. The reference instead replaces the condition with an uninformative input that the model has learned to handle. For an image condition, the image is placed at its noise end, a state the joint task trains the model to see next to text; for a text condition, we use the clean states of the generic caption “a photo.”, which says almost nothing about the image. We use w{=}5 for text-to-image generation, w{=}3 for captioning and question answering, and no guidance for joint generation and planning. Appendix  A.6.2 gives the formulas, and Table  20 compares the text reference with placing the caption at its noise end.
 Coupled Sampling. 
In joint generation, both velocity fields are computed from the current joint state (z_{\mathrm{i}}^{t_{\mathrm{i}}},z_{\mathrm{t}}^{t_{\mathrm{t}}}) before either modality is updated. We call this coupled sampling . Each stream is thus conditioned, step by step, on the other stream as it evolves. Independent sampling works differently. It integrates each stream separately and leaves the other one at its initial noise for the whole trajectory, which amounts to sampling each modality from its marginal. Sequential sampling first generates one modality completely and then the other conditioned on it, with either the image or the text going first.
4  Pretraining Study
Putting both modalities in continuous encoder spaces under one denoiser raises the four questions of Section  1 , and this section takes them in turn. Should text be generated as continuous states or as tokens (Section  4.2 )? Which denoising tasks should training cover (Section  4.3 )? Which encoder spaces should the model read and write (Section  4.4 )? And do the answers change with model size (Section  4.5 )? All runs share the setup below, and each study ends with a takeaway.
4.1  Setup
All studies share one pretraining recipe and vary one design choice at a time. The reference model has an MM-RiT backbone with 668M trainable parameters over frozen SigLIP2-SO400M image features and Flan-T5-Small text states. It is trained for 50k updates at a global batch size of 4,096 with AdamW ( \beta_{1}{=}0.9 , \beta_{2}{=}0.95 , weight decay 0.01) and a constant learning rate of 4{\times}10^{-4} after 5,000 warmup steps, on 38M pairs of images and captions  [ 39 , 7 ] and Cambrian-7M  [ 40 ] , using the default task ratios (55.6% joint, 22.2% T2I, 22.2% I2T; Appendix  A.6.1 ).
For image generation we report GenEval (GE)  [ 41 ] and DPG-Bench (DPG)  [ 42 , 43 ] , with 25 Euler steps and guidance w{=}5 against the generic caption reference (a fully masked caption for the base D-DiT-style framework; Appendix  A.6.2 ). For captioning we report CIDEr  [ 44 ] and CLIPScore (CLIP, \times 100 )  [ 45 ] on 5,000 pairs held out from training, with 16 steps and w{=}3 (Section  3.5 ).
Each configuration is trained once. The framework and representation comparisons change complete pipelines, since a representation comes with its own decoder, normalization, and noise settings; they therefore compare design choices as they would be deployed rather than isolated components. Appendices  A and  B give the full configurations.

4.3  Training Task Mixtures
Because both modalities are continuous and each has its own noise level, JUICE can be trained not only on conditional tasks, where one modality is clean, but also on joint denoising, where both are noisy and each is predicted next to a noisy partner. In the 2D time domain (t_{\mathrm{i}},t_{\mathrm{t}}) , the conditional T2I and I2T tasks lie on the edges and joint denoising covers the interior (Section  3.4 ; Figure  2 ). Edges and interior.
Training in a single direction specializes: the T2I only and I2T only models do well in the direction they are trained for and fail in the other. Training only the edges (Multi-task) supports both directions, but training only the interior (Joint only) is worse on all four metrics. Joint only never sees the boundary. At conditional inference the condition is exactly clean, and training on the interior never produces that state. Mixing trains on both and is the best bidirectional recipe on every metric; it also stays ahead of Multi-task throughout training. The rule is simple: train where sampling goes. T2I and I2T sampling follow the edges, while joint sampling goes through the interior. At the shared guidance scale, Mixing is as good as the T2I only model at image generation, but it is still behind the I2T only model on CIDEr (88.80 vs. 107.22). The ordering of the bidirectional recipes also holds with the guidance scale tuned per recipe: with each recipe at its best scale in w\in[1,5] , Mixing (48.41) still leads Multi-task (44.29) and Joint only (31.33) on GenEval, while T2I only rises to 51.23 (Appendix  A.6.2 ).
More targets or cross-modal exchange?
Extra supervision explains only part of the gain. Mixing does supervise each modality more often than Multi-task (on 77.8% vs. 50% of examples), so we control for target count (lower block of Table  3 ; Appendix  B.2.1 ). One control replaces each joint example with two conditional ones at the same target count (matched dual conditional, DC), at 1.56 times the backbone work; another blocks cross-modal attention on joint examples. Both lower every metric relative to Mixing, by 2.51 and 2.92 GenEval points, respectively. In the other direction, a joint branch that keeps both streams noisy but supervises only one of them (single target joint) beats Multi-task on all four metrics with the same number of targets. Training Multi-task for longer, until it sees as many targets as Mixing, closes the CIDEr gap (89.31) but not the GenEval gap (43.16). The noisy partner and the exchange with it each contribute.
 Takeaway: Train where you sample. Sampling uses both the edges of the square and its interior, and training has to cover both. Joint only never sees a clean condition and reaches just 31.33 GenEval at its best guidance scale; Multi-task never trains on the interior and reaches 44.29; Mixing gets 48.41. Matching the target count does not close the gap, so the joint examples give more than extra supervision. 
4.4  Representation Spaces
JUICE reads and writes in the spaces of its frozen encoders, so the encoder determines both what the model sees and what it must generate. Encoders differ in what their spaces capture: some are trained to reconstruct their input, others to capture semantics, and some are aligned with the other modality. We explore these tradeoffs on both sides. With T5 fixed for text, we compare SigLIP2  [ 11 ] and WebSSL-DINO  [ 12 ] (DINO) image features with FLUX VAE latents  [ 46 ] and raw pixels  [ 34 , 2 ] . With SigLIP2 fixed for images, we compare T5 with Qwen3-Embedding (Qwen3-E)  [ 47 ] , Qwen3-VL-Embedding (Qwen3-VL-E)  [ 48 ] , and SigLIP2 text features (Figure  5 ; full metrics in Table  28 ).
 Figure 5: Semantic encoder spaces outperform VAE latents and pixels, and T5 leads among text spaces. Top: image spaces with T5 text. Bottom: text spaces with SigLIP2 images.
Image spaces.
Semantic encoders outperform spaces built for reconstruction on all four metrics: SigLIP2 and DINO reach 48.41 and 49.07 GenEval, against 35.42 for the VAE and 24.48 for pixels. This ordering is broadly the reverse of reconstruction quality, as the FLUX VAE reconstructs best among the learned spaces (ImageNet rFID 0.288, against 0.435 for SigLIP2; Table  30 ). The two semantic encoders are close on image generation, but captioning separates them: SigLIP2, which is aligned with language, leads DINO by 20.0 CIDEr and 8.5 CLIPScore. For a model that both generates and conditions on the same features, semantic structure thus matters more than reconstruction fidelity. The order of the two semantic encoders on image generation changes with model size (Section  4.5 ).
Text spaces.
Flan-T5 states lead on all four metrics, ahead of larger embedding models and of SigLIP2 text features, which are aligned with the image space. Each text space is decoded with its own parallel decoder, which recovers 97.5% to 99.2% of tokens from clean states, and even a linear readout recovers 96.5% to 98.5% (Table  29 ). All four spaces thus retain most of the token information. The decoder affects only the captioning metrics, and the GenEval differences, which do not involve it, indicate that the ordering reflects how well each space serves as a generation target and condition. Qwen3-E comes close to T5 on CIDEr (88.50 vs. 88.80) but trails it by 5.3 GenEval points, and the SigLIP2 text features give the lowest GenEval (33.08). Alignment with the image space therefore does not make a text space a better target or condition here.
 Takeaway: Semantics over reconstruction. Good reconstruction does not make a good generation space. The FLUX VAE has the best rFID among the learned spaces (0.288 vs. 0.435 for SigLIP2), but GenEval in it drops to 35.42, from 48.41 with SigLIP2. SigLIP2 and DINO are close on generation, and SigLIP2’s alignment with language shows up in captioning (20.0 CIDEr ahead). On the text side, Flan-T5 states beat larger embedding models and SigLIP2 text features. 
4.5  Scaling
Do these preferences survive as the model grows? Keeping the recipe above, we train MM-RiT backbones from 179M to 2.1B trainable parameters (d12 to d28; Appendix  A.5 ) on both the SigLIP2 and the DINO image space (Figure  6 ). All four metrics improve with size in both spaces, but scale treats the two roles of the image space differently. For image generation the order flips. DINO is well ahead at 179M (32.90 vs. 18.99 GenEval), the gap shrinks as the backbone grows, and by 2.1B SigLIP2 is ahead (56.81 vs. 52.78). Captioning shows no such flip: SigLIP2 leads at every size and reaches 86.64 CLIPScore at 2.1B, against 72.32 for DINO. The two trends separate the roles of the image space: DINO’s advantage as a generation target at small scale fades as capacity grows, whereas the benefit of SigLIP2’s language alignment for producing text persists. After finetuning, the two 2.1B models of Section  5 differ by less than 0.02 GenEval (DINO 0.88, SigLIP2 0.87), so neither image space keeps a clear GenEval advantage, while SigLIP2 leads on almost every understanding benchmark.
 Figure 6: Scaling the backbone from 179M to 2.1B parameters. DINO’s early lead in image generation disappears with scale, while SigLIP2 leads captioning at every size. DPG-Bench in this figure uses the time swap text reference (Appendix  A.6.2 ).
 Takeaway: Scale reorders image spaces for generation, not captioning. DINO’s lead in image generation fades as the model grows, while SigLIP2’s language alignment helps captioning at every size. 
5  Experiments
A single model of the joint distribution of images and text should provide three abilities: generating images from text, answering questions about images, and producing image and text outputs that are individually correct and mutually consistent. We now evaluate JUICE on each of them at scale.
5.1  Setup
We train two 2.1B models, one per image space. Each is pretrained with the recipe of Section  4.1 , but for 60k instead of 50k updates, and then finetuned for 15k updates at a global batch size of 1,024 on 20.6M text-to-image and visual understanding examples (Appendix  A.7 ). Text-to-image generation uses 25 Euler steps with guidance w{=}5 ; visual question answering uses 16 steps with w{=}3 , with the parallel decoder reading out all answer positions at once. For joint reasoning, we adapt the finetuned models to each task separately (both models to mazes, the SigLIP2 model to visual spatial planning), read the moves with a linear head trained for each task, and sample without guidance (Section  5.4 ). All input and output images are 224{\times}224 . We compare with unified models of 1B to 3B parameters; Appendix  C includes comparisons with larger models.

On visual question answering, JUICE is competitive on several perception benchmarks and weak on those that need domain knowledge (Table  6 ). All other models in the table start from a pretrained language or text-to-image model, whereas JUICE trains MM-RiT from scratch and sees images at 224{\times}224 .

The weak side is domain knowledge. On AI2D, JUICE scores 34.4, far below models initialized from LLMs (58.5 to 71.4). On MMMU (29.6), it is above Show-o and Muddit, comparable to JanusFlow and OneFlow, and below Harmon, FUDOKI, Janus-Pro, Show-o2, and TUNA (34.3 to 39.1).

5.4  Joint Reasoning
Some tasks need an image and a text output that are each correct and that agree, such as a route drawn on a map and the moves that describe it. When several answers are valid, being correct is not enough: the two outputs must also commit to the same answer. Sampling each modality from its own marginal can pick a different valid answer in each, which we call a cross-modal mode mismatch. A model of the joint distribution can instead produce both outputs together, with each modality conditioning on the other’s evolving state (coupled sampling; Section  3.5 ). We first look at unconditional joint generation and then turn to two planning tasks, in which the input is a map image and a text question and the model generates the map with the route drawn on it together with the move sequence.
Joint generation.
Without any condition, both streams start from noise and the model generates an image and a caption together (selected samples in Figure  10 ). In these samples, the caption describes the image it is generated with, so the two streams agree even though neither is given. Planning tasks test the harder case, in which the outputs must also be correct.

Coupled sampling comes close to the better sequential order without having to know which order that is. If each modality is sampled without seeing the other (independent sampling), joint success is only 54.6% for SigLIP2 and 42.7% for DINO, since the two streams often settle on different routes. Sequential sampling avoids this disagreement, but then the order matters, because mistakes in the first modality are passed on to the second. Image first and then text reaches 95.2% and 82.7%; the reverse order reaches 85.3% and 59.9%. The better order starts with the more reliable modality, here the image, whose validity alone is higher than that of the text. For a new task, though, which modality is more reliable is not known in advance. Coupled sampling needs no order. It reaches 93.7% and 81.7%, within 1.5 and 1.0 points of the better order and 8.4 and 21.8 points above the worse one, and it loses at most 0.3 points when the budget is halved to 8 calls (Appendix  D.2 ). These numbers are means over two seeds. For SigLIP2, the gap to image first is 0.6 points for one seed and 2.4 for the other, and a paired test still finds image first better ( p{=}0.017 ; Appendix  D.2 ).
Coupled sampling does not reach agreement by collapsing onto one canonical route. On the mazes it solves, coupled sampling returns the stored reference route in 43.5% and 47.0% of cases, close to the 44.1% and 44.4% expected if it chose uniformly among the shortest routes, so the model picks among valid routes. Figure  11 follows two mazes, with both samplers starting from the same state. Under coupled sampling, whichever output settles later changes to match the other: the drawn route in one maze, the moves in the other. Independent sampling ends with two valid but different routes. Because either modality can be the one that changes, coupled sampling is not simply copying one modality into the other, which fits with the fact that it does not need to know in advance which modality is more reliable.

Visual spatial planning.
VSP  [ 15 ] asks for a path through a FrozenLake grid from the start to the goal without stepping into a hole; any such path counts as a success. We adapt the finetuned SigLIP2 model for 5k updates on 55.9k maps of sizes 3{\times}3 to 6{\times}6 , synthesized by us or drawn from ThinkMorph  [ 57 ] , with all benchmark maps removed, and evaluate on the official 100 maps per level (Table  8 ). JUICE solves 99.8% of maps at levels 3 to 6, on par with the fine variant of LatentUM (99%), which plans visually step by step, and above its coarse variant (85%), ThinkMorph (76%), and InternVL3.5-38B (20%). On larger map sizes not seen in training, it solves 94% at level 7 and 69% at level 8. Its image and text outputs agree on 99.6% of our own test maps. Appendix  D.4 describes the setup and the baselines.
On the unseen sizes, the drawn path is right more often than the moves, although the image test is the stricter one. At levels 7 and 8, the drawn path is a shortest route on 99% and 96% of maps, while the moves reach the goal on 94% and 69% (Figure  12 a shows a solved map of each size). Of the 37 failures there, 29 lose one move from the reference route while the drawn path is exactly that route. The rate of lost moves grows with the length of the run of repeated moves, from under 2% of runs of one or two moves to 7 of the 9 runs of six, a length that a shortest route cannot have on the sizes used in adaptation. The outputs mostly settle early: on 574 of the 600 maps, both are fixed from the first of 16 steps. Coupled sampling can still restore a lost move (Figure  12 b). On nine maps the moves fail at the first step and solve the map at the end, seven of them with the drawn path fixed throughout, and no map solved at the first step ends unsolved. On the unseen sizes, as on mazes, the image is the more reliable modality, and these text errors match the cost in exact wording that Section  4.2 finds for continuous text.
6  Conclusion
JUICE models the joint distribution of images and text within frozen pretrained representation spaces, using a single flow matching backbone. Because each modality has its own noise level, image generation, visual understanding, and joint generation become paths through one square of noise levels, all handled by the same model. Our pretraining studies point to four principles. Text can be generated as continuous states at little cost to image generation, which stays close to that of a model trained only for text-to-image generation; what suffers is mainly exact wording. Training needs both the edges of the square, where sampling is conditioned on clean context, and the interior, which joint generation passes through. Semantic encoder spaces are better generation targets than spaces built for reconstruction. And a preference between image spaces found at one scale carries over across 179M to 2.1B parameters for captioning but not for generation. At 2.1B parameters and without language model initialization, the backbone is competitive in compositional image generation, in generation that needs world knowledge, and, on several perception benchmarks, in visual understanding. Since the model learns the joint distribution, it can also draw a route and write the matching moves together. On mazes with multiple solutions, coupled sampling makes the two agree without a fixed generation order, and on visual spatial planning it solves nearly all maps of the training sizes. In such a model, the order in which modalities are generated is no longer fixed by the architecture: it becomes a choice at sampling time, and coupled sampling can leave it open. Pretrained continuous spaces thus let one model understand, generate, and coordinate images and text without putting either one first.
 Limitations and Future Work. 
The models run at a native 224{\times}224 resolution, which limits fine visual detail. Exact wording lags behind autoregressive text, and without a language model backbone the models trail most of those initialized from LLMs on benchmarks that need domain knowledge, such as AI2D and MMMU. Each pretraining configuration was run once, and the joint reasoning tasks are synthetic planning problems. The next steps follow from these limits: higher resolution, more accurate continuous text, and joint generation on real tasks in which several pairs of image and text outputs are valid.
