Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) is an effective way to strengthen the long-context reasoning of large language models (LLMs), but it needs training data that are long, diverse, challenging, and verifiable.
Human annotation of such data is expensive, naturally occurring data are too scarce to sustain RL, and existing synthesis methods depend on external corpora or stronger teacher models, which become harder to obtain as the trained models get stronger.
Recently, RL from zero data, in which a model learns from tasks it proposes itself, has achieved excellent performance, but existing methods target short-context tasks.
Therefore, we propose LongZero , a method that lets an LLM improve its own long-context reasoning without any external data.
Instead of writing questions and answers over existing documents, LongZero first has the model synthesize the question and answer with its supporting evidence in a compact structured form, and only then separately expands the evidence into a long multi-document context.
LongZero combines these self-written multi-hop reasoning tasks with self-written multi-round retrieval tasks and trains the model with GRPO.
We evaluate LongZero on two LLMs across five long-context reasoning benchmarks and compare it with RL on data synthesized from external corpora.
Experimental results show that LongZero generalizes to real tasks and outperforms both baselines, suggesting a viable and promising path to improving long-context reasoning of LLMs. 1 1

1 Introduction
Figure 1: Core idea of LongZero . (a) Existing methods build long-context RL data from external corpora. (b) LongZero lets the policy write the question, evidence, and answer, expand them into long-context data, and learn with RL from answering its own questions.
Reasoning over long context, such as collections of reports, long conversations, or entire books, is required in many applications of large language models (LLMs) ( Bai et al., 2025 ; Krishna et al., 2025 ) .
Following the success of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) on short-context reasoning ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ; Team et al., 2025 ; Lambert et al., 2024 ; Yu et al., 2025b ) , a growing body of work applies RL to long-context reasoning ( Wan et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025a ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) .
These studies consistently show that training data are a decisive factor: useful RL data demand (i) substantial length, (ii) evidence retrieval and consolidation, (iii) sufficient difficulty for producing a learning signal, and (iv) automatically verifiable answers ( Wang et al., 2025a ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Lv et al., 2026 ) .
Data with all of these properties are difficult to obtain.
Human annotation of long-context questions is expensive, and naturally occurring long-context QA data are far scarcer than what RL consumes.
Existing pipelines therefore build training tasks from existing resources (Figure 1 a): they extend short multi-hop QA datasets with distractor documents ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , ask a model to write questions over real documents ( Yang et al., 2025b ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Lv et al., 2026 ) , combine knowledge graphs with search-agent trajectories ( Lin et al., 2026 ) , or simply rely on a stronger teacher model to generate training data ( Mukherjee et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024b ) .
All of these approaches depend on external data or models. Real-world facts also introduce a confound: a model may answer from its parametric knowledge instead of the given context ( Longpre et al., 2021 ) , and public text may overlap with evaluation data ( Sainz et al., 2023 ) .
At the same time, RL from zero data has emerged as an alternative to curated training sets.
In these methods the model proposes its own tasks and learns from solving them ( Zhao et al., 2025a ; Huang et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ) .
This line of work has achieved excellent performance in short-context domains, such as mathematics, code, games, and tool-using agents ( Xia et al., 2025 ; Yue et al., 2026 ; Acikgoz et al., 2026 ) .
This leads to our research question: Can an LLM also improve its long-context reasoning from zero data?
Long-context tasks are harder to synthesize from zero data: a task consists of a long input together with a question about it, and the model must produce both.
Letting the model write a long document and then pose a question about it inherits the very weakness the task is meant to address, since a model that cannot yet reason reliably over long contexts cannot reliably write or verify questions about the long text it has just written.
Our key observation is that the order of generation can be reversed.
If the model first writes the question, evidence, and answer as a short structured specification, and only then expands the evidence into a long multi-document context, the answer is fixed before any long text exists, and the only remaining risk of error lies in the expansion.
Building on this observation, we propose LongZero (Figure 1 b).
LongZero uses the model being trained to synthesize two complementary kinds of long-context tasks: reasoning tasks, in which multi-hop questions over a fictional world must be answered from eight model-written documents, and retrieval tasks in the style of multi-round co-reference resolution (MRCR; Vodrahalli et al., 2024 ), in which the model must reproduce one specific earlier response in a long, model-written conversation.
The same model then answers each reasoning question several times, and only the questions it sometimes answers correctly and sometimes incorrectly are kept, because these are the questions on which group-relative policy optimization (GRPO; Shao et al., 2024 ) receives a non-zero learning signal.
Our main contributions are as follows:
•
We propose LongZero , a long-context RL method in which the LLM itself first synthesizes the question, evidence, and answer in a structured format, then expands them into long-context data, and is finally trained on these data.
•
We evaluate LongZero on two base models and five long-context reasoning benchmarks, and experimental results show that LongZero can generalize from self-generated tasks to general long-context reasoning tasks.
•
We compare LongZero with two synthetic-data baselines built from external corpora, and experimental results show that LongZero outperforms both baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of LongZero in improving long-context reasoning of LLMs without external data.
2 Related Work
Reinforcement Learning from Zero Data.
Learning without human data dates back to self-play systems such as AlphaGo Zero ( Silver et al., 2017 ) , and LLMs have long been used to generate their own instructions, rationales, or rewards ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Zelikman et al., 2022 ; Yuan et al., 2024 ) .
Since RLVR became the standard recipe for reasoning ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ) , two lines of work have reduced its reliance on curated data.
The first removes labels: rewards are derived from majority voting ( Zuo et al., 2025 ) or from the model’s own confidence ( Zhao et al., 2025b ) .
The second removes the questions themselves: the model proposes tasks and learns from solving them, verified by code execution in Absolute Zero ( Zhao et al., 2025a ) , by a co-evolving challenger and solver in R-Zero ( Huang et al., 2025 ) , or by asymmetric self-play from a single topic prompt ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , and the idea has been extended to games ( Liu et al., 2025 ) and agents ( Xia et al., 2025 ; Yue et al., 2026 ) .
Programmatic task generators play a similar role for logic puzzles ( Xie et al., 2025 ; Stojanovski et al., 2025 ) .
All of these methods target short-context tasks, where a task is a short string; in long-context reasoning, the long context must itself be synthesized and kept consistent with the question and answer, which is the problem LongZero addresses.
Long-Context Reinforcement Learning.
Long-context ability is usually built by context extension and continued pre-training ( Peng et al., 2024 ; Fu et al., 2024 ; Gao et al., 2024 ) , followed by fine-tuning on synthetic long-context data ( Bai et al., 2024a ; An et al., 2024 ; Xiong et al., 2024 ) .
RL has recently been applied directly to long-context reasoning, and most work focuses on how to construct training tasks: QwenLong-L1 and QwenLong-L1.5 write questions over real documents ( Wan et al., 2025 ; Shen et al., 2025 ) , LoongRL hides HotpotQA questions behind UUID chains among distractors ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , SPELL lets one model question, answer, and verify over raw documents ( Yang et al., 2025b ) , LongTraceRL builds questions from knowledge-graph walks and search trajectories ( Lin et al., 2026 ) , and GoLongRL curates a multi-task mixture ( Lv et al., 2026 ) .
Other work improves the reward, for example with verifiable context rewards ( Chen et al., 2026 ) , or processes long inputs with memory agents ( Yu et al., 2025a ) .
These methods all obtain their contexts or questions from existing corpora, QA datasets, knowledge bases, or larger models; LongZero synthesizes both with the model being trained and is complementary to better rewards and algorithms.
Evaluation of Long-Context Reasoning.
Early evaluations relied on synthetic retrieval probes such as RULER ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) , which revealed, for instance, that models miss information in the middle of long inputs ( Liu et al., 2024a ) . Benchmarks have since moved toward realistic tasks and reasoning, such as LongBench and LongBench v2 ( Bai et al., 2024b ; Bai et al., 2025 ) , MRCR ( Vodrahalli et al., 2024 ) , LongReason ( Ling et al., 2025 ) , and AA-LCR ( Artificial Analysis, 2025 ) .

Let π θ \pi_{\theta} denote the LLM to be trained.
LongZero uses π θ \pi_{\theta} itself to synthesize a training set 𝒟 = 𝒟 rea ∪ 𝒟 ret \mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{rea}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{ret}} of long-context tasks with verifiable answers, and then optimizes π θ \pi_{\theta} on 𝒟 \mathcal{D} with GRPO (Figure 2 ).
3.1 Data Synthesis
LongZero synthesizes two kinds of data: reasoning data 𝒟 rea \mathcal{D}_{\text{rea}} , which require finding facts scattered across long documents, rejecting similar-looking distractors, and combining the facts into an answer, and retrieval data 𝒟 ret \mathcal{D}_{\text{ret}} , which require locating and reproducing one specific item among many similar ones in a long conversation.
In both cases, the answer is determined by construction rather than by a model’s judgment of a long text.
3.1.1 Reasoning Data Synthesis
A reasoning sample is drawn from a world : a set of fictional entities and facts rendered as M M long documents of different genres, together with multi-hop questions that require combining facts from different documents.
The difficulty of a question q q over a context x x grows with the length of the context, the number of hops, and the number of distractors:
Difficulty ⁡ ( x , q ) ∝ | x | ⋅ H q ⋅ K q , \operatorname{Difficulty}(x,q)\propto|x|\cdot H_{q}\cdot K_{q},
(1)
where | x | |x| is the number of tokens in x x , H q H_{q} is the number of hops of q q , and K q K_{q} is the number of distractor facts that imitate its supporting facts.
We use this relation as a design guide rather than as a fitted model: the specification controls H q H_{q} and K q K_{q} , and the expansion controls | x | |x| .
The central design choice is to generate the world top-down : π θ \pi_{\theta} first writes a compact specification that fixes the questions, their supporting facts, and their answers, and only then expands it into long text.
All entities are fictional, which reduces reliance on parametric knowledge; fictional biographies are likewise used to study knowledge storage and extraction ( Allen-Zhu and Li, 2023 ) .
A program first samples a domain, a set of genres, and a style card that fixes the time period, naming style, and banned names, so that worlds are diverse. The remaining steps are shown on the left of Figure 2 , and their prompts are given in Appendix A.2 .
Specification.
π θ \pi_{\theta} writes a world specification 𝒮 = ( 𝒱 , ℱ , ℱ − , 𝒬 ) \mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{-},\mathcal{Q}) as strict JSON.
𝒱 \mathcal{V} contains named entities, among which at least two people share a surname and at least two organizations have confusably similar names.
ℱ \mathcal{F} contains atomic facts forming Q Q question chains that partially share bridge entities, where the chain of question q q has H q H_{q} hops, and ℱ − \mathcal{F}^{-} contains K q K_{q} distractor facts per chain that involve the same bridge entity but differ in role, time, or attribute.
Each question q ∈ 𝒬 q\in\mathcal{Q} comes with its answer y q ∗ y^{\ast}_{q} and supporting facts F q ⊆ ℱ F_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{F} ; it refers to intermediate entities by description, such as “the founder of the magazine’s parent press”, so that answering requires resolving each hop, whereas the answer must be a concrete name, place, product, title, or number appearing verbatim in a fact.
Because the specification is short, it can be generated and checked reliably, and it fixes the answer before any long text is written.
Plan and Expansion.
π θ \pi_{\theta} assigns every fact in ℱ ∪ ℱ − \mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}^{-} to one of the M M documents, spreading the supporting facts of each question across different documents and placing each distractor away from the fact it imitates, so that answering requires locating evidence in several documents and rejecting nearby distractors.
It then writes each document independently in the planned genre, stating each assigned fact exactly once and not stating facts assigned to other documents.
Finally, a program shuffles the M M documents into the context x x and keeps a question only if its answer or an equivalent alias appears verbatim in the expanded text; this faithfulness gate removes questions whose answer-bearing fact was dropped or altered during expansion.
Each surviving question yields a sample ( x , q , y q ∗ ) (x,q,y^{\ast}_{q}) .
3.1.2 Retrieval Data Synthesis
Our retrieval data follow the task format of MRCR ( Vodrahalli et al., 2024 ; OpenAI, 2025 ) : in a long conversation, the user repeatedly asks for short pieces of the same kind, such as “write a poem about tigers”, and finally asks the model to reproduce the k k -th of them, prefixed by a random string s s .
Instead of using existing conversations, LongZero lets π θ \pi_{\theta} write all conversational content itself, as shown on the right of Figure 2 .
Given only a small set of formats and common nouns taken from the public OpenAI MRCR dataset ( OpenAI, 2025 ) , π θ \pi_{\theta} responds to every request “ write a {format} about {noun} ” R R times, yielding a pool of self-written responses.
A program then assembles each sample from the pool: it selects a target request, inserts N N of π θ \pi_{\theta} ’s responses to that request, called needles a 1 , … , a N a_{1},\dots,a_{N} , at random positions among responses to other requests until the conversation reaches a length drawn from a fixed range, and asks for the k k -th needle prefixed by s s .
Because the program decides which response is the k k -th needle, the reference answer y ∗ = s | a k y^{\ast}=s\,\|\,a_{k} is correct by construction, while solving the task requires distinguishing the model’s own near-duplicate responses and tracking their order over a long context.
We sample 1,152 training and 128 validation conversations without filtering.

GRPO normalizes rewards within a group of responses to the same prompt, so a question that the policy always answers correctly, or always answers incorrectly, contributes no gradient.
In our inspection, consistently failed questions were also frequently defective, for example because two documents assigned conflicting values to the same attribute.
LongZero therefore lets π θ \pi_{\theta} measure the difficulty of its own questions and keeps only the unstable band .
Concretely, π θ \pi_{\theta} answers each question with the full context in small waves of samples, scored by the same matching rule as the training reward (§ 3.2 ); a question is kept as soon as it has received both a correct and an incorrect answer, and discarded if its first W W answers agree.
Because the same model generates and filters the data, the difficulty of the kept questions adapts to the model that will be trained on them.
From the unstable questions we hold out a validation set by world and form the training set from 2,304 reasoning questions and 1,152 retrieval conversations, a 2:1 mixture. As Table 1 shows, Qwen3-8B keeps a smaller share of its own questions than Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, 13.1% versus 17.7%, and therefore needs more synthesis to obtain the same amount of data.

Reward Design.
Rewards are rule-based and selected by the data source of each sample.
For reasoning data, the prompt asks the model to end with “ The answer is <answer>. ”
Let y ^ \hat{y} be the text after the last “the answer is” in the output. The reward is
r rea = { 1 , if ​ y ^ ​ matches a reference answer , 0 , otherwise . r^{\text{rea}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\hat{y}\text{ matches a reference answer},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
(6)
The reference answers are the answer y q ∗ y^{\ast}_{q} and its aliases. y ^ \hat{y} matches a reference answer if it contains that answer as a whole-word span, so that “15” does not match “5”, and is at most L L characters longer than it, which prevents rewarding answers that list many candidates.
Before matching, both texts are lowercased, quotation marks are unified, punctuation is replaced by spaces, and whitespace is collapsed.
For retrieval data, the reward is 0 if the final answer after the thinking part does not start with the prefix s s .
Otherwise, it is the similarity between the rest of the answer and the needle a k a_{k} , measured by the SequenceMatcher ratio of Python’s difflib as in the MRCR evaluation ( OpenAI, 2025 ) ; this dense reward gives partial credit for nearly correct reproductions.

4.1 Settings
Training Setup.
We use (i) Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and (ii) Qwen3-8B in thinking mode ( Yang et al., 2025a ) .
Each model synthesizes and filters its own data (Table 1 ) and is trained with GRPO in verl ( Sheng et al., 2025 ) for 72 steps on Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and 108 steps on Qwen3-8B, about 1.3 and 2 epochs of our data, with 64 prompts and G = 8 G{=}8 responses per step, a constant learning rate of 10 − 6 10^{-6} , ε = 0.2 \varepsilon{=}0.2 , β = 0.001 \beta{=}0.001 , and at most 32,768 prompt and 8,192 response tokens.
All methods, including the baselines, share this configuration, and we report the final checkpoint of each run; Appendix A.1 lists all hyperparameters.
Benchmarks.
We use five long-context reasoning benchmarks: (i) the multi-document QA subsets of LongBench ( Bai et al., 2024b , LB1;) , namely HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, and MuSiQue ( Yang et al., 2018 ; Ho et al., 2020 ; Trivedi et al., 2022 ) with F1; (ii) LongBench v2 ( Bai et al., 2025 , LBv2;) , 503 four-choice questions; (iii) FRAMES ( Krishna et al., 2025 ) with oracle Wikipedia articles, 824 questions scored by answer containment; (iv) AA-LCR ( Artificial Analysis, 2025 ) , 100 questions over document sets of 72K–115K tokens, judged by GPT-5.6-Luna with the official judging prompt; and (v) the 32K split of LongReason ( Ling et al., 2025 ) , 794 multiple-choice questions.
We sample with temperature 0.7 and top- p p 0.95, since greedy decoding is not recommended for Qwen3 thinking models, run each benchmark at least twice, and report the mean of two runs.
Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 natively supports 262K tokens, but Qwen3-8B supports only 32K. Following the Qwen3-8B model card, we apply YaRN ( Peng et al., 2024 ) with a scaling factor of 4 at evaluation time, without additional training; RL training does not use YaRN.
Baselines.
Besides the base models, we compare with two baselines that differ from LongZero only in their training data:
(i) Enhanced HotpotQA (from LoongRL) : 5,120 difficulty-filtered HotpotQA ( Yang et al., 2018 ) questions whose contexts are extended to 16K tokens with distracting documents by the data pipeline of LoongRL ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , trained with LoongRL’s prompt and exact-match reward;
(ii) Teacher-synthesized QA : multi-hop questions written and verified by Qwen3.5-397B-A17B over Ultra-FineWeb ( Wang et al., 2025b ) document clusters from SPELL ( Yang et al., 2025b ) ; each base model keeps 2,304 questions it answers correctly 1–7 times out of 8, mixed 2:1 with the same self-synthesized retrieval data and trained with the same prompt and reward, so that only the source of the reasoning questions differs. Appendix A.1 describes both baselines in detail.
4.2 Results and Analysis
Overall Performance.
Table 2 shows that LongZero achieves the highest average score on both base models compared to the baselines. All three RL methods improve over the base models on average, but LongZero gains the most and is best on four of the five benchmarks for both models, counting a tie on AA-LCR for Qwen3-8B; the exceptions are AA-LCR on Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and LBv2 on Qwen3-8B. Notably, the teacher-synthesized baseline does not surpass ours, although its questions and answers are written and verified by a 397B-parameter model. A stronger generator thus does not guarantee better training data: questions written by another model over real documents need not match the ability of the policy, whereas those of LongZero are written by the policy itself, verifiable by construction, and filtered to its current ability.

Generalization across Tasks.
Figure 3 shows that validation scores on held-out synthesized data rise over training for both data types and both models, with fluctuations but no early saturation. More importantly, these in-distribution gains transfer to the general long-context reasoning benchmarks, which do not share the entities of the training data. On Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, LongZero improves on every benchmark, including LB1 and FRAMES, whose questions concern real Wikipedia entities, although every training world is fictional. Because questions about fictional entities cannot be answered from memory, the policy can only improve on them by reading the context more carefully, and this skill carries over to real documents. The benchmark gains are smaller than the validation gains, as expected under distribution shift, but appear across extractive, multiple-choice, and model-judged free-form answers. The baselines generalize less evenly: they often improve on certain tasks while declining on others. On Qwen3-8B, each falls below the base model on two benchmarks, whereas LongZero does so only on LBv2, and on Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, Enhanced HotpotQA does not improve on the HotpotQA subset of LB1 (Table 6 ) despite training on HotpotQA.
Generalization across Models.
LongZero improves both base models even though each one synthesizes and filters its own data, so the method does not depend on a particular generator. The improvement is clearly larger on Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, which is also the stronger long-context reasoner of the two. This is consistent with the self-synthesis loop: a stronger model is likely to write more faithful specifications and documents, and it wastes less of its synthesis on questions it can never answer (Table 1 ), so its training data are both cleaner and better matched to its ability. Qwen3-8B does not improve on LBv2, and all methods score near zero on AA-LCR, whose 72K–115K-token inputs leave little room for output within the 131K-token window that YaRN provides, so the thinking model often runs out of tokens before giving a final answer: 120 of the 200 AA-LCR responses of LongZero on Qwen3-8B contain no answer.
Length Generalization and Efficiency.
All training contexts are short compared with the benchmarks: reasoning and retrieval samples contain tens of thousands of tokens at most, whereas LBv2 and AA-LCR require reading inputs that are several times longer. LongZero nevertheless improves Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 on both of these benchmarks, so the skills learned on short synthetic contexts, namely locating scattered evidence and rejecting look-alike distractors, generalize to much longer inputs. Moreover, the whole pipeline runs on the model being trained: unlike the teacher-synthesized baseline, which needs a 397B-parameter model to write, answer, and verify every question, LongZero requires no calls to a larger model, and its data cost scales with the size of the policy rather than with that of a teacher.
Data Complementarity.
Table 3 shows that the two kinds of synthesized data are complementary. Reasoning data alone already give most of the improvement on LB1 and FRAMES but fall below the base model on AA-LCR and LongReason. Retrieval data alone lower the average. Combining them is better on average than either alone, and the 2:1 mixture used by LongZero is the best variant on four of the five benchmarks and improves over the base model on all of them, avoiding the losses that each data type causes on its own. We attribute this to the different skills the two tasks train: reasoning data teach the policy to chain facts across documents, while retrieval data teach it to locate and reproduce one exact item among many similar ones. The better result of the 2:1 mixture over the 1:1 mixture further suggests that reasoning data should dominate, with retrieval data acting as a basic signal.
5 Conclusion and Future Work
We presented LongZero , a method for improving the long-context reasoning of LLMs from zero data.
LongZero has the model being trained write a structured specification of the question, evidence, and answer before expanding it into long documents, so that answers are correct by construction without any external corpus, human annotation, or teacher model; it combines these reasoning tasks with self-synthesized retrieval tasks and keeps only the questions the model answers inconsistently.
On two base models and five long-context reasoning benchmarks, LongZero achieves the highest average score and outperforms RL on Enhanced HotpotQA and on questions written by a much larger teacher model, although it uses no data beyond the model itself. Its gains transfer from fictional to real-world documents and from short training contexts to much longer inputs, and its two data types are complementary.
Future work includes iterating LongZero so that the trained model regenerates and refilters its own data, synthesizing longer contexts by writing more documents per world, adding question types that require counting, comparison, or arithmetic over the specification, and exploring better ways to ensure the correctness of the data.

A.1 Experiment Details
Seed lists.

Reasoning data generation.
All generation calls use the model being trained, served with vLLM ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) .
Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 always reasons before answering; we treat each call’s token budget as the total for reasoning and answer, and reject outputs without a closing </think> .
For Qwen3-8B, thinking is enabled for the specification and disabled for planning and expansion.
Table 4 lists the per-stage settings.
The specification prompt asks for 8–14 entities, Q = 4 Q{=}4 question chains of H q = 3 H_{q}{=}3 –5 hops, and K q = 2 K_{q}{=}2 –3 distractor facts per chain, and each world has M = 8 M{=}8 documents.
The specification and the plan are each attempted at most twice; a world is rejected if either fails to parse. For Qwen3-8B, a world is also rejected if its specification does not contain exactly four questions or its plan does not contain exactly eight documents.
A program keeps only questions whose supporting facts all exist, and keeps only aliases that are equivalent surface forms of the answer (added or removed articles and company suffixes, numbers written as words, or sub-spans of at least two content words); aliases that restate the question are removed.
The plan is repaired by removing hallucinated or duplicated fact IDs and placing any missing fact into the currently smallest document; each document lists up to 15 facts of other documents as forbidden.
Expansion targets 2,400–2,700 words per document for Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and 4,000–4,500 words for Qwen3-8B, which writes shorter documents for the same target; documents longer than 14,000 characters are truncated at the last paragraph boundary, and a document that leaks instructions into the text (e.g., “facts to embed”) is regenerated once and otherwise rejected.
Worlds whose estimated context exceeds 27.5K tokens are discarded. The faithfulness gate normalizes case, whitespace, and punctuation; bare-year answers are exempt from it, since years frequently appear in unrelated sentences (at most one question per world may have such an answer).
In the training prompt, the eight shuffled documents are concatenated without document markers.
Difficulty filtering.
The filter uses a separate prompt that presents the eight documents with <CHUNK_i> markers and asks for the evidence documents and a short answer in <answer> tags (Appendix A.2 ); the answer is matched with the same whole-word rule as the reasoning reward. Each wave draws two samples (temperature 0.7, top- p p 0.95), and a question receives at most W = 4 W{=}4 answers; requests that fail at the server are not counted as answers. The validation split is defined by a hash of the world identifier, so that no world appears in both splits.
Retrieval data generation.
The 10 formats are diary entry, email, formal letter, poem, riddle, short essay, short news article, short scene in a play, social media post, and song; the 434 nouns are those of the OpenAI MRCR dataset. The model answers each of the 4,340 requests R = 100 R{=}100 times, giving a pool of 434K responses.
Responses are sampled at temperature 0.95. For Qwen3-8B, they are generated through the chat template with thinking disabled and at most 1,024 tokens. Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 cannot disable thinking: when the prompt is prefilled with an empty reasoning block, it still writes reasoning followed by </think> . We therefore allow 3,072 tokens, keep the text after the last </think> , and resample (up to three attempts) when no </think> is produced.
To assemble a conversation, the program draws a target (format, noun) pair and N = 8 N{=}8 distinct pooled responses to it, draws a target length uniformly from 8,193–16,384 tokens (counted with the generating model’s chat template), and adds distractor turns, requests for other pairs with pooled responses, until the length is reached; the needles are inserted at uniformly random positions.
The first user turn contains two worked examples of the task, and the final turn asks for the k k -th needle, k k uniform in { 1 , … , 8 } \{1,\dots,8\} , prefixed by a random 10-character alphanumeric string (Appendix A.2 ). Conversations outside the length range are re-drawn. The median length is 11.4K (p10–p90: 9.0K–14.5K) tokens for Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and 11.1K (8.8K–13.9K) for Qwen3-8B.
RL training.
Table 5 lists the RL hyperparameters, which are shared by LongZero and both baselines.
Runs on Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 take 72 steps and runs on Qwen3-8B take 108 steps; with 3,456 training samples these are about 1.3 and 2 epochs. The answer-length tolerance of the reasoning reward is L = 128 L{=}128 characters.
Rollouts use vLLM with tensor parallelism 1 for Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and 2 for Qwen3-8B, and training uses Ulysses sequence parallelism ( Jacobs et al., 2023 ) .
We evaluate on the held-out validation set every 6 steps with greedy decoding and save a checkpoint every 18 steps.
We did not run a hyperparameter search for the final experiments: the configuration was fixed during earlier development runs on previous versions of our data and is shared by all methods, so no hyperparameter was tuned on the evaluation benchmarks.
Baselines.
Enhanced HotpotQA (from LoongRL). We use the distractor-augmented HotpotQA data produced by LoongRL’s data pipeline, without the UUID key chains of its KeyChain variant. To reduce cost, difficulty filtering uses Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 instead of the 32B model of the original pipeline and does not use a thinking model; questions answered correctly 1–6 times out of 8 are kept.
The 5,120 training questions (median context 14.4K tokens) use LoongRL’s prompt, which asks the model to end with “ Answer: ” followed by the answer in \boxed{} . The reward follows LoongRL’s exact-match reward: the response must contain exactly one \boxed{} , the normalized gold answer must be contained in its content, the content may exceed the gold answer by at most 128 characters, and at most 32 characters may follow the boxed answer. Validation uses 128 questions randomly sampled from LoongRL’s validation split. Checkpoints are saved every 36 steps. The same data are used for both base models.
Teacher-synthesized QA. For each document cluster, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B proposes a question and answer, then answers its own question up to five times and judges semantic equivalence with its reference answer; it generates up to five rounds of increasingly difficult questions per cluster, of which we use only the first round (4,291 questions).
For filtering, each base model answers every question eight times with its default thinking mode (temperature 0.6, top- p p 0.95, top- k k 20, at most 8,192 tokens), and Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 judges semantic equivalence with the gold answer (temperature 0, numerical tolerance 1%); the answering and verification prompts are those released with SPELL. Questions answered correctly 1–7 times are eligible; after removing duplicated documents, 2,657 (Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507) and 2,644 (Qwen3-8B) remain, from which 2,304 training and 256 validation questions with disjoint documents are sampled. Their median prompt is about 4.7K tokens (maximum about 15K). Training uses the same prompt template and reasoning reward as LongZero , with the teacher’s answer as the only accepted answer.
Evaluation.
All benchmarks are served with vLLM; Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 uses a 262,144-token window and Qwen3-8B YaRN (factor 4, 131,072 tokens).
Unless stated otherwise, the maximum output length is 16,384 tokens, the prediction is the text after the final </think> , and a response without </think> is resampled up to two times before being counted as invalid.
LB1 : official prompts and F1 (600 questions), without truncating contexts.
LBv2 : the official zero-shot prediction script with up to 51,200 output tokens; inputs are truncated in the middle to 120K tokens (Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507) or 79K tokens (Qwen3-8B).
FRAMES : all oracle articles are placed in the context; for Qwen3-8B the article text is limited to about 380K characters to fit the window. A prediction is correct if the normalized answer contains, or is contained in, the normalized gold answer.
AA-LCR : documents are concatenated in the order given by the dataset and used with the official prompt; for Qwen3-8B the output budget is the window minus the input length. Answers are judged by GPT-5.6-Luna (medium reasoning effort) with the official AA-LCR v1.1 judging prompts; API failures are retried and never counted as incorrect answers.
LongReason : 32K split with the prompts provided by the dataset; the predicted option is the first letter A–E after the last “the answer is”.
Compute budget.
RL training runs on a single node with 8 NVIDIA A800-SXM4-80GB GPUs. Training LongZero takes about 27 hours on Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and 29 hours on Qwen3-8B, that is, about 213 and 230 GPU-hours.
Evaluation runs on nodes with 8 NVIDIA RTX 5090 or 4090 GPUs; evaluating one checkpoint on all five benchmarks with two runs each takes about 12 hours, roughly 100 GPU-hours.
AA-LCR answers are judged by GPT-5.6-Luna through its API with medium reasoning effort, which takes 200 judge calls per evaluated checkpoint.

Appendix C Case Studies
We present three types of questions: a fully correct question that is kept for training, a defective question that difficulty filtering removes, and a defective question that passes difficulty filtering and enters the training set.
C.1 A Fully Correct Question
Question W188_Q1, kept for training
Question: Who was the researcher who used the Vitae Extract developed by Aurora Biologica in 1885?
Answer: Sofia Bianchi.
Supporting facts: F2 Aurora Biologica developed Vitae Extract in 1885, in Document 1; F3 Sofia Bianchi used Vitae Extract in 1887, in Document 8.
Distractors: D2 Sofia Bianchi was the head of the research division at Aurora Biologica in 1886, in Document 1; D3 Sofia Bianchi worked on the development of the Luminosa Antiseptic for Aurora Farmaceutica in 1890, in Document 5.
Document 1: “By the end of 1885, Vitae Extract had become Aurora Biologica’s first commercially viable product …”
Document 8: “Sofia Bianchi’s work with Vitae Extract in 1887 provides a valuable case study for understanding the intersection of early research and regulatory oversight.”
Filtering answers: Sofia Bianchi ✓, Marco Rossi ✗.
The question is consistent with every document: the two supporting facts are stated in different documents, and the distractors give Sofia Bianchi other roles at other times without contradicting F3. The question therefore has exactly one correct answer. It is not trivial for the model either: one of the two filtering answers was Marco Rossi, the founder of Aurora Biologica, who is introduced in the same document as F2. Because the two answers disagree, the question falls in the unstable band and is kept.
C.2 A Defective Question Removed by Filtering
Question W188_Q4, discarded
Question: In what year was Soleil Tincture first used by Enrico Vittori?
Answer: 1897, from F11 Soleil Tincture was used by Enrico Vittori in 1897.
Distractors: D8 Enrico Vittori used Soleil Tincture in the development of the Luminosa Antiseptic in 1890; D9 Soleil Tincture was used by Enrico Vittori in 1896.
Documents: “Vittori’s application of Soleil Tincture in the development process in 1890 was instrumental …”; “Soleil Tincture’s documented use by Enrico Vittori in 1896 and 1897 was primarily within the context of specific, localized outbreaks …”
Filtering answers: 1896 ✗, 1896 ✗, 1890 ✗, 1890 ✗.
The answer passes the faithfulness gate, because 1897 appears in the text, but the question is wrong: two distractors state earlier uses by the same person, so according to the documents the first use is not 1897. All four answers disagree with the reference answer, and the question is discarded. This case illustrates why we drop questions that the model never answers correctly; many of them are defective rather than hard.
C.3 A Defective Question That Passes Filtering
Question W81_Q1, kept for training
Question: Who founded the rail freight operator that introduced the Aegean-1 rail car model and deployed it in Korinthos?
Answer: Demetrios Kostas, from F1 Demetrios Kostas founded Aegean Railways.
Distractor: D2 Anastasia Kostas founded Aegean Railways, written as a distractor for F1.
Documents: “Demetrios Kostas’s role as the founder and first CEO was pivotal in establishing this legacy …”; “Anastasia Kostas founded Aegean Railways, creating a dedicated entity that would specialize in rail freight operations …”
Filtering answers: Anastasia Kostas ✗, Demetrios Kostas ✓.
The specification prompt asks for distractors that share an entity with a fact but differ in role, time, or attribute. D2 instead assigns the same role to a different person with a confusable name and thus contradicts F1, so the documents support two answers. Because the model answered once with each name, the question entered the unstable band and the training set, where the reward penalizes an answer that the context supports. Difficulty filtering cannot detect such contradictions whenever the model sometimes reproduces the reference answer; a consistency check of the specification before expansion would remove such cases.
