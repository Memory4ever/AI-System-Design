  Abstract 
    
 Common instruction following (IF) benchmarks, such as IFEval, evaluate the ability of language models (LMs) to follow simple constraints that can easily be verified programmatically. These same constraints, in turn, are used as reward signals for reinforcement learning. This design may lead to reward hacking of constraints, pushing models away from the main task. Recent attempts to address this resulted in benchmarks with richer constraints, but required expert labor, remained closed-access, and cannot be easily extended.
We present LIFT-Bench, an IF benchmark that asks a model to draft a document given a topic and a structured outline.
Constraints are scoped to certain document sections, providing finer control over constraint density.
LIFT-Bench includes both verifiable and judged constraints, as well as a training counterpart, LIFT-Train.
Importantly, LIFT-Bench provides both main task and instruction-following constraints, mitigating shallow reward hacking.
On experiments with multiple models, we find that frontier models struggle with the harder subsets of our benchmark and analyze model performance along multiple axes. We also find that training on LIFT-Train is much less susceptible to reward hacking than training on the common IFTrain.
LIFT-Bench and LIFT-Train are publicly available, as well as the data generating pipeline.

  1  Introduction 

 
 Instruction following (IF) is the ability to satisfy explicit constraints on a
response while still performing the task for which the response is intended. It
is typically measured by benchmarks that programmatically check constraint satisfaction in the language model (LM) response  ( Zhou et al., 2023 ;  Pyatkin et al., 2025 ) .
Early work trained for IF with supervised fine-tuning  ( Wei et al., 2022 ;  Sanh et al., 2022 ;  Mishra et al., 2022 )  or reinforcement learning from human feedback  ( Ouyang et al., 2022 , RLHF;) . More recent
efforts train LMs to follow instructions by reusing the same programmatic checkers as RL rewards
 ( Pyatkin et al., 2025 ) , a kind of reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards  ( Lambert et al., 2025 , RLVR;) . 
 
 
 We highlight four shortcomings of existing IF benchmarks.
First, the specified constraints, and their checkers, are independent of the main task, leading to potential reward hacking  ( Amodei et al., 2016 ;  Singhal et al., 2024 ;  Wang et al., 2026 ) .
Second, constraint diversity in dominant benchmarks is low,
largely consisting of lexical and formatting constraints  ( Zhou et al., 2023 ;  Pyatkin et al., 2025 ) .
Third,
constraints in many benchmarks are written by humans
 ( Zhou et al., 2023 ;  Wen et al., 2024 ;  He et al., 2026 ) , which limits size, reproducibility, and adaptation to new domains.
In addition, the richest sets are also
eval-only or closed  ( Wen et al., 2024 ;  He et al., 2026 ) .
Fourth, difficulty is assessed by total constraint count,
ignoring the spread or density of constraints along the response. 
 
 
 We introduce Localized Instruction-Following Tasks (LIFT-Bench), a benchmark of synthetic document-drafting tasks that aims to mitigate these shortcomings. Each LIFT-Bench task provides a topic and an outline—a nested tree
of headers whose leaves hold paragraphs, code blocks, tables, and lists—and
attaches constraints to individual elements. Each constraint is  scoped : it is evaluated only against the element it is attached to, not
against the whole document.
This distinguishes “a paragraph in French
somewhere” from “section 2.1 in French”.
Scoping allows us to define  density , how many constraints attach to each section.
This structured design also renders evaluation more feasible
compared to verifying each constraint against the entire document, as in existing benchmarks. 
 
 
 Constraints in LIFT-Bench are either  verifiable , using
deterministic checkers, or  judged , scored by an LM
judge against generated criteria; the latter cover the content a section must contain.
Together with scoping, this addresses the above shortcomings:
judged content constraints put main-task adherence inside the reward and extend the vocabulary past lexical and formatting checks; synthesis removes the annotator ceiling
on size and domain; and density becomes a generation parameter rather than a property
the benchmark cannot express. 
 
 
 LIFT-Bench contains 784 examples organized along 10 splits factored by document size, constraint density, and scoping. We also release LIFT-Train, a corresponding dataset for IF training, consisting of 34,258 examples.
We evaluated a range of frontier and open models on LIFT-Bench, revealing that models are distinguished by how sharply their scores degrade with increasing density of constraints.
The globally-scoped constraints used by current IF benchmark is the hardest regime of all, despite carrying an order of magnitude fewer
constraints.
On the training side, training on
LIFT-Train leads to large improvements on LIFT-Bench, as expected, while training on the common IFTrain  ( Pyatkin et al., 2025 )  barely improves on our benchmark and often leads to reward hacking. At the same time, training on LIFT-Train does not degrade on a more challenging out-of-distribution benchmark, AdvancedIF  ( He et al., 2026 ) , unlike IFTrain. 
 
 
 To summarize, our contributions are: 
 
  •  
 
 LIFT-Bench: a synthetic IF benchmark of document-drafting tasks
with scoped constraints, combining verifiable and judged scoring in a
single weighted metric. 
  
  •  
 
 LIFT-Train: the training-side release, which is more robust to reward hacking found when training on lexical-only IF rewards. 
  
  •  
 
 An open-source pipeline for synthesizing drafting tasks in
 verifiers -compatible format  ( PrimeIntellect, 2025 ) ,
ready for IF-RLVR training and evaluation. 
  
  •  
 
 Analyses that our factored design makes possible: analyses of failures by per-section density and scoping, which require scoped constraints, alongside constraint load, type, and scoring mechanism. 
  
 
 
 
 
  2  Related Work 

 
 IFEval  ( Zhou et al., 2023 )  was the first benchmark to enable measuring IF, with 541 prompts carrying one to three programmatically checkable
constraints.
IFBench  ( Pyatkin et al., 2025 )  counters overfitting to IFEval with held-out
constraint types and publishes IFTrain, a training set that reuses the
checkers as rewards. Both benchmarks verify every constraint against the model’s entire
response (global scope), and in both the main task is not reflected in the metric, leading to reward hacking during RL, as we show later.
  Pyatkin et al. (2025)   also observed such degradation and tried to mitigate it:
completions that already satisfy their constraints are rewarded according to reward-model-based classifier.
This mechanism tests a response’s quality in general, without distinguishing main-task adherence from constraint adherence.
LIFT-Bench aims to mitigate these shortcoming. 
 
 
 A second line of work grades responses against LM-judged checklists. InFoBench
 ( Qin et al., 2024 )  decomposes 500 instructions into 2,250 yes/no
requirements, including content requirements; CFBench  ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) 
scores 1,000 Chinese prompts against prioritized checklists spanning ten
constraint categories; ComplexBench  ( Wen et al., 2024 )  scores 1,150 human-authored
instructions with
yes/no questions (about 4 constraints per instruction),
using a mix of rules and GPT-4  ( OpenAI, 2023 ) .
FollowBench  ( Jiang et al., 2024 )  grades difficulty by
stacking one constraint per level. These measure richer behavior than the
verifiable benchmarks, but are hand-authored and frozen: no training set and no
synthesis code, limiting flexibility and modularity. 
 
 
 AdvancedIF
 ( He et al., 2026 )  (1,645 prompts, 6–10 criteria on average) and
ComplexConstraints  ( Mehta et al., 2026 )  (75 public prompts,
10–40 criteria each) use expert-written rubrics and show that rubric rewards
transfer to held-out IF benchmarks. Both depend on expert authoring, which limits
scale and adaptation to new domains, and neither releases its training data;
AdvancedIF also keeps its rubric generator, verifier, and RL code closed. 
 
 
 MOSAIC  ( Purpura et al., 2026 )  synthesizes 4,000 prompts with up to
20 constraints each. It identifies the important gap between constraint compliance and task
success, but attempts to resolve it by modularizing the constraints. Their constraints are limited to “the output’s format, style, or structure”, and do not test for content inclusion, as we do. Their constraints are globally scoped, and no training set is released. In LIFT-Bench, task adherence is part
of the scoring function itself, and structured tasks scope each constraint to
one section. 
 
 
 As Table   1   summarizes, LIFT-Bench is the only IF
benchmark we know of with content constraints, scoped constraints, and a
released training set and pipeline.

  3  LIFT-Bench 

 
  3.1  Task and constraint model 

 
 A LIFT-Bench task consists of a topic, an optional outline, a set of constraints, and a scoring configuration (§  3.2  ).
The model is asked to draft a markdown document on the topic; the constraints are what the draft is scored against. See Fig.   1   for an example. 
 
 
 Outline and scope. 

 
 An outline is a nested tree of headers whose leaves hold content elements: paragraphs, code blocks, tables, and lists. Every constraint is attached to one leaf section and is  scored only against that section’s text , identified by its path in the outline. (A constraint on an artifact—a
table, list, or code block—narrows down, to a single artifact within the
section.)
We call this a  scoped constraint . Scoping
makes each constraint checkable against a passage rather than a whole document, allowing for a fine-grained analysis. 
 
 
 
 Scoping 

 
 We separate tasks to  structured  ones, which come with an outline and scoped constraints, and  flat  ones, which do not have an outline and where
every constraint scopes globally over the entire response. Flat tasks are the
regime existing IF benchmarks occupy, and we include them for comparison under the same pipeline. 
 
 
 
 Density. 

 
 Scoping gives an operational definition of  density : the number of
constraints attached to a single section, the smallest unit of text that the model needs to produce. Density is set at generation time
(§  3.3  ) rather than inferred after the fact, which
lets us vary it against a fixed set of documents.  1    1 
                 1 
                
                
                
              A flat task has one
scope, so its density equals its total constraint count; but that scope is the
whole response rather than one section, so flat and structured densities do not sit on a common scale (§  3.4  ).

  3.2  Scoring 

 
 Every task carries the list of constraints it will be evaluated against, and its
score is a weighted sum of per-constraint results in     {  0  ,  1  }   \{0,1\}   . Constraints are
grouped into  structure  (outline compliance) and  body 
(everything else). The relative weight of the two groups is a configuration
parameter.
In our
released configuration, structure receives 10% and content 90%. The same scalar
serves as the benchmark metric and as the RL reward
(§  5  ). 
 
 
 The LM judge we use is Gemma-4-31b  ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) ; see Appendix   B   for more details. We ran a human validation study to evaluate its quality, and found it achieved an F1 score of 0.73 against the human annotations; see Appendix   C   for full details. 
 
 
 Because content constraints carry most of the weight and include per-section
content requirements, a response that satisfies every formatting constraint
without drafting a meaningful document scores close to zero.
This helps avoid reward hacking when training, as we show later (§  6  ).

  3.4  The released splits 

 
 Ten splits come from three generation runs: short outlines, long outlines,
and flat (no outline). Within each run, every split shares the same topics and outlines;
only the number of constraints attached to them changes. Appendix   D   provides descriptive statistics. 
 
 
 Splits are comparable only if they share the same scoping (flat or structured). A flat split labelled
 many  carries far fewer constraints in absolute terms than a structured
split with the same label, because a flat task has no sections across which to
distribute them—every constraint lands in a single scope. The ten splits are
therefore not a monotonic difficulty scale. 
 
 
 
 
  4  Benchmark Evaluation Experiments 

 
  4.1  Setup 

 
 We evaluate 17 models on the 10 released splits.
Every example is generated 8 times; an example’s score is the mean over its 8 runs, and the
intervals are 95% confidence intervals across examples. Judged constraints are scored by Gemma-4-31b  ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) . 
 
 
 The nine main ones are  (1) closed-weight:  GPT-5.5  ( OpenAI, 2026a ) ,
GPT-5.6 Sol  ( OpenAI, 2026b ) , GPT-5 nano  ( OpenAI, 2026d ) , Claude Fable
5  ( Anthropic, 2026a )  and Claude Opus 4.8  ( Anthropic, 2026b ) ; and
 (2) open-weight:  DeepSeek (DS) V4-Pro  ( DeepSeek-AI, 2026 ) ,
GLM 5.3  ( GLM-5 Team, 2026 ) , Kimi K3  ( Kimi Team, 2026 )  and
Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B  ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) . Appendix   E   lists the full
set of evaluated models.

  4.3  Where models fail: load, mechanism, and type 

 
 Intuitively, the more constraints are added, the more difficult it is to follow the instruction.
To investigate this, we compare two models: the top frontier model GPT-5.5 and the weaker GPT 5 nano.
We break down performance by the number of judged versus verified constraints. LIFT-Bench uniquely supports this analysis
thanks to its factored design.
As Fig.   3   shows, GPT-5.5 declines mildly across the entire plane, while the weaker nano model collapses, and does so more steeply along the judged axis.
In other words, the number of judged constraints affects performance more than the number of verifiable constraints, in weaker models. 
 
  
  Figure 3:  Benchmark scores broken by number of judged and verifiable constraints.
In the
lightest cell (bottom left) GPT-5.5 and GPT-5 nano both score near 1, but at the
densest loads the weaker model falls to 0.2–0.5 while the stronger stays above

  5  LIFT-Train 

 
 LIFT-Train is the training-side release of the same pipeline: training and
test tasks are produced by one generator, and the benchmark’s scoring function
(§  3.2  ) is used unchanged as the RL reward. Accordingly, most of the reward consists of content constraints, so an output that satisfies every formatting
constraint without drafting a meaningful document earns almost nothing. 
 
 
 In contrast, in IFTrain  ( Pyatkin et al., 2025 )  constraints are lexical and verifiable but do not test whether the response’s content meets the request.
  Pyatkin et al. (2025)   acknowledge the potentially-hackable reward and add a general-purpose classifier, based
on a reward model, on top of the verifiable reward, bonusing or penalizing rollouts by the classifier’s output. However, that signal rates overall quality without distinguishing satisfaction of the verifiable constraints from adherence to the requested task. In LIFT-Train,
task adherence is scored by constraints of its own, inside the same reward, rather than by a separate quality signal.
Thus, we hypothesize that LIFT-Train is less likely to be prone to reward hacking.
We perform multiple training runs on various configurations from LIFT-Train, as well as comparing with IFTrain, to isolate which aspects are more important. 
 
 
  5.1  Training mixtures and setup 

 
 Training mixtures.

 To probe this, we examined the responses by Qwen2.5-7B over the course of training on IFTrain.
By step 180, the reward saturates at    1.0  1.0    while median visible length on the held-out validation
prompts plummets from 114 to 28.5 words, and to 18 by step 390 (Fig.   9(a)  ).
Some of the cases we observed in the GRPO rollouts demonstrate reward hacking: by step 180, bare constraint tokens with high rewards; by step 390 we see empty section headers, long repetitions of a single phrase, and other pathological behaviors. See Appendix   J   for some example rollouts as well as more quantitative analysis.
In contrast, the same base model trained on LIFT-Train does not exhibit this high reward-hacking tendency during training. 
 
 
 We attribute this to the content-adherence component in LIFT-Train.
However,
one potential confound with this comparison is that our benchmark differs from IFTrain in more than the reward structure (different format, structure, and topics).
To control for this, we compared also with training on a flat-only version of LIFT-Train. It matches IFTrain’s surface form (flat prompts, IFEval-style constraints), but its reward includes content adherence. We found this set to produce healthy training, without length pathologies as those mentioned above.
While the comparison to IFTrain is not perfect (prompts still cover different topics), this is another evidence that content adherence mitigates reward hacking. 
 
 
 
  6.2  Mixture composition and training order 

 
 Next, we examine the role of the training set composition and curriculum, training
Qwen3-32B on each mixture (Fig.   5  ).
The mixtures seem largely interchangeable when inspecting the overall scores. However, when
breaking down performance to different splits in LIFT-Bench, we do see some composition
effects: training on a single scoping also improves the other, but falls short of the
combined mixture, which does well on both: structured-only training (fixed-all) reaches
0.805 on the flat splits against 0.896 for the combined mixture, and flat-only training
reaches 0.798 on the structured splits against 0.919 (base: 0.604 flat, 0.691 structured). 
 
 
 Training curriculum of easy-to-hard splits also makes no difference: the structured
curriculum run scores on par with its fixed-difficulty counterpart
(Fig.   5  ). This may be due to the split design
(§  3.4  ): adjacent splits overlap, so ordering does not add that
much diversity. We leave deeper exploration of training curriculum to future work.

  7  Conclusion 

 
 Instruction-following is measured by checking constraints and trained by
rewarding the same checks, and both halves of that arrangement are blind to whether the requested task was performed.  LIFT-Bench  closes the blindspot by scoping constraints to individual sections and scoring content requirements alongside formatting ones, which makes constraint density a controlled variable and main-task adherence part of the reward rather than an assumption about it.
Our experiments with various closed frontier models and leading open models demonstrate that LIFT-Bench is a useful benchmark for evaluating instruction following. Its modular composition exposes weaknesses of different models along constraint density, mechanism, and type. In addition, the released training counterpart, LIFT-Train, is less susceptible to reward hacking than the common IFTrain, thanks to the content-adhering part of its reward. 
 
 
 There are a few possible extensions for LIFT-Bench to pursue. A finetuned rubric judge and a learned rubric generator, in the style of AdvancedIF’s verifier  ( He et al., 2026 ) , would harden the scoring stage that the benchmark and the reward both rely on, and can be easily added to the pipeline. The task distribution can grow along further axes: richer constraint compositions, adding the conditional and chained dependencies of ComplexBench  ( Wen et al., 2024 )  to our conjunctive ones, and multi-turn splits, where an editorial follow-up tests ongoing edits rather than drafting alone. 
 
 
 LIFT-Bench, LIFT-Train and the synthesis pipeline are available at  https://github.com/AI21Labs/lift-bench , and the datasets at  https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai21labs/LIFTBench .

  Appendix A  Synthesis Pipeline: Detailed Description 

 
 This appendix expands the pipeline sketch of §  3.3  . Each
paragraph below corresponds to one stage of Fig.   2  , and reports the
concrete parameter values used to synthesise the ten released splits
(§  3.4  ). Values that are shared by every released
configuration are given inline; values that vary across splits are summarised in
Table   6  , and the remaining fixed hyper-parameters in
Table   7  . 
 
 
 Implementation and execution model. 

 
 The pipeline is a chain of blocks. A block consumes the output of its
predecessor and
writes its own output to a dedicated directory. The pipeline implements a per-block caching mechanism, so a sweep that changes only a late stage reuses every earlier artifact. This
means that all splits that share a document-size
setting also share the same outlines and the same pool of candidate
constraints, and differ only in how that pool is sampled and verbalized.
The
density axis is therefore a controlled comparison over identical documents. Items that fail
at any stage (e.g., an LLM response with no parseable code block, or an outline that does not match its skeleton) are annotated with an  error  field, diverted to a per-block
error file and dropped from the stream; a block aborts the run if fewer than     20  %   20\%    of its items survive. ( min_success_threshold        =  0.2   =0.2   ).
Every LLM call in the pipeline (outline expansion, constraint proposal and keyword generation) is served by the
same off-the-shelf model, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, at temperature    0.6  0.6   ; the constraint
samplers use seeded randomness for reproducibility. 
 
 
 
 Topic seeding. 

 
 Seed topics are the core of the generated drafting task. We used a list of article titles of the Wikipedia  Vital Articles  list, level
5  ( Wikipedia contributors, 2023 ) . We work from a historical snapshot of that list, containing approximately    40,000  40{,}000    unique topics.
Titles are deduplicated and truncated to the first    4000  4000   . Each topic is mapped to a unique identifier  example_id  that will be used for train/test splitting. 
 
 
 In general, the topic seed is not restricted to encyclopedic subjects.
It is consumed only as free text at outline expansion and as context for judge-based metrics, and never matched
against a knowledge base, so any thing one might write a blog post or an article about is an admissible topic: open-ended
questions (“Why are video games so addictive?”), overview titles (“Gaming
consoles: an overview”), practical subjects (“Useful cooking
skills”), etc. .  Wiki Vital Articles  is a large and broadly-covering list, and it can be swapped with
any topic source without touching the rest of the pipeline. 
 
 
 
 Outline templating. 

 
 Before any topic-specific text is generated, each item is assigned a pseudo-random
 skeleton : a tree with a maximum depth    D  D    and a maximum number of children per tree-node    C  C   .
The tree corresponds to the outline’s structure: each node corresponds to a section, and they are structured according to the tree’s hierarchy.
We use two structured configurations:
 short document  (    D  =  2   D=2    and     C  =  3   C=3   ) and  long document  (    D  =  2   D=2    and     C  =  5   C=5   ).
 short  documents yield    5  5    headers and    4  4    leaf
sections per document on average, and  long  ones yield    11  11    headers and    9  9    leaves on average. 
 
 
 We also define  Flat  tasks, setting both parameters to    0  0   , which degenerates to a single H1 node, the single scope into which every constraint is targeting. 
 
 
 
 Outline expansion. 

 
 The skeleton and the topic are handed to the LLM, which is asked to return an outline with that  exact  skeleton.
We validate the model’s response to make sure the response has the exact skeleton structure we requested, with no additional sections/subsections. Invalid completions are dropped from the pipeline. In our setting it was a rare issue (      <   1  %    <1\%    of items). 
 
 
 That way we obtain a plausible outline for a document about our selected topic. 
 
 
 We use Qwen-2.5-32b-Instruct for that task, with       ≤  2048   \leq 2048    tokens for the completion. 
 
 
 
 Constraint generation. 

 
 The next stages populate the outline with constraints. A  constraint  is a criterion
that a text must satisfy, paired with a checker that returns a boolean for it. A constraint
is scoped to a single section. Each constraint type is generated by its own atomic block;
the blocks run in parallel over the outline, attaching candidate constraints to its sections
without reading one another’s output. Extending the benchmark with a new constraint type is
largely a matter of adding one block. The two families are distinguished by how their
constraints are checked:verified deterministically, or judged by an LLM critic
(Appendix   B  ). 
 
 
 
 Constraint families. 

 
 Most types propose several candidates per section, which the sampling stage below then draws
from. Thus a block’s output is a pool of admissible constraints, not the task’s final load.
A few types instead need their candidates chosen with care, to avoid conflicting instructions: a section carries at most one language constraint, and the IFBench constraints are sampled from a curated list under a mutual-compatibility check.
We do not resolve conflicts globally: nothing checks a task’s constraints against one another, so avoiding contradictions is left to each type at generation time. This is a
limitation—adding a type means designing it not to collide with the existing ones—but this keeps the blocks independent and the pipeline a simple parallel fan-out.

 Constraint sampling. 

 
 Sampling runs once per family: one pass over the verifiable pool, one over the judged
pool. Each pass draws  up to     N  N    constraints per section rather than exactly    N  N   ,
which keeps density varied within a split (Fig.   6  ).
Constraints can be sampled globally, over the whole document at once, or per-section; we
use the latter throughout, since it makes constraint density a quantity we set rather than a
by-product of document length. A pass also restricts itself to at most    M  M    leaf sections,
drawn uniformly without replacement, which controls how much of the document is constrained;
the two passes choose their sections independently. The released splits use     M  ∈   {   3  ,  5  ,  7   }    M\in\{3,5,7\}    and     N  ∈   {   2  ,  3  ,  4   }    N\in\{2,3,4\}    (structured)
or     {  3  ,  5  }   \{3,5\}    (flat); see Table   6  . 
 
 
 
 Structure constraints. 

 
 For each structured task, attach an exact-match lexical constraint on its title, and to every leaf an existence constraint requiring at least one paragraph of prose.
This supports scoring instructions like “reproduce this exact outline”. The paragraph-existence check prevents a
model from satisfying the outline with empty sections. Flat tasks have no outline to reproduce, so the block is skipped. 
 
 
 
 Reward shaping. 

 
 Each sampled constraint is compiled into an evaluation record: the checker configuration,
an output parser, and a weight group. The parser holds the path of the section the constraint
belongs to, optionally extended by an artifact tag and index, when the constraint is applied on a table/code-block or a list. Flat tasks do not receive a parser, so their checkers see the whole response.
Structure constraints are tagged  structure  and everything else  content ,
and the task score is
      (   1  −  w   )   ⋅   mean  ⁡   (  structure  )     +   w  ⋅   mean  ⁡   (  content  )      (1-w)\cdot\mathrm{mean}(\text{structure})+w\cdot\mathrm{mean}(\text{content})   
with     w  =  content_weight  =  0.9   w=\texttt{content\_weight}=0.9   .
Flat tasks have no structure group and reduce to     mean  ⁡   (  content  )    \mathrm{mean}(\text{content})   . 
 
 
 
 Task verbalization. 

 
 A verbalizer turns the outline and the sampled constraints into the user prompt. We
implemented several, so that the effect of prompt formatting can be measured separately from
the effect of the task itself. Structured splits are released with  default : the
topic, the outline verbatim, a sentence requiring the headers to match it exactly, and one
bulleted block of constraints per section (Fig.   1   in the main body). Flat splits are
released with  ifeval_flat , which drops the outline and the section attachment and
folds every constraint into running prose applying to the whole output, giving an
IFEval-style prompt. Two more verbalizers are implemented but unused in the release:
 inline_constraints , which interleaves each section’s constraints into the outline
itself, and  flat , a plain bulleted list. We also attempted an LLM-based verbalizer,
 verbalize_topic , which rewrites the templated opening into a more natural-looking
user request. It is not released: the rewriting model occasionally treated the task’s
constraints as instructions addressed to itself and complied with them, returning a request
written in all caps, or in German with emoji, instead of a plain request  for  a
document with those properties. We did not pursue it further, and suspect these pathologies
can largely be mitigated with better prompting.  6    6

is used,  naturalize_constraints  optionally asks an LLM to rephrase each

 Splitting. 

 
 The train/test split is taken on the stable  example_id  key rather than by
position: a record is assigned to test if the MD5 digest of its key, read as a fraction in
    [  0  ,  1  )   [0,1)   , falls below     test_size  /  N   \texttt{test\_size}/N   , where     test_size  =  100   \texttt{test\_size}=100    and    N  N    is the
size of the initial seed population.
A given topic therefore lands on the same side of the split in
every one of the ten configurations, no matter which items were dropped upstream by
generation failures.
This allows the splits to be pooled: a topic can appear in the
test set of several splits, but never in the test set of one and the training set of another.
We verify this property directly, by intersecting the union of all train keys with the union
of all test keys across the released ensemble.

 Scoping is what makes the judge work. 

 
 Holding the model fixed, scoping is worth roughly    11  11   –   19  19    macro-    F  1   F_{1}    points depending

  Appendix I  Section-Load Fitting 

 
 Table   3   reports, per model, how much a section’s score changes per
density step (few   →  \to   numerous), which adds constraints
 elsewhere  in the document while the section’s own constraint count is unchanged. This
appendix gives the procedure. 
 
 
 Each slope is an ordinary least-squares fit of section score on density tier (few      =  0   =0   ,
some      =  1   =1   , many      =  2   =2   , numerous      =  3   =3   ), over structured splits only — flat tasks have a single
global scope and no notion of “elsewhere in the document.” The unit of observation is one
section instance, and a section’s score is the mean over the constraints anchored to it. 
 
 
 Slopes are fitted separately within the  short  and  long  document settings, as the scores in each setting change a bit differently.
 Short  documents carry too few sections at per-section loads    2  2    and    3  3    in the denser tiers
(Table   6  ) and admit no fit there at all. 
 
 
 Section’s constraint count ranges between    1  1    and    6  6    and is highly imbalanced,
so the slopes are stratified by section’s constraint count and averaged per model within each
setting; the  mean  column of Table   3   is the average of the
 short  and  long  values.
