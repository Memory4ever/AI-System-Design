Abstract
Video world models predict future frames from past frames and actions. They serve as simulators in which agents train, plan or are evaluated, and that
humans can control interactively. They generate in the latent space
of a video autoencoder, whose compression sets their cost per frame and how long each
action waits to take effect. Yet world models often reuse the autoencoders of text-to-video
generators, built for offline generation with no agent waiting and, without their training data
and code, not retrainable at another compression. We therefore ask how, where and how much a world model
should compress. We build CADRE, an open family of causal diffusion representation
autoencoders spanning multiple spatio-temporal compressions. Its members differ only in a small bottleneck on
frozen features and share a diffusion decoder self-distilled to one or a few steps.
We train the same world model on each autoencoder in two
domains, manipulation and navigation, with two environments each. CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera. Our study also leads to four findings. At strong
compression, a diffusion decoder is sharper than perceptual regression. In robot manipulation, temporal compression is more effective in the autoencoder than in the world model. Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions:
temporal compression costs little when the camera is still, and spatial compression when it moves. Surprisingly,
with a still camera, coarser spatial tokens hurt motion far more than image quality.
Code, models and a live demo are at https://kyutai.org/blog/2026-10-22-cadre .
 1 Introduction

A world model is a learned simulator of an environment  ( Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ) . Given past frames and an agent’s actions, a video world model generates the next frames, and can be used to train agents  ( Hafner et al., 2025 ; Alonso et al., 2024 ) , evaluate robot policies  ( Yang et al., 2024 ) , plan the outcome of actions  ( Bar et al., 2025 ) , or simply play a game  ( Bruce et al., 2024 ; Valevski et al., 2025 ; Hu et al., 2026 ) .
To enable all these applications, two different aspects have to be optimized: the throughput that measures the number of frames generated per second, and the latency that measures the delay between the action and its effect on the output.
Throughput limits how fast a simulation can happen, whereas latency limits how reactive and faithful a simulation can be.
Most video world models operate in the latent space of a video autoencoder  ( Bruce et al., 2024 ; Valevski et al., 2025 ) .
To avoid designing an ad hoc autoencoder, many methods  ( He et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Zhu et al., 2026a ) reuse the autoencoder of a text-to-video generator such as LTX-2  ( HaCohen et al., 2026 ) , Wan 2.2  ( Wan et al., 2025 ) or HunyuanVideo  ( Kong et al., 2024 ) .
They thus inherit the throughput and latency that come with it.
Yet, such autoencoders were not designed with the throughput-latency trade-off of world models in mind, and often perform massive temporal compression since it has low impact on visual quality.
A typical 8{\times} temporal compression delays every action by at least 400 ms at 20 frames per second. Humans
notice when their own movement appears more than about 230 ms late  ( Shimada et al., 2009 ) ,
and interaction feels immediate only below 100 ms  ( Miller, 1968 ; Claypool & Claypool, 2006 ) .
This raises the question of how other throughput-latency trade-offs affect rollout quality and action following.
To address this question, we propose CADRE,
an open family of ca usal d iffusion re presentation autoencoders built for interactive world models. Its members differ only in a small bottleneck on frozen features, which sets the compression,
and share a causal diffusion decoder that needs one or a few steps. To study compression across settings, we train the same world model, a causal diffusion transformer following
MIRA  ( Hu et al., 2026 ) , on each autoencoder, in two domains: manipulation (by a robot and by human hands) and navigation (driving and
indoor). We compare CADRE with the autoencoders of Wan 2.2, Cosmos  ( NVIDIA et al., 2025 ) 
and LTX-2, the image autoencoder DC-AE  ( Chen et al., 2025b ) and the MIRA autoencoder
retrained on our data. CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera (Figure  1 ).
Interestingly, our study points to four findings. (1) At strong compression, a diffusion decoder is sharper than perceptual regression, and a few-step flow model trained by progressive self-distillation is a good fit. (2) In robot manipulation, temporal compression is more effective in the
autoencoder than in the world model: patching inside the world model loses detail,
and at eight frames also action following. (3) Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions. In manipulation the camera is still, so
temporal compression costs little while spatial compression is costly. In navigation the camera moves and the balance
reverses, so it pays to keep frames and compress spatially, the low-latency choice. (4) Surprisingly, with a still camera, coarser spatial tokens hurt motion far more than image quality, and, in robot manipulation, action following too.

 2 What Compression Controls in an Interactive World Model
Before studying how to compress, we make explicit what compression controls. An interactive world model generates
in the latent space of an autoencoder that compresses a clip by \tau temporally and s spatially, written \tau{\times}s{\times}s : 2{\times}32{\times}32 compresses 2\times temporally
and 32\times spatially. These two factors set the world model’s cost, action latency and throughput. The
compression itself can happen in the autoencoder or in the world model (Section  5.1 ).
 Compression sets the world model’s cost. The world model we study is a causal, space-time-factorized diffusion transformer that predicts the next latent
frame from the previous ones and the current action or action chunk (Section  4 , Appendix  D ). Its cost grows with the number
of tokens it processes, which only the spatial and temporal compression set: a clip of T frames of size H{\times}W 
becomes N=(T/\tau)(H/s)(W/s) latent tokens for the world model.

 A latent frame waits for all its actions. Every frame registers one action. A latent frame spans \tau frames, so the world model can predict it only once
all \tau conditioning actions are known. It therefore consumes actions in chunks, and the actions of one chunk shape the next
(Figure  2 ). The agent thus commits to \tau actions before seeing their effect, as action-chunking policies do  ( Zhao et al., 2023 ; Chi et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025 ) . Causality holds between chunks. Within a chunk, the \tau frames
decode from the same latent, and their order is learned from real video.
 Temporal compression delays every action. The first action of a chunk waits \tau/f for the chunk to close, where f is the
frame rate, then t_{\text{gen}} for the world model to generate the latent frame and t_{\text{dec}} for the autoencoder to decode
it into \tau frames, which sets the throughput:
 \text{latency}=\underbrace{\tau/f}_{\text{chunk closes}}+\underbrace{t_{\text{gen}}+t_{\text{dec}}}_{\text{generate and decode}},\qquad\text{throughput}=\frac{\tau}{t_{\text{gen}}+t_{\text{dec}}}. 
 (1) 
No speed-up shortens the first term, which at \tau=8 and 20 frames per second is 400 ms, beyond the 230 ms lag humans
notice in their own movement  ( Shimada et al., 2009 ) . In exchange, a larger \tau raises the
throughput and, at a fixed spatial factor, cuts the token count N .
 3 CADRE: Causal Diffusion Representation Autoencoders
Studying compression means training the autoencoder at many compression points. The autoencoders of
text-to-video generators release their weights but not their training data or code, so they cannot be retrained. We instead design an open family of autoencoders for world models, one per
compression point (Figure  3 ). Its members share one recipe, differ only in their
compression and are each trained from scratch, so the world models trained on them differ by compression alone.

 3.1 Architecture
 Frozen features and one trained layer encode the clip. As in MIRA  ( Hu et al., 2026 ) , the encoder is that of a representation
autoencoder  ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) . A frozen DINOv3
extractor  ( Siméoni et al., 2026 ) encodes each frame into one feature per p{\times}p patch, aggregating several
layers as in RAEv2  ( Singh et al., 2026 ) . One trained 3D convolution with
kernel (\tau,k,k) and stride (\tau,s^{\prime},s^{\prime}) compresses \tau frames of s^{\prime 2} patches into a deterministic
latent, with s=p\,s^{\prime} (Appendix  B ).
 A causal diffusion decoder keeps textures sharp. A strongly compressed latent keeps a scene’s layout and semantics but little texture, so a perceptual regression decoder
averages the textures consistent with it into a blur  ( Xu et al., 2026 ) . We therefore use a diffusion
decoder  ( Preechakul et al., 2022 ; Pernías et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2025d ) ,
which samples the clip conditioned on the latent. It is a space-time-factorized
transformer  ( Bertasius et al., 2021 ; Arnab et al., 2021 ) on a q{\times}q 
patch grid, whose blocks attend over space, then causally over time. Most blocks process one token per latent frame, that is,
per \tau video frames. A linear map then splits each token into \tau tokens, one per frame, so the last blocks
resolve motion within a latent frame. The latent conditions the diffusion decoder in three places: (i) lifted by a transposed convolution and summed with the patchified noisy clip, (ii) through
a spatial AdaLN  ( Park et al., 2019 ; Zhu et al., 2020 ; Dufour et al., 2022 ; Yu et al., 2026 ) in every block, which also receives the flow time
 t and the jump length d , the size of the denoising step the decoder takes (Section  3.2 , Appendix  B ), and (iii) through a zero-initialized skip into a light
convolutional head. The decoder is time-causal, so it streams chunk by chunk.
 3.2 Training
 The decoder learns by flow matching and perceptual losses. The decoder g_{\theta} uses the x -prediction, x -loss form of flow
matching  ( Li & He, 2026 ) . It predicts the clean clip x from the latent z and a
noisy clip x_{t}=t\,x+(1-t)\,\epsilon , with \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I) and t=0 pure noise. As in diffusion
forcing  ( Chen et al., 2024a ) , each chunk of \tau frames draws its own
 t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) . The loss is
 \mathcal{L}_{\text{fm}}\;=\;\mathrm{E}_{x,\epsilon,t}\;\ell_{\text{rec}}\big(g_{\theta}(z,\,x_{t},\,t,\,0),\;x\big), 
 (2) 
where the last argument, the jump length d , is zero here. The term \ell_{\text{rec}} sums a mean absolute error,
two perceptual distances (LPIPS  ( Zhang et al., 2018 ) and its analog in DINO
feature space) and a 3D wavelet term as in LTX-Video  ( HaCohen et al., 2024 ) , weighted by
gradient balancing. CADRE trains on a 76k-hour mixture of web, robot, driving and camera video (Appendix  C ).
 Self-distillation makes one or two decoding steps enough. Flow-matching trajectories curve, so following them takes many steps, while an interactive world model needs single-
or few-step decoding. Shortcut models  ( Frans et al., 2025 ) and self-distilled flow
maps  ( Boffi et al., 2025 ) condition the network on its step size and train one large
step to agree with two smaller ones. Following them, g_{\theta}(z,x_{r},r,d) predicts where a jump from r to r{+}d 
lands, through x_{r+d}=x_{r}+\tfrac{d}{1-r}(\hat{x}-x_{r}) with implied velocity v_{\theta}=(\hat{x}-x_{r})/(1-r) . One
jump from r to t is trained to land where two half jumps through u=(r+t)/2 do (dropping z from the notation),
 \mathcal{L}_{\text{psd}}\;=\;\mathrm{E}\,\Big\|(1-r)\Big(v_{\theta}(x_{r},r,t{-}r)-\operatorname{sg}\big[\tfrac{1}{2}\big(v_{\theta}(x_{r},r,u{-}r)+v_{\theta}(\hat{x}_{u},u,t{-}u)\big)\big]\Big)\Big\|_{2}^{2}, 
 (3) 
where \operatorname{sg} stops the gradient and \hat{x}_{u} is the point reached by the first half jump. Unlike the
consistency decoder  ( OpenAI, 2023 ) and SSDD  ( Vallaeys et al., 2026 ) ,
which distill a decoder in a second stage, the CADRE decoder is its own teacher and learns one- or few-jump decoding in one stage.
 4 Judging an Autoencoder by Its World Model
An autoencoder is trained to reconstruct its input, yet in latent generation, reconstruction and generation
trade off  ( Yao et al., 2025 ; Hansen-Estruch et al., 2025 ) , and a
line of work shapes the latent for the generator
instead  ( Leng et al., 2025 ; Kouzelis et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ; Xie et al., 2026 ; Ramanana Rahary et al., 2026b ) .
We therefore score rollouts instead of reconstructions (Appendix  F ). As in MIRA  ( Hu et al., 2026 ) ,
we judge each autoencoder by the rollouts of a world model trained on its latents; the world model, clips
and actions are shared, so only the latent varies.
 Four environments span manipulation and navigation. We train world models in four environments, two of manipulation and two of navigation (Table  1 ), so
that no conclusion rests on a single environment. In manipulation the view is static for the robot and nearly static for the head-mounted camera of the
human, so mainly the arms or hands and the objects they handle move. In navigation the view itself moves, so the whole frame changes; we
separate driving, a dynamic scene where other road users move on their own, from indoor navigation in static scenes.
Action complexity ranges from 2 dimensions for driving to 102 for two hands. All videos are resampled to 20 frames per second at
 288{\times}512 . Evaluation uses held-out episodes, and the official split for driving.

their coefficient of determination ( R^{2} ) is normalized by the probe’s R^{2} on real video, so an ARR of one means real-video recoverability
(Appendix  E ).

 4.1 Should the decoder use perceptual regression or diffusion?
A decoder can regress one video from the latent or sample one by diffusion. MIRA  ( Hu et al., 2026 ) 
takes the first route: it replaces the adversarial losses used since VQGAN  ( Esser et al., 2021 ) 
with perceptual losses (LPIPS and DINO) and works well on Rocket League. It is also the only autoencoder besides CADRE that runs at 20 frames per second within the 230 ms lag humans notice (Figure  1 ). To test this route beyond
games, we retrain the MIRA recipe on our mixture (Appendix  C ) at 2{\times}32{\times}32 with
our extractor and budget (Figure  4 a). Against the CADRE decoder, it gives higher PSNR but worse FID and FVD in every environment. In one step, the CADRE decoder lowers FVD by 1.4 to 2.5 \times with similar
action following. A second step lowers FID by up to 23%.
 Should the decoder use perceptual regression or diffusion? 
 Decode a compressed latent by diffusion. Such a latent fits many videos. Perceptual regression outputs
their blurry average, while diffusion outputs one sharp video. Self-distilled, it decodes in one step as fast as
regression with 1.4 to 2.5 \times lower FVD and similar action following; a second step improves it further. 
 4.2 How do the autoencoders trade quality for latency?
To compare the autoencoders on both axes at once, we plot rollout quality against action latency
(Figure  4 b for robot manipulation, Figure  1 for driving, Figure  14 for all); an autoencoder lies on the Pareto frontier when no other is both
faster and better. CADRE autoencoders form most of this frontier in every environment. In 42 of the 48 comparisons
with MIRA, Wan 2.2, Cosmos and LTX-2 (four environments; FID, FVD and ARR), a CADRE autoencoder is at least as fast
and better by more than 2%; DC-AE , faster than all, is compared below. With two decoding
steps, the best FID of every environment belongs to CADRE, most often to CADRE- 4{\times}16{\times}16 , and so does the
best FVD, except in robot manipulation, where CADRE and Cosmos tie. The gap widens with a moving camera: in navigation, all CADRE autoencoders but one beat
every other on FID and FVD, while Wan 2.2’s FVD grows from 53 in robot manipulation to 600 or more in navigation and LTX-2’s ARR falls
to 0.04 indoors. CADRE also drifts least in 64 s robot manipulation rollouts (Appendix  H ).
At the fast end, CADRE- 1{\times}48{\times}48 reacts about as fast as DC-AE and beats it in navigation; DC-AE wins in manipulation. Few autoencoders are both fluid and responsive: streaming on one H100 with eight denoising steps, only
CADRE- 2{\times}32{\times}32 , CADRE- 2{\times}16{\times}16 and MIRA run at 20 frames per second under the 230 ms lag humans
notice  ( Shimada et al., 2009 ) (Figure  1 ). The world models of
Figure  5 run fluidly, at 29 to 58 frames per second (Appendix  D ), and,
from the same context, follow actions never recorded with it, such as holding
still or turning the other way (Figure  22 ).

 5 How to Compress for Interactive World Models
We now ask three questions: which component should compress temporally, how many frames one latent
should span, and what coarser spatial tokens trade for their speed. Comparisons follow the protocol of
Section  4 , so arms differ only in how the video is compressed.
 5.1 Should the autoencoder or the world model compress temporally?
 Figure 6: Temporal compression in the autoencoder or in the world model , at the same token grid in robot
manipulation. The autoencoder gives sharper frames, closer to the recorded future, and more plausible motion at all but
 2{\times}32{\times}32 . Action following barely changes, except at 8 frames.
Temporal compression can happen in the autoencoder or in the world model. The alternative to CADRE is an image autoencoder that leaves frames
temporally uncompressed, with the world model patching its latent temporally. Both
routes give the same token grid, but a different model learns the compression. In robot manipulation, we reach
each grid a second time by patching the latent of CADRE- 1{\times}16{\times}16 inside the world model
(Figure  6 ). Only the compression changes. Actions and noise levels stay per frame, and a patched token
receives the actions of every frame it covers.
 Temporal compression in the autoencoder gives better frames than in the world model. Compressing in the autoencoder gives better FID, FVD, PSNR and LPIPS at every temporal grid, except FVD at 2{\times}32{\times}32 , where space is compressed too. The FVD
gap grows with the factor, from 9 points when compressing 2 frames to 28 when compressing 8. Decoder capacity does not
explain it, since the patched arm decodes with CADRE- 1{\times}16{\times}16 , the best reconstruction among them
(Table  6 ). We attribute this to the training objectives. The autoencoder is trained to reconstruct pixels, so its compression keeps appearance. The world model is trained to
predict latents, so its compression keeps only what helps that prediction. This matches image and video generation,
where compressing in the autoencoder beats patching  ( Chen et al., 2025b ; Gupta et al., 2024 ) .
 Where temporal compression happens barely changes action following. The exception is 8 frames, where patching drops
ARR from 0.72 to 0.56. Action following depends on the motion the actions cause, which the world
model’s compression is trained to keep.
 Should the autoencoder or the world model compress temporally? 
 Let the autoencoder compress temporally. At the same number of tokens, its compressed frames look sharper and
move more realistically, with similar action following. It learns its compression to reconstruct pixels, while the world
model keeps only what helps it predict. 
 5.2 How many frames should one latent span?
 Figure 7: Temporal compression and spatial tokens. Green raises \tau at a fixed token size; orange
at a fixed token budget, so its grid coarsens (spatial factor s ). A static view fits eight frames in one latent
at no cost; a moving view loses quality with more frames. Full results in Figure  15 .
To separate temporal compression from the cost of coarser tokens, we vary \tau in two sweeps (Figure  7 ). The first holds s=16 and isolates temporal compression.
The second holds about 1100 tokens per 16-frame clip, so at a given \tau its gap to the first measures the cost of coarser spatial
tokens. All world models are matched in training steps and total training frames.
 Latents should span many redundant frames in manipulation and few in navigation. In manipulation,
the camera is mostly still, so consecutive frames are redundant and one latent can span many of them at no cost
(green curve in Figure  7 ). In navigation, the camera moves and the view shifts by more than a token
within one long latent (Appendix  I ), so long latents degrade motion, raising FVD by up to 64% over two-frame latents. Action
following changes less (Appendix  G ). Camera motion is our leading explanation, though the domains
also differ in actions and scenes.
In 64 s robot manipulation rollouts, long latents do not make errors accumulate: CADRE- 8{\times}16{\times}16 is
best from about 13 s on and ends with the lowest FVD (Appendix  H ). Long latents do leave seams, visible jumps
between chunks. A boundary excess compares the frame-to-frame
change across chunk boundaries with the change within chunks, relative to real video (Appendix  K ). It
grows with \tau in all four environments, to 1.6–1.8 at \tau=8 (Table  10 ). With a moving view, the seams
also show in FVD, which grows 1.6 to 2.1 \times more at \tau=8 than at \tau=2 .
 At a fixed token budget, navigation favors few frames per latent and manipulation many. Along the
orange curve of Figure  7 , autoencoders give about 1100 tokens per 16-frame clip. In navigation, the low-latency
CADRE- 2{\times}32{\times}32 is best, with two frames and coarser tokens. In manipulation,
CADRE- 8{\times}16{\times}16 gives the most plausible motion, with eight frames and fine tokens.
 How many frames should one latent span? 
 Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions. In manipulation the camera is still, so
each token sees temporally redundant content: temporal compression costs little, but spatial compression loses
the small objects being handled. In navigation the camera moves, so each token sees new content: temporal compression
shows seams, but spatial compression costs little because the whole view shifts. 
 5.3 What do coarser spatial tokens trade for their speed?
At a given \tau , coarser spatial tokens cut the world model’s cost, and the gap between the two sweeps isolates what they lose in quality and action following.
 Fine spatial tokens matter most when the action is small and stays in view. In robot manipulation,
the action shows in a small region that stays in view. At \tau=2 , coarser spatial tokens multiply FVD by 2.4 and
lower action following, with almost no change in FID (orange against green in Figure  7 ). In
navigation, the action moves the whole frame, which coarse tokens already capture, and the gap nearly vanishes
(Appendix  G ). FID may miss errors confined to small regions, but ARR, which reads the motion itself, drops
too (Appendix  G ).
 What do coarser spatial tokens trade for their speed? 
 Surprisingly, coarser tokens hurt motion and action following far more than image quality, above all in manipulation. 
In manipulation, per-frame quality barely changes, but motion degrades, and so does action following in robot manipulation. In navigation, where the
whole view shifts, they cost little. 
 6 Related Work
 World models and their autoencoders. Video world models serve
driving  ( Russell et al., 2025 ) , robotics  ( Yang et al., 2024 ) and open
scenes  ( Bruce et al., 2024 ) . Interactive ones stream in real
time  ( Valevski et al., 2025 ; Alonso et al., 2024 ) , often by distilling a text-to-video generator whose
autoencoder they inherit  ( He et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025a ) . Few study that autoencoder.
SANA-WM  ( Zhu et al., 2026a ) swaps two of them offline, and
MIRA  ( Hu et al., 2026 ) judges its own through the world model, but only on a single game.
We compare twelve autoencoders of varied compression through world models in four environments.
 Latent structure and compression. Generation quality depends on the structure of the
latent  ( Rombach et al., 2022 ; Yao et al., 2025 ) .
Representation autoencoders generate in the space of a frozen self-supervised
encoder  ( Zheng et al., 2026 ; Xie et al., 2026 ) ,
at one compression rate. Video autoencoders mostly compress 8{\times} in space and 4{\times} in
time  ( Wan et al., 2025 ; Kong et al., 2024 ) , some far
more  ( HaCohen et al., 2024 ; HaCohen et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2025b ) . A
generator can also compress through its patch
size  ( Peebles & Xie, 2023 ; Gupta et al., 2024 ) . For
images, DC-AE  ( Chen et al., 2025b ) finds that compressing in the autoencoder beats patching. We test this for video world models and
build representation autoencoders that span seven spatio-temporal compressions.
 Few-step diffusion decoders. Diffusion decoders avoid adversarial
losses  ( Preechakul et al., 2022 ; Chen et al., 2025d ) 
but need many steps, or a second stage to reach one  ( OpenAI, 2023 ; Vallaeys et al., 2026 ) . Shortcut
models and flow maps learn few-step generation in one stage from their own
predictions  ( Frans et al., 2025 ; Boffi et al., 2025 ) . Our causal video decoder learns one- or two-step
decoding this way, with no adversarial loss or second stage. Appendix  L extends this section.
 7 Conclusion
An interactive world model inherits its cost per frame, its action latency and much of its quality from its
autoencoder, so its compression is a design choice. To study this choice, we build
CADRE, an open family spanning seven compressions, and train the same world model on each in
manipulation and navigation. CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera, and our study points to four findings. At strong compression, a
diffusion decoder is sharper than perceptual regression, even in one or two steps. In robot manipulation, temporal compression is more effective in the autoencoder
than in the world model. Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions: temporal compression costs
little when the camera is still, and spatial compression when it moves. Surprisingly, with a still camera, coarser spatial tokens hurt
motion far more than image quality, and, in robot manipulation, action following too.
Our world models each cover one environment; whether these findings carry over to a generalist one, and to policy
training and planning, remains open. Our manipulation data use still or nearly still cameras; wrist-mounted cameras move, so our account predicts they favor the compression of navigation. We hope open autoencoders will let practitioners fit a world model’s compression
to its scenes and to the latency and throughput its users need.
