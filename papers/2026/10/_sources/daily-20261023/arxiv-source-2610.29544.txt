RISK-KV: Quality-Aware Selective KV Cache Reconstruction for Distributed LLM Inference
Wang Yuekai
Affiliation: Harbin Institute of TechnologyEmail: york.wang.ict@gmail.com
Abstract
Request migration and interruption recovery in distributed large language model (LLM) inference rely on historical key–value (KV) caches. Retaining or transferring a complete cache incurs substantial state overhead, whereas replaying historical tokens requires additional computation. This paper proposes RISK-KV, a quality-aware method for selective historical KV reconstruction in pretrained models. RISK-KV uses exact KV states on both sides of a target layer and cross-layer ridge regression to predict the missing layer’s attention-projection input; it then folds in the original model’s KV projection matrices to reduce predictor storage. Reconstruction risk is measured by the change in negative log-likelihood (NLL) caused by actual reconstruction, and low-overhead topology search with quality gating selects the layer groups to reconstruct. Independent tests on three language models show that representative recovery configurations omit 18.2%–33.3% of historical KV, with perplexity changes below 0.5%. In a local request-recovery experiment with a 512-token history, RISK-KV achieves a 1.51–3.31 × \times speedup over full Token Replay. These results indicate that selective KV reconstruction can reduce historical-cache overhead with small changes in predictive quality and improve request-recovery efficiency under the evaluated deployment conditions.

I Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in distributed and edge-computing environments to support interactive and context-intensive applications. Autoregressive inference relies on a key–value (KV) cache to store the attention states of historical tokens and avoid redundant computation during subsequent decoding. However, KV-cache size grows with context length, model depth, and the number of concurrent requests, resulting in substantial memory and data-movement costs. vLLM demonstrates the importance of efficient KV management for LLM serving, while grouped-query attention (GQA) reduces cache size by sharing key/value heads [ 4 , 1 ] . In distributed inference, request migration and interruption recovery also involve transferring and reloading historical states. DistServe and Splitwise illustrate the potential of organizing inference computation across devices and highlight the effect of state management on system efficiency [ 14 , 9 ] . In bandwidth-constrained edge environments, transferring the complete historical KV cache can be costly; discarding it instead requires recomputing the historical prefix, which also increases recovery latency.
Existing research reduces KV-cache overhead from several directions. H 2 O and SnapKV use attention features to select historical tokens for retention, while KVQuant reduces cache storage through low-precision representations [ 13 , 5 , 3 ] . Another line of work exploits redundancy across Transformer layers: MiniCache compresses the cache by merging states across layers, whereas YOCO reuses KV states through its architectural design [ 7 , 10 ] . For state transfer and recovery, CacheGen studies KV compression and streaming, while HCache uses intermediate activations to restore state and balance computation against I/O costs [ 8 , 2 ] . Krul dynamically selects cross-layer KV-sharing strategies based on attention similarity between layers; EdgeFlow studies KV migration and recovery in geographically distributed edge environments [ 11 , 12 ] . These works show that exploiting cache redundancy and balancing storage, communication, and computation are important ways to improve inference efficiency.
A key question remains for reconstructing omitted historical KV without modifying a pretrained model: which layers are suitable for reconstruction, and which must retain exact KV to reduce cache overhead while controlling degradation in predictive quality? Existing methods typically make selection decisions using signals such as attention importance, tensor similarity, numerical reconstruction error, or recovery cost, but these signals do not necessarily reflect how reconstructed KV affects language-model predictions [ 13 , 5 , 7 , 6 , 8 , 2 ] . Large numerical errors in KV may cause only a small increase in predictive loss; conversely, reconstruction errors in sensitive layers may propagate through subsequent Transformer layers and substantially change model outputs. Moreover, the effect of reconstructing several layer groups together need not equal the sum of their individual reconstruction losses. Therefore, maximizing the number of omitted layers alone may lead to an unfavorable trade-off between cache savings and model quality.
A key observation is that actual KV-reconstruction risk varies substantially across models and layers. With the remaining layers’ KV kept exact, historical KV is replaced one layer at a time, and model degradation is measured by the change in negative log-likelihood (NLL) under teacher-forced inference. For example, on TinyLlama-1.1B, reconstructing L2 alone increases mean NLL by only about 0.00010, whereas reconstructing the adjacent layer L3 increases it by about 0.09336. Qwen2.5-0.5B also exhibits a marked risk difference between adjacent layers. Further experiments show that even when artificial Gaussian perturbations are matched in magnitude to actual reconstruction errors, the resulting rankings of layer sensitivity can differ. This indicates that recovery strategies should be calibrated to the target model and the specific reconstruction mechanism, rather than determined solely by layer position, reconstruction error, or generic perturbation sensitivity.
Based on these observations, this paper introduces RISK-KV, a quality-aware framework for selective historical KV reconstruction in distributed LLM inference. As illustrated in Fig. 1 , in a Transformer with grouped-query attention (GQA), historical KV is obtained by applying the key and value projections to attention-normalized hidden representations, with rotary positional embedding (RoPE) additionally applied to keys. RISK-KV uses exact historical KV retained on both sides of a target layer as cross-layer anchors. A lightweight ridge regressor predicts the target layer’s normalized attention input, and the frozen key and value projection matrices of the original model generate the missing cache. Because both the predicted mapping and KV projections are affine transformations, they can be algebraically folded so that the online stage generates reconstructed KV directly from the anchor states, reducing additional parameter storage and intermediate-representation computation. This process neither modifies pretrained-model parameters nor requires storing complete historical hidden states.
To identify layer groups suitable for reconstruction, RISK-KV defines functional risk as the mean NLL change caused by replacing actual KV. The system first measures the independent recovery risk of each candidate group and ranks the groups accordingly. It then progressively forms joint recovery configurations along this ranking and remeasures the actual predictive loss for each configuration, rather than simply summing the individual group risks. For G G candidate groups, the method evaluates only 2 ​ G 2G distinct recovery configurations, compared with 2 G 2^{G} for exhaustive search. RISK-KV also uses a conservative quality gate based on bootstrap confidence bounds to select a configuration that satisfies the empirical quality budget while saving substantial historical KV, falling back to exact KV when necessary. Because the search is restricted to a single ordered path, it does not guarantee a global optimum, but it reduces offline configuration-evaluation cost.
RISK-KV is evaluated on three pretrained models: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0. On independent test data, representative recovery configurations omit 18.2%–33.3% of historical KV, with changes in perplexity relative to the full exact-KV baseline below 0.5%. The quality-gating experiments further show that more conservative selection can reduce observed budget violations in independent evaluations, typically at the cost of some cache savings. In local request-recovery experiments with a 512-token history, RISK-KV achieves a 1.51–3.31 × \times speedup over full historical Token Replay. The current latency experiments do not cover real wireless cross-node migration, and the complete KV cache is still materialized during subsequent decoding.
Contributions.
This paper makes the following contributions:
1.
A method is proposed for reconstructing historical KV caches from cross-layer information. To address the loss or unavailability of some historical KV states during request recovery in distributed LLM inference, the method uses real KV retained in other layers to reconstruct missing keys and values in target layers. It requires neither changes to pretrained-model parameters nor storage of complete historical hidden states or recomputation of the entire historical prefix. By folding the model’s KV projection matrices into the predictor, it reduces additional reconstruction storage and provides an implementation path for reducing persistent storage and transfer of historical KV.
2.
A functional-risk assessment and adaptive selection method is proposed for KV reconstruction across models and layers. The results show substantial variation in the tolerance of different models and Transformer layers to historical KV-reconstruction errors; KV numerical error or random-perturbation sensitivity alone cannot reliably identify suitable layers. Accordingly, the increase in language-model NLL caused by actual KV reconstruction is used as a measure of model-degradation risk. A small number of singleton recovery experiments assess candidate-layer risks, and low-overhead topology search under a quality constraint, together with conservative quality gating, automatically determines which historical KV states require exact preservation and which can be reconstructed, enabling a controlled trade-off between predictive quality and cache savings.
II Related Work
II-A KV Cache Management and Compression
KV caching avoids recomputing attention states for historical tokens, but its memory footprint grows with context length and request concurrency. Existing methods address this cost in different ways. PagedAttention organizes KV storage in blocks to improve memory allocation efficiency and reduce fragmentation in LLM serving [ 4 ] . Grouped-query attention (GQA) changes the attention architecture by letting multiple query heads share key/value heads, thereby reducing the number of KV heads that must be stored [ 1 ] . Token-selection methods directly reduce the number of retained positions: H 2 O dynamically preserves recent tokens and a small set of attention heavy hitters, while SnapKV selects salient positions based on attention observed near the end of the prompt [ 13 , 5 ] . KVQuant uses low-bit quantization to reduce the storage cost of KV representations [ 3 ] . These methods therefore act on memory allocation, KV head count, token positions, or numerical precision.
II-B Cross-Layer KV Sharing and State Reconstruction
Another line of work exploits dependencies across Transformer depth. MiniCache merges KV states from adjacent layers based on their similarity [ 7 ] . FusedKV constructs top-layer KV caches by learning to fuse informative KV states from lower layers [ 6 ] . Krul dynamically selects cross-layer sharing strategies based on attention similarity between layer pairs and combines compressed-cache loading with recomputation to restore state [ 11 ] . These methods explore different mechanisms for cross-layer sharing and restoration. Layer-wise functional risk under a particular reconstruction mechanism cannot be equated directly with attention similarity or tensor similarity.
Systems for transferring and restoring request state address complementary costs. CacheGen encodes KV caches as compact bitstreams and streams them to reduce context-loading overhead, adapting its compression scheme to changing bandwidth [ 8 ] . HCache restores model state from stored intermediate activations and schedules computation and I/O to balance the use of different resources during restoration [ 2 ] . RISK-KV targets pretrained models. It uses exact historical KV retained on both sides of a target layer as cross-layer information anchors, then learns a mapping to reconstruct the normalized hidden state input to the target layer’s attention module. The pretrained model’s original KV projection matrices then map this reconstructed hidden representation to the missing historical keys and values, combining cross-layer state correlations with the model’s existing projection structure. RISK-KV further measures each layer’s functional risk using the NLL change caused by actual KV reconstruction, and selects which historical KV states to preserve exactly or reconstruct under a given quality budget. This approach trades cache savings against predictive quality.
III The RISK-KV Framework
III-A System Architecture and KV Cache Model
RISK-KV addresses historical KV management and request state restoration in wireless distributed LLM inference. Consider a pretrained autoregressive model with L L Transformer decoder layers. Its parameters remain frozen throughout KV reconstruction. During inference, each layer stores the keys and values of historical tokens to avoid recomputing their attention states during subsequent decoding. Although grouped-query attention (GQA) reduces the number of KV heads, each Transformer layer still maintains its own historical KV cache [ 1 , 4 ] .

As shown in Fig. 2 , RISK-KV combines offline calibration with online recovery. Offline, historical states from the target model train a cross-layer KV predictor; experiments that actually replace KV determine layer-specific functional risk and produce a model-specific retention policy. When a request is migrated or restored online, the system retains or transfers exact historical KV only for selected layers and reconstructs the other layers with the deployed predictor.
For each candidate recovery group, RISK-KV places exact KV anchors on both sides along Transformer depth. Let G g G_{g} be group g g , with left and right anchor sets 𝒜 g − \mathcal{A}_{g}^{-} and 𝒜 g + \mathcal{A}_{g}^{+} . To reconstruct a target layer’s KV for one historical token, the predictor uses exact anchor KV at the same token position. Anchor layers retain their complete, exact histories and are never reconstructed. The procedure neither changes base-model parameters nor requires a complete historical hidden-state checkpoint or replay of the entire prefix.

III-C Quality-Constrained KV Recovery Selection
Although two-sided anchors make reconstruction possible, its effect on predictive quality varies substantially by layer. Some layers tolerate numerical KV reconstruction error with little effect on predictions, whereas errors in others cause substantial loss. RISK-KV therefore measures the loss caused by actual KV replacement and selects recovery groups under a quality budget instead of reconstructing all candidates indiscriminately.

t resume replay = t fetch token + t prefill ​ ( T ) + t dec exact , t_{\mathrm{resume}}^{\mathrm{replay}}=t_{\mathrm{fetch}}^{\mathrm{token}}+t_{\mathrm{prefill}}(T)+t_{\mathrm{dec}}^{\mathrm{exact}},

where the terms read historical token IDs and rerun the length- T T prefix to rebuild exact KV. Thus, a recovery benefit depends on whether saved transfer or recomputation time exceeds prediction and cache-assembly costs. Longer contexts may offer greater potential savings against replay, but end-to-end performance depends on hardware, storage, and network conditions. Our local latency measurements cover only 512-token histories.
V Performance and Analysis
V-A Experimental Setup
We evaluate RISK-KV on three pretrained autoregressive language models: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0, with 24, 28, and 22 Transformer layers, respectively. Base-model parameters remain frozen in every experiment; only the cross-layer KV recovery predictors are fitted on training data.
Experiments use WikiText-2 with a 512-token historical context. Predictive quality after recovery is evaluated by teacher forcing over the following 512 tokens. The three models have 5, 6, and 5 two-layer candidate recovery groups, respectively, with exact KV always retained at both anchors. Their full historical KV sizes are 6, 14, and 11 MiB.
RISK-KV predicts each target layer’s complete normalized attention-projection input using ridge regression with an intercept, then generates reconstructed KV through the original frozen projections. Deployment-cost experiments additionally evaluate folded FP32 and FP16 predictors. Every recovery configuration is compared with a full exact-KV baseline.
We measure predictive quality using mean change in negative log-likelihood ( Δ ​ NLL \Delta\mathrm{NLL} ) and perplexity (PPL). Omitted raw KV bytes and their fraction of the full cache measure potential cache savings. The statistical unit is a request: recovered and exact KV are compared on the same request, and confidence intervals are estimated by bootstrap. Predictive-quality experiments use three random seeds with eight evaluation requests per seed; the local recovery-latency experiment below uses eight validation requests per model.
The data splits serve distinct purposes. Training data fit the ridge predictors; validation data select recovery groups; an independent test split evaluates the final frozen recovery configurations. The quality-gate experiment also uses separate requests for risk calibration and outcome assessment to avoid reusing evaluation data in selection.

For a batch of 24 requests, measured GPU prediction and full-cache assembly take 3.974 and 3.810 ms, respectively. We further compare RISK-KV against replaying the complete history of tokens during request restoration. Token Replay reruns the historical prefix. RISK-KV uses retained exact KV to reconstruct the omitted cache and avoids that full historical recomputation.
The local experiment uses an RTX 3060 Laptop GPU, a 512-token history, and batch size 1. Each model contributes eight validation requests. After five warm-up iterations, each method is measured 30 times per request. The suspended state resides in local CPU memory. Token Replay reads token IDs and performs one prefill over the 512 historical tokens plus the known current token. RISK-KV transfers retained exact KV for the 512 historical positions, builds two-sided anchor features, reconstructs omitted layers with a Folded FP32 predictor already resident on the GPU, assembles the full cache, and processes that same known current token. Both methods therefore produce next-token logits at the same position. Timing includes CPU-to-GPU state transfer, cache recovery, and the next inference step; the model and predictor are loaded and warmed before measurement. The complete GPU cache is present during subsequent decoding.

VI Conclusion
We proposed RISK-KV, a quality-aware method for selective KV-cache reconstruction in distributed LLM inference. It uses exact KV retained on both sides of a target group to reconstruct missing historical KV, assesses functional risk through predictive loss caused by actual reconstruction, and selects groups under a quality budget. Folding the learned recovery map with the model’s original KV projections reduces predictor storage without changing pretrained model weights or replaying the complete historical prefix.
Across three pretrained language models, layer-specific reconstruction risks vary substantially. Risk-aware selective recovery reduces historical KV storage by 18.2%–33.3% in representative configurations with small observed changes in predictive quality. Conservative quality gates reduce observed budget violations in an independent evaluation sample. For local restoration with a 512-token history, RISK-KV is 1.51 × \times –3.31 × \times faster than complete Token Replay, demonstrating potential to reduce historical-state recomputation. Actual wireless cross-node migration has not yet been evaluated. Future work will examine longer contexts, larger models, and real distributed deployments.
