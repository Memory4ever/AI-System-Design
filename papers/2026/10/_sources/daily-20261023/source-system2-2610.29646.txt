From Association to Decision Value:
Testing Paired Instruction Dependence for VLA Corpus Selection
Nurmukhambet Serikbayev
Abdul Basit

Abstract
Choosing among robot demonstration corpora is a ranking problem: an upstream diagnostic must preserve which candidates produce better downstream policies, not merely correlate with average performance. We study paired instruction dependence , the rate at which a demonstrator changes its first decision when only the goal changes. Across 136 independently seeded quadruped-navigation corpora, predictions developed on 56 corpora are evaluated prospectively on 80 held-out corpora. The audit is reliable and associated with downstream paired correctness, and lowers mean absolute prediction error when added to a correctness-aware baseline, but the primary proper-log-score increment is + 0.059 +0.059 nats (95% corpus-bootstrap CI [ − 0.027 , 0.133 ] [-0.027,0.133] ) and the prespecified top-20% comparison does not establish an incremental selection gain. An exploratory response decomposition suggests a structural source of this predictive–ranking mismatch: under the tested predictor specification, separating both-correct and both-wrong responses yields larger held-out gains than their unsigned switch statistic. Seed-to-seed agreement indicates that the held-out endpoint retains recoverable rank signal. These results show that counterfactual response structure can be reliable and predictive while providing limited incremental decision value in this setting when compressed into an unsigned statistic, motivating diagnostics that preserve outcome-relevant structure and are validated against the decisions they are intended to support.

1 Introduction
The downstream value of a robot demonstration corpus is usually known only after a vision–language–action (VLA) policy has been fine-tuned on it. Evaluating every candidate therefore pays much of the cost that an upstream diagnostic is meant to avoid. The practical question is consequently decision-focused: can a measurement rank candidate corpora well enough to select the ones that will produce better policies?
Language-conditioned behavior creates a tempting diagnostic. If two goals require opposite turns from the same observation, a demonstrator that follows language should usually change its decision when the goal changes. We call the resulting switch rate paired instruction dependence , A d A_{d} . Unlike ordinary correctness, it uses a controlled same-state intervention and tests whether the response depends on the instruction. This distinction is relevant to generalist VLAs, whose success can otherwise conceal shortcut policies that weakly use language ( Geirhos et al., 2020 ; Brohan et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Ghosh et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025 ) .
Dependence, however, is unsigned. A demonstrator can switch in the correct direction for both goals or in the wrong direction. With binary left/right responses, A d A_{d} is exactly the sum of these both-correct and both-wrong cases; the observed halt action makes that identity approximate rather than exact in our data. The relevant question is therefore narrower than whether language sensitivity matters in general: does unsigned same-state response structure improve prediction and selection after marginal demonstrator correctness is known?
This question also separates four criteria that are often conflated:
reliable measurement ⟶ association ⟶ incremental prediction ⟶ decision value . \text{reliable measurement}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{association}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{incremental prediction}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{decision value}.
A signal may track broad outcome differences or lower mean error while failing to preserve the ordering near a selection cutoff. Figure 1 illustrates this decision-value gap .
We test it prospectively on 136 independently seeded quadruped-navigation corpora. Nested probabilistic predictors are developed on 56 corpora, while 80 disjoint corpora form the held-out test population. The correctness-aware baseline M 0 M_{0} uses per-goal demonstrator correctness and corpus entropies; M 1 M_{1} adds only A d A_{d} . We train 544 adapters across Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen3.5-VL-0.8B and evaluate in-distribution and under a controlled scene shift. The primary selection endpoint is paired first-decision correctness; closed-loop evaluation separately tests how this local endpoint relates to full navigation. Predictors, held-out corpus identifiers, and the top-20% operating point were prespecified before held-out outcomes were revealed.
The study makes three main contributions:
1.
A prospective decision-value evaluation: we separate reliability, association, proper probabilistic prediction, and top- k k selection for a same-state robot-data diagnostic, using the corpus as the unit of inference.
2.
Correctness-resolved response structure locates a candidate source of the predictive–ranking mismatch: the unsigned switch statistic mixes both-correct and both-wrong behavior; separating them yields larger exploratory held-out gains under the tested predictor specification.
3.
Decision resolution and practical alternatives: a cross-seed resolution benchmark establishes recoverable corpus ordering, while demonstrator and proxy baselines locate the audit relative to realistic selectors.
2 Related Work
Vision–language–action learning:
Language-conditioned robot learning spans task-specific imitation ( Lynch and Sermanet, 2021 ; Mees et al., 2022 ; Nair et al., 2022 ) , large pretrained embodied models ( Driess et al., 2023 ; Brohan et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Ghosh et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025 ) , and heterogeneous robot corpora ( O’Neill et al., 2024 ) . Physical VLM-to-VLA transfer is increasingly practical ( Hancock et al., 2026 ) ; NaVILA separates language-level navigation decisions from low-level legged control ( Cheng et al., 2025 ) , as we do. We study the preceding data decision: which fixed demonstration corpus should train a policy?
Language grounding and counterfactual evaluation:
Vision-and-language navigation established grounded instruction following ( Anderson et al., 2018 ) , but success can hide instruction-insensitive shortcuts. Controlled tests expose memorized action sequences and semantic failures ( Fei et al., 2025 ; Zhou et al., 2025 ; Hou and Zhao, 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026 ) , while mismatched instructions can supply corrective supervision ( Kondoh et al., 2026 ) . Our intervention similarly holds the state fixed while changing the goal, but it measures a data-generating demonstrator and predicts a separately trained learner. Crucially, we distinguish unsigned response change from correctness-resolved response structure.
Robot-data selection and valuation:
Robot policies are sensitive to demonstration composition and scale ( Mandlekar et al., 2022 ; Saxena et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2025 ) . Data valuation uses marginal contribution, training dynamics, gradients, or standardized retraining ( Ghorbani and Zou, 2019 ; Swayamdipta et al., 2020 ; Xia et al., 2024 ; Gadre et al., 2023 ) . DemInf scores demonstrations through state–action mutual information ( Hejna et al., 2025 ) ; DataMIL learns policy-aware datamodels ( Dass et al., 2026 ) . Unlike end-to-end selectors such as DataMIL, we test whether an interpretable pre-training corpus diagnostic retains incremental value at the downstream selection decision. Our unit is an entire candidate corpus, and the nested comparison conditions on marginal demonstrator correctness.
Proxy selection and decision-aware evaluation:
Selection via Proxy uses a smaller model to rank data efficiently ( Coleman et al., 2020 ) ; compute-aware selection makes the appropriate proxy depend on its cost ( Yin and Rush, 2025 ) . Small training runs can preserve data-recipe rankings inconsistently across proxy configurations ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . Upstream probes can correlate with control ( Dong et al., 2026 ) , and proxy environments can be judged by absolute prediction and rank preservation ( Quevedo et al., 2026 ) . Prior work therefore distinguishes absolute prediction quality from rank preservation and selection utility. We extend this distinction to prospective robot-corpus selection with prespecified decisions, a proper-score test, a seed-based resolution benchmark, and direct comparison with practical selectors.
3 Method
Figure 2 summarizes the prospective comparison and the disjoint roles of audit, training, and held-out evaluation data.
3.1 Same-state goal-swap audit
For each audit state, we query the demonstrator twice with an identical visual observation and action history. Only the object goal changes, and the two goals require opposite branches. Let d d index a corpus, u u a state, x − , x + x^{-},x^{+} the goals, and B d ​ ( u , x ) B_{d}(u,x) the first response. For a deterministic demonstrator, paired instruction dependence is
A d = 1 n A ∑ u = 1 n A [ B d ( u , x − ) ≠ B d ( u , x + ) ] . A_{d}=\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{u=1}^{n_{A}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[B_{d}(u,x^{-})\neq B_{d}(u,x^{+})\right].
(1)
It is an unsigned response statistic: changing correctly for both goals and changing incorrectly for both goals can each increase A d A_{d} . Under the balanced deterministic intervention, each unequal pair identifies the goal and contributes one bit, so Eq. 1 equals state-averaged conditional mutual information (Appendix A ). This equivalence does not make A d A_{d} a correctness or downstream-value measure.
The audit requires counterfactual access to a queryable demonstrator and is unavailable from a fixed corpus containing only one instruction per state. It is therefore applicable in settings such as automated simulation or online data generation, where the same state can be replayed under alternate goals before committing to a large collection. The study therefore concerns this access model rather than arbitrary human demonstrations.
3.2 What does the switch rate contain?
Let a d a_{d} , b d b_{d} , and c d c_{d} be the fractions of audit states for which both goal responses are correct, both are wrong, or exactly one is correct. Marginal per-goal demonstrator correctness is
p d = a d + 1 2 ​ c d = 1 2 + 1 2 ​ ( a d − b d ) . p_{d}=a_{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}c_{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(a_{d}-b_{d}).
(2)
If responses are restricted to left/right, opposite required branches imply A d = a d + b d A_{d}=a_{d}+b_{d} . Thus, in the binary case, ( p d , A d ) (p_{d},A_{d}) is an invertible reparameterization of ( a d , b d ) (a_{d},b_{d}) : p d p_{d} contains their signed difference, whereas A d A_{d} contains their unsigned sum. Our demonstrators may also emit halt ; this breaks the exact A d = a d + b d A_{d}=a_{d}+b_{d} identity in our data, although Eq. 2 remains exact. The decomposition makes the primary question precise: does unsigned joint response structure improve prediction beyond marginal correctness and corpus composition?

3.5 Resolution and comparator analyses
Finite evaluation states and learner seeds limit any ranking method. We use cross-seed ranking as an empirical resolution benchmark and compare against demonstrator and proxy selectors. We also repeat M 0 / M 1 M_{0}/M_{1} comparisons after excluding the two least mature checkpoints. These exploratory analyses do not alter the prespecified primary test.
4 Experimental Setup
4.1 Corpus population
We study Unitree Go2 object-goal navigation in Isaac Lab ( Mittal et al., 2023 ) . Given forward RGB, instruction text, and action history, the VLA emits a discrete navigation command for a fixed low-level controller; only that controller sees proprioception. Every corpus uses a separately trained tabular Q-learning demonstrator with unique training, layout, physics, and rendering seeds. Checkpoints span 10, 50, 250, 1,000, and 50,000 episodes. The held-out population has 16 corpora per level; development has 12 at 10 episodes and 11 at each remaining level. Checkpoint maturity is excluded from the deployable baseline because it is an experimental assignment, not a property available for an arbitrary corpus.
Each corpus contains 24 paired audit states, 48 unpaired multi-step training trajectories, and 48 paired in-distribution (ID) evaluation states. Audit, training, and evaluation packets are disjoint at the state, image, and trajectory levels. All 6,528 attempted training rollouts passed prespecified structural-validity checks, so acceptance is constant across corpora and cannot explain their ranking; first-branch correctness instead ranges from .375 .375 to .813 .813 . Appendix I gives the complete validity checklist.
Across 136 corpora, the study contains 6,528 training trajectories (43,084 steps), 3,264 audit states, and 6,528 ID evaluation states. The 56 development and 80 held-out corpora share no demonstrator seeds or scene realizations. Figure 3 summarizes the data roles and evaluations.
The audit requires 48 demonstrator queries and costs a median 4.18 GPU-min per corpus. A Qwen2.5 fit and ID evaluation average 11.46 GPU-min for one seed, or 2.74 × 2.74\times the audit cost. Appendix Table 16 reports the complete measured cost comparison.
4.2 VLA training and evaluation
The primary learner is Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct ( Bai et al., 2025 ) ; Qwen3.5-VL-0.8B is both a supporting backbone and an exploratory proxy selector ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) . Within each backbone, only corpus and learner seed vary. We hold the vision tower fixed and train rank-8 LoRA adapters ( Hu et al., 2022 ) on language-model projections. Outputs are left/right turns, forward, halt, or done; invalid generations are errors. The full factorial gives 544 adapters. A Qwen2.5 fit averages 10.68 GPU-min and Qwen3.5 averages 13.41 GPU-min over the measured runs.

(b) Audit, training, and evaluation observations are disjoint. (c) Controlled scene shift balances a × 2 2\!\times\!2 geometry/appearance design. ID and controlled shift:
The ID endpoint has 48 paired states per corpus; both goals must receive the required branch. The 24-state shifted set crosses two spawn/target geometries with two lighting settings, six states per cell. Camera, warehouse, objects, prompt, action space, and controller remain fixed, so this is controlled rather than open-world shift.
Closed-loop simulation:
For 16 held-out corpora at evenly spaced A d A_{d} ranks, the first Qwen2.5 seed runs 24 scenes and both goals (768 episodes). We record first-command correctness, target reach, collision/fall, path efficiency, and recovery. These aggregate rates and exploratory rank correlations describe the stratified subset, not all 80 held-out corpora.
Physical evidence:
Stationary evaluation uses 36 human-screened Go2 images across three layouts, three surrogate-object pairs, two sides, and two difficulties (72 queries per adapter). Closed-loop deployment uses one adapter per backbone, chosen before physical evaluation by simulation outcomes from the 16 committed roots. The VLA acts from onboard RGB and history; a depth safety mask can veto forward motion. The 48 physical runs are descriptive because terminal state was not externally adjudicated and parts of the recorded protocol departed from the initial design (Appendix G.1 ).
4.3 Inference protocol
The primary metric is Eq. 6 . Secondary diagnostics include absolute error, Pearson/Spearman association, top-20% correctness, regret, and oracle overlap. Predictors, held-out corpus identifiers, and the top-20% operating point were prespecified before held-out outcomes were revealed. Inferential intervals resample whole corpora, preserving nested states, paired goals, and crossed seeds. The main endpoint and selection rule were unchanged after reveal; decomposition, resolution, proxy, calibration, and closed-loop correlation analyses are explicitly exploratory. Appendix I records the content-addressed prospective procedure.
5 Results
5.1 Reliability, association, and incremental value diverge
Figures 4 – 5 and Table 1 summarize the held-out association, predictive, and selection results.
Across all 136 corpora, A d A_{d} has Spearman ρ = .467 \rho=.467 (95% CI [ .300 , .616 ] [.300,.616] ) with Qwen2.5 paired correctness; on the 80 held-out corpora, ρ = .356 \rho=.356 ( [ .111 , .565 ] [.111,.565] ). Audit reliability is .802 .802 overall and .766 .766 on held-out corpora. The audit therefore measures a demonstrator property with substantial estimated reliability and tracks broad learner differences.
The incremental comparison is more uncertain. Adding A d A_{d} to M 0 M_{0} gives a Qwen2.5-ID mean log-score gain of + .059 +.059 nats (95% corpus-bootstrap CI [ − .027 , .133 ] [-.027,.133] ). 5.2 Unsigned switching hides correctness-resolved structure
The demonstrator emits halt in 10.9% of audit queries, and 107/136 corpora contain at least one nonbinary pair. Thus the binary identity A d = a d + b d A_{d}=a_{d}+b_{d} is not exact: A d − ( a d + b d ) A_{d}-(a_{d}+b_{d}) averages .100 .100 and reaches .250 .250 . The marginal-correctness identity in Eq. 2 is exact. At the 10-episode checkpoint, mean ( a d , b d , c d ) = ( .156 , .016 , .827 ) (a_{d},b_{d},c_{d})=(.156,.016,.827) ; at 50 episodes it is ( .389 , .074 , .537 ) (.389,.074,.537) . Both-wrong behavior is present but does not dominate these checkpoint averages.
The decomposition identifies a candidate source of the mismatch. In exploratory held-out tests, M 0 + a d M_{0}+a_{d} gains + .104 +.104 nats ( [ .049 , .153 ] [.049,.153] ) and M 0 + b d M_{0}+b_{d} gains + .110 +.110 ( [ .022 , .188 ] [.022,.188] ), each exceeding the + .059 +.059 gain from their unsigned, halt -affected mixture. Their selected correctness is .846 .846 and .842 .842 , compared with .811 .811 for M 1 M_{1} . Under the same exploratory modeling protocol, these correctness-resolved parameterizations yield larger held-out gains than the unsigned switch rate. This ordering persists when the ridge penalty is selected separately on development corpora: the selected penalty is 1 1 for all three alternatives, with gains + .126 +.126 for a d a_{d} , + .123 +.123 for b d b_{d} , and + .046 +.046 for A d A_{d} (Appendix E ). The comparison remains post-hoc and can reflect the nonbinary halt cases, finite-sample estimation, or regularized model geometry; it does not establish an information-theoretic advantage.
5.3 The endpoint contains resolvable rank signal
The two Qwen2.5 learner seeds have rank agreement ρ = .567 \rho=.567 across 80 corpora. Selecting 16 corpora by seed 1 and evaluating with seed 2 gives correctness .874 .874 , oracle overlap .50 .50 , and regret .057 .057 ; reversing the seeds gives the same selected correctness, .50 .50 overlap, and .052 .052 regret. This empirical resolution benchmark is imperfect, but it is materially above both M 0 M_{0} ( .838 .838 selected) and M 1 M_{1} ( .811 .811 ), so finite-state and learner-seed variation do not fully explain the selection result.
The ranking gap persists after excluding the 10- and 50-episode checkpoints that create the clearest low-performance cluster. Among the remaining 48 corpora, M 0 M_{0} has Spearman .349 .349 and selected correctness .821 .821 , whereas M 1 M_{1} has Spearman .172 .172 and selected correctness .791 .791 . The ordering problem is therefore not solely an artifact of immature demonstrators.

5.5 Local response and navigation success diverge
Across 768 simulated episodes, first-command correctness is .829 .829 (637/768), while target reach is .655 .655 (503/768), a 17.4-point gap ( [ 10.4 , 24.9 ] [10.4,24.9] ). Of 637 correct-first episodes, 134 fail later; none of 131 wrong-first episodes recovers. Across the 16 corpora, target-reach rank correlations are .368 .368 for A d A_{d} , .361 .361 for M 0 M_{0} , and .423 .423 for M 1 M_{1} . These small-sample descriptive values do not establish a corpus selector for embodied success. They show that the local endpoint and full navigation are distinct.
On 48 physical Go2 runs, the first choice is correct in 47, but strict terminal success is not estimable under the recorded protocol. We therefore retain physical results as qualitative transfer evidence and separate objective logs, operator reports, interventions, and protocol departures in Appendix G.1 .
6 Scope and Limitations
The audit requires counterfactual demonstrator access and is tested on one simulator, one tabular demonstrator family, short corpora, and a largely maturity-driven population. The resulting claim is specific to this controlled corpus population. Generalization to different demonstrator families, corpus-generation mechanisms, and larger real-world corpora remains untested.
A d A_{d} tests one unsigned additive summary. Correctness-resolved parameterizations yield larger exploratory gains under the tested predictor specification, including after separate development-set penalty tuning, but these alternatives were not prespecified and do not by themselves identify the cause of that difference. Corpus value is defined relative to the fixed learner architecture and fine-tuning recipe used in each comparison. Qwen3.5 is a related backbone rather than an independent architecture family, and the proxy comparison uses outcomes collected for this study. Finally, closed-loop and physical evaluations support endpoint interpretation but do not enlarge the evidence for held-out corpus selection.
7 Conclusion
Paired instruction dependence provides a controlled case study of how proxy structure can be lost through compression. The switch rate is measured with substantial reliability and associated with learner behavior, but its unsigned form mixes both-correct and both-wrong responses; correctness-resolved parameterizations yield larger exploratory held-out gains under the tested predictor specification. Cross-seed agreement shows that corpus ordering remains recoverable. Together, these results motivate preserving outcome-relevant structure in counterfactual data diagnostics and validating them against the decisions they are intended to support.

Appendix C Prospective Corpus-Population and Precision Design
The 56-development/80-test allocation, ordered corpus IDs, fitted predictors, and predictions were stored in a content-addressed internal commit before held-out outcomes were revealed. The accompanying certificate records the commit hash and forbids subsequent refitting. This is an auditable prospective record, not a public preregistration; the anonymized commit and certificate are included with the artifacts. The top-20% operating point was prespecified, whereas its confidence interval was computed exploratorily.
Candidate allocations varied development corpora, test corpora, and learner seeds under a generative model fitted to preliminary development results. The effect coefficient was the smallest value on the prespecified grid whose calibration mean exceeded the 0.105-nat magnitude benchmark; the discrete grid produced a simulated mean gain of 0.174 nats. Among the three highest-power cells, the rule required estimated power of at least .82 and zero failed fits, then selected minimum Qwen compute, more test corpora, and higher power in that order. The chosen 56/80/two-seed cell had screening power .898 over 2,000 simulations. An independent 5,000-simulation confirmation gave .8934 (Monte Carlo 95% interval [ .8848 , .9020 ] [.8848,.9020] ), with zero failed fits. This documents the design rule, not 89% power for an effect of exactly 0.105 nats; only 12.5% of the realized corpus-bootstrap replicates exceed that magnitude reference.

All 36 prescreened images were retained, including documented surrogate-prop and incidental-scene variation; no model output entered screening. The props are semantic surrogates rather than replicas of simulated objects, so this setting tests external-image robustness only.
