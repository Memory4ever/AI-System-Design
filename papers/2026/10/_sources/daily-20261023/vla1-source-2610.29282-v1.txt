Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) have shown strong potential for robot manipulation by jointly learning visual dynamics and executable actions.
However, their video and action objectives do not explicitly constrain the 3D geometry that is crucial for precise manipulation.
Recently, geometry-aware WAMs introduce depth or 4D prediction branches, whereas representation alignment methods supervise intermediate WAM features with pretrained 3D foundation models.
Yet we find that such pointwise alignment benefits the video features but degrades action learning, since dense geometric feature targets overly restrict compact action features.
To address this limitation, we propose Relational Geometry Forcing (RGF), which transfers geometric priors from a frozen 3D foundation model by aligning pairwise feature relations instead of pointwise features.
Specifically, RGF applies three complementary relational objectives, where intra-frame and cross-view relations transfer scene geometry and multi-view correspondence to the video features, while action-temporal relations couple action dynamics to geometric scene evolution.
Applied only during training, RGF requires no explicit geometry branch or extra inference module.
Extensive experiments in simulation and the real world demonstrate the effectiveness of Relational Geometry Forcing, showing consistent gains across diverse manipulation scenarios and WAM paradigms.

 1 Introduction
 Figure 1: Geometry transfer paradigms for WAMs. 
(a) Explicit geometric modeling adds a geometry branch that predicts depth or 4D outputs.
(b) Pointwise geometric alignment regresses intermediate features toward dense geometric targets, which benefits the video expert but degrades action learning.
(c) RGF aligns pairwise feature relations instead, benefiting both the video and action experts.
Robot policies for manipulation require accounting for interaction-induced scene changes when generating precise action trajectories.
World Action Models (WAMs) incorporate predictive world modeling into action learning by coupling future visual prediction with action generation  ( Ye et al. 2026b ; Li et al. 2026b ; Kim et al. 2026 ; Bi et al. 2025 ) .
Typically, WAMs include a video expert to predict future RGB frames and an action expert to generate action chunks, while scene geometry receives no direct supervision  ( Yuan et al. 2026 ; Ye et al. 2026a ) .
Accordingly, limited geometric perception of the physical scene remains a key challenge for WAMs in precise manipulation  ( Shridhar, Manuelli, and Fox 2023 ; Ze et al. 2024 ) .
Recent geometry-aware WAMs address this challenge by adding explicit geometry branches to the video–action framework, as illustrated in Figure  1 (a).
These branches introduce depth or 4D prediction as additional training objectives  ( Guo et al. 2026 ; Ma et al. 2026 ; Li et al. 2026d ; Zhang et al. 2026a ) .
We therefore ask whether geometric priors can directly regularize the video and action representations of WAMs without explicit geometric reconstruction.
Representation alignment offers an alternative to explicit geometric modeling by directly supervising intermediate WAM features.
REPA aligns diffusion features with pretrained visual representations  ( Yu et al. 2024 ) , while subsequent methods use geometric targets from pretrained 3D foundation models to supervise video generators and robot policies  ( Wu et al. 2025 ; Li et al. 2025 ) .
As illustrated in Figure  1 (b), these methods primarily use pointwise alignment to regress intermediate features in WAMs toward dense geometric targets from 3D foundation models.
However, pointwise alignment assumes that each WAM feature and its geometric target encode comparable content.
Video alignment satisfies this assumption because both representations describe local image regions.
In action alignment, compact action features instead summarize trajectory transitions, whereas dense geometric targets describe scene content.
Directly regressing action features toward scene-level geometric targets therefore conflates representations with different roles.
Consistent with this analysis, extending pointwise alignment to the action expert reduces task success below the unaligned baseline (Figure  2 b).
 Figure 2: Cross-view correspondence and LIBERO performance. 
(a) Wrist-view similarity maps for a query patch in the agent view, where RGF peaks near the ground-truth correspondence.
(b) Comparison of average success rate on the same WAM: extending pointwise alignment to action features degrades performance below the baseline.
To address this problem, we introduce Relational Geometry Forcing (RGF), which transfers geometric priors to WAM features without explicit geometric reconstruction.
As illustrated in Figure  1 (c), RGF aligns pairwise relations among WAM features with those induced by a frozen 3D foundation model, avoiding direct feature regression.
Matching these relations does not require WAM and 3D features to share the same dimensionality or embedding space  ( Park et al. 2019 ; Zhang et al. 2026b ) .
Using frozen VGGT features as supervision  ( Wang et al. 2025 ) , RGF transfers scene geometry and multi-view correspondence to video features through intra-frame and cross-view objectives, while its action-temporal objective couples action dynamics to geometric scene evolution.
All auxiliary components are used only during training, leaving inference unchanged.
Experiments on LIBERO  ( Liu et al. 2023 ) , RoboTwin 2.0  ( Chen et al. 2025 ) , and real-world tasks on a Franka robot show that RGF achieves competitive performance across single-arm and bimanual manipulation.
On the real robot, RGF further generalizes to unseen objects, background distractors, and height changes.
Moreover, RGF yields consistent gains across diverse WAM paradigms.
Qualitatively, Figure  2 (a) shows that the RGF similarity map peaks near the ground-truth cross-view correspondence.
Our contributions are as follows:
 • 
We identify a representation mismatch in pointwise alignment between dense geometric targets from 3D foundation models and compact action features in WAMs, explaining the performance degradation observed when this alignment is extended to the action expert.
 • 
We propose Relational Geometry Forcing, which transfers geometric priors into WAM representations by aligning intra-frame, cross-view, and action-temporal feature relations, without explicit geometric reconstruction or additional inference modules.
 • 
Extensive experiments on simulated and real-world tasks validate RGF, demonstrating competitive performance, generalization to unseen real-world conditions, and consistent gains across WAM paradigms.
 2 Related Work
World Action Models.
World Action Models combine future video prediction with action generation to learn action-relevant representations from scene evolution.
Early generative policies use predicted frames or visual plans to guide action generation  ( Du et al. 2023 ; Wu et al. 2024 ; Hu et al. 2024 ) , while recent video-action models adapt large-scale video priors through zero-shot, inverse-dynamics, causal, or action-centered formulations  ( Ye et al. 2026b ; Pai et al. 2025 ; Li et al. 2026b ; Bi et al. 2025 ; Kim et al. 2026 ; Ye et al. 2026a ) .
Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al. 2026 ) shows that video co-training can improve action learning without inference-time future generation.
X-WAM generates multi-view RGB-D sequences  ( Guo et al. 2026 ) , whereas GeoSem-WAM and WAM4D introduce auxiliary depth prediction  ( Ma et al. 2026 ; Li et al. 2026d ) .
MECo-WAM adds a training-time 4D expert  ( Zhang et al. 2026a ) .
Unlike these methods, RGF regularizes video–action representations through pairwise feature relations without training a depth or 4D predictor.
Representation Alignment.
Aligning intermediate features with frozen foundation models transfers visual and geometric priors to downstream models  ( Yan et al. 2026 ; Wang et al. 2026 ) .
REPA aligns diffusion features with self-supervised visual features  ( Yu et al. 2024 ; Oquab et al. 2024 ) , while Geometry Forcing and Spatial Forcing use pointwise feature alignment with 3D foundation models for video generation and VLA policies, respectively  ( Wu et al. 2025 ; Wang et al. 2025 ; Li et al. 2025 ) .
Relational distillation instead preserves pairwise distances or similarity structures between teacher and student representations  ( Park et al. 2019 ; Tung and Mori 2019 ) , with Whitened Similarity Distillation further transferring cross-modal similarity matrices  ( Huang, Wang, and Wang 2022 ) .
VideoREPA uses pairwise relation alignment to transfer physical priors from foundation models into text-to-video generation  ( Zhang et al. 2026b ) .
In contrast, RGF aligns intra-frame, cross-view, and action-temporal feature relations in dual-expert WAMs, avoiding direct pointwise correspondence between compact action features and dense geometric targets.
 Figure 3: Overview of Relational Geometry Forcing (RGF). 
The WAM predicts future multi-view frames and an action chunk through its video and action experts, while a frozen 3D foundation model encodes the same frames into per-timestep 3D features.
RGF aligns pairwise relations among intermediate video and action features with those induced by the 3D foundation model through intra-frame, cross-view, and action-temporal objectives.
All auxiliary components are removed during inference.
Geometry-Aware Robot Learning.
Geometric perception is crucial for spatial reasoning and precise manipulation in robot policies  ( Ke, Gkanatsios, and Fragkiadaki 2024 ) .
PerAct, RVT, Act3D, and DP3 represent 3D observations with voxels, multi-view features, 3D feature fields, and compact point clouds, respectively  ( Shridhar, Manuelli, and Fox 2023 ; Goyal et al. 2023 ; Gervet et al. 2023 ; Ze et al. 2024 ) .
Recent VLAs further incorporate point clouds, 3D position encodings, or input-output alignment into language-conditioned control  ( Li et al. 2026a ; Qu et al. 2025 ; Li et al. 2026c ) .
Alternatively, RoboUniView learns unified-view features via 3D occupancy prediction, while 3D-VLA generates goal images and point clouds for planning  ( Liu et al. 2024 ; Zhen et al. 2024 ) .
These methods explicitly encode or reconstruct geometry  ( Han et al. 2026 ) .
In contrast, RGF uses a frozen 3D foundation model only for training-time supervision and retains the original video–action inference pathway at deployment.
 3 Method
World Action Models (WAMs) jointly predict future visual observations and executable actions from synchronized multi-view RGB observations and language instructions.
Building on this formulation, RGF transfers geometric priors from a frozen 3D foundation model to intermediate features of the video and action experts through pairwise relation alignment.
Figure  3 presents the resulting training framework and its three relational objectives.
We begin by formulating WAMs and the geometric teacher in Section  3.1 , proceed to the relational objectives in Section  3.2 , and conclude with the training objective and inference procedure in Section  3.3 .
 3.1 Preliminaries
World Action Models.
Let \mathcal{O}_{t}=\{\mathbf{I}_{t}^{v}\}_{v=1}^{V} denote a synchronized RGB observation from V camera views at time t , let \mathbf{c} denote the language condition, and let \mathbf{A}_{0:T_{a}-1}=\{\mathbf{a}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T_{a}-1} denote an action chunk.
Conditioned on the current observation and language instruction, a WAM models the joint distribution
 p_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{O}_{1:T_{v}},\mathbf{A}_{0:T_{a}-1}\mid\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathbf{c}\right), 
 (1) 
where \mathcal{O}_{1:T_{v}} contains the future multi-view observations.
We consider a general architecture with a video diffusion transformer (Video DiT) as the video expert and an action diffusion transformer (Action DiT) as the action expert.
The two experts may exchange information through attention or predicted visual representations, and are jointly optimized with video and action flow-matching losses \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{v} and \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{a} , respectively.
We apply geometric supervision to an intermediate layer indexed by \ell in both experts.
Let \mathbf{H}_{\ell,t,v}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times d_{v}} denote the N_{v} video tokens for view v at time t .
We denote the action representation by \mathbf{H}_{\ell}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{a}\times d_{a}} , which contains one token \mathbf{h}_{\ell,t}^{a} for each action step.
This notation does not assume a particular number or arrangement of cameras.
Geometric Teacher.
We use the frozen Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer (VGGT)  ( Wang et al. 2025 ) as the geometric teacher.
At each time t , VGGT jointly processes the synchronized views \mathcal{O}_{t} and produces a patch representation
 \{\mathbf{P}_{t}^{v}\}_{v=1}^{V}=\Phi_{\mathrm{3D}}(\mathcal{O}_{t}),\qquad\mathbf{P}_{t}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{v}\times d_{g}}, 
 (2) 
where M_{v} is the number of teacher patches in view v .
The per-view patch features retain spatial resolution and are used for video supervision.
For action supervision, we summarize the scene at each step by spatially pooling every view and concatenating the resulting vectors:
 \mathbf{s}_{t}=\left[\operatorname{Pool}(\mathbf{P}_{t}^{1});\ldots;\operatorname{Pool}(\mathbf{P}_{t}^{V})\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{Vd_{g}}, 
 (3) 
VGGT is evaluated independently at each time step while processing all synchronized views jointly.
Its outputs serve only as training targets and are never provided as inputs to the WAM.
 3.2 Relational Geometry Forcing
Pairwise Feature Relations.
Pointwise feature alignment assumes that WAM features and their geometric targets encode comparable content in a shared feature space.
This assumption does not hold uniformly in WAMs: video tokens encode local visual regions, whereas action tokens summarize control transitions.
RGF therefore matches the pairwise feature relations induced by each representation instead of directly regressing geometric targets.
For a token set \mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}]^{\top} , let \bar{\mathbf{X}} denote row-wise L_{2} normalization.
We define self- and cross-relation operators as
 \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X})=\bar{\mathbf{X}}\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{\top},\qquad\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\bar{\mathbf{X}}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}. 
 (4) 
The entries are pairwise cosine similarities.
Although WAM features and geometric targets may differ in dimensionality, their relation matrices have the same shape once the corresponding token sets are aligned.
We use trainable projection heads g_{\mathrm{vid}} and g_{\mathrm{act}} to decouple the supervised features from the video and action hidden states.
The projected features need not match the dimensionality of the geometric targets.
For a WAM relation matrix \mathbf{R}^{s} and its geometric target \mathbf{R}^{g} , we compute an element-wise Huber (smooth L1) loss over the valid entries \Omega :
 \mathcal{D}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{R}^{s},\mathbf{R}^{g})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}\operatorname{Huber}\left(R_{ij}^{s}-R_{ij}^{g}\right). 
 (5) 
For self-relations, \Omega excludes the diagonal; it also removes entries associated with padded tokens.
Video Relational Alignment.
We partition the video features by time and camera view and align the VGGT patches with the corresponding WAM feature grid.
When their spatial resolutions differ, the VGGT grid is resampled to obtain \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times d_{g}} .
The intra-frame objective matches the patch relations inside each view:
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intra}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,v}\left[\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{R}(g_{\mathrm{vid}}(\mathbf{H}_{\ell,t,v}^{v})),\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{v})\right)\right]. 
 (6) 
This objective transfers VGGT’s relative organization of spatial regions without direct feature regression.
Intra-frame relations do not capture how different camera projections correspond to the same scene.
For every pair of views v<u , we therefore match their cross-relation matrices:
 \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cross}} 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,v<u}\Big[\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}\Big( 
 (7) 
 \displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(g_{\mathrm{vid}}(\mathbf{H}_{\ell,t,v}^{v}),g_{\mathrm{vid}}(\mathbf{H}_{\ell,t,u}^{v})\right), 
 \displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{v},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{u}\right)\Big)\Big]. 
The resulting matrix has one axis for each view and preserves the cross-view similarity pattern induced by VGGT.
Action Relational Alignment.
Each action token \mathbf{h}_{\ell,t}^{a} represents the transition from observation \mathcal{O}_{t} , establishing a temporal correspondence with the geometric scene representation \mathbf{s}_{t} .
However, RGF does not regress an action token to this scene vector.
Instead, it compares the step-to-step similarity structure of the action sequence with the temporal similarity structure of the corresponding multi-view scenes.
Let \mathbf{S}=[\mathbf{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{T_{a}-1}]^{\top} .
The action-temporal objective is
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temporal}}=\mathcal{D}_{\Omega_{a}}\left(\mathcal{R}(g_{\mathrm{act}}(\mathbf{H}_{\ell}^{a})),\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{S})\right), 
 (8) 
where \Omega_{a} excludes self-relations and all rows and columns associated with padded action steps.
Thus, the one-to-one temporal correspondence is used to construct aligned sequences, while supervision is applied to relations between pairs of time steps.
 3.3 Training Objective and Inference
Overall Objective.
We combine the three relational terms as
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RGF}}=w_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intra}}+w_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cross}}+w_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temporal}}, 
 (9) 
and train the WAM with the full objective
 \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{v}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{a}+\lambda_{\mathrm{RGF}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RGF}}, 
 (10) 
where w_{i} , w_{c} , and w_{t} control the three relational terms and \lambda_{\mathrm{RGF}} balances geometric supervision with the original WAM objectives.
Inference.
VGGT, the projection heads, and relation computations are used only during training and removed at inference.
The WAM then follows its original video–action pathway using only RGB observations and language instructions, with no geometric inputs or additional inference modules.
Since supervision acts only on intermediate features, RGF applies to direct action prediction, joint video–action denoising, and action prediction conditioned on predicted video features.
 4 Experiments
In this section, we structure our experiments to answer the following questions:
 • 
How does RGF-WAM compare with existing VLA, WAM, and geometry-aware methods? (Section  4.2 )
 • 
How do the pretrained foundation models and alignment formulation affect geometry transfer across WAM paradigms? (Section  4.3 )
 • 
How do the relational objectives contribute individually and jointly? (Section  4.4 )
 • 
How does RGF affect learned intra-frame, cross-view, and action-temporal relations? (Section  4.5 )
 • 
Does RGF consistently improve performance across real-world manipulation tasks? (Section  4.6 )
 4.1 Experimental Setup
Simulation Benchmarks.
We evaluate RGF-WAM on LIBERO  ( Liu et al. 2023 ) and RoboTwin 2.0  ( Chen et al. 2025 ) , following the training and evaluation protocols of Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al. 2026 ) .
LIBERO includes four suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long.
Each contains 10 tasks and 500 demonstrations.
We evaluate each suite over 500 rollouts with different random seeds, totaling 2,000 rollouts across 40 tasks, and report per-suite and average success rates.
RoboTwin 2.0 includes 50 bimanual manipulation tasks covering diverse object interactions.
For each task, the training set contains 50 demonstrations in the clean setting and 500 demonstrations under scene randomization.
We evaluate 100 rollouts per task in each setting and report the clean, randomized, and average success rates.
Baselines.
We compare with representative VLA methods, including OpenVLA-OFT  ( Kim et al. 2025 ) , \pi_{0.5}   ( Intelligence et al. 2025 ) , and Spatial Forcing  ( Li et al. 2025 ) , as well as WAMs including LingBot-VA  ( Li et al. 2026b ) , Motus  ( Bi et al. 2025 ) , and Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al. 2026 ) .
We further include geometry-aware WAMs X-WAM  ( Guo et al. 2026 ) , GeoSem-WAM  ( Ma et al. 2026 ) , WAM4D  ( Li et al. 2026d ) , and MECo-WAM  ( Zhang et al. 2026a ) .
Implementation Details.
Following Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al. 2026 ) , we build RGF-WAM on Wan2.2-TI2V-5B  ( Wan et al. 2025 ) with a 1B Action DiT and evaluate three representative WAM variants: Uncond, Joint, and IDM, using Uncond by default.
All variants use an action horizon of H=32 , 9 video frames per training chunk, 10 denoising steps, and CFG scale 1.0 at inference.
Our default RGF configuration uses frozen final-layer VGGT-1B features and applies the three relational objectives with equal weights at WAM layer 15, with \lambda_{\mathrm{RGF}}=0.5 and Huber loss.

Foundation Model and Alignment.
Table  3 shows that pointwise alignment yields only marginal gains when applied to video representations.
Extending it to action features instead drops below the baseline in all three variants, exposing the weakness of pointwise feature alignment.

RGF yields gains of +1.05, +0.60, and +0.55 for the Uncond, Joint, and IDM variants, respectively, exceeding pointwise video alignment in each case.
The consistent gains across distinct video–action generation schemes show that RGF is compatible with different WAM paradigms.
Together, the DINOv2 and cross-paradigm controls show that the gains are not specific to VGGT or a particular WAM paradigm, supporting the relational formulation as the common source of improvement.

Table  4 shows that individual objectives remain close to the baseline, indicating that no relation type is sufficient alone.
Both top-performing two-objective variants include cross-view supervision, highlighting cross-view correspondence as an important geometric cue.
Combining cross-view with action-temporal supervision yields +0.40, whereas combining intra-frame with action-temporal supervision yields - 0.25, further showing that cross-view correspondence stabilizes temporal supervision.
Although action-temporal supervision alone yields - 0.15, adding it to intra-frame and cross-view supervision raises the gain from +0.30 to +1.05.
This reversal suggests that action-temporal relations become more useful when jointly grounded by intra-frame and cross-view relations.
The full model reaches +1.05, compared with +0.40 for the best two-objective variant, indicating non-additive complementarity among the three objectives.
Loss Function.
KL divergence normalizes relation scores into probability distributions, discarding their absolute scale.
Huber instead matches each relation value directly and uses a linear penalty for large residuals, preserving magnitude while limiting outlier influence.
Huber’s 98.65% result versus 98.05% for KL supports retaining relation magnitudes in the supervision signal.

Action-Temporal Relations.
For action-temporal analysis, we compare the action feature relation matrix with the scene-evolution reference.
The rollout in Figure  4 (c) starts slowly and accelerates, so early frames remain similar over a longer interval than later frames.
The scene-evolution reference reflects this motion as a wide band that progressively narrows.
The unaligned WAM produces a nearly uniform-width band, whereas RGF recovers the nonuniform pattern.
RGF therefore aligns action-temporal relations with the changing rate of geometric scene evolution.
 4.6 Real-World Experiments
Robot Setup.
As shown in Figure  5 , we evaluate real-world manipulation on a Franka Research 3 arm observed by three ZED 2i cameras.
Each policy predicts 32-step chunks of end-effector (EEF) actions.
Tasks and Protocol.
The four basic tasks cover single-object transfer, placement onto a rack, and two-stage manipulation of multiple objects.
For each task, we collect 50 demonstrations and fine-tune all methods on the same task data.
We evaluate each method over 20 rollouts per setting with randomized initial object placements shared across methods.
To assess generalization, we construct three Bowl-to-Rack variants with an unseen object color, background distractors, or altered placement heights, none of which appear during training.
Results.
Table  5 shows that RGF-WAM attains the highest average success rate, outperforming Fast-WAM by 8.6 percentage points and achieving the best or tied-best result in six of seven settings.
On the basic tasks, the largest gains over Fast-WAM occur on Two Bowls to Plate and Two Lids cover Blocks , with absolute gains of +10 and +15, respectively.
Both tasks require coordinating multiple objects over successive manipulation stages, suggesting that relational supervision becomes more beneficial as spatial and temporal dependencies increase.
On the original Bowl-to-Rack task, RGF-WAM and Fast-WAM both achieve 85%, providing a matched baseline for the three generalization settings.
Under unseen color, distractor, and height variations, RGF-WAM improves over Fast-WAM by +5, +5, and +20, respectively.
The larger gain under height variation suggests that relational supervision is particularly useful when the distribution shift changes spatial configuration.
 5 Conclusion
In this paper, we investigated how to transfer geometric priors to World Action Models without explicit geometric reconstruction.
We identified a representation mismatch in pointwise alignment between dense geometric targets and compact action features, and introduced Relational Geometry Forcing (RGF) to transfer geometry through pairwise feature relations without direct feature regression.
RGF combines intra-frame, cross-view, and action-temporal objectives to supervise video and action features while preserving the original video–action inference pathway.
Experiments in simulation and on a real Franka robot demonstrate competitive manipulation performance, real-world generalization, and consistent gains across diverse WAM paradigms.
These results indicate that relational alignment provides a compatible interface for geometric supervision across heterogeneous WAM features.
The current formulation focuses on pairwise cosine relations, leaving explicit metric scale and contact structure unexplored.
Future work may incorporate these richer geometric cues and longer-horizon dynamics while retaining RGF’s training-time relational supervision.
