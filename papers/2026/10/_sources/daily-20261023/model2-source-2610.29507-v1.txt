  Abstract
 Temporal reasoning is widely assumed to decompose into two modes:  temporal arithmetic  (date and time calculations), and  temporal semantics  (understanding temporal and causal relations). This dichotomy shapes how benchmarks are designed, how models are trained, and how progress is measured, yet its empirical validity has never been directly tested. We provide two lines of evidence that challenge it.
First, we test whether tasks labelled as arithmetic or semantic by the benchmarks support transferable gains within their purported mode. Finetuning on these subsets yields predominantly dataset-specific gains (    +  6.8   +6.8    to     +  23.1   +23.1    percentage points), while transfer to a different dataset in the same mode ranges from     −  3.2   -3.2    to     +  1.1   +1.1    percentage points. Across the two benchmark families used in the transfer analysis, this pattern provides little evidence of coherent, transferable temporal abilities.
Second, using cognitive-demand-based model evaluation, we examine which capabilities actually drive performance on these benchmarks. We find that quantitative and logical reasoning, the assumed proxies for temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics, are not the primary drivers; knowledge is.
Together, these findings show that current temporal reasoning benchmarks do not cleanly realise the arithmetic-semantics distinction and instead largely reward models for  remembering  how time typically works.
  1  Introduction
 Which cognitive abilities are required to reason about time? Prior work on large language models (LLMs)commonly decomposes temporal reasoning into two modes:  temporal arithmetic , involving calculations over times and dates, and  temporal semantics , involving the interpretation of before–after relations and temporal causality  ( Wang and Zhao, 2024 ;  Chu et al., 2024 ;  Fatemi et al., 2025 ;  Islakoglu and Kalo, 2025 ) . Both require an understanding of time, whereas temporal arithmetic additionally requires mathematical capabilities. This distinction has become a common organising principle for benchmark design, model training, and performance reporting  ( Wang and Zhao, 2024 ;  Chu et al., 2024 ;  Su et al., 2024 ;  Fatemi et al., 2025 ) . However, it remains unclear whether it reflects meaningful differences in the cognitive demands tested by current benchmarks.
 This question matters because benchmarks may not measure what they claim to measure due to poor construct validity  ( Bean et al., 2025 ) . As benchmarks become more complex, the true bottleneck may diverge from the target ability named in the task description  ( Rameshkumar et al., 2025 ;  Varela et al., 2026 ) .
For example, success on a temporal arithmetic question may depend more on numerical calculation than on temporal understanding, whereas success on a temporal semantics question may depend more on language comprehension or memorised world knowledge. The arithmetic/semantics distinction may therefore classify tasks by their surface form and not by the abilities that actually drive performance.
 We test this assumption in two ways. First, we ask whether the arithmetic/semantics distinction predicts transfer across benchmarks. We train LLMson temporal reasoning datasets and evaluate whether performance improvements generalise to unseen datasets, both within and across the two modes. If each mode reflects a stable capability with shared reasoning patterns, transfer should be strong between datasets in the same mode  ( Shao et al., 2024 ;  Hu et al., 2026 ;  Ionov et al., 2026 ) . Instead, we find that improvements remain highly localised to the training data and barely generalise to unseen data in the same purported mode.
 Second, we test whether the arithmetic/semantics split reflects differences in cognitive demands. Using the Demand-Level-Annotation (DeLeAn)rubrics  ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) , we score each benchmark item on 18 general cognitive dimensions. We compare arithmetic and semantic datasets across all DeLeAndimensions, but find no clear separation between the two modes. We then focus on the Quantitative Reasoning (QLq)and Logical Reasoning (QLl)cognitive demands, which are most naturally aligned with temporal arithmetic  ( Su et al., 2024 ;  Fatemi et al., 2025 )  and temporal semantics  ( Xiong et al., 2024 ;  Tang et al., 2025 ) , respectively. We train models on data with relatively high demands for each of the two dimensions and test whether this produces larger gains in the corresponding mode. It does not. Instead, we find that tasks depend more strongly on the cognitive demands for knowledge.
 Taken together, these results suggest that temporal reasoning benchmarks are better characterised as knowledge-heavy tasks than as tests of distinct arithmetic or semantic capabilities, with performance depending more on learned regularities and background knowledge than on explicit reasoning over temporal quantities or relations.
  2  Background
  Temporal reasoning  benchmarks commonly distinguish tasks requiring semantic temporal understanding from those that also require mathematical operations. For example,   Su et al. (2024)   note that 19 of the 38 tasks in TRAM  ( Wang and Zhao, 2024 )  involve mathematical reasoning. TimeBench  ( Chu et al., 2024 ) , which covers 10 temporal reasoning benchmarks, distinguishes symbolic tasks such as date arithmetic from those requiring only semantic temporal understanding, while Test of Time (ToT)  ( Fatemi et al., 2025 )  explicitly has an Arithmetic and a Semantic subset. Across these efforts, the arithmetic/semantic split is treated as a meaningful axis of variation, yet none tests whether it reflects separable underlying abilities.
 However, growing evidence questions the  construct validity  of LLM benchmarks—the extent to which they measure the abilities they claim to measure. Reviewing 445 benchmarks from major NLP and ML venues,   Bean et al. (2025)   find that many studies do not establish a clear link between the ability named by a benchmark and the abilities required to perform well on it.   Alaa et al. (2025)   identify a similar gap in medicine, where high scores on exam-style benchmarks do not reliably predict performance on real clinical data. More broadly,   Rameshkumar et al. (2025)   and   Varela et al. (2026)   show that the main performance bottleneck can diverge from the capability named in the task description as tasks become more complex. These findings suggest that benchmark categories require empirical validation. We apply this principle to temporal reasoning by testing whether the arithmetic/semantic labels of existing benchmarks predict transfer behaviour and cognitive abilities.
 If temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics reflect genuinely distinct and coherent abilities, then improvements in one should  transfer , at minimum, to other tasks within the same mode. This logic follows a standard rationale in the reasoning literature: training in a narrow
domain should generalise to tasks that draw on the same underlying capability.   Shao et al. (2024)   and   Guo et al. (2025)   show that reinforcement learning on mathematical benchmarks reliably transfers to other mathematical tasks and even to non-mathematical reasoning benchmarks, confirming that fine-tuning  can  yield cross-benchmark transfer when the underlying capability is shared. Whether the same holds for temporal reasoning
remains untested.   Xiong et al. (2024)   show that fine-tuning on temporal data can improve temporal reasoning, but do not test cross-mode transfer, and recent work suggests that transfer is not guaranteed across all domains  ( Hu et al., 2026 ;  Huan et al., 2026 ) . We use fine-tuning
transfer as a direct probe of the arithmetic/semantic distinction: if gains transfer within mode, the distinction has empirical grounding; if they do not, it lacks support as a description of separable abilities.

  3.1.2  Operationalising the arithmetic/semantic distinction
 We use fine-tuning transfer (see   3.3  ) between temporal arithmetic and semantics as a probe of their separability. If both modes reflect distinct abilities, then training on one should improve performance on tasks from the same mode, and not on tasks from the other. We use two complementary operationalisations to partition the data into these modes.
 The first is a  dataset-level  operationalisation based on existing mode labels. We restrict this controlled transfer analysis to ToTand TRAM, as these are the only benchmarks that provide explicit or reconstructable arithmetic/semantic partitions, minimising confounds from differences in dataset construction, such as annotation assumptions, or task format. ToTprovides an explicit split: its Arithmetic subset covers time/date arithmetic, duration, scheduling, and time zone conversion tasks, while its Semantic subset targets event–event/time, before/after, timeline, and first/last relations. TRAM does not provide such a binary partition. Instead, we follow   Su et al.  ’s (  2024  ) classification of TRAM subtasks into  math-time tasks  (equivalent to arithmetic mode) and  pure-time tasks  (equivalent to semantic mode) (see Appendix   B.2   for details).
 The second is an  item-level  operationalisation based on DeLeAnannotations (Section   3.1.1  ). We partition items by their QLqand QLldemands to construct training sets intended to probe both temporal reasoning modes. Specifically, we filter all datasets to items where demand for QLqexceeds that for QLl, and vice versa, yielding six training conditions. These include two zero-demand controls (Tab.   2  ). It is worth noting that QLqand QLlare rarely the most demanding dimensions in our data. Out of 8,530 training items, QLqis the dominant dimension in only 15 items (1,288 including ties), and QLlin only 1 item (771 including ties). We will discuss the limited role these dimensions seem to play in current benchmarks later.

  3.2  Evaluation
 We use accuracy for evaluation and additionally normalise temporal expressions before scoring, following   Abbood et al. (2025)  . The expected answer type in the dataset guides the normalisation of the model’s response. E.g., “11 Aug. 2023” and “11/08/2023” are then treated as equivalent. This prevents surface-level differences in temporal formatting from being counted as reasoning failures (details in Appendix   C  ).
 Aggregate accuracy, however, does not isolate performance on any single cognitive demand dimension, since each item carries a multidimensional DeLeAnlabel. To disentangle the contribution of individual dimensions, we construct  subject characteristic curves  using dominant slices. For a dominant dimension    i  i    and level    l  l   , we retain only items satisfying      x  i   (  j  )    =  l   x_{i}^{(j)}=l    and     x      i        ′          (  j  )    ≤  l   x_{i^{{}^{\prime}}}^{(j)}\leq l    for all     i        ′    ≠  i   i^{{}^{\prime}}\neq i   , i.e., items for which dimension    i  i    is the dominant demand. Accuracy is averaged within each level and a two-parameter logistic curve is fitted to the resulting five points, using the weighting scheme of   Zhou et al. (2026)  . We report the area under the curve (AUC)of the fitted curve as the model’s ability on that dimension.
 Four of the 18 dimensions are excluded from this analysis due to insufficient coverage in our data.   Zhou et al.   suggests excluding dimensions that have a range narrower than 1–3 or have fewer than 30 items. Spatio-physical Reasoning (SNs)and Verbal Expression (CEe)span only demand levels 1 and 2, too narrow a range for meaningful curve fitting. Conceptualisation, Learning and Abstraction (CL)and Knowledge of Natural Sciences (KNn)yield only 9 and 25 items, respectively, across all dominant slices, making estimates unreliable.
  3.3  Fine-tuning
 Our main method for investigating the separability between temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics is to fine-tune models on data for each mode and test the transfer of performance to the corresponding mode.
 GRPO.
 Temporal reasoning tasks can require multi-step reasoning. We initially attempted supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces from a larger teacher model  ( Hsieh et al., 2023 ) , without success. We attribute this to a mismatch between the teacher’s and student’s response style  ( Li et al., 2025 ) . Instead, we use Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)  ( Guo et al., 2025 ) , which sidesteps this issue by optimising directly on self-generated reasoning traces. To ensure comparability across experiments despite substantial variation in dataset sizes, we fix the compute budget to 400 optimisation steps with a batch size of 16. We use 8 generations per rollout for dataset-level operationalisation and 16 for the item-level operationalisation, due to training instabilities with 8 rollouts (details in Appendix   D  ).
 Reward.
 We define two reward settings to probe the effect of training from complementary directions. In the  accuracy  setting, the reward is 1 if the model’s response is correct and 0 otherwise, determined by the semantic equivalence metric described in Section   3.2  . In the  one-minus-accuracy  setting, we invert this signal to explore a form of targeted unlearning similar to   Zaradoukas et al. (2026)  . The model receives a reward of 1 only if its answer is incorrect but correctly formatted, 0.5 if the answer is both correct and correctly formatted (for richer feedback), and 0 otherwise. The format requirement mitigates reward hacking  ( Skalse et al., 2022 )  by preventing models from maximising reward through degenerate outputs. If temporal arithmetic and semantics are separable, unlearning one mode should degrade performance primarily on the same mode, leaving the other largely intact. For experiments using the item-level operationalisation, we additionally scale the reward by     1  +  QLq  +  QLl   1+\text{QLq}+\text{QLl}   , so that items with higher quantitative or logical demand contribute a stronger optimisation signal. The goal is to offset the low-resolution binary reward signal, which initially provided limited learning signal across many samples.
 Models.
 We run our experiments with six models from three families and two size ranges, ensuring that our findings are not an artefact of a single architecture or scale. Specifically, we use Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B  ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct  ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) , gemma-3-4B-it, and gemma-3-12B-it  ( Gemma Team, 2025 ) . Qwen models are run in non-thinking mode for comparability with the other models. During evaluation, we allow up to 4,000 output tokens and set the temperature to 0 for deterministic decoding.
  4  Results
  4.1  Cognitive demand profiles do not reflect the temporal reasoning dichotomy
 If the arithmetic/semantic distinction reflects a genuine structural property of temporal reasoning, it should be recoverable through the data’s cognitive demands. Specifically, items labelled as temporal arithmetic should exhibit elevated QLqdemands relative to QLl, while items labelled as temporal semantics should show the reverse. We test this prediction in two ways: first by examining the joint distribution of QLqand QLlacross all benchmarks, and then by examining whether the full 18-dimensional demand profiles cluster by mode.
 QLq and QLl do not diverge.
 Fig.   1   shows that the joint distribution of QLqand QLldemand levels across all benchmark items is mostly similar. Items primarily concentrate near the diagonal, with the modal demand level at 1 for both dimensions. QLltends to exceed QLqslightly more often and reaches higher levels, but the two dimensions are broadly correlated rather than orthogonal.

  4.2  Performance gains are dataset-specific and do not transfer across modes
 The cognitive demand analysis above examines what benchmarks  require ; we now test what models actually  learn . If temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics are separable abilities, then fine-tuning on one mode should improve performance on other datasets from the same mode, and not on datasets from another mode.
 To test transfer, we treat the arithmetic and semantic portions of each benchmark as separate subsets, yielding four dataset-mode combinations: ToT-Arithmetic, ToT-Semantic, TRAM-Arithmetic, and TRAM-Semantic. We fine-tune six models separately on each combination and evaluate resulting variants on all four test sets. This design allows us to test transfers across datasets within and across temporal reasoning modes.  Within- and cross-mode transfer fails.
 In the accuracy reward setting, in-domain gains are substantial and consistent (diagonal in Fig.   3  , left). These gains, however, do not transfer across benchmarks, even when the source and target task share a mode. Instead, off-diagonal transfer is essentially absent, with one partial exception: training on TRAM arithmetic improves TRAM semantic by 11.1 pp (though the within-dataset gain is far larger, 23.1 pp), and training on TRAM semantic improves TRAM arithmetic by 3.4 pp (with a 15.2 pp gain on TRAM semantic itself). Training on ToTarithmetic, by contrast, degrades TRAM performance (    −  3.2   -3.2    and     −  2.9   -2.9    pp). Inspecting model responses shows within-TRAM transfer is largely a formatting effect. The two TRAM modes share the prompt template and a single multiple-choice-letter answer space. Thus, improving answer formatting in one mode recovers otherwise-unparseable responses in the other. The effect does not replicate for ToT, whose two modes differ in both content and answer format (structured JSON vs. event-identifier sets).
 Unlearning confirms the pattern.
 The one-minus-accuracy experiments (Fig.   3  , right) provide a complementary perspective. Performance degradation mirrors the localised, dataset-specific nature of accuracy gains. This effect is most pronounced for ToTarithmetic (    −  26   -26    pp) and ToT Semantic (    −  22.8   -22.8    pp). However, degradation spreads more broadly off-diagonal than improvement does.
 Notably, gains and losses are not symmetric across reward settings. For TRAM, accuracy training transfers arithmetic improvements to semantics more strongly than the reverse, whereas unlearning reverses this asymmetry. This suggests that the two TRAM subsets share surface-level regularities that are learned and unlearned asymmetrically, rather than drawing on a common underlying ability.
 Overall, these results suggest that abilities learned during fine-tuning are highly localised to individual datasets rather than transferable improvements in temporal arithmetic or semantic reasoning. Per-model results (Appendix   F  ,
Tab.   22  ) confirm that this pattern is consistent across all six models and not driven by individual outliers.
 An exploratory experiment with Qwen3-32B produces smaller and mixed transfer effects, including a 3.7-point gain from TRAM Arithmetic to ToTArithmetic, but does not establish a consistent mode-specific pattern (Appendix   H  ).

 QLq and QLl’s subordinate role in improving temporal reasoning.
 The arithmetic and semantic modes contrast temporal reasoning that requires explicit calculation with temporal reasoning that does not. If true, we would expect QLq-dominant training to primarily improve QLq(and hence temporal arithmetic), and the equivalent for QLl-dominant training. This is not what we observe. Training on data where     QLl  =   QLq  +  1    \text{QLl}=\text{QLq}+1    yields the largest improvements for both QLqand QLl, rather than selectively boosting QLl. The     QLl  =   QLq  +  2    \text{QLl}=\text{QLq}+2    condition produces weaker gains, possibly due to its small dataset size (54 items). QLq-dominant training, where     QLq  =   QLl  +   {   1  ,  2   }     \text{QLq}=\text{QLl}+\{1,2\}   , also does not preferentially improve QLq. Because QLqdemands are generally lower than QLldemands across the benchmarks (Fig.   6   in the Appendix), QLq-dominant items may not provide a sufficiently challenging learning signal for that dimension.
 A stronger QL signal does not improve temporal accuracy.
 To test whether low QL demand in the temporal training data (mean demand of 1.57) explains the limited performance gains, we fine-tune models on QL-dominant, non-temporal data (mean demand of 4.13). Although QLland QLqboth increase by 0.15 AUC—the largest capability gains among all dimensions—accuracy on the temporal mix data remains unchanged (mean     Δ  =   −  0.06    \Delta=-0.06    pp; range     −  1.23   -1.23    to     +  0.55   +0.55   ; Appendix   G  , Tables   23  ,  24  ). Thus, stronger QL capability is insufficient to improve temporal reasoning and QL is not the principal bottleneck in model performance.
 Knowledge benefits the most.
 The most revealing finding (Fig.   4  , top) emerges when we examine which dimensions benefit most across all six training conditions. Gains in Knowledge of Social Sciences (KNs), Customary Everyday Knowledge (KNc), and Verbal Comprehension (CEc)consistently exceed gains in QLland QLq(often by a wide margin). This pattern holds regardless of whether the training data was selected for its quantitative or logical demands. In other words, even when we deliberately select items that emphasise arithmetic or logical reasoning, models primarily improve on KNc, KNsand CEc. The one-minus-accuracy results (Fig.   4  , bottom) corroborate this from the reverse direction: the largest capability degradations occur for the KNsdimension, confirming that these are the abilities most exercised by current temporal benchmarks. The per-benchmark demand distributions (Appendix   B.4  , Fig.   7  ) provide further support. Most benchmarks concentrate their QLqand QLldemands at levels 0–2, with few items at the higher levels, which would be necessary to expect reasoning bottlenecks.
 Relabelling with Gemini largely reproduces the KN-over-QL pattern seen in Fig.   4  , although KNsrather than KNcbenefits most and CEcgains are smaller (details in Appendix   E  ).
 These results suggest that logical and quantitative reasoning are not the primary drivers of performance on current temporal reasoning benchmarks. Training on examples selected for their logical or quantitative demands does affect those dimensions, but the dominant effect falls on knowledge dimensions. This finding reinforces the conclusion from Sections   4.1   and   4.2  : existing temporal reasoning benchmarks do not cleanly instantiate the arithmetic/semantics distinction. Instead, they largely measure models’ capacity to retrieve and apply learned temporal regularities.
  5  Conclusions
 This study scrutinises whether temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics correspond to distinct reasoning abilities. Capability-aware analyses of the dataset and fine-tuning experiments show that they do not. Cognitive demand profiles fail to separate arithmetic from semantic benchmark items, despite supposed differences in required quantitative and logical reasoning. Furthermore, finetuning gains do not transfer between benchmarks of the same temporal reasoning mode.
 Capability changes after finetuning on temporal data show that DeLeAnknowledge dimensions improve more than QLqor QLl. Specialised QL-heavy training could raise QL capability, but still did not improve performance in either temporal reasoning modes. Our results suggest that current benchmarks test the retrieval and application of time-related regularities, not arithmetic or semantic abilities. The arithmetic/semantics taxonomy may describe task form, but it should not be treated as a salient decomposition of temporal reasoning. Future benchmarks should control for knowledge demands and validate proposed categories through capability-based analyses.
  6  Limitations
 Our experiments use models of up to 12 billion parameters. In one instance, we trained a larger model, which showed small transfer (improvement of       ≈  3   \approx 3    percentage points) in the arithmetic mode. Future work could explore transfer dynamics in even larger models; however, the consistency of our findings across three families and two scales suggests that this pattern is robust. Furthermore, our transfer experiments are limited to ToTand TRAM, which are the only benchmarks with explicit or reconstructable arithmetic and semantic partitions. Extending this analysis to future benchmarks that provide more detailed task taxonomies would strengthen the generalisability of our findings.

  Appendix A  Demand level annotations (DeLeAn)
 Demand-Level Annotations (DeLeAn) are a set of general-scale, human-interpretable rubrics that map a benchmark item     X  j   X_{j}    to a multi-dimensional  demand profile  intended to capture which cognitive and knowledge demands a fully competent solver would need to meet in order to answer correctly  ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) . Concretely, each item is assigned an annotation vector      x   (  j  )    ∈    {  0  ,  …  ,   5  +   }   18    x^{(j)}\in\{0,\dots,5^{+}\}^{18}   , where each component     x  i   (  j  )    x_{i}^{(j)}    is an open-scale demand level on dimension    i  i    (0 = no meaningful demand; larger values indicate increasing demand;     5  +   5^{+}    indicates demands beyond the highest anchor point of the rubric). In this paper, we use these demand vectors as structured, interpretable item-level features for analysing which cognitive capabilities current temporal reasoning benchmarks actually require.
 The DeLeAn rubrics build upon seven broad capabilities proposed in  ( Tolan et al., 2021 ) , which are refined into 11 core cognitive dimensions. In addition, the framework incorporates five knowledge-related dimensions and two extraneous ones (atypicality and volume) designed to capture task-specific complexity that goes beyond basic cognitive or knowledge demands. While the authors also define an additional extraneous dimension, unguessability, computed algorithmically based on prompt-specific characteristics, it is not considered in this work.   B.2  Partitioning TRAM into arithmetic and semantic tasks

  Appendix C  Metrics
 To reduce spurious mismatches caused by formatting variability, we evaluate model outputs with benchmark-specific post-processing before computing final scores. Across our evaluation modules, normalisation generally takes one of three forms: extracting the answer from a larger generated response, canonicalising the representation of the answer, and then comparing either normalised strings, normalised structured objects, or normalised sets. The exact procedure differs by benchmark.
  C.1  TRAM accuracy
 For TRAM, we normalise predictions by extracting the final option letter from responses matching the template “The final answer is (X)”, with     X  ∈   {   A  ,  B  ,  C  ,  D   }    \text{X}\in\{A,B,C,D\}   . We then compute exact-match accuracy against the gold option label. This removes variability in preceding reasoning text but still requires a standardised final-answer template.
  C.2  TCP accuracy
 For TCP, the evaluator expects the model’s final answer to appear in boxed LaTeX notation, using the pattern  \boxed{...} . It extracts the boxed span with the regular expression  \\boxed\{([^}]*)\}  and strips surrounding white space from the extracted content. Comparison is then performed by exact string match.
 In the TCP Short subset only, the evaluator removes the literal string “GMT” from both the prediction and the reference before comparison; no analogous normalisation is applied for TCP Long. The system prompt includes the exact expected answer format.
  C.3  Test of Time
 For ToT, we extract the first valid JSON object from the model output, after escaping unescaped control characters inside JSON strings. We discard the explanation field and compare only the answer-bearing content. In the Arithmetic subset, we additionally normalise schema mismatches such as age versus answer, lowercase items in  ordered_list  and  unordered_list  answer types, cast prediction values to the reference types when possible, and sort  unordered_list  values to make comparison order-invariant. In the Semantic subset, we normalise both prediction and reference by converting them to strings before exact comparison.
  C.4  TimeBench derived benchmarks
 For TimeBench tasks, we first extract the final answer from the text following the marker “Thus, the correct answer is:”. We retain only the first subsequent line, strip trailing sentence punctuation, and canonicalise any response containing unanswerable to the label unanswerable. For TempReason, TimeQA, and MenatQA, the extracted answer is scored with standard SQuAD exact match and F1. For Date Arithmetic, predictions and references are parsed into date objects and normalised by setting the day to 1, so comparison is effectively at the month-year level. For TimeDial, prediction and reference are mapped to sets of selected options in A,B,C,D and scored using set exact match and set-based F1.
  C.5  Non-temporal multiple-choice accuracy
 For single-answer multiple-choice examples from the Civil Service Examination, LSAT, MMLU-Pro, and the relevant Math tasks, we inspect the final 300 characters of the completion. We preferentially extract the final option letter in     {  A  ,  …  ,  J  }   \{\mathrm{A},\ldots,\mathrm{J}\}    appearing in an answer-bearing expression such as “the answer is X”, “answer: X”, “option X”, or “choice X”, or as a standalone line. If no such expression is found, we fall back to the final isolated letter in this range. The extracted letter is uppercased and compared by exact match with the gold option label.
  C.6  TruthQuest accuracy
 For TruthQuest, the reference annotation is converted to the set of option labels corresponding to knights. From the final 300 characters of the completion, we collect option letters in answer-bearing expressions; if none are present, we collect all isolated letters in     {  A  ,  …  ,  J  }   \{\mathrm{A},\ldots,\mathrm{J}\}   . Both prediction and reference are treated as sets, so ordering and duplicate labels are ignored. A reward of 1 is assigned only when the predicted and reference sets are identical.
  C.7  Math and OmniMath accuracy
 Math examples use several answer representations. Multiple-choice items follow the option-letter procedure above. For integer-valued Math and OmniMath items, we extract the final integer appearing in the last 200 characters of the completion, cast it to an integer, and compare it exactly with the reference. For sequence-valued Math items, we extract the last valid JSON array from the complete response and compare it directly with the reference list; this comparison is order-sensitive.
 For fraction-valued OmniMath items, we parse a LaTeX fraction written using  \frac ,  \dfrac , or  \tfrac . If no valid LaTeX fraction is found, we use the final plain-text fraction of the form     p  /  q   p/q   . The numerator and denominator are converted to an exact rational number and reduced to the canonical string     p  /  q   p/q    before exact comparison with the similarly normalised reference.
  C.8  ChemLLMBench and Reasoning accuracy
 For the SMILES-valued ChemLLMBench examples, we scan the completion from right to left for a whitespace-delimited SMILES-like token that can be parsed by RDKit. The predicted molecule is converted to canonical isomeric SMILES and compared by exact string match with the canonicalised reference.
 For free-text Reasoning examples, we inspect the final 300 characters of the completion, lowercase the text, remove punctuation, and collapse consecutive whitespace. We apply the same normalisation to the reference and award a match when the normalised reference occurs as a substring of the normalised completion.
  Appendix D  Training settings
 We used Transformers 4.57.3  ( Wolf et al., 2020 )  to load the models, TRL 0.27.2  ( von Werra et al., 2020 )  for GRPO, and PEFT 0.18.0  ( Mangrulkar et al., 2022 )  for LoRA fine-tuning  ( Hu et al., 2022 ) . LoRA used rank     r  =  16   r=16    and scaling parameter     α  =  32   \alpha=32    and was applied to the  q_proj ,  k_proj ,  v_proj , and  o_proj  modules. The maximum GRPOcompletion length was 3,000 tokens.
 All GRPO runs used a learning rate of     5  ×   10   −  5     5\times 10^{-5}    with linear decay, training seed 42, no KL penalty (    β  =  0   \beta=0   ), and no warmup. Dataset splits used seed 6; the temporal mixture’s initial stratified train/test split used seed 42.
 Training used PyTorch 2.9.0  ( Ansel et al., 2024 ) , Accelerate 1.12.0  ( Gugger et al., 2022 ) , and DeepSpeed 0.18.3  ( Rasley et al., 2020 ) . Regular single-node training used DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 , whereas Qwen3-32B training used ZeRO-3  ( Rajbhandari et al., 2020 ) . Evaluation was performed using vLLM 0.11.2  ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) .
 DeLeAn-filtered and non-temporal training runs allocated one GPU for training and one GPU for vLLM generation. Single-dataset training runs allocated two GPUs for training and one GPU for generation. Qwen3-32B training used three compute nodes equipped with four NVIDIA GH200 GPUs each: eight GPUs across two nodes were used for training, while four GPUs on the third node were used for generation. Evaluation used one GPU per run. Full training runs generally took several hours, with longer runs approaching or exceeding 12 hours. Individual evaluations generally took approximately 1–8 minutes, although some larger evaluations took longer.

  Appendix F  Per-model results
 The transfer results reported in Section   4.2   are averaged across six models. Tab.   22   disaggregates these results, reporting the baseline accuracy range (min, median, max across models), the mean and median performance delta, and the number of models that improved versus worsened for each train–test combination under both reward settings.
 Several patterns are worth noting. First, in-domain gains under the accuracy reward are consistent across all six models: every model improves on its own training set (6/0 in all diagonal cells). Second, the absence of cross-benchmark transfer is not driven by individual outliers. For most off-diagonal cells, the number of models that improve versus worsen is close to 3/3, indicating that the lack of transfer is a stable property of the task structure rather than an artefact of averaging. Third, under the one-minus-accuracy reward, unlearning is similarly concentrated: for example, training on ToTArithmetic degrades all six models on ToTArithmetic (0/6) and five of six on TRAM Arithmetic (1/5), confirming that the unlearning signal is reliably absorbed. Finally, the wide baseline range (e.g., 25.74–87.74 for TRAM Arithmetic) reflects genuine variation in model strength, yet the direction of transfer effects is consistent across this range, further supporting the robustness of our conclusions.

  Appendix H  Larger models
 We also explored scaling the experiments to larger models, but the computational cost of GRPO prevented us from running the full experimental suite. For example, the length of some ToTprompts, which can span several thousand tokens, would have been much higher than for TRAM. Nevertheless, we trained Qwen3-32B separately on the TRAM Arithmetic and Semantic subsets using the standard accuracy-reward configuration and evaluated both variants on all TRAM and ToTsubsets. Figure   9   reports the resulting changes in accuracy.
 Training on TRAM Arithmetic improves ToTArithmetic by 3.7 percentage points, nearly matching the 3.9-point in-domain gain, while ToTSemantic improves by 1.9 points. Training on TRAM Semantic improves ToTSemantic by 1.9 points, but also increases TRAM Arithmetic by 2.7 points. Overall, however, the changes range from     −  0.2   -0.2    to     +  3.9   +3.9    points, substantially smaller than the in-domain gains of     +  6.8   +6.8    to     +  23.1   +23.1    points observed across the models in Figure   3  . These results therefore provide tentative evidence of arithmetic transfer, but not a consistent mode-specific transfer pattern. Because they come from a single larger model, it remains unclear whether the observed pattern reflects model scale or ordinary performance variation.
