Abstract
Agent harnesses have become a central component of modern agentic systems,
shaping how models use tools, manage context, and coordinate long-horizon
interaction. Realizing the benefits of these harnesses depends on how
effectively the underlying model can make use of them, motivating
reinforcement learning directly through the harness. However, existing
approaches typically train through a single fixed harness, which can lead
to specialization and limited transfer across harness configurations.
To address this, we introduce Harness Scaling , a new scaling
dimension for agentic RL that extends training from a single harness to
diverse execution configurations.
Concretely, we construct a factorized
harness space, select a compact training subset with balanced factorial
coverage, and organize cross-harness training to combine global coverage
with controlled variation for the same task.
Across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling
consistently improves generalization to unseen white-box configurations.
Moreover, models trained through structured white-box variation transfer
effectively to unseen external black-box harnesses, outperforming models
trained through a single complex black-box harness. These results establish
harness diversity as a meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL and
highlight the importance of how this diversity is organized for
cross-harness generalization.
1 Introduction
Agent harnesses are playing an increasingly central role in modern
agentic systems [ 31 ] . By supporting sustained interaction, tool use, state
maintenance, and recovery from failures [ 4 ] , harnesses have substantially
improved agent performance on complex, long-horizon tasks [ 38 , 32 , 16 ] . Yet the
benefits of a capable harness depend not only on its design, but also on
whether the underlying policy can use it effectively [ 14 ] . This naturally motivates optimizing the policy directly through the harness
with reinforcement learning [ 36 , 39 ] , which strengthens its ability to exploit the
available interaction mechanisms during task solving.
However, existing approaches typically optimize the policy through a
single fixed harness [ 7 , 34 ] . While effective within the training harness, such
optimization may encourage specialization to a particular execution
configuration and weaken transfer across harnesses. We therefore introduce
Harness Scaling , extending policy optimization from a single
harness to diverse execution configurations.
Unlike task or environment
scaling [ 29 , 6 , 3 , 35 ] , which expands what agents solve or where they operate, Harness
Scaling introduces diversity in how policies execute and interact with the
same underlying task and environment, promoting generalization across
execution configurations.
A key consideration in Harness Scaling is how to model harness variation
and select a compact set of configurations for training.
Given the modular
nature of agent harnesses [ 7 , 23 ] , we factorize harness variation along five
components: system prompt, tool interface, observation, context management, and workflow.
Each component contains multiple realizations, whose combinations define a
factorial space of complete harness configurations. As variation along these
components increases, the space expands rapidly, while exhaustive training
can introduce substantial redundancy under a fixed budget. We therefore
preserve low-order factorial structure in a compact training set, balancing
individual component realizations and their pairwise combinations through
an orthogonal design [ 21 , 8 ] . During reinforcement learning, the same task is paired
with structurally nearby harness configurations, providing controlled local
variation within each task while maintaining broad coverage across the
training space.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of Harness Scaling, we evaluate
cross-harness generalization across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and
RL algorithms. By training across diverse harness configurations, Harness
Scaling reduces specialization to any single harness and encourages more
generalizable agent behavior. Empirically, it consistently outperforms
single-harness RL on unseen white-box configurations.
More importantly, policies trained through structured white-box harness
variation transfer effectively to complex external black-box harnesses [ 18 , 1 ] ,
outperforming single-harness black-box training.
These results establish harness diversity as a
meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL, while showing that effective
scaling depends critically on how this diversity is organized during
training. In summary, our contributions are threefold:
•
We introduce Harness Scaling as a new scaling dimension
for agentic reinforcement learning, distinct from conventional scaling
over tasks and environments, and study how training across diverse
harness configurations affects generalization beyond a single execution
setting.
•
We develop a structured framework for Harness Scaling that
factorizes harness variation, selects a compact training set with balanced
factorial coverage, and organizes cross-harness training by assigning
structurally nearby configurations to the same task across the training space.
•
We demonstrate across benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms
that Harness Scaling substantially improves generalization to unseen
white-box and external black-box harnesses, establishing both the value
of scaling across harnesses and the importance of organizing this scaling
structurally.
2 Related Work
2.1 Agentic Reinforcement Learning
Reinforcement learning has emerged as an important paradigm for improving
the capabilities of LLMs [ 40 , 24 ] . Recent work has extended RL from single-turn
reasoning to increasingly complex agentic settings, including tool use [ 2 , 28 ] , web
navigation [ 20 ] , software engineering [ 27 ] , and professional workflows [ 33 ] . As agentic RL moves toward broader
capability, recent efforts have increasingly scaled training along the
task and environment dimensions. Task scaling [ 30 , 3 , 5 ] broadens the
distribution of problems used for policy optimization, while environment
scaling [ 6 , 29 , 35 ] expands the range of external settings in which agents interact and
receive feedback. These directions expand what agents learn to
solve and where they learn to operate, while often retaining a fixed
execution harness. In contrast, we study harness diversity as an additional
scaling dimension for agentic RL, focusing on policy learning and
generalization across execution configurations.
2.2 Harness Design and Optimization
The design of an agent harness can substantially shape how a language model
uses tools, maintains context, and performs long-horizon tasks [ 38 , 14 , 16 ] .
Early agent systems largely relied on manually designed harnesses, while
subsequent work has explored automated harness improvement through search,
editing, and evolution based on task outcomes or execution
trajectories [ 13 , 11 , 23 ] . A complementary line of work directly optimizes
model policies within complex agent harnesses, including reinforcement
learning through black-box harnesses [ 36 , 7 , 26 , 15 , 17 , 9 ] . These directions either
improve the harness itself or adapt a policy to a particular harness.
Harness Scaling instead studies policy optimization over a structured combinatorial space of harness configurations, with the goal of improving generalization to unseen configurations.

3.1 Problem Formulation
To study harness scaling systematically, we first formalize the space of
execution configurations and the generalization problem defined over it.
We construct a factorized harness space, define a structural notion of
proximity among its configurations, and formulate the generalization
objectives that motivate the structured harness selection and multi-harness
training procedures shown as in Figure 1 .
3.1.1 Factorized Harness Space
A harness serves as the execution layer through which a policy interacts
with its environment. Rather than treating it as a monolithic implementation,
we factorize the harness into five components: system prompt ,
tool interface , observation , context management ,
and workflow , denoted by S , T , O , C , S,T,O,C, and W W , respectively.
These components govern the instructions provided to the policy, tool access,
environment feedback, interaction history, and execution procedure,
collectively shaping how the policy perceives, acts, and maintains state
throughout task execution. This factorization provides an operational
decomposition of the harness, while allowing the effects of different
components to interact.
We instantiate each component through a family of component-specific
transformations. For a component F ∈ { S , T , O , C , W } F\in\{S,T,O,C,W\} , let F ( 0 ) F^{(0)}
denote its base realization and τ F ( ℓ ) \tau_{F}^{(\ell)} the ℓ \ell -th
transformation applied to it:
F ( ℓ ) = τ F ( ℓ ) ​ ( F ( 0 ) ) . F^{(\ell)}=\tau_{F}^{(\ell)}\left(F^{(0)}\right).
(1)
A transformation modifies only the target component while preserving the
remaining components, task semantics, and environment. For example, it may
reorganize the system prompt, alter tool schemas or naming conventions,
change observation feedback, modify context retention or compression, or
impose a different execution procedure. Detailed definitions and
implementations are provided in Appendix A .
Let 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , 𝒯 \mathcal{T} , 𝒪 \mathcal{O} , 𝒞 \mathcal{C} ,
and 𝒲 \mathcal{W} denote the resulting realization sets. A complete harness
configuration selects one realization from each set, defining the factorized
harness space
ℋ = 𝒮 × 𝒯 × 𝒪 × 𝒞 × 𝒲 . \mathcal{H}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{W}.
(2)
Each configuration h ∈ ℋ h\in\mathcal{H} therefore specifies a complete execution
setting for the same underlying task and environment.
The product structure of ℋ \mathcal{H} also induces a natural notion of
structural proximity. For h i , h j ∈ ℋ h_{i},h_{j}\in\mathcal{H} , the Hamming distance
d H ​ ( h i , h j ) d_{\mathrm{H}}(h_{i},h_{j}) counts the number of component dimensions on
which they differ. Equivalently, ℋ \mathcal{H} forms a Hamming graph whose
adjacent configurations differ in exactly one component. We use this
distance to organize local cross-harness variation during training.
3.1.2 Generalization Objective
The central objective of Harness Scaling is to learn policies that remain
effective beyond the harness configurations observed during training. We
consider two complementary forms of cross-harness generalization: broad
combinatorial generalization within the constructed harness space and transfer
to complex real-world black-box harnesses beyond it.
In-Space Combinatorial Generalization.
Let ℋ tr ⊂ ℋ \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}\subset\mathcal{H} denote the harness
configurations used for training. We evaluate on held-out configurations in
ℋ ∖ ℋ tr \mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} , whose complete
combinations are unseen during training. This setting provides broad coverage
of unseen combinations within the controlled factorized space.
Out-of-Space Generalization.
We further evaluate transfer to complex real-world black-box harnesses whose
execution configurations are not represented in ℋ \mathcal{H} . This setting
examines whether training over structured white-box harness variation can
transfer to external execution systems beyond the constructed design space.
3.2 Harness Scaling with Structured Selection
As the factorized harness space grows combinatorially with its component
dimensions, exhaustive training can introduce substantial redundancy.
We therefore construct a compact training set that preserves broad factorial
coverage under a fixed optimization budget.
3.2.1 Factorial Coverage
The harness space in Eq. 2 can be viewed as a
factorial design, where each harness component corresponds to a factor
and its realizations correspond to different levels [ 21 , 8 ] . To construct a compact
subset without losing basic coverage of this combinatorial space, we preserve
its low-order factorial structure: the representation of individual component
realizations and their pairwise combinations. These correspond to the
first- and second-order projections of the full factorial space, respectively.
Component Balance.
We first require the realizations of each component to be represented
evenly across the selected configurations. This ensures coverage along
every component dimension without overrepresenting particular realizations.
Pairwise Combination Balance.
Component balance alone does not control how realizations from different
components are combined. We therefore additionally balance all pairwise
combinations. Indexing the five component dimensions by
i , j ∈ { 1 , … , 5 } i,j\in\{1,\ldots,5\} , let N i ​ j ​ ( ℓ , ℓ ′ ) N_{ij}(\ell,\ell^{\prime}) denote the number
of selected configurations containing the ℓ \ell -th realization of
component i i together with the ℓ ′ \ell^{\prime} -th realization of component
j j . Pairwise balance requires
N i ​ j ​ ( ℓ , ℓ ′ ) = λ , N_{ij}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})=\lambda,
(3)
where λ \lambda is the common occurrence count for every realization
pair of any i ≠ j i\neq j . In factorial-design terminology, this corresponds
to strength-2 balance, under which every pair of realizations from any two
components appears equally often in the selected set.
3.2.2 Harness Set Construction
We construct the training harness set using a strength-2 orthogonal design
over the component realizations in ℋ \mathcal{H} . This provides a direct
construction of a compact subset satisfying the component-level and pairwise
balance defined above, without searching over possible subsets of the full
harness space.
The size of such a design is constrained by its pairwise coverage.
Suppose there are M M component dimensions, each with L L realizations,
and every pairwise realization combination occurs λ \lambda times.
Since any two components admit L 2 L^{2} realization combinations, the number
of selected configurations N N satisfies
N = λ ​ L 2 . N=\lambda L^{2}.
(4)
Moreover, a strength-2 orthogonal design must satisfy the Rao
bound [ 21 , 8 ] ,
N ≥ 1 + M ⁡ ( L − 1 ) . N\geq 1+M(L-1).
(5)
Pairwise balance determines the replication structure of the design, while
the Rao bound provides a lower bound on the number of configurations.
These conditions restrict the admissible design sizes.
We instantiate a strength-2 orthogonal design satisfying these constraints,
yielding the training harness set
ℋ tr ⊂ ℋ \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}\subset\mathcal{H} . The resulting set preserves
exact component-level and pairwise balance using only a compact subset of the
full factorial space. While these low-order projections are balanced, many
complete harness combinations remain unseen during training, enabling
evaluation of in-space combinatorial generalization. Further details of the
construction and the resulting training configurations are provided in
Appendix B .
3.3 Reinforcement Learning across Harnesses
Training each task under multiple harnesses exposes the policy to different
execution configurations for the same underlying problem, encouraging the
learned behavior to generalize beyond a single harness. We realize this over
the selected harness set ℋ tr \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} by pairing each task
with a small group of structurally related configurations. The assignment is
designed to combine controlled variation within each task with broad coverage
across the training harness space, and the resulting task–harness instances
form the basis for rollout collection and policy optimization.
3.3.1 Cross-Harness Task Assignment
We use a locality-aware strategy to organize how harness configurations are
assigned to tasks. For each task x x , we assign K K configurations from
ℋ tr \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} .
An anchor harness
h x ∼ Unif ⁡ ( ℋ tr ) h_{x}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}) is first sampled
uniformly from the training set, and the remaining K − 1 K-1 configurations
are chosen from ℋ tr ∖ { h x } \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}\setminus\{h_{x}\} as those
with the smallest Hamming distance d H ​ ( h x , h ) d_{\mathrm{H}}(h_{x},h) to the anchor.
This associates each task with a
small set of nearby configurations while allowing different tasks to cover
different regions of the training harness space. The resulting harness set
for task x x is
ℋ x = { h x , h x ( 1 ) , … , h x ( K − 1 ) } , \mathcal{H}_{x}=\left\{h_{x},\,h_{x}^{(1)},\ldots,h_{x}^{(K-1)}\right\},
(6)
where h x ( 1 ) , … , h x ( K − 1 ) h_{x}^{(1)},\ldots,h_{x}^{(K-1)} denote the K − 1 K-1 nearest
configurations to h x h_{x} under d H d_{\mathrm{H}} .
This assignment combines local cross-harness variation with broad coverage
over training. For a given task, nearby harnesses provide related but distinct
execution configurations, while random anchor selection distributes different
tasks throughout ℋ tr \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} . Each task therefore yields
K K task–harness instances for rollout collection and policy optimization.
When K = 1 K=1 , only the sampled anchor is assigned to the task, while different
tasks remain distributed across ℋ tr \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} .

harness, producing a trajectory τ \tau and the corresponding reward.
The K K harnesses assigned to the same task are used over K K
successive training steps, allowing the policy to receive learning signals
from different execution configurations for the same underlying task.

and then report the results and detailed analysis.
4.1 Experiment Settings
Harness Scaling Setup.
We instantiate five harness components with three realizations each,
yielding 3 5 = 243 3^{5}=243 configurations in the full harness space and forming
the Hamming graph H ⁡ ( 5 , 3 ) H(5,3) . Following the strength-2 orthogonal-design
construction in Section 3.2.2 , the resulting

ℋ tr \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} .
Training.

For GRPO, each step uses 16 tasks with 8 rollouts per task, while PPO uses
128 tasks with one rollout per task. We set K = 2 K=2 harnesses per task and train for 300 optimization
steps. Additional implementation details
and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix C.1 .

Evaluation.
We evaluate on three benchmarks with varying domains and difficulty levels.
ClawGym-Bench [ 3 ] covers workspace-based general agent tasks, PinchBench [ 19 ] focuses
on realistic tasks involving research, coding, scheduling, and file
operations, and JobBench-Easy [ 12 ] evaluates more challenging professional
workflows over heterogeneous workspace files.
All evaluation tasks are disjoint from the ClawGym-Task training set.
For in-space generalization, we evaluate on 18 fixed held-out configurations
from ℋ ∖ ℋ tr \mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} , selected for balanced
component coverage and diversity across complete harness configurations.
The same held-out set is used across all benchmarks, model scales, and RL
algorithms. For out-of-space generalization, we use Claude Code [ 1 ] as the main
external black-box harness and additionally evaluate on OpenClaw [ 18 ] .
We report Pass@1 averaged over three independent evaluation runs, with
in-space scores further averaged across the 18 held-out configurations.
Further evaluation details and OpenClaw results are provided in
Appendix C.2 .
Baselines.
We compare against four baselines: the original backbone model without
additional training; single-harness RL, where the policy is optimized
through the fixed base configuration
h base = ( S ( 0 ) , T ( 0 ) , O ( 0 ) , C ( 0 ) , W ( 0 ) ) h_{\mathrm{base}}=(S^{(0)},T^{(0)},O^{(0)},C^{(0)},W^{(0)}) ;
black-box SFT, which fine-tunes the policy on trajectories collected through
a complex black-box harness; and black-box RL, which optimizes the policy
through the same black-box harness. We use OpenClaw as the black-box training
harness, while Claude Code is reserved as an unseen external harness for
evaluation. All RL baselines use matched optimization budgets where
applicable.

4.2 Main Results
∙ \bullet Combinatorial Generalization to Unseen Harnesses.
Compared with Single-Harness RL, Harness Scaling consistently improves
performance on held-out harness configurations whose complete combinations
are unseen during training, as summarized in Table 1
and further illustrated by Figure 2 .
Averaged over the three benchmarks, the in-space gains reach + 9.80 +9.80
and + 3.57 +3.57 points with GRPO and PPO on Qwen3-4B-Instruct, and
+ 5.21 +5.21 and + 4.90 +4.90 points on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct.
The advantage also persists when Single-Harness RL is trained on three
additional fixed harnesses selected solely by their structural positions
in ℋ tr \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}} . Detailed sensitivity results are reported
in Appendix C.3 .
These results support the benefit of Harness Scaling for combinatorial
generalization within the constructed harness space.

∙ \bullet Consistency across Model Scales and RL Algorithms.
Harness scaling yields consistent gains across both backbone sizes and optimization methods (Table 1 ). For Qwen3-4B-Instruct, the overall average improves from 29.81 to 39.04 with GRPO and from 33.91 to 36.93 with PPO. Similar improvements are observed for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, from 37.29 to 42.78 with GRPO and from 36.31 to 41.89 with PPO. The improvement therefore persists across both model scale
and RL algorithm rather than being specific to a single training setting.
Additional out-of-space evaluation on OpenClaw shows the same trend in

∙ \bullet Transfer to External Black-Box Harnesses.
Harness Scaling further transfers to the external black-box
harness.
As shown in
Table 2 , it achieves average Pass@1 scores of
37.22 and 41.20 with Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct,
respectively, exceeding black-box SFT (35.08 and 38.08) and black-box RL
(33.38 and 36.28). Both black-box baselines are trained through OpenClaw
and transferred to Claude Code without further adaptation. This comparison
shows that structured training over diverse white-box harnesses can yield
stronger cross-harness transfer than direct training through a single
complex black-box harness.

5.1 Harness-Related Capability Analysis
Harness Scaling strengthens capabilities that are directly relevant to
operating under different harness configurations.

execution procedures, and recovery from failures. Instruction following

particularly strengthens capabilities needed to sustain effective execution
as the surrounding harness changes.

6 Conclusion
In this work, we study Harness Scaling as a new scaling dimension for agentic
reinforcement learning, motivated by the limitations of optimizing policies
through a single fixed harness. We formalize harness diversity as a structured configuration space and
develop a framework that integrates structured harness selection with
cross-harness policy training. Across benchmarks,
model scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling substantially improves
generalization to unseen white-box configurations and transfers effectively
to complex external black-box harnesses. Our results further show that the
benefits of scaling depend on how harness diversity is selected and incorporated into training, rather than on diversity alone.
More broadly, these findings expand the scope of agentic RL scaling beyond
what agents solve and where they operate to how they interact and execute,
positioning harness variation as a complementary dimension for improving
generalization across diverse execution systems.

Appendix A Harness Component Variants
We instantiate the factorized harness space through a model-driven
transformation procedure. Starting from the base harness, Opus-4.7 generates
alternative realizations for each of the five components: system prompt
( S S ), tool interface ( T T ), observation ( O O ), context management
( C C ), and workflow ( W W ). Each transformation targets a single
component and introduces a specified form of variation while preserving the
underlying task, environment, and functionality of the remaining harness
components.
The transformation procedure explicitly constrains non-target components to
remain unchanged in task semantics, environment behavior, and functionality,
so that each resulting realization primarily reflects variation along the
designated component dimension.

Appendix B Additional Details on Structured Harness Selection
B.1 Orthogonal Design and Coverage
We provide additional details on the structured harness selection in
Section 3.2.2 . Recall that the harness space
contains M M component dimensions, each with L L realizations. We
construct the training harness set using a strength-2 orthogonal design,
under which every realization pair from any two component dimensions occurs
equally often.
Proposition B.1 (Coverage of a Strength-2 Orthogonal Design) .
Consider a strength-2 orthogonal design over M M component dimensions,
each with L L realizations. If every pairwise realization combination
occurs exactly λ \lambda times, then every realization of each component
appears exactly λ ​ L \lambda L times, and the total number of selected
configurations satisfies
N = λ ​ L 2 . N=\lambda L^{2}.
(8)
Proof.
Consider any two component dimensions. By the strength-2 property, each of
their L 2 L^{2} realization pairs occurs exactly λ \lambda times. Fixing one
realization of the first component and summing over all L L realizations
of the second shows that the fixed realization appears exactly
λ ​ L \lambda L times. Therefore, every realization within each component is
represented equally often.
Moreover, the L 2 L^{2} realization pairs between any two components
partition the selected configurations. Since each pair occurs
λ \lambda times, the total number of configurations is
N = λ ​ L 2 . N=\lambda L^{2}.
∎
A strength-2 orthogonal design additionally satisfies the classical Rao
bound [ 21 , 8 ] ,
N ≥ 1 + M ⁡ ( L − 1 ) . N\geq 1+M(L-1).
(9)
The bound provides a lower bound on the number of configurations required
for a strength-2 orthogonal design.
In our harness space, we use M = 5 M=5 component dimensions with
L = 3 L=3 realizations per component. Proposition B.1
requires
N = 9 ​ λ , N=9\lambda,
(10)
while the Rao bound gives
N ≥ 1 + 5 ​ ( 3 − 1 ) = 11 . N\geq 1+5(3-1)=11.
(11)
Since N N must be a multiple of 9 9 , the smallest design size permitted
by these constraints is N = 18 N=18 . We instantiate a corresponding
strength-2 orthogonal design with λ = 2 \lambda=2 . Under this design, each
realization of every harness component appears six times, while every
realization pair across any two components appears exactly twice.
The resulting 18-harness set provides balanced first-order and pairwise
coverage of the full factorial space while leaving many higher-order
configurations unseen during training. This provides a compact training set
for Harness Scaling while retaining held-out combinations for evaluating
in-space combinatorial generalization.

Training with Context Compaction.
Context compaction can split a single rollout into multiple training
segments. All segments originating from the same rollout share the same
rollout-level reward and participate in policy optimization, causing the
number of training segments in a batch to vary dynamically. Segment
expansion does not alter the rollout-level grouping used by GRPO: for a
group of eight original rollouts, reward normalization is always computed
over the eight rollout rewards, regardless of how many segments each
rollout produces. Segments inherit the group identity and normalized
advantage of their parent rollout and do not introduce additional entries
when computing the group mean or standard deviation.
Policy loss is aggregated with token-level mean reduction over all valid
policy-generated tokens in the batch. Each valid token therefore
contributes once to the loss regardless of how its parent rollout is
segmented, so splitting a rollout into multiple segments does not introduce
additional sample-level weight. This preserves both the rollout-level
reward semantics and consistent token-level weighting while allowing all
valid post-compaction segments to participate in training.
Black-Box Baselines.
For the black-box baselines, we use OpenClaw v2026.4.5 as the training
harness and reserve Claude Code v2.1.205 as an unseen external harness
for evaluation. Black-Box SFT and Black-Box RL represent two complementary
adaptation strategies to the same single black-box harness: the former
fine-tunes the policy on 12,800 trajectories collected on ClawGym-Task
through OpenClaw for three epochs, while the latter performs online policy
optimization through OpenClaw on the same training set for 300 updates
with 128 rollouts per update. This gives both settings approximately
38,400 trajectory-level training exposures, although SFT reuses a fixed
trajectory set across epochs whereas RL collects trajectories online.
For the comparison in Table 2 , Black-Box RL
uses GRPO, and Harness Scaling uses the corresponding GRPO-trained
checkpoint. Both RL settings use the same backbone models, matched
optimization budgets, the same main GRPO hyperparameters, and the same
evaluation protocol.

Scoring.
We report Pass@1 averaged over three independent evaluation runs. For each
run, every task is executed once under the corresponding harness setting,
and the resulting task-level scores are aggregated following the benchmark
evaluation protocol. The reported score is then obtained by averaging the
Pass@1 results across the three runs. For rubric-based evaluation requiring
a model judge, we use GPT-5.4 with the same judging protocol across all
methods and harness settings.

Appendix D Additional Analysis Details
D.1 Behavioral Metrics
We use six trajectory-level behavioral metrics to characterize
harness-related capabilities beyond task-level performance.
Instruction Following measures whether the policy correctly follows
the system instructions and execution constraints specified by the harness.
Interface Handling measures whether the policy correctly interprets
and uses the available tool interface, particularly when tool names or
schemas differ from the base harness.
Observation Reading measures whether the policy extracts
task-relevant information from tool feedback despite additional formatting
or distracting metadata.
State Tracking measures whether the policy maintains an accurate
understanding of task progress, intermediate results, and the evolving
workspace state throughout long-horizon execution.
Workflow Compliance measures whether the policy follows procedural
requirements imposed by the harness, such as explicit planning or periodic
verification.
Error Recovery measures whether the policy recognizes execution
failures, adapts its strategy, and continues toward task completion rather
than repeating or propagating the same error.
D.2 Metric Evaluation
We evaluate the six capabilities from complete agent trajectories using
GPT-5.4 as the trajectory judge. For each model scale and each of the
18 held-out in-space harnesses, we collect 200 complete trajectories for
both Single-Harness RL and Harness Scaling on the same 200 ClawGym-Bench
tasks. For each trajectory, the judge is provided with the task instruction,
harness specification, and complete interaction history, including policy
actions, tool calls, and tool observations.
Each capability is scored independently on a five-point scale. A score of
1 indicates that the corresponding capability is largely absent or repeatedly
violated; 2 indicates substantial deficiencies; 3 indicates partial or
inconsistent satisfaction; 4 indicates generally correct behavior with
minor deviations; and 5 indicates consistent and effective satisfaction of
the criterion throughout the trajectory. The judge assigns each dimension
separately rather than producing a single overall trajectory score.
We use the same judging prompt, rubric, and judge model across both
approaches and all harness settings. For each behavioral dimension, we first
average scores over the 200 trajectories within each held-out harness, and
then average equally across all 18 harnesses. For visualization, the
five-point scores are normalized by dividing by five, resulting in values
between 0.2 and 1.0 as reported in
Figures 4(a) and 4(b) .
