Abstract
Today’s multimodal assistants are typically prompted to interpret the visual evidence presented to them. To offload the cognitive burden of remembering across long horizons, wearable assistants should proactively acquire and maintain useful memory as the user’s surroundings evolve. Remembering the ambience on the user’s behalf requires identifying what matters, understanding in real time, and initiating memory maintenance in the background while the user focuses on activities. We introduce ProactEgoMem , a benchmark that evaluates proactive memory in streaming egocentric video. Three tasks ground the evaluation in ambient environmental needs: Information Capture ( IC ) preserves transient yet dense visual information, State Tracking ( ST ) follows changing object states, and Location Tracking ( LT ) records object movements. The benchmark proposes the protocol at two levels of
proactiveness: the Notification Level asks for one timely notification that satisfies the user’s intent; the deeper Intervention Level asks
for memory maintenance with an active start-to-stop tool lifecycle on the user’s behalf. Parallel ProactEgoMem-Daily and ProactEgoMem-Sci tracks contain 300/100 clips for both daily and scientific use cases, paired with 1000/500 questions in ProactEgoMem-QA, which measures the quality of proactively acquired memory. Across proprietary,
open-source, video-language, and streaming baselines, even the strongest models fail to proactively capture the memory timely and accurately. ProactEgoMem is a rigorous evaluation foundation for multimodal
proactive assistance and co-existing video agents.

 1 Introduction
A wearable assistant on AI glasses can reduce the burden of remembering everyday details while the user attends to other activities ( Engel et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) : for example, alert when a faucet remains running unattended while cooking, remembering where a tool is placed during multitasking
(Figure  1 ). Such assistance requires continuous background observation, where the assistant infers the user’s intent and detects relevant events, maintains ambient environment states, and alerts or remembers proactively for later use without explicit instructions. Useful information may be transient, and the dynamically complex ambient evidence may be intertwined continuously. This motivates our central question: How can a wearable assistant proactively maintain memory in the background as the user’s surroundings change over long time horizons?

Remembering on the user’s behalf requires more than recognizing relevant information in a video. An assistant must infer intents, capture transient details before they disappear, and keep track of changing states and object locations as activities unfold. Such memory needs extend over time: the assistant must determine when to begin acquisition, maintain tracking through subsequent observations, and stop when the relevant event ends, all while the user attends to other activities. This calls for two levels of proactive assistance: notifying the user when relevant information warrants attention, and intervening on the user’s behalf to acquire it as memory.
We formulate this capability as proactive memory : autonomously acquiring and maintaining useful evidence from an unfolding egocentric stream. Conventional video QA evaluates answers from post-hoc complete videos ( Mangalam et al., 2023 ; Fu et al., 2025 ) , while streaming QA evaluates responses as video arrives ( Lin et al., 2024b ) . ProactEgoMem evaluates how useful memory is acquired in the first place, how an assistant initiates and maintains background processing and intervenes proactively, and whether the resulting memory supports subsequent assistance.
We introduce ProactEgoMem (Figure  2 ), a benchmark for proactive memory over
streaming egocentric video, built around three object-centric tasks for ambient memories from first-person recordings:
 Information Capture ( IC ), State Tracking ( ST ),
and Location Tracking ( LT ). We further propose to evaluate two levels of easy-to-hard proactiveness: the Notification Level asks for a single timely, semantically
correct notification once the observation satisfies the user’s intent; the
advanced Intervention Level requires the agent to track the situation in the background and operate memory tools on the user’s behalf, such as maintaining lifecycles for recording or status and location tracking. The benchmark comprises two parallel tracks: ProactEgoMem-Daily , built from everyday
first-person recordings, with 300 clips and 1000 questions, and
 ProactEgoMem-Sci , built from egocentric recordings in wet-labs as co-scientist assistants, with
100 clips and 500 questions.
Our Benchmark makes three contributions:
 • 
 Formulation. We pose proactive memory for egocentric assistants: deciding from the visual stream when to intervene and what to remember. It has three core components, streaming egocentric
perception, proactive initiative, and co-existent background
operation, framing it as object-centric ambient memory with a
 measurable memory lifecycle .
 • 
 Evaluation protocol. We design a protocol built on
multi-level proactiveness, three ambient-memory tasks, and a question
answering dataset that tests the quality of proactive memory; evaluation metrics score jointly on timing and semantics of proactive initiatives.
 • 
 Benchmarking. We benchmark
proprietary, open-source, video-language, and streaming models on both daily and scientific scenarios and
identify failure modes: missed calibrations , delayed initiation , false alarms , lifecycle maintenance , and invalid tool calls .
 2 Related Work
 Streaming and egocentric video understanding. 
First-person datasets ( Grauman et al., 2022 ; Damen et al., 2022 ; Grauman et al., 2024 ; Perrett et al., 2025 ) 
and always-on wearables ( Engel et al., 2023 ) support evaluation of
long-horizon egocentric QA ( Mangalam et al., 2023 ; Chandrasegaran et al., 2024 ) ,
long-context video QA ( Fu et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2025 ) ,
streaming QA ( Lin et al., 2024b ; Li et al., 2025 ) , and egocentric
episodic memory ( Forte et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) . All of them are
query-driven (Table  5 ); in ProactEgoMem the agent
decides when to act with no query and maintains a memory across a
start–maintain–stop lifecycle.
 Video-language models. 
Proprietary ( OpenAI, 2024 ; Gemini Team, Google, 2024 ) and open
video-language models
 ( Chen et al., 2024b ; Li et al., 2024 ; An et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2024a ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2025 ; Qwen Team, Alibaba Group, 2026a ; Qwen Team, Alibaba Group, 2026b ; Zhang et al., 2025a ; Gemma Team, 2026 ) 
consume a whole clip and emit an answer. Streaming systems decide online
through respond-versus-wait objectives ( Chen et al., 2024a ; Wang et al., 2025c ) ,
real-time memory ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) , decoupled perception and
reaction ( Qian et al., 2025 ) , when-to-speak activation
 ( Kim et al., 2026 ) , offline-to-proactive conversion
 ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , proactive companions ( Yan et al., 2026 ) ,
and full-duplex omni-models ( OpenBMB Team, 2026 ; Qwen Team, Alibaba Cloud, 2025 ) ;
each reduces proactivity to a single respond-or-not event.
 Proactive assistance and proactivity benchmarks. 
Proactive assistants span mixed-initiative interaction
 ( Horvitz, 1999 ; Allen et al., 1999 ) , conversational recommendation
 ( Christakopoulou et al., 2016 ; Jannach et al., 2021 ) , proactive dialogue systems
 ( Deng et al., 2023 ) and LLM agents ( Lu et al., 2024 ) , and view-sharing wearables
 ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , all triggered by a conversational turn; HoloAssist
 ( Wang et al., 2023 ) predicts intervention from first-person video
offline. Multimodal proactivity benchmarks
 ( Zhang et al., 2025c ; Wang et al., 2025d ; Wang et al., 2025b ) score one
“should I respond now?” decision, refined by user profiles
 ( Ran et al., 2026 ) , out-of-plan recovery
 ( Kundu et al., 2026 ) , and one-second grading
 ( Du et al., 2026 ) ; Ego2Web ( Yu et al., 2026 ) acts on user
instructions, and concurrent work studies intervene-or-abstain decisions
 ( Gong et al., 2026 ) and memory as an intervention mechanism
 ( Wu et al., 2026 ) . ProactEgoMem is, to our knowledge, the first
benchmark in which proactivity produces a memory : the agent decides
when to act, sustains a memory through a start–maintain–stop
lifecycle, and is scored on whether it answers later questions.
 3 Problem Formulation
 3.1 Streaming Setting
 Streaming setting. 
An episode is a timestamped stream of egocentric frames
 V=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{T}) . At time t , the agent receives only frames from oldest to newest up to the
current moment and a logical tool state. At every moment the agent decides what to initiate, operations of memory tool call in the background, a message to the user, or nothing at all.
 • 
 Tool calling. 
The first kind of initiative is an operation of a tool call (recording,
state tracking, or location tracking) that acquires memory in the background. At every moment the agent
outputs one JSON object whose list of tool calls, possibly empty, starts or
stops tools; several tool instances may be active in parallel.
 • 
 Proactive message. 
The second kind of initiative is a message to the user in the message field: grounded in its
observations and acquired memory, the agent generates notifications or interventions. The agent keeps silent when no action is required.

 Proactive memory. 
We use proactive memory that the agent acquires and
maintains on its own initiative: the agent decides what to
store, keeps memory tools active in the background, and surfaces the memory when the question answering (QA) warrants, with no re-inspection of the full video.
 Question answering. 
Each event is paired with multiple-choice questions
(Section  4 ) whose answers must be grounded in what the
agent’s memory tool calls captured during the stream.

 Two- level proactive modes .
Offloading mental burden can be multi-faceted. Inspired by the distinction between proactively informing a user and acting
autonomously on their behalf ( Kraus et al., 2021 ) , we operationalize
proactivity at two depths (Figure  3 ; Table  1 ):
 Notification Level. 
The agent should infer the user’s intent and send a timely notification in
response to a visual cue. For example, after inferring that the user is shopping for items, the agent sends the proactive assistant message “I noticed a lot of nutrition information” when the user shows interest in an item. The
ground truth is a timestamped opportunity and a semantic target message.
 Intervention Level. 
At this advanced level, the agent should infer the user’s intent, act proactively on their behalf, maintain a memory tool
lifecycle for proactive memory acquisition, and report the result. For
example, when the user is performing multitasking cooking, turns on a faucet, an oven, etc, the agent starts status trackers for each object in the background,
keeps them active while the items remain on, stops them once they are
turned off, and reports that the tracking has been resolved.

 3.3 Three Ambient-Memory Tasks
From 4,000 hours of video, we define three core
ambient-memory tasks that offload mental burden by proactively preserving
transient information, object states, and location movements
(Table  1 ).
 IC : Information Capture. 
The agent actively preserves transient dense visual information in the ambient environment that may be useful later, such as a
document, sign, label, screen, poster, recipe, or instructions, etc. QA questions
ask about the proactively captured dense information, etc.
 ST : State Tracking. 
The agent tracks object states such as on/off, open/closed,
active/inactive, filled/empty, or connected/disconnected during multitasking. QA questions ask how often a state
changed, what remains active, the order of activations, or which object was
active longest, etc.
 LT : Location Tracking. 
The agent remembers where an object was placed elsewhere. The task requires tracking where an object is originally placed, when it is picked up, placed, or last observed if placed at a different location.
QA questions ask where an object was placed, its original location, the
order of locations, or total times it moved, etc.

 5 Experiments
 5.1 Metrics and Baselines
 Common metrics. 
Predictions are matched and scored separately at the two proactive levels.
Predictions at both levels are matched to ground truth by Hungarian
matching  ( Kuhn, 1955 ) , which requires that an LLM-judge’s semantic
similarity exceeds a threshold and that the temporal offset falls within the
matching window; among admitted edges the assignment minimizes the cost
 \alpha(1-\mathrm{sim})+(1-\alpha)\,(|\Delta|/W)^{p}   ( Zhang et al., 2025c ) . Intervention
predictions are first converted into intervals and matched on temporal
overlap. Three metric groups are then shared by the two levels:
(1)  Timing (Start@ \tau ): the fraction of matched actions whose
start falls within various \tau seconds of the ground-truth moment. We
report Start@0.2 as the main result: the 200 ms tolerance reflects the
residual inter-annotator disagreement on notification timing
(Table  3 ) and is widely used in real-time streaming
models.
(2)  Abs Error ( |\Delta_{s}| , \sigma ): the mean absolute start
offset and its standard deviation, in seconds, measuring how far and how
consistently a model deviates from the annotated moment.
(3)  Detection (Prec, Recall, F1): precision over emitted actions,
recall over ground-truth events, and their harmonic mean F1,
penalizing spurious interruptions and missed ones.
 Intervention -level specific metrics. 
The Intervention-level metrics score the lifecycle itself through two further groups:
(4)  Overlap (mIoU): interval IoU averaged over all 
ground-truth opportunities, scoring unmatched opportunities as zero, so a high
score requires starting, maintaining, and stopping the tool lifecycle at the
right boundaries; various thresholds in IoU@ \tau are also reported.
(5)  QA (Acc): accuracy of the ProactEgoMem-QA dataset requiring
proactive memory.
 Baselines. 
The comparison includes proprietary models (Gemini 2.5 Flash ( Comanici et al., 2025 ) and
GPT-5.4-mini ( OpenAI, 2026 ) ), general open-source models (Gemma4-31B ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) ,
Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, Alibaba Group, 2026c ) , Qwen3.6-27B ( Qwen Team, Alibaba Group, 2026b ) ,
Qwen3.5-9B ( Qwen Team, Alibaba Group, 2026a ) ), video-language models (VideoLLaMA3-7B ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) , LLaVA-OV-1.5-8B ( An et al., 2025 ) ,
MiniCPM-o-4.5 ( OpenBMB Team, 2026 ) ), and streaming models (MMDuet ( Wang et al., 2025c ) , VideoLLM-Online ( Chen et al., 2024a ) , Inkling-Small ( Thinking Machines Lab, 2026 ) , and JoyAI-VL ( Yao et al., 2026 ) ).
All baselines are run with the exact same streaming setting as follows.
 5.2 Streaming Evaluation Protocol
Every baseline receives the same prompt for a given task and proactive level; model-specific code
only wraps it in the model’s chat template. FPS rate of 1 is used throughout the evaluation. At every step the prompt is followed by a JSON
assistant context and the window of video frames, and the model answers with one JSON object. Complete description in Appendix  K . Per turn: one request per video second simulating the streaming setting, with the task prompt,
a JSON context, and the 10 most recent
frames up to current timestamp t , sampled at 1 fps,
each with frame number and timestamp. Earlier turns: only through
the context, listing active tool instances and the 5 latest
completed intervals (Intervention) or notifications (Notification). Output: one JSON object determining the tool calls at timestamp t ; unparseable output counts as action of silence.

 5.4 Effects of Proactive Memory Acquisition
 The headroom is in proactive memory acquisition. Timing and
lifecycle metrics score when an agent acts to acquire memory; ProactEgoMem-QA asks what that action leaves behind. A proactive assistant is useful only if the evidence it chooses to keep can answer the user’s questions later, so we test the acquired memory directly. Every question is answered
after the stream ends under three memory conditions
(Figure  6 ): uniform frames ( No Memory ), the frames the agent kept throughout its tool calls ( Proact Memory ), and the ground-truth event windows ( Oracle Memory ). Proact Memory beats No Memory on
 IC for every model with valid output and splits evenly on ST and
 LT . In 34 
of the 36 valid model–task cells the oracle memory sits at least 20 accuracy
points above the proactive one; with oracle memory ceiling at IC 
 94.9\% while ST and LT stay below 70\% . Even with oracle memory
fed to the video QA model, the benchmark remains hard to solve, and the gap
between No Memory and Oracle Memory defines the improvable headroom for proactive memory.

 5.5 From Daily Life to the Laboratory
 Scientific scenarios expose the central bottleneck in
proactive memory acquisition. 
We measure it as the memory acquisition gap , oracle QA accuracy minus QA
accuracy under proactively acquired memory. On IC the gap nearly doubles in
the laboratory, from 27 – 30 to 45 – 52 points for the open-source and
proprietary groups (Figure  7 b, IC ). The additional complexity of managing and maintaining the video data in a scientific context adds to the overall burden of proactive memory.
 This burden concentrates on IC and ST. Laboratory video
lowers notification recall for both groups on all three tasks, halving the model-mean
recall (Figure  7 a), most sharply on
ST, where open-source recall falls from 35.3\% to 8.9\% 
(Figure  7 b, ST ); the ST gap widens to 33 – 40 points,
while LT gaps narrow to 22 – 27 points
(Figure  7 b, LT ). IC and ST are harder in the lab, whose objects are hard to recognize; LT is easier, as lab objects rarely move.

 6 Conclusion
 ProactEgoMem evaluates proactive ambient memory in streaming egocentric video through
two proactive modes and three task families, IC , ST and
 LT , across daily-life and laboratory co-scientist tracks. The
experiments show that today’s multimodal models remain far from reliable
proactive assistants: they identify the right moment only under loose timing
windows, and they struggle with precision, event tracking over long-horizon
location, and proactive memory acquisition. It charts a concrete path toward
wearable agents that remember useful context without being asked.
AI Use Statement
In this work, we used generative AI tools as components of the benchmark
pipeline: a VLM writes the dense captions from which candidate
events are filtered (Section  4 ), an LLM generates the IC 
questions from human-authored dense annotations of visible information
(Section  4 ), an LLM-judged semantic similarity gates the
matching of predicted to ground-truth actions (Section  5.1 ). Regarding LLM, we use DeepSeek-V4-Flash judges the correctness of proactive messages
(Appendix  D ). We have not used generative AI tools
for other tasks with required disclosure, and the rest of the
required disclosure tasks are not applicable to this work. Additionally, we
used generative AI tools for improving the manuscript text and its L a T e X 
layout and for polishing the scripts that draw the figures. We have reviewed all
AI-assisted work. Three independent annotators per clip mark the event
boundaries, the LLM-generated questions were reviewed in three rounds, and AI-assisted text and figure code were checked, e.g. figure values
verified against the result tables by the authors according to the raw experiment logs. We take responsibility for
the final content of this work, including text, claims or artifacts produced
with the aid of generative AI.
Reproducibility
The ground-truth answers stay private: an evaluation
server accepts blind submissions and returns only the scores, which keeps the
benchmark out of web-scale training data for foundation models.
