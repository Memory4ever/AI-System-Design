  Abstract 
    
 Long-context continual pre-training (LCCP) is widely used to improve long-context capability, but how its benefits scale with the number of tokens used for LCCP remains unclear.
We investigate this question through modeling, monitoring, and validating the benefits of scaling LCCP.
First, we establish an empirical additive power law relating long-context performance to model size and the respective token budgets of short-context pre-training and LCCP.
Experiments across five model scales and more than one hundred short- and long-context budget configurations show that the fitted law reproduces the observed trends and extrapolates well to held-out model scales.
Second, we introduce  Constant-Last4 , a training and observation protocol that averages the four most recent consecutive checkpoints from a constant-learning-rate run to enable stable monitoring along a single extensible training trajectory.
Under this protocol, the same scaling-law form fits well and accurately predicts the LCCP performance trajectory of a 1.22B-parameter model trained on approximately 100B LCCP tokens.
Finally, downstream evaluations along this trajectory show continued improvements in long-context performance, with no clear sign of saturation within the evaluated range.
These gains persist after both 128K context extension and supervised fine-tuning, with benefits extending to long-context understanding and agentic tasks.
Together, these results argue for continuing to scale LCCP rather than stopping early: its gains are stably observable along an extensible trajectory, quantitatively predictable, and transferable to downstream tasks.   1    1

  1  Introduction 

 
 Long-context capability is fundamental to language models, supporting complex long-horizon tasks and agentic workflows  ( Kimi Team et al., 2026 ;  Zeng et al., 2026 ;  Liu et al., 2025 ) . However, training on long sequences from the beginning of pre-training incurs substantial computational costs  ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) . Long-context continual pre-training (LCCP) is therefore widely adopted, often as a brief stage following short-context pre-training. It uses longer sequences and a long-context-oriented data mixture to extend the context window and improve long-context performance  ( Xiong et al., 2024 ;  Gao et al., 2025 ) . A range of studies and models adopt relatively limited additional training to extend context windows  ( Liu et al., 2024 ;  Fu et al., 2024 ;  Hu et al., 2025 ) . 
 
 
 As applications increasingly demand long-context understanding and sustained agentic interaction, some training recipes devote substantial token budgets to long-context pre-training  ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ;  Zeng et al., 2026 ) . Yet how long-context performance scales with the LCCP training budget remains insufficiently characterized. We therefore ask:  Does continued LCCP yield sustained improvements in long-context capability, and can these gains be quantitatively predicted?  
 
 
 In this work, we address this question by  modeling, monitoring, and validating  the benefits of scaling up LCCP.
As illustrated in Figure   1  , we conduct a systematic empirical study of how long-context performance evolves with increasing LCCP budgets, spanning short-context pre-training, 32K LCCP, subsequent 128K context extension, and supervised fine-tuning(SFT).
We first establish an empirical scaling law for  modeling  long-context performance during LCCP, jointly accounting for model size, short-context pre-training tokens, and long-context training tokens.
We then introduce a stable observation protocol for extensible LCCP, enabling performance  monitoring  along a constant-learning-rate trajectory and, together with the scaling law, prediction from early checkpoints.
Finally, we conduct downstream evaluations on long-context and agentic tasks, thereby  validating  whether gains from additional LCCP translate into downstream improvements and persist through subsequent training stages. 
 
  
  Figure 1:  Overview of our framework for modeling, monitoring, and validating long-context performance during LCCP scaling. We develop an empirical scaling law for long-context performance, introduce  Constant-Last4  as an extensible training and observation protocol, and evaluate downstream performance gains after context extension and supervised fine-tuning. 
 
 
  Modeling LCCP Scaling. 
Unlike pre-training from scratch, LCCP starts from a model that has already acquired basic capabilities, meaning that its long-context performance may depend on the preceding short-context pre-training stage  ( Wu et al., 2025b ) . We therefore jointly model the non-embedding parameter count    N  N   , short-context pre-training tokens     D  s   D_{s}   , and long-context training tokens     D  l   D_{l}   . Using LongPPL  ( Fang et al., 2025 )  as a proxy for long-context performance, we evaluate 109 training configurations across five model scales under a linear-decay learning-rate schedule. We find that an additive power law with an offset accurately captures the observed performance trends and extrapolates to held-out model scales under the evaluated budget settings, providing a quantitative basis for predicting long-context performance under different training budgets. 
 
 
  Monitoring LCCP Scaling. 
To study continued LCCP scaling, we need a training and observation protocol that enables consistent performance monitoring along a single extensible trajectory.
Although linear-decay schedules provide stable endpoint performance, they require a predefined training budget and are therefore inconvenient for open-ended training. In contrast, a constant learning rate avoids this endpoint dependence, allowing cumulative long-context training tokens     D  l   D_{l}    to serve as a common coordinate for monitoring training progress. 
 
 
 Raw constant-learning-rate checkpoints, however, exhibit substantial performance fluctuations and underperform budget-matched linear-decay models. Motivated by prior work on training dynamics and checkpoint merging  ( Wen et al., 2024 ;  Tian et al., 2026 ) , we introduce the  Constant-Last4  protocol, which averages the four most recent checkpoints for evaluation. Across evaluated configurations, this protocol provides more stable observations and closely matches linear-decay performance. Under this protocol, the same scaling-law form fits well and extrapolates from smaller models to the approximately 100B-token LCCP trajectory of a 1.22B model. Moreover, when model size and the short-context training budget are fixed, the law simplifies to a power law in     D  l   D_{l}    that predicts later LongPPL from a fit to early LCCP checkpoints. Together,  Constant-Last4  and the empirical scaling law support stable monitoring and performance prediction when scaling up LCCP. 
 
 
  Validating Capability Gains. 
The practical value of the scaling law depends on whether the performance improvements it captures translate into gains in downstream long-context and agentic tasks.
We therefore validate the scaling trend along the 32K LCCP trajectory using RULER  ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) , where aggregate performance improves as the long-context training budget increases, with no clear sign of saturation within the evaluated range.
Since LCCP is typically not the final training stage, we further evaluate checkpoints obtained at different LCCP budgets after 128K context extension and SFT for long-context and agentic tasks, using identical training procedures within each setting.
The benefits of LCCP scaling remain observable after both subsequent training stages and across multiple long-context and agentic benchmarks.
Together, these findings provide a quantitative basis for assessing the downstream benefits of continued LCCP. 
 
 
 
  2  Related Work 

 
  2.1  Scaling Laws for Large Language Models 

 
 Scaling laws relate pre-training loss to model size and training data and characterize their trade-offs under fixed compute budgets  ( Kaplan et al., 2020 ;  Hoffmann et al., 2022 ) .
Subsequent work has extended scaling-law analysis to continual pre-training, examining the effects of model size, additional training data, and data-mixture ratios  ( Que et al., 2024 ;  Gu et al., 2024 ) , and characterizing learning dynamics  ( Wang et al., 2025a ) , as well as to downstream performance prediction  ( Ivgi et al., 2022 ;  Hu et al., 2024a ;  Chen et al., 2024 ) .
However, scaling-law analyses of long-context performance remain comparatively limited.
Prior work has studied long-context scaling in hybrid architectures under single-stage pre-training  ( Qiao et al., 2026 ) .
We instead study LCCP, jointly modeling long-context performance using model size and separate short- and long-context training budgets. 
 
 
 
  2.2  Long-Context Continual Pre-Training 

 
 LCCP typically starts from a short-context pre-trained model and extends its context window through positional adjustments and continued training on longer sequences  ( Xiong et al., 2024 ;  Chen et al., 2023 ) , using a long-context-oriented data mixture to improve performance  ( Gao et al., 2025 ;  Fu et al., 2024 ) .
Some methods emphasize token-efficient context extension  ( Wu et al., 2025a ;  Peng et al., 2024 ) , whereas some leading large language models use long-context training budgets on the order of a trillion tokens  ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ;  Liu et al., 2025 ) .
These differing budget choices highlight the importance of understanding the benefits of continued LCCP and assessing whether training is sufficient. 
 
 
 Assessing the benefits of continued LCCP requires appropriate evaluation metrics.
LongPPL   ( Fang et al., 2025 )  uses a reference model to identify tokens sensitive to distant context, providing a probabilistic measure of long-context performance.
Recent work analyzes LCCP learning dynamics at the behavioral, probabilistic, and mechanistic levels and introduces a retrieval-head-based metric  ( Liang et al., 2026 ) .
The findings suggest that behavioral scores can plateau while probabilistic and mechanistic metrics may continue to capture changes during training.
Complementing these analyses of learning dynamics, we focus on stable observation and quantitative prediction during continued LCCP.
Drawing on studies of learning-rate dynamics in pre-training  ( Wen et al., 2024 ;  Hägele et al., 2024 )  and checkpoint averaging  ( Tian et al., 2026 ;  Sanyal et al., 2023 ) , we develop  Constant-Last4 , an extensible protocol for LCCP training and LongPPL evaluation, and examine whether capability gains from additional LCCP persist after subsequent training stages. 
 
 
 
 
  3  An Empirical Scaling Law for LCCP 

 
 To quantify how long-context performance scales during LCCP, we develop an empirical scaling law that jointly accounts for model size and the token budgets of the two training stages.
We evaluate both its fit to observed configurations and its predictive accuracy beyond the fitting range, across model scales and training budgets. 
 
 
  3.1  Experimental Setup and Evaluation Metric 

 
 Long-context performance after LCCP may depend not only on the amount of long-context training, but also on the model’s short-context pre-training history  ( Wu et al., 2025b ) .

Specifically, we use three predictors: the non-embedding parameter count    N  N   , the number of short-context pre-training tokens     D  s   D_{s}   , and the number of long-context training tokens     D  l   D_{l}   .

  4  An Extensible Training and Observation Protocol 

 
 Under the linear-decay setup, we successfully fit an additive power-law model for     log  ⁡   (  LongPPL  )    \log(\mathrm{LongPPL})   , which suggests that increasing the LCCP training budget can yield continued and predictable benefits within the studied range.
Although the linear-decay schedule provides stable model performance, the learning-rate schedule is tied to a prespecified training endpoint, making it less convenient for open-ended LCCP scaling.
In this section, we therefore explore a training and observation protocol that supports both extensible training and stable performance evaluation, facilitating the application of the scaling law to LCCP scaling in practice. 
 
 
  4.1  Constant-Last4: Constant Learning Rate with Model Merging 

 
 We use a constant learning rate to support open-ended LCCP without prespecifying a final training budget  ( Liu et al., 2024 ;  Blakeman et al., 2026 ) .
The cumulative number of long-context training tokens     D  l   D_{l}    then serves as a natural coordinate along a single training trajectory, which brings two practical benefits: additional long-context data can be incorporated by resuming from the latest checkpoint,
and model performance can be evaluated at any training budget without running a separate experiment for each budget.
To mitigate the elevated and fluctuating evaluation loss that can arise under constant-learning-rate training  ( Wen et al., 2024 ) , we consider checkpoint merging without additional training  ( Tian et al., 2026 ) .
We treat merging as an  observation  mechanism: evaluation uses merged checkpoints, while the training trajectory remains unchanged. 
 
 
  Observation rules and metrics. 
We take the raw constant-learning-rate checkpoint as the simplest observation rule and compare it with a family of checkpoint-merging methods.
As the reference for all rules, we use the  Linear-Decay  checkpoint trained with the same     (  N  ,   D  s   ,   D  l   )   (N,D_{s},D_{l})   . 
 
 
 For judging observation rules, we retain LongPPL as the long-context metric and introduce ShortPPL as a complementary diagnostic.
Reusing the same reference model and token-level statistics as in LongPPL, we select tokens for which the local chunk yields a lower loss than the full context and compute perplexity over these positions: 
 

  
  
      𝒦  short   =   {  t  :   Δ  t   <  0  ,   ℓ  ref  full    (   x  t   )   <  2  }   ,  ShortPPL   (  M  )   =  exp   (   1   |   𝒦  short   |     ∑   t  ∈   𝒦  short      ℓ  M  full    (   x  t   )   )   .   \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{short}}=\left\{t:\Delta_{t}<0,\;\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{full}}(x_{t})<2\right\},\qquad\mathrm{ShortPPL}(M)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{short}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{short}}}\ell_{M}^{\mathrm{full}}(x_{t})\right).    
    
 
 Since the selected tokens do not benefit from distant context, ShortPPL primarily reflects general language-modeling performance, and thus serves as a complementary metric.

 To further assess the stability of  Last4  merging, we compare  Constant-Raw  and  Constant-Last4  with  Linear-Decay  across all S4 configurations in Figure   4(b)  .
Across training budgets,  Constant-Last4  closely matches the corresponding  Linear-Decay  checkpoint on both ShortPPL and LongPPL.
In summary,  Constant-Last4  combines the extensibility of constant-learning-rate training with the stable performance of a decayed endpoint in our setting, providing a practical training and observation protocol for LCCP scaling. 
 
 
 
  4.2  Scaling-Law Validation under Constant-Last4 

 
 For the new  Constant-Last4  protocol, we need to test whether the empirical scaling law Eq.   1   remains valid under it. We adopt a     (    D  s   /  N   ,    D  l   /  N   )   (D_{s}/N,D_{l}/N)    budget grid for S1–S4 similar to the one in Section   3  . The realized token counts and details are provided in Appendix   A.3  .
Because a single constant-learning-rate trajectory provides checkpoints at multiple     D  l   D_{l}    values, one run covers several long-context training budgets and reduces the experimental cost relative to  Linear-Decay .
We run each LCCP trajectory independently three times from the same short-context checkpoint and use the mean LongPPL across runs for S1–S4 models. 
 
 
 We follow the scale-extrapolation setup used in Section   3  .
First, we fit the scaling law on S1–S3 and evaluate it on S4. We then fit it on S1–S4 and extrapolate to the S5 trajectory.
Whereas the  Linear-Decay  experiments used nine S5 configurations, here we extend pointwise scale extrapolation to prediction along an entire training trajectory.
Specifically, we fix the short-context budget at      D  s   =   450  ​  N    D_{s}=450N    and continue constant-learning-rate training to      D  l   =   120  ​  N    D_{l}=120N    (approximately 100B tokens).
This setting provides dense observations of     log  ⁡   (  LongPPL  )    \log(\mathrm{LongPPL})    as a function of     D  l   D_{l}    at a fixed model scale and short-context budget. For S5, we save checkpoints at     1  ​  N   1N    intervals and apply  Last4  to the four most recent checkpoints every     5  ​  N   5N   , yielding 24 merged evaluation points. Appendix   B.1   shows that the results are insensitive to checkpoint spacing within the evaluated range. 
 
 
 As shown in Figure   5  , Eq.   1   fits the  Constant-Last4  configurations well and extrapolates accurately to an unseen model scale.
Notably, the S5 trajectory reaches approximately 100B long-context training tokens, more than eight times the largest absolute long-context budget in the fitting set, yet the scaling law accurately predicts     log  ⁡   (  LongPPL  )    \log(\mathrm{LongPPL})    across the entire trajectory.
Thus, the scaling law works under both  Linear-Decay  and  Constant-Last4  within the tested range.

  4.3  A Simplified Law for Fixed-Model LCCP 

   
  Figure 6:  Prefix fitting of     log  ⁡   (  LongPPL  )    \log(\mathrm{LongPPL})    along the S5  Constant-Last4  run. 
 
 
 In practical LCCP, one often starts from a fixed model that has undergone substantial short-context pre-training and continues training it with additional long-context data.
In this setting, the main question is how performance evolves with     D  l   D_{l}    and whether early
observations can predict the benefits of further training. When    N  N    and     D  s   D_{s}    are fixed, their contributions in Eq.   1   can be absorbed into a single offset, yielding 
 

  
  
       log  ⁡   (  LongPPL  )    =     B  l    D  l   β  l     +   C  ′     ,   \log(\mathrm{LongPPL})=\frac{B_{l}}{D_{l}^{\beta_{l}}}+C^{\prime},    
  
  (2)    
 
 where      C  ′   =    A  /   N  α    +    B  s   /   D  s   β  s     +  C    C^{\prime}=A/N^{\alpha}+B_{s}/D_{s}^{\beta_{s}}+C   .We re-estimate     B  l   B_{l}   ,     β  l   \beta_{l}    and     C  ′   C^{\prime}    jointly from the given trajectory rather than taking them from the joint fit.
This lower-dimensional form is practical for fixed-model LCCP because it can be fitted using a small number of early checkpoints from a single training trajectory, avoiding the costly multi-scale, multi-budget training grid required for fitting the joint law. 
 
 
 We evaluate the S5  Constant-Last4  trajectory with     D  s   D_{s}    fixed at     450  ​  N   450N    and     D  l   D_{l}    extending to     120  ​  N   120N   , evaluating one merged checkpoint every     5  ​  N   5N   .
We fit Eq.   2   using Huber loss on prefixes up to     20  ​  N   20N   ,     40  ​  N   40N   , and     60  ​  N   60N   , respectively, and evaluate each fit on all subsequent held-out checkpoints through     120  ​  N   120N   .
As shown in Figure   6  , even a short prefix provides a reasonable estimate of the remaining trajectory, suggesting that early observations can help estimate later LongPPL for a fixed-model LCCP trajectory.
We further fit the same power-law form to ShortPPL and compare it with that of LongPPL to assess LongPPL’s usefulness as a long-context proxy; details are provided in Appendix   D.1  . 
 
 
 
 
  5  Downstream Validation of LCCP Scaling 

 
 For both  Linear-Decay  and  Constant-Last4 , LongPPL decreases predictably as the LCCP budget increases.
To test whether this improvement translates into long-context capability, we evaluate from three complementary perspectives: capability gains during 32K LCCP, their transfer to 128K context extension, and their preservation after SFT.
We conduct these evaluations using checkpoints sampled from the S5 32K  Constant-Last4  trajectory introduced in Section   4.2  . Additional training and evaluation details for this section are provided in Appendix   C  . 
 
 
  5.1  Capability Gains during 32K LCCP 

   
  Figure 7:  RULER-32K scores along the 32K LCCP trajectory of the S5 model. 
 
 
 To test whether the decrease in LongPPL translates into genuine long-context capability, we evaluate the LCCP trajectory of the S5 model using RULER  ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) . We construct 500 samples for each subtask and evaluate both  Constant-Raw  and  Constant-Last4  checkpoints every     5  ​  N   5N    along the trajectory. 
 
 
 As shown in Figure   7  , the RULER scores of  Constant-Last4  exhibit an overall upward trend throughout the observed LCCP trajectory, with no clear sign of saturation.  Constant-Raw  follows a similar trend but shows larger fluctuations and generally lower scores at comparable training budgets.
Detailed per-subtask results in Appendix   C.1   show that the aggregate gains are driven primarily by intermediate-difficulty retrieval subtasks.
These results link continued LCCP scaling to improved long-context benchmark performance within the evaluated range, with  Constant-Last4  providing a more stable capability signal under a constant learning rate. 
 
 
 
  5.2  Capability Gains after 128K Context Extension 

 
 Multi-stage LCCP is common in practical training pipelines, where a model is first trained extensively at a moderate context length and then extended to a longer context with a smaller additional budget  ( Grattafiori et al., 2024 ;  The Microsoft AI Team, 2026 ) .
To test whether gains from 32K LCCP transfer to a longer context, we select checkpoints at       D  l   /  N   ∈   {   10  ,  20  ,  40  ,  60  ,  80  ,  100  ,  120   }    D_{l}/N\in\{10,20,40,60,80,100,120\}    from the previous S5 32K LCCP trajectory and apply the same 128K context-extension procedure to both  Constant-Raw  and  Constant-Last4  models.
Specifically, we use ABF  ( Xiong et al., 2024 )  to increase the RoPE base frequency from    500,000  500{,}000    to    5,000,000  5{,}000{,}000   , use a 128K-stage data mixture with a higher proportion of long-context sequences than the 32K LCCP mixture, and continue training for 2.10B tokens at a sequence length of 128K.
The learning rate starts from the constant rate used during 32K LCCP and follows a linear decay to one hundredth of its initial value. 
 
 
 For evaluation, we construct 100 128K-length samples per RULER subtask.
As shown in Figure   8  (a), the performance of both  Constant-Raw  and  Constant-Last4  models generally improves with increasing 32K budget, especially on NIAH-related tasks.
These results suggest that the benefits of a larger 32K LCCP budget carry over to the longer-context regime after context extension. 
 
   
  Figure 8:  Downstream performance after 128K extension and SFT at varying 32K LCCP budgets. Shaded regions in (b) show the range across 3 training seeds. Across these subsequent training stages, larger 32K LCCP budgets generally lead to improved downstream performance. 
 
 
 
  5.3  Capability Gains after Supervised Fine-Tuning 

 
 The goal of scaling LCCP is to improve downstream model performance. Since models typically undergo SFT to adapt their pretrained capabilities to practical use, we evaluate whether the benefits of larger LCCP budgets persist after SFT. We consider two settings, long-context SFT and agentic SFT, using the same checkpoints of the 32K LCCP trajectory of the S5 model as in Section   5.2  . Within each setting, we apply the same SFT procedure to checkpoints obtained at different LCCP budgets.
More training details are provided in Appendices   A.3  –   A.4  ; evaluation protocols and supplementary results are provided in Appendix   C.3  . 
 
 
  Long-context SFT. 
We apply the same 2.10B-token SFT recipe to each checkpoint, using the non-thinking response format.
We evaluate the SFT models on four long-context benchmarks: LongBench  ( Bai et al., 2024 ) , GraphWalks  ( OpenAI, 2025 ) , LongReason  ( Ling et al., 2025 ) , and BABILong  ( Kuratov et al., 2024 ) , and report their equal-weight average.
As shown in Figure   8  (b), downstream performance generally improves with the LCCP training budget under the same SFT procedure. Each point reports the mean over three independent SFT runs with different data seeds, with the shaded region indicating the minimum-to-maximum range across the three runs. 
 
 
  Agentic SFT. 
We further examine whether the benefits extend to agentic scenarios. We consider two task categories: search agents, evaluated on GAIA  ( Mialon et al., 2024 )  and BFCL-WebSearch  ( Patil et al., 2025 ) , and tool-use agents, evaluated on     τ  2   \tau^{2}   -telecom  ( Barres et al., 2025 ) . Each checkpoint undergoes the same 4.20B-token agentic SFT recipe with thinking mode enabled during training. Each benchmark score is averaged over three evaluation runs, and the two search benchmark scores are further averaged with equal weights.
As shown in Figure   8  (c), performance in both categories generally improves with larger LCCP budgets, despite fluctuations across checkpoints. 
 
 
 Taken together, these evaluations show that, for the 1B-scale S5 model, the benefits of scaling 32K LCCP to approximately 100B tokens remain evident after 128K context extension and after SFT for both long-context and agentic tasks. These results support further LCCP scaling as a promising direction for improving downstream capabilities when sufficient long-context data are available. 
 
 
 
 
  6  Conclusion 

 
 We establish an empirical scaling law for LCCP that relates long-context performance to model size and separate token budgets for short-context pre-training and LCCP, while supporting extrapolation to held-out model scales and token budgets. We introduce  Constant-Last4 , enabling stable observation along an extensible trajectory. In our 1B-scale experiments, we observe continued improvements in long-context performance along the evaluated 32K LCCP trajectory, up to       ≈   100  ​  B    \approx 100B    tokens. These gains remain after 128K context extension and SFT, including downstream long-context and agentic tasks. Taken together, these findings establish continued LCCP as a predictable and practically effective path toward improving long-context and agentic capabilities.

 Ethics Statement 

 
 This work studies the scaling behavior of long-context continual pre-training. Improved long-context and agentic capabilities may amplify existing language-model risks, including harmful outputs,
bias, privacy leakage, and misuse of automated tools. The use of large-scale training corpora also warrants attention to data provenance, licensing, and potentially sensitive content.
Our capability evaluations do not establish deployment safety; downstream applications require task-specific safety evaluation and appropriate safeguards. Further scaling should weigh capability gains against these risks and the computational and environmental costs of training.

  Appendix A  Experimental Setup and Reproducibility 

 
  A.1  Details about LongPPL and ShortPPL 

 
 This section supplements the metric definitions in the main text and records statistics of the test set used for all PPL evaluations. LongPPL and ShortPPL use the same GovReport documents  ( Huang et al., 2021 ) , reference model,
and full-context forward pass; they differ only in the token positions over
which losses are aggregated. 
 
 
 Token selection. 

 
 We precompute token annotations with Llama-3.1-8B and its tokenizer and keep them fixed for every evaluated model. As in the main text, let 
 

  
  
       Δ  t   =     ℓ  ref  chunk   ​   (   x  t   )    −    ℓ  ref  full   ​   (   x  t   )      ,   \Delta_{t}=\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{chunk}}(x_{t})-\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{full}}(x_{t}),    
    
 
 
 
 where the local context contains 4,096 tokens. LongPPL selects positions that are easy for the reference model under full context but become substantially harder without distant context, whereas ShortPPL selects a complementary set of positions that are also easy under full context but have lower loss with only local context: 
 

  
  
     𝒦  long   \displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{long}}    
        =   {  t  :    ℓ  ref  full   ​   (   x  t   )   ​   <   2  ,   Δ  t    >   ​  2   }    ,   \displaystyle=\left\{t:\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{full}}(x_{t})<2,\;\Delta_{t}>2\right\},    
    
  
  
     𝒦  short   \displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{short}}    
     =  {  t  :  ℓ     ref      full   (  x     t   )  <  2  ,  Δ     t   <  0  }  .   \displaystyle=\left\{t:\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{full}}(x_{t})<2,\;\Delta_{t}<0\right\}.    
    
 
 
 
 The two sets are therefore disjoint. At evaluation time, each document is decoded to text, retokenized with the evaluated model’s tokenizer, and the fixed character intervals are mapped to that model’s token positions. This permits the same text spans to be used when the evaluated tokenizer differs from the reference tokenizer. 
 
 
 
 Test-set construction. 

 
 The test set contains 10,000 GovReport documents. Candidate documents contain 32,768–39,999 tokens under the Llama-3.1 tokenizer; each document is then retokenized with the evaluated model’s tokenizer and truncated to at most 32,768 tokens. Because the tokenizers have different granularities, a small fraction of the retokenized sequences are shorter than this cap. In the final evaluation inputs, 9,434 sequences contain exactly 32,768 tokens; across all 10,000 sequences, the mean and median lengths are 32,751 and 32,768 tokens, respectively.

 Offset constraints. 

 
 A zero-offset additive law, as used in some prior scaling analyses  ( Qiao et al., 2026 ) , has a plausible asymptotic interpretation: loss may approach zero as model and data scales grow, especially for the chosen predictable key tokens in LongPPL.
It is nevertheless substantially less accurate over the finite experimental range under both learning-rate settings.
For both settings, constraining     C  ≥  0   C\geq 0    gives the same result as the zero-offset law because the optimizer drives    C  C    to numerical zero.
The fitted values are     1.6  ×   10   −  48     1.6\times 10^{-48}    and     4.6  ×   10   −  37     4.6\times 10^{-37}    for the  Linear-Decay  S1–S3 and S1–S4 fits, respectively, and     2.5  ×   10   −  49     2.5\times 10^{-49}    and
    2.0  ×   10   −  36     2.0\times 10^{-36}    for their  Constant-Last4  counterparts.
Allowing    C  C    to be free instead yields     C  =   −  0.60    C=-0.60    and     C  =   −  0.82    C=-0.82    for the  Linear-Decay  S1–S3 and S1–S4 fits, respectively, and     C  =   −  0.71    C=-0.71    and     C  =   −  0.61    C=-0.61    for the corresponding  Constant-Last4  fits. 
 
 
 Importantly, a negative offset alone does not imply negative predictions within the relevant scaling regime, since the model- and data-dependent terms remain positive.
To stress-test this behavior, Figure   9   plots the zero contours of the S1–S4 fits at several extrapolated model sizes.
Finite zero contours emerge only at larger model scales and extreme data budgets. For example, at     N  =  10   N=10   B, setting      D  s   =   D  l    D_{s}=D_{l}    gives a zero crossing at approximately    11.0  11.0   T tokens per stage for  Linear-Decay  and    29.5  29.5   T tokens per stage for  Constant-Last4 . These budgets are orders of magnitude beyond the S1–S4 fitting range, whose maxima are approximately    0.11  0.11   T short-context tokens and    0.012  0.012   T long-context tokens. Predictions below zero imply     LongPPL  <  1   \mathrm{LongPPL}<1   , indicating that the fitted law has been extrapolated beyond its valid range. The negative offset should therefore be interpreted as a finite-range correction rather than an asymptotic loss floor.

 Combining the two training stages. 

 
 We test whether short-context pre-training and LCCP data can be collapsed into a single data variable using three formulations. The combined-budget baseline,      D  s   +   D  l    D_{s}+D_{l}   , assumes that the two types of tokens are interchangeable. The effective-data formulation,      D  l   +   K  ​   D  s     D_{l}+KD_{s}   , learns a fixed conversion factor between them, while the scale-dependent formulation,      D  l   +   K  ​    N  ~   ν   ​   D  s     D_{l}+K\widetilde{N}^{\nu}D_{s}   , allows this conversion factor to vary with model scale. 
 
 
 All three formulations are less accurate than our additive law, which models     D  s   D_{s}    and     D  l   D_{l}    separately. On the linear-decay data, our law achieves      R  log  2   =  0.9663   R^{2}_{\log}=0.9663    on S4 and    0.9516  0.9516    on S5, compared with    0.8935  0.8935    and    0.8494  0.8494    for the combined-budget baseline. The effective-data formulation improves some results but remains inferior overall, while the scale-dependent formulation is unstable across extrapolation settings. The same conclusion holds for the constant-learning-rate data, where our law achieves      R  log  2   =  0.9747   R^{2}_{\log}=0.9747    on S4 and    0.9718  0.9718    on S5 and outperforms all three alternatives. 
 
 
 
 Interaction terms. 

 
 We test two types of interaction terms to examine whether the additive law overlooks coupling among the scaling variables.
First, motivated by a previously proposed coupled form  ( Videau et al., 2026 ) , we replace each pairwise sum of the terms associated with    N  N   ,     D  s   D_{s}   , and     D  l   D_{l}    by      (    x  i   +   x  j    )   k   (x_{i}+x_{j})^{k}    in turn, while leaving the third term additive. The three resulting formulas are given explicitly in Tables   3   and   4  .
We constrain the coupling exponent to     0  <  k  ≤  2   0<k\leq 2   . Across both datasets, both held-out scales, and all three pairings, the fitted value reaches the upper bound to numerical precision. The tested constrained fits provide no consistent extrapolation advantage over the additive law. 
 
 
 Second, following the motivation of prior work on continued pretraining  ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) , we consider the interaction
    C  +   A  /   N  α    +    B  s   /   D  s   β  s     +    B  l   /   (    D  l   β  l    ​   N  η    )     C+A/N^{\alpha}+B_{s}/D_{s}^{\beta_{s}}+B_{l}/(D_{l}^{\beta_{l}}N^{\eta})   . This formulation allows model size to modulate the contribution of LCCP data to long-context learning.
This interaction is less accurate than the free-offset additive law in all four extrapolation tests. On the linear-decay data, it achieves      R  log  2   =  0.7557   R^{2}_{\log}=0.7557    on S4 and    0.7163  0.7163    on S5. On the constant-learning-rate data, it remains relatively accurate on S4, with      R  log  2   =  0.9537   R^{2}_{\log}=0.9537   , but deteriorates to      R  log  2   =   −  0.2800    R^{2}_{\log}=-0.2800    on S5. 
 
 
 Overall, the tested interaction parameterizations provide no consistent extrapolation benefit. This does not rule out interactions among the scaling variables, but indicates that these particular forms do not outperform the simpler additive law over the measured range.

 LCCP data processing. 

 
 Documents longer than the target sequence length are split into consecutive segments of the target length, starting from the beginning of each document, with a one-token overlap between adjacent segments.
All resulting segments are retained rather than selecting a random window. Instruction-form examples that exceed the target length are filtered instead of segmented to preserve their supervision structure. The 32K LCCP stage uses standard causal attention without document-boundary masking, whereas the 128K extension uses span-based attention to isolate packed documents.
We perform exact document-level matching between the LCCP training corpus and the GovReport documents used for LongPPL evaluation, and find no exact matches. This check is limited to exact document overlap. 
 
 
 
 Supervised fine-tuning data.

  A.5  Scaling-Law Fitting Details 

 
 Eq.   1   contains seven parameters to estimate. Although S1–S4 provide 100 configurations in total, a direct seven-parameter nonlinear fit can be ill-conditioned or nearly non-identifiable: distinct parameter combinations can yield similar predictions over the observed range, making coefficients and exponents sensitive to noise and extrapolation unreliable. We therefore use the experimental-grid structure to stabilize estimation. 
 
 
 At each model scale, the grid varies the short- and long-context
budgets separately. Every fixed     (  N  ,   D  s   )   (N,D_{s})    pair has five observations
at distinct     D  l   D_{l}    values, and every fixed     (  N  ,   D  l   )   (N,D_{l})    pair has five
observations at distinct     D  s   D_{s}    values. With    N  N    and     D  s   D_{s}    fixed, the remaining terms and offset are constant on the     D  l   D_{l}    slice, so we fit
    y  =     b  l   ​   D  l   −   β  l      +  c    y=b_{l}D_{l}^{-\beta_{l}}+c    to obtain a local estimate of     β  l   \beta_{l}   .
We repeat this over all available slices in the training split and use the median as a training-data-driven reference; the analogous procedure yields the reference for     β  s   \beta_{s}   .
Taking the median limits the effect of a small number of anomalous slices. These slice fits provide initialization and empirical thresholds
for the exponent regularization; they do not replace the final joint fit. 
 
 
 The joint fit uses Huber loss in     log  ⁡   (  LongPPL  )    \log(\mathrm{LongPPL})    space,
with equal weight assigned to each training configuration.
We set  f_scale=0.05  by pilot fits on the training set
before held-out evaluation.
We apply the one-sided exponent regularizer 
 

  
  
         λ  s   ​    [   max  ⁡   (    β  s   −    β  ¯   s    ,  0  )    ]   2    +    λ  l   ​    [   max  ⁡   (    β  l   −    β  ¯   l    ,  0  )    ]   2     ,    λ  s   =   λ  l   =  1    .   \lambda_{s}\bigl[\max(\beta_{s}-\bar{\beta}_{s},0)\bigr]^{2}+\lambda_{l}\bigl[\max(\beta_{l}-\bar{\beta}_{l},0)\bigr]^{2},\qquad\lambda_{s}=\lambda_{l}=1.    
    
 
 The thresholds      β  ¯   s   \bar{\beta}_{s}    and      β  ¯   l   \bar{\beta}_{l}    are obtained
from the slice fits for the corresponding training split.
The regularizer penalizes above-threshold exponents without fixing
them or directly constraining the fitted coefficients and offset    C  C   . 
 
 
 For each held-out-scale experiment, slice-based exponent estimates,
initialization settings, and regularization hyperparameters are
determined exclusively from the training split.
Specifically, the S4 extrapolation uses only S1–S3 for these choices,
whereas the S5 extrapolation uses only S1–S4.
The held-out scale is used only for evaluation. 
 
 
 Additionally, the one-variable experiments in Section   4.3   show that an axis exponent can be estimated reliably when the other two variables are fixed and sufficient observations are available along that axis.
For future fits of the same scaling law, the multiple     D  l   D_{l}    observations on a single  Constant-Last4  trajectory can first define a reasonable range for     β  l   \beta_{l}   , followed by joint fitting.

 For a weighted parameter average, we write

  Last   M  M    .  
 Last   M  M     assigns equal weight to the final    M  M    consecutive checkpoints and zero weight to all earlier checkpoints: 
 

  
  
      w  i   =   {       1  /  M   ,       i  >   K  −  M    ,        0  ,       i  ≤   K  −  M    .        w_{i}=\begin{cases}1/M,&i>K-M,\\

This rule performs a local trajectory average: it suppresses checkpoint-level fluctuations while avoiding the stale parameters of much earlier checkpoints. 
 
 
  1-Sqrt. 
Let     t  i   t_{i}    denote the training iteration of checkpoint    i  i   , and define its normalized age relative to the newest checkpoint as 
 

  
  
       a  i   =     t  K   −   t  i      t  K   −   t  1      .   a_{i}=\frac{t_{K}-t_{i}}{t_{K}-t_{1}}.    
  
  (5)    
 
 The unnormalized and normalized  1-Sqrt  weights are 
 

  
  
         w  ~   i   =   1  −    a  i      ,    w  i   =     w  ~   i     ∑   j  =  1   K     w  ~   j       .   \widetilde{w}_{i}=1-\sqrt{a_{i}},\qquad w_{i}=\frac{\widetilde{w}_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\widetilde{w}_{j}}.    
  
  (6)

Unlike a rank-only rule, our implementation uses the actual iteration gaps through     t  i   t_{i}   .

We implement post-hoc exponential moving averaging over all available checkpoints.

  (7)

We evaluate the  DARE-linear  variant  ( Yu et al., 2024 ) .

 Interpreting the Advantage of Simple Averaging. 

 
 TIES and DARE were developed primarily to control interference when combining task vectors from models with different fine-tuning objectives or acquired capabilities.
Our setting is structurally different: all checkpoints lie on one continuous training trajectory, so their updates are strongly correlated and temporally nested rather than representing independent capability directions.
In this regime, local or recency-weighted parameter averaging directly smooths trajectory noise while preserving the many small, consistently accumulated updates.
Magnitude trimming in  TIES  and random dropping in  DARE  can remove part of this shared trajectory signal, while their use of the full checkpoint range also assigns influence to relatively stale states.
Consistent with this interpretation, the best tested  TIES  setting (    ρ  =  0.5   \rho=0.5   ) obtains a LongPPL of    3.7950  3.7950   , which is worse than the raw final checkpoint at    3.6233  3.6233   , while the best tested DARE setting (    ρ  =  0.5   \rho=0.5   ) reaches    3.5217  3.5217    but remains behind  1-Sqrt  at    3.4724  3.4724    and  EMA  with     β  =  0.9   \beta=0.9    at    3.4720  3.4720   . 
 
 
 These results do not imply that  TIES  or  DARE  is generally inferior. Rather, they suggest that the mechanisms designed to resolve interference are less well suited to our setting, where checkpoints are highly correlated and lie along a single training trajectory. In this regime, simple weighted averaging is more effective, stable, and inexpensive.

  Appendix D  Additional Analyses 

 
  D.1  Comparing LongPPL and ShortPPL Improvement Rates 

 
 Although LongPPL is widely used as a measure of long-context performance  ( Willette et al., 2026 ;  Song et al., 2026 )  and correlates well with a range of long-context downstream tasks  ( Qiao et al., 2026 ;  Fang et al., 2025 ) , it is still a perplexity-based metric. A lower LongPPL need not result exclusively from better use of distant context: it can also reflect broad next-token prediction gains, adaptation to the evaluation domain, or changes in local token statistics. We therefore compare changes in LongPPL and ShortPPL to examine whether tokens with different dependence on distant context improve at different rates during LCCP. 
 
 
 We use ShortPPL as a matched control metric. It is computed on tokens whose prediction has limited dependence on distant context, and thus provides a proxy for perplexity changes that do not require long-range information. ShortPPL is not a pure measure of general language-modeling ability, but it helps capture broad improvements under the same model, data, and evaluation procedure. Along the S5  Constant-Last4  trajectory, we fit the same saturating power-law form independently to both metrics over 24 checkpoints, from      D  l   =   5  ​  N    D_{l}=5N    to      D  l   =   120  ​  N    D_{l}=120N    (approximately    100  100   B tokens): 
 

  
  
        log  ⁡   M  ⁡   (   D  l   )     =    E  M   +    A  M   ​   D  l   −   α  M        ,   M  ∈   {  LongPPL  ,  ShortPPL  }     .   \log M(D_{l})=E_{M}+A_{M}D_{l}^{-\alpha_{M}},\qquad M\in\{\mathrm{LongPPL},\mathrm{ShortPPL}\}.    
    
 
 
 
 For metric    M  M   , we define the log-scale elasticity 
 

  
  
        e  M   ​   (   D  l   )    =   |    ∂   log  ⁡  M     ∂   log  ⁡   D  l      |    .   e_{M}(D_{l})=\left|\frac{\partial\log M}{\partial\log D_{l}}\right|.    
    
 
 For the fitted form, this is       e  M   ​   (   D  l   )    =    α  M   ​   A  M   ​   D  l   −   α  M       e_{M}(D_{l})=\alpha_{M}A_{M}D_{l}^{-\alpha_{M}}   , so it measures the relative rate of improvement at a given training budget. We then compare the two metrics using 
 

  
  
       R  ⁡   (   D  l   )    =     e  Long   ​   (   D  l   )      e  Short   ​   (   D  l   )      .   R(D_{l})=\frac{e_{\mathrm{Long}}(D_{l})}{e_{\mathrm{Short}}(D_{l})}.    
    
 
 This ratio is a descriptive comparison: it indicates whether LongPPL is changing faster than the matched ShortPPL control, but it is not an exact causal decomposition of the total perplexity gain. 
 
   
  Figure 17:  Ratio of the log-scale elasticities of LongPPL and ShortPPL along the S5  Constant-Last4  LCCP trajectory. Markers show measurements through (     D  l   =   120  ​  N    D_{l}=120N   ). Solid curves show fits over the measured range; dashed curves extend these fits to (     D  l   =   240  ​  N    D_{l}=240N   ). 
 
 
 As shown in Figure   17  ,     R  ⁡   (   D  l   )    R(D_{l})    is largest at the beginning of the trajectory and gradually decreases as LCCP proceeds. Numerically, it declines from approximately    3.35  3.35    at     5  ​  N   5N    to    2.22  2.22    at     120  ​  N   120N   , but remains above    2  2    throughout the observed range. Thus, LongPPL improves substantially faster than ShortPPL, although the difference in the relative improvement rates of LongPPL and ShortPPL narrows as training proceeds.
In conjunction with the downstream evaluations in Section   5  , the result supports using LongPPL as a useful proxy for long-context performance during extended LCCP.

 During extended LCCP,  Constant-Last4  lowers LongPPL and improves average RULER-32K performance relative to  Constant-Raw .
Table   14   summarizes these differences and compares performance after subsequent training initialized from the corresponding raw and merged checkpoints.
After 128K context extension, models initialized from  Constant-Last4  outperform those initialized from  Constant-Raw  by    0.60  0.60    points on average on RULER-128K.
Similarly, after 2.10B tokens of SFT, the four-benchmark average is    0.51  0.51    points higher for  Constant-Last4 .
However, with a larger SFT budget of 4.20B tokens, this difference narrows and slightly favors  Constant-Raw  by    0.16  0.16    points. In agentic SFT,  Constant-Last4  retains a small    0.15  0.15   -point advantage over  Constant-Raw . Thus, the advantage of checkpoint merging observed during LCCP does not consistently persist through subsequent training. 
 
 
 A related finding is that learning-rate decay can improve pre-training metrics while yielding worse performance after SFT than training without decay  ( Yano et al., 2026 ) .
Although this concerns learning-rate schedules rather than checkpoint merging, it similarly highlights that better performance before SFT need not imply better performance afterward.
Our limited experiments do not establish when merging advantages persist.
One tentative hypothesis is that these advantages may survive a modest amount of additional training or with a similar objective, as in context extension, but diminish or reverse with longer training or larger shifts in training data and objectives, as in SFT.
Further controlled experiments are needed to distinguish these factors. 
 
 
 We therefore primarily view checkpoint merging as a stable  observation  mechanism for assessing long-context performance along an extensible constant-learning-rate trajectory, rather than as a strategy for consistently improving subsequent training outcomes.
In this protocol, merging is used for evaluation, while continued LCCP proceeds from the latest raw checkpoint. 
 
 
 
  D.3  Dynamics of the Retrieval-Head during LCCP 

 
 Previous work suggests that retrieval-head metrics can serve as mechanistic probes of model development over the course of LCCP  ( Liang et al., 2026 ) .
Following the definition in that work, we measure the copy-retrieval behavior of individual attention heads. For each teacher-forced answer token, a head records a retrieval hit when its maximum-attended input position lies within the reference sentence and contains the same token_id as the current target token. The per-instance score of a head is the fraction of answer tokens producing such hits, and its checkpoint-level retrieval score is obtained by averaging the per-instance scores over our 2,400 LongBio single-needle instances  ( Yang et al., 2026 ) . A head is classified as a retrieval head if its checkpoint-level score exceeds    0.1  0.1   .
For longitudinal comparison, we identify 10 retrieval heads at the raw     5  ​  N   5N    checkpoint and track the mean score of this fixed set at all subsequent checkpoints. The retrieval-head count, by contrast, is recomputed independently at each checkpoint as the number of heads whose checkpoint-level score exceeds    0.1  0.1   . 
 
   
  Figure 18:  Retrieval-head metrics and RULER-32K performance along the S5
 Constant-Last4  trajectory. The three square panels show the
fixed-head retrieval score, retrieval-head count, and RULER-32K score. 
 
 
 As shown in Figure   18  , the retrieval-head metrics capture substantial learning during the early stages of training. The mean score of the fixed retrieval heads rises rapidly, while the number of heads above the threshold increases from 10 to 13–14. After around      D  l   =   30  ​  N    D_{l}=30N   , however, the fixed-head score reaches a noisy plateau and exhibits no sustained upward trend.
This plateau does not coincide with saturation in behavioral performance. The RULER-32K score continues to improve up to      D  l   =   120  ​  N    D_{l}=120N   , with additional gains on several multi-key and multi-query tasks.
These results suggest that the selected retrieval-head probes do not capture all behavioral improvements that occur later in LCCP.
Retrieval-head metrics are therefore useful as early-stage diagnostic signals, but they should be considered alongside LongPPL and behavioral evaluations rather than used alone to determine whether LCCP training has saturated.
