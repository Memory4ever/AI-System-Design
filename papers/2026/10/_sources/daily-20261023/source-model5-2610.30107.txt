Abstract
Flow matching trains a continuum of velocity regression problems but usually
reports a loss averaged over interpolation time, obscuring which regions are
limited by intrinsic ambiguity and which remain data-limited. We introduce the
Velocity Ambiguity Profile (VAP), 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t) , the time-local Bayes-risk floor of
velocity prediction, and decompose time-resolved loss as
L N ​ ( t ) = 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) + R ⁡ ( t , N ) L_{N}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{v}(t)+R(t;N) , where only the excess term R ⁡ ( t , N ) R(t;N) is reducible by more
data or a different estimator. For N N training samples and a K K -component mixture in the structured
example, we prove two statistical benchmarks. In an intrinsic local-regression benchmark, 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t) enters the time-dependent noise
factor of the standard nonparametric rate. In separated isotropic Gaussian
mixtures, the Bayes velocity becomes locally affine, and a label-oracle
residual-regression benchmark has parametric excess-risk scale K ​ 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) / N K\mathcal{A}_{v}(t)/N ,
matched within the same separated residual Gaussian-location subproblem.
Controlled Gaussian-mixture experiments validate this diagnostic through a
dense transition sweep, calibrated oracle-to-blind and empirical-proxy audits,
and a local controlled time-sampler stress test in which a frozen
reducible-error proxy reduces reducible loss while cross-cell sensitivity
delimits aggressive sampling. On CIFAR-100 DINOv2 feature flow, guided time
sampling reduces uniformly evaluated velocity loss by 6.78 % 6.78\% and global
feature Fréchet distance (FD) by 23.6 % 23.6\% , while class-conditional kernel inception
distance (KID) shows no improvement. VAP turns an averaged flow-matching objective into
a time-resolved diagnostic for locating ambiguity-limited and data-limited
regions.
1 Introduction
Flow matching reduces distribution learning to a continuum of velocity
regression problems indexed by interpolation time, but training and evaluation
usually report a path-averaged loss. This hides where additional data can help
and where the target remains intrinsically ambiguous.
This paper asks a simple diagnostic question: which interpolation times
remain limited by reducible estimation error, and which are already limited by
intrinsic velocity ambiguity? A high loss at time t t is actionable only when
it exceeds the Bayes-risk floor. It may reflect an under-trained estimator, or
it may reflect multiple data realizations remaining plausible under the same
noisy interpolant. Finite-data error can be reduced; posterior ambiguity
cannot. The conditional-mean orthogonality behind this decomposition is
classical. Our contribution is to make the induced posterior ambiguity
profile path-specific, expose its mode and information-set structure, and use
it for time-local diagnostics.
Velocity ambiguity as time-resolved Bayes risk.

diagnostic. Let a a be an expert action, ε ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , I ) \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I) be
Gaussian noise, c c be the observation-language context, and let
x t = ( 1 − t ) ​ a + t ​ ε x_{t}=(1-t)a+t\varepsilon , u t = ε − a u_{t}=\varepsilon-a , with the convention that t = 0 t=0
is the data endpoint and t = 1 t=1 is the noise endpoint. VAP is the conditional
velocity variance averaged over the induced interpolant distribution
𝒜 v ​ ( t ) ≔ 𝔼 x t , c ​ [ tr ⁡ Cov ⁡ ( u t ∣ x t , c , t ) ] . \mathcal{A}_{v}(t)\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{E}_{x_{t},c}\!\left[\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(u_{t}\mid x_{t},c,t)\right].
(1)
For t > 0 t>0 , 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t) is equivalently the posterior action variance rescaled by
1 / t 2 1/t^{2} . VAP specializes conditional Bayes risk to the velocity regression
problems induced by the flow path and makes it diagnostic along flow-matching
time. For any trained velocity estimator, the time-resolved loss decomposes as

VAP complements empirical scaling laws and time-aggregated generative-model
baselines [ 14 , 6 , 24 , 16 ] . It asks which

controlled models separate these regimes. Figure 1 previews

Main results.
The paper develops VAP at three levels. First, we prove the exact Bayes-risk
decomposition above and instantiate a time-local intrinsic regression
benchmark: on compact time intervals, the local excess-risk rate has the usual
nonparametric exponent, with time dependence controlled by 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t) up to
bounded factors. Second, in the separated regime of an isotropic Gaussian
mixture, we obtain a sharper label-oracle residual-regression benchmark: the
Bayes velocity is locally affine, the residual benchmark has parametric scale
K ​ 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) / N K\mathcal{A}_{v}(t)/N , matched by a separated residual Gaussian-location lower bound.
Third, controlled Gaussian-mixture experiments validate the diagnostic when
the ambiguity floor is observable. They show separated-regime parametric
scaling under a reliability-filtered readout, oracle-to-blind agreement with
the analytic between-component label cost, and small but measurable blind
neural residuals. A local time-sampler stress test further shows that a frozen
proxy from disjoint source runs can produce same-sign paired reducible-loss
reductions in the canonical balanced cell, while cross-cell sensitivity
delimits aggressive pure sampling. Fourth, a CIFAR-100 DINOv2 feature-flow
experiment tests transfer to natural feature distributions: guided sampling
reduces uniformly evaluated velocity loss by 6.78 % 6.78\% and global feature FD by
23.6 % 23.6\% . Macro conditional KID shows no gain, and lower recall and effective
covariance rank reveal a diversity cost.

4 Main Theoretical Results
We now ask when 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t) predicts the sample-complexity scale, not only the
Bayes-risk floor. Theorem 4 gives a compact local-regression
benchmark in which 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t) enters a nonparametric rate up to bounded factors

We reserve label-oracle residual benchmark for the theorem-level
information set: the estimator receives true component labels and fits the
residual affine response in each separated cell. A label-and-action
oracle is stronger: it also uses the supervised pair to recover
a = x t − t ​ u t a=x_{t}-tu_{t} and estimate component means directly. An analytic oracle
uses the known mixture law only to compute diagnostic quantities such as

compares these controlled oracle arms with estimators that do not receive
labels or analytic responsibilities.

6 Related Work
Flow matching, imitation learning, and schedules.
Flow matching and rectified-flow objectives transport noise to data
[ 20 , 21 , 2 , 36 , 28 , 11 ] .
Imitation scaling and behavioral-cloning theory study data composition and
policy error [ 14 , 6 , 30 , 31 , 12 , 3 ] ,
while schedule methods change the training-time distribution
[ 15 , 13 , 5 , 9 ] . VAP instead
diagnoses the induced regression problems along interpolation time.
Statistical theory and adaptive structure.
Diffusion and score theory characterize minimax rates under manifold and
smoothness assumptions [ 24 , 16 , 4 , 7 , 18 ] ,
and adaptive regression theory studies intrinsic or compositional structure
[ 32 , 35 , 22 , 29 ] .
Our intrinsic result is a time-resolved velocity analog; the separated
Gaussian-mixture result is the scoped label-oracle residual benchmark.
7 Conclusion
VAP turns the aggregate flow-matching loss into a time-resolved diagnostic by
separating the Bayes-risk floor from reducible excess error. The theory gives
two scoped benchmarks: a local intrinsic regression rate with bounded-factor
time dependence and a sharper separated-GMM label-oracle residual scale
K ​ 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) / N K\mathcal{A}_{v}(t)/N . Controlled experiments validate the analytic floor,
separated-regime collapse prediction, oracle-to-blind calibration, proxy-floor
guards, and local time-sampler intervention, whose sensitivity boundary is
given in Appendix D.8 .
On CIFAR-100 DINOv2 feature flow, guided time sampling reduces uniformly
evaluated velocity loss by 6.78 % 6.78\% and global feature FD by 23.6 % 23.6\% .
Macro conditional KID shows no gain, while lower recall and effective
covariance rank reveal a diversity cost. Turning these diagnostics into a budget rule or intervention policy remains open.
Limitations.
The intrinsic result is for the local benchmark
𝒢 t comp \mathcal{G}_{t}^{\mathrm{comp}} , not the full ambient observation, with 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \mathcal{A}_{v}(t)
entering as a bounded-factor prefactor. The GMM result is a separated
label-oracle residual benchmark, not a full-information lower bound because
( x t , u t , t ) (x_{t},u_{t},t) determines a = x t − t ​ u t a=x_{t}-tu_{t} . The natural-data result is limited to the
frozen DINOv2 feature space: it makes no pixel-quality, universal-sampler, or
uniform-metric-improvement claims.

Remark 13 (Comparison with score estimation) .
Oko et al. [24] establish the minimax rate for score estimation in

(i) we work with the velocity parameterization rather than the score;

The time-resolved perspective is essential for our curve collapse prediction.
Appendix B Proof of the Gaussian-Mixture Benchmark
We prove Theorem 5 , the Gaussian-mixture result in the separated
regime.
First, we derive the exact posterior and the Bayes-optimal velocity field.

concentrates on a single component with exponentially small leakage, so the
Bayes velocity is locally affine up to exponentially small error.
This reduces the label-oracle residual estimation problem to a collection of
local Gaussian location subproblems, yielding a label-oracle residual upper
bound of parametric order and a matching lower bound for the corresponding
separated-regime subproblem.

We prove regime-wise normalized collapse by combining the factorization

R ¯ ​ ( t , N ) = R ⁡ ( t , N ) / 𝒜 v ​ ( t ) \bar{R}(t;N)=R(t;N)/\mathcal{A}_{v}(t) .

D.7 CIFAR-100 DINOv2 feature-flow materials
This appendix records the feature extraction, training, deployment, and
metric definitions supporting Section 5.4 . The experiment uses
five paired training seeds throughout; reported confidence intervals and
seed-level signs are computed over those paired runs.
Representation and training protocol.
Images from the official CIFAR-100 train and test splits are passed through
frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14. We retain the final-LayerNorm CLS representation
with dimension 384 384 , then apply train-split centering and a global RMS
calibration. The calibration uses all 50,000 50{,}000 official training rows and
does not access test features. Each target seed uses a fine-balanced subset of
25 25 examples per class, or 2,500 2{,}500 labeled examples; the five reference
models use the full 50,000 50{,}000 labeled training rows. The same transform is
used for every arm and for the held-out reference features. There is no PCA,
whitening, learned projection, or pixel decoder. The flow-matching model is
conditioned on the fine label and uses a 432 → 512 → 512 → 512 → 512 → 384 432\mathbin{\to}512\mathbin{\to}512\mathbin{\to}512\mathbin{\to}512\mathbin{\to}384 MLP, Adam, learning rate
3 × 10 − 4 3\times 10^{-4} , and batch size 512 512 . Uniform training samples t t from the
prescribed interval; the VAP-guided arm samples from the frozen proxy with
q 4 ​ ( t ) ∝ R ^ ​ ( t ) 4 q_{4}(t)\propto\widehat{R}(t)^{4} on 64 64 categorical bins and no importance
correction. Evaluation uses a uniformly weighted grid of 25 25 time points.
Training readouts.
The primary training comparison evaluates both arms at the common step
11,500 11{,}500 on the 25 25 -point uniform-time grid. The VAP-guided arm lowers
velocity loss by 6.78 % 6.78\% relative to the paired uniform arm, with all
5 / 5 5/5 paired seeds favoring the guided arm and a paired 95 % 95\% interval of
[ − 7.11 % , − 6.44 % ] [-7.11\%,-6.44\%] . The seed-mean own-best comparison gives a 6.49 % 6.49\%
reduction; this secondary readout is retained to distinguish a common-step
comparison from a per-method trajectory summary.
Deployment checkpoint selection.
For closed-loop evaluation, each method is materialized once at the mode of
its five per-seed own-best steps. This yields step 8,500 8{,}500 for the uniform
baseline and step 10,500 10{,}500 for the VAP-guided arm. The selected checkpoints
are then held fixed for all paired ODE rollouts. The VAP-guided arm was chosen
after inspecting the parent training trajectories, so this is a descriptive
post-selection comparison rather than an arm-independent preregistered test.
Each rollout starts from paired Gaussian noise and integrates the learned field
from t = 1 t=1 to t = 0 t=0 with fixed-step Heun, 100 100 integration steps and 200 200 network
function evaluations. The state is float32 and the integration grid is
float64; the literal primary policy calls the network on the full path,
including t ∈ ( 0.95 , 1 ] t\in(0.95,1] and t ∈ [ 0 , 0.05 ) t\in[0,0.05) . Each method contributes 6,000 6{,}000
generated features, 60 60 per fine class, and is compared with a disjoint 6,000 6{,}000 -
feature test reference bank, also 60 60 per class. The checkpoint rule is a
trajectory-selection convention; it is not an additional training run.
Closed-loop metrics.
The generated 384 384 -dimensional features are compared with held-out CIFAR-100
DINOv2 features. Table 15 reports method
means over the five paired seeds. Feature FD is a global Gaussian
Fréchet distance in feature space. Precision, density, and coverage use
fixed k k -nearest-neighbor radii ( k = 3 k=3 for precision and k = 5 k=5 for density

Bridge estimator definitions.
The true-label plug-in oracle ​ _ ​ ols \mathrm{oracle\_ols} estimates each component mean
by ordinary least squares (OLS), which here reduces to its labeled sample
mean, and uses the known within-component variance and the true component
label at prediction time. The blind EM-OLS arm fits a spherical Gaussian
mixture with the known component count K K to unlabeled training actions
using EM. It plugs the fitted means and mixture weights, together with the
arithmetic mean of the fitted component variances, into the marginal
Gaussian-mixture velocity formula and averages over posterior component
probabilities given ( x t , t ) (x_{t},t) . Thus, neither fitting nor velocity prediction
uses true labels. The OLS suffix is an experimental arm name inherited from
the oracle comparison; the blind arm does not perform a separate OLS
velocity regression. Its prediction is closed form conditional on the
iteratively fitted mixture parameters.

F.3 Representative task descriptions
Turning on a radio.
The robot first uses its onboard cameras to identify the radio on the
tabletop and approach it. The right gripper is then used to take hold of
the device, after which the left gripper depresses the power button; once
the radio has been switched on, it is returned to the table surface. What
makes this scenario non-trivial is the joint requirement of locomotion
plus dual-arm operation: the right hand has to keep a stable grip so that
the reaction force produced by the button press does not dislodge the
radio. At the start of every rollout the radio is placed at a different
pose on the table, although it is always within the robot’s field of
view and reach. Inference is allowed at most 6,000 6{,}000 steps.
Figure 9: Turning on a radio. BEHAVIOR-1K task visualization
used for application-context reporting.
Picking up trash.
In this scenario the right gripper holds a refuse container throughout
the entire rollout while the robot navigates in turn to three soda cans
distributed across the scene. At each can, the left gripper is used to
lift the can and drop it into the container kept by the right hand;
after all three cans have been collected, the filled container is set
back onto the floor. The difficulty arises from a long-horizon mobile
manipulation pattern that imposes asymmetric bimanual roles: the right
arm acts as a passive but stable carrier, while the left arm is in
charge of repeated grasp-and-release motions. Per rollout, both the
container and the cans are spawned at randomized positions so that
nontrivial navigation is required, and the robot’s initial base pose is
also drawn from a bounded distribution to probe generalization. The
inference budget for each rollout is 13,000 13{,}000 steps.
Figure 10: Picking up trash. BEHAVIOR-1K task visualization
used for application-context reporting.
Spraying fruit trees.
Here the robot uses vision to find a sprayer, picks it up with the left
gripper, and then visits two fruit trees one after the other. Upon
arrival at each tree, the right gripper toggles the sprayer’s switch to
the on position, performs the watering action, and toggles the switch
back off before the platform moves on to the next tree. The chief
difficulty is bimanual tool use combined with mobile manipulation: the
left arm must hold the fluid container steady so that the spray remains
aimed at the target, while the right arm produces the contact force on
the switch without disturbing this alignment. The sprayer and both trees
are spawned at random workspace positions, forcing the robot to actively
search the scene and replan its path; the starting base pose of the
robot is also varied across rollouts. The inference horizon is capped at
10,000 10{,}000 steps.
Figure 11: Spraying fruit trees. BEHAVIOR-1K task visualization
used for application-context reporting.
Cooking hot dogs.
This task strings together articulated-object handling with intermediate
buffering of cargo. The robot first opens the refrigerator, takes out
two hot dogs, and shuts the door; one of the items is set aside on the
tabletop while the other is loaded into the microwave; the buffered hot
dog is then transferred from the table into the microwave, the
microwave door is closed, and the start switch is engaged. The
fundamental challenge is long-horizon planning across multiple
workstations (the storage compartment, the staging area, and the
cooking appliance), with the kinematics of the appliance doors having
to be coordinated with pick-and-place operations. Initial object poses
and the robot’s starting base pose are varied between rollouts to
evaluate generalization, and the inference horizon is set to 15,000 15{,}000
steps.
Figure 12: Cooking hot dogs. BEHAVIOR-1K task visualization
used for application-context reporting.
Washing a baseball cap.
The robot moves to a washing machine, opens its door, and finds the two
baseball caps that need to be cleaned. With one cap held in each
gripper, it walks back to the washer and inserts both caps into the
narrow drum cavity one after the other; the door is then shut and the
power switch is pressed. The hard part of this task is a long-horizon
mobile manipulation pipeline that combines articulated-object operation
with carrying objects in both hands at the same time: the robot has to
navigate while its grippers are occupied, and then perform
collision-free placements inside a tightly bounded workspace. Both the
poses of the two caps and the robot’s initial base pose are randomized
across trials, and inference is limited to 10,000 10{,}000 steps.
Figure 13: Washing a baseball cap. BEHAVIOR-1K task visualization
used for application-context reporting.
