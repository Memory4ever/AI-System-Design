 Blocks. Each block runs bidirectional space attention within a latent frame, then a SwiGLU MLP; blocks 0, 4,
8, 12 and 15 add causal time attention in between. Attention uses 16 query and 4 key/value heads, QK-normalization, a
gate and rotary embeddings (2D in space, 1D in time). Blocks are pre-norm, without LayerScale or registers.
 Conditioning. Each latent frame is conditioned on its flow time and on the \tau actions that produced it,
action a_{j} producing frame j+1 . Actions are normalized per environment; their per-frame embeddings are
concatenated, projected and passed through an MLP. The flow time gets a sinusoidal embedding and an MLP, and the sum
of both embeddings drives AdaLN  ( Peebles & Xie, 2023 ) before space attention, time attention and the MLP of every block. In human
manipulation, each hand contributes the frame-to-frame change in wrist position (3), wrist orientation (3) and 15
finger-joint rotations (45).
 Objective and recipe. Under diffusion forcing  ( Chen et al., 2024a ) , each latent
frame draws its own flow time t_{i} , and the model regresses the velocity z_{1}-z_{0} from
 z_{t_{i}}=t_{i}z_{1}+(1-t_{i})z_{0} (Figure  9 ). PSD-U, the progressive self-distillation of Boffi et al. (2025) with a uniformly sampled intermediate time, is added on a
fixed quarter of every batch. The recipe (Table  3 ) is the same for every autoencoder; only the action
encoder follows the environment. The world model does not patch the latent further, except in
Section  5.1 .

 Inference. The context is encoded once. At each chunk boundary, the world model reads the
previous latent frame and the \tau actions entered during the chunk and predicts the next latent frame in 10
denoising steps. The frame is decoded for display, re-noised to level 0.2 and cached (Figure  11 ).
Since a_{t} produces frame t+1 , an action first shows \tau frames after it is entered. This timing comes from
supervision: the architecture only keeps an action from changing frames already shown, and within a latent frame each
decoded frame can depend on all \tau actions, so frame 5 reflects a_{4} and earlier actions such as a_{3} .
 Latency. Figures  4 b and  14 plot the action latency of
Equation ( 1 ) for the first action of a chunk, the worst case, at f=20 frames per second. We time
 t_{\text{gen}} , one world model call with its 10 denoising steps and cache pass, and t_{\text{dec}} , the decode of
one new latent frame, on one H100 at batch 1 in bf16 with torch.compile , averaged over 32 latent frames after
warm-up on the robot-manipulation world models (Table  4 ). Each decoder runs its most efficient
streaming decode: causal decoders decode only the new latent and reuse the context (exactly for Wan 2.2; for CADRE,
MIRA and Cosmos we time the decode of the new latent, the cost of a decoder cache), DC-AE decodes each frame alone,
and the non-causal LTX-2 re-decodes an 8-latent window. The rollouts of Table  7 are decoded over each
decoder’s trained window. The resulting maximum frame rate, \tau/(t_{\text{gen}}+t_{\text{dec}}) , differs from the displayed rate, which is always
20 frames per second; it only tells whether the world model runs fluidly. Every CADRE autoencoder with \tau\geq 4 
does, as does CADRE- 2{\times}32{\times}32 with one step; those with \tau=1 reach only 7 to 13 frames per second. Raising \tau raises both the action latency and the maximum frame
rate (Figure  1 ). With fewer denoising steps, every
CADRE autoencoder with \tau\geq 2 runs fluidly, while at \tau=1 this needs one decoding step and
at most four denoising steps (Figure  10 ).

 Appendix E Inverse dynamics probes for action recoverability
A frozen probe, one per environment, reads the actions back from decoded frames, so it scores every autoencoder in
the same space.
 Architecture. The probe maps a window of 8 frames to one action per frame. A frozen DINOv3
ViT-B/16  ( Siméoni et al., 2026 ) gives patch features from layers 2, 5, 8 and 11, combined by a learned softmax.
Six factorized space-time blocks with bidirectional attention follow, then a per-frame attention pool and a
two-layer head (27M trained parameters).
 Training. The probe trains on real clips from the world models’ training split with a smooth L_{1} loss on
standardized actions and photometric augmentation only. It uses AdamW (learning rate 3{\times}10^{-4} , batch 384)
with cosine decay over 60k steps; we keep the 55k-step checkpoint (60k for human manipulation).
 Score. For each action dimension d , R^{2}_{d} compares the recovered actions with the commanded ones over the
last 16 generated frames of all clips, and ARR averages it normalized by the probe’s R^{2} on the real continuation,
 \mathrm{ARR}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{\max\big(R^{2}_{d},\,-1\big)}{R^{2}_{d}(\mathrm{real})}. 
 (7) 
On real video, the probe reaches R^{2} of 0.91 in robot manipulation, 0.93 in driving and 0.83 in indoor navigation.
Human manipulation has 102 action dimensions, and the probe barely reads the 90 finger-joint dimensions at 288{\times}512 , so
 \mathcal{D} holds only the 12 wrist dimensions there (real-video R^{2} 0.82).
 Control. We also score the recovered actions against the clip’s own commands shifted by 48 frames, which the
rollout never received. A world model that ignores its actions scores alike on both, so the gap between the two
raw R^{2} values measures action following (Table  5 ). Every CADRE autoencoder keeps a clear gap in
every environment. In driving, the gap of Wan 2.2, Cosmos and LTX-2 is small (0.04 to 0.14),
and so is that of Cosmos and LTX-2 in human manipulation (0.24 and 0.12): their rollouts reproduce motion common to
the environment, such as forward travel in driving.
