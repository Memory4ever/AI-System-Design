How Much Gradient Does Truncated Backprop Lose in Looped Transformers?
Aur Shalev Merin aur.merin@gmail.com
Affiliation: Independent researcher
Abstract
Looped transformers reuse one block of layers many times and are often trained by backpropagating through only the last k k loops. We measure how much of the weight gradient this throws away, exactly and for every k k from one backward pass, on nine looped language models, and report three findings. First, on a trained model this lost gradient is set near the output: once the loop count is well above k k , it barely changes with the loop count (within about 0.03 from 32 to 64 loops on the three models we tested, as a pre-registered test predicted for Parcae). Where the backward signal shrinks, ρ k \rho^{k} , with ρ \rho the signal’s fitted shrink per loop, approximates it at moderate loop counts but overstates it at long ones, because the fitted ρ \rho drifts up as the fit takes in more loops. On Ouro the signal grows, and keeping two of four loops still loses 81% of the final output’s gradient. Second, the lost gradient falls during fine-tuning, by 0.07–0.22 within 300 steps on three of four models, on held-out problems too. The fall is not just the read-out’s share of the gradient; training only the layers outside the loop reproduced up to half of it, and it also falls under truncated training. So a trained model’s value overstates what truncation removes later. Third, we could not show that it predicts the training cost of truncation better than rules that need no measurement: on two new models it tied Parcae’s cost at the same k k , in a test too small to separate them.
1 Introduction
Looped transformers reuse one block of layers many times in a row instead of stacking many different blocks. Examples are Huginn ( Geiping et al., 2025 ), Parcae ( Prairie et al., 2026 ) and Ouro ( Zhu et al., 2025 ). Running the block more times lets the model compute longer without adding parameters.
Backpropagating through every loop means storing activations for every loop. So looped models are often trained by sending the learning signal back through only the last k k of R R loops:
•
Huginn samples the number of loops at random and backpropagates through the last 8 ( Geiping et al., 2025 , Sec. 3.3 );
•
Parcae uses about the last half ( Prairie et al., 2026 , Sec. 4.2 );
•
the converted models of McLeish et al. (2026) use at most the last 8.
The choice is made by hand. Prior work has measured training cost against k k ( Prairie et al., 2026 , App. I ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Xiong et al., 2026 ), what truncation does to pretraining at fixed compute ( Schwethelm et al., 2026a ), how rare, very large gradient spikes appear at large k k ( Wang et al., 2026 , App. C.1 ), and the agreement between truncated and full gradients at single settings ( Huang et al., 2026 , App. C.3 and C.8 ). For recurrent networks unrolled over time, Aicher et al. (2019) bound the bias of truncation geometrically and estimate its decay rate from data. For looped models, how much gradient truncation loses at each k k has not been measured.
We measure it on nine looped language models (seven published, two our own conversions), follow it into fine-tuning, and ask what it says about the cost of training with truncation. The cost results come from 600-step fine-tunes of already-trained models, where truncation costs little in absolute terms (on Parcae, keeping one of 32 loops cost about 0.04 nats and the blended settings of S5 0.005–0.016 nats, against a 0.18-nat benefit of training the loop at all). So they are a narrow proxy for choosing k k in pretraining. We pre-registered sixteen studies, S1–S16, listed in Appendix B (Table B ). What we find:
1.
On a trained model, the lost-gradient curve is set near the output, and ρ \rho drifts (Section 3 ). One full backward pass per held-out sequence gives the lost gradient for every k k . Once the loop count is well above k k it barely changes with the loop count (within about 0.03 at k ≤ 8 k\leq 8 from 32 to 64 loops; S10), as a settled forward pass predicts. Where the backward signal shrinks, ρ k \rho^{k} was within about 20% of it at the loop counts of Table 1 (a range we observed, not a threshold set in advance). The fitted ρ \rho drifts up with the loop count, because a longer fit takes in more of the slowly fading far loops, related to the effect Aicher et al. (2019, Sec. 5.4) describe for recurrent networks; so ρ k \rho^{k} overstates the lost gradient at longer loop counts (S10). A two-mode model gives the drift a closed form, and its pre-registered extrapolation to 96 and 128 loops held on Parcae-140m (S14), though a trend linear in log(loop count) would have passed too. The exception is Ouro, whose backward signal grows: keeping one or two of its four loops loses 92% or 81% of its final output’s gradient, and keeping two loses 85% under its own training objective without the entropy term (S15). These are statements about the gradient: we did not train Ouro with truncation.
2.
It falls during fine-tuning (Section 4 ). After 300 full-backprop steps it was 0.07–0.22 lower per sequence on three of four models, and about as much on held-out GSM8K problems ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ; S12, S13). Most of the fall remains when the gradient that gets no credit through the loop is left out (S15). A separate fine-tune that trained only the layers outside the loop, with the loop’s weights fixed, reproduced about half of that loop-credited fall on two models (0.48 and 0.50) and little of it on the third (0.14; S16). That compares two runs; it does not split the full run’s fall. A fine-tune through only the last 2 loops lowers it too, by 61–98% as much (S15). With the text moving it by up to 0.12 as well, the number is a property of a checkpoint and a text, not of the model.
3.
We could not show that it predicts training cost better than rules that need no measurement (Section 5 ). Within one model it is a relabelling of k k . Across models, a pre-registered test on two new models found that a cost curve fitted on Parcae did no better than Parcae’s own cost at the same k k , the better of two such rules fixed in advance (S11), although those two models’ costs differed by up to a factor of two. The test was small (six settings on two models) and could not have shown a modest advantage.
Appendix B keeps the study-by-study record: questions, freeze commits, verdicts, amendments and exclusions. Code, pre-registrations, results and the record of when each freeze was pushed are at https://github.com/Aur-Shalev-Merin/lost-gradient-looped-transformers .
2 Measuring the lost gradient
Notation. A looped model applies the same block to a hidden state R R times and then reads out. Counting loops back from the output ( j = 0 j=0 is the last loop):
•
λ j \lambda_{j} , the backward signal: the gradient of the loss with respect to the hidden state after loop j j (the adjoint);
•
c j c_{j} , loop j j ’s contribution to the gradient of the shared weights: λ j \lambda_{j} pushed back through that loop’s dependence on those weights;
•
g g , the full gradient, and g k g_{k} , the gradient when backprop runs through only the last k k loops, which drops the contributions of the loops j ≥ k j\geq k (the layers after the loop contribute the same term to both);
•
D k = g − g k D_{k}=g-g_{k} , the sum of the dropped contributions.
The lost gradient is the relative size of what truncation drops, for one held-out sequence:
lost gradient = | g − g k | | g | . \text{lost gradient}=\frac{|g-g_{k}|}{|g|}.
It can exceed 1 when the truncated gradient points partly against the full one.
Aicher et al. (2019) define the relative bias of truncated backprop as the same ratio for expected gradients, | 𝔼 ​ g − 𝔼 ​ g k | / | 𝔼 ​ g | |\mathbb{E}\,g-\mathbb{E}\,g_{k}|/|\mathbb{E}\,g| . That is the quantity their convergence bound prices, and closer to what an optimiser’s averaged update sees. Our tables report a different statistic: the median over held-out sequences of the per-sequence ratio, on the trained model. Section 4 and Appendix compare the two, before and during fine-tuning (S12).
Which number. Several protocol choices move the lost gradient by about as much as the effects we report, so we fix one and name the others where they appear. The primary statistic is the per-sequence, all-parameter ratio, as a median over held-out sequences on the trained model. It needs no batch or optimiser state, and it is what the decay rule below describes. On Parcae-140m at 32 loops and k = 1 k=1 , the alternatives read as follows (Appendix , Table ):
•
the clipped average over 64 sequences reads 0.97, against 0.80 per sequence;
•
the unclipped expected gradient of Aicher et al. loses 100% at every k k , because a few sequences with very large gradients dominate it;
•
after 300 fine-tuning steps the clipped averages agree with the per-sequence median within 0.05 (Section 4 ).
Leaving out the gradient that gets no credit through the loop raises the value by 0.01–0.04 on the trained models and by up to 0.09 after fine-tuning (Appendix ). The text moves it by up to 0.12 (Section 3 ), and the starred rows of Table 1 use a probe over part of the loop. For what truncation removes from a training update, the per-sequence median after some fine-tuning is the closest of these. For comparing models, use one protocol throughout.
Measuring it exactly. One backward pass measures the lost gradient for every k k :
1.
Run the full backward pass once on the trained model with the weights frozen, and record the backward signal λ j \lambda_{j} at every loop.
2.
Push each loop’s signal through that loop’s own weight Jacobian, one vector-Jacobian product per loop. This gives each contribution c j c_{j} .
3.
Sum the contributions from the far end. That gives D k D_{k} , and so the lost gradient, at every k k (Appendix A ).
Equivalently, give each loop its own copy of the shared weights; standard autograd then returns every c j c_{j} from one backward pass, at the price of storing a gradient per loop. Tables 1 and 2 use one truncated backward pass per k k ; Appendix uses the per-loop contributions. On the same sequences the two agree within 0.001 on Parcae (fp32) and within 0.005 on Huginn (bf16). A cheaper stand-in, the summed backward signal, keeps only a running sum of the backward signal over loops, the size of a hidden state rather than of the parameters. It came within 0.01–0.03 of the loop’s lost share on Parcae and Huginn, but was off by up to 0.18 on two other models (Appendix ).
A one-number summary, ρ \rho . From the same pass, ρ \rho is the backward signal’s average shrink per loop: the slope of a straight-line fit to log ⁡ | λ j | \log|\lambda_{j}| over all R R loops, as a factor, with the median taken over held-out sequences. If the signal shrinks by ρ \rho per loop and the loops push the shared weights in broadly similar directions, the dropped contributions are the tail of a geometric series, and
lost gradient ≈ ρ k . \text{lost gradient}\approx\rho^{k}.
With ρ = 0.8 \rho=0.8 and 4 loops kept, about 41% of the gradient is lost; with ρ = 0.35 \rho=0.35 , about 1.5%. Appendix A gives exact forms for a finite number of loops. Geometric bounds of this kind are known ( Aicher et al., 2019 , Thm. 1 ; Liao et al., 2018 ; Movahedi et al., 2026 , Prop. 1 ). Aicher et al. (2019, Sec. 4.2) also estimate the rate from data, with a log-linear fit to batch-averaged gradient norms over only the farthest 10% of lags. We fit every loop of each sequence, a choice that matters at long loop counts (Section 3 ). The decay is close to geometric (Figure 1 ): the median R 2 R^{2} of the straight-line fit is 0.99 for Huginn and SmolLM2 and 0.85–0.96 for Parcae, whose decay slows after about 8 loops (the two-mode shape of Appendix ). When the signal grows ( ρ > 1 \rho>1 ), only the exact finite-loop forms apply.
Figure 1: Size of the backward signal at each loop, counting back from the output and relative to the last loop (median over sequences; log scale). The legend gives the median R 2 R^{2} of a straight-line fit per sequence. Ouro’s signal grows.
A dial for ρ \rho . We can slow the fade on purpose by blending each loop’s output with its input, a damped iteration: x ← ( 1 − a ) ​ x + a ​ F ​ ( x ) x\leftarrow(1-a)\,x+a\,F(x) . This is the iteration Geng et al. (2021) use for phantom gradients. For a loop that fully settles, it leaves the settled state, and the gradient you would get with unlimited loops, unchanged; only the per-loop shrink rate moves (Appendix A ). At the finite loop counts we use, the loop has not fully settled, so the model’s function changes too. The change in the trained model’s loss, before any fine-tuning:
•
Parcae-140m at 64 loops ( a = 0.3 a=0.3 ): +0.001 on validation text, +0.02 on test text;
•
Parcae-140m at 48 loops ( a = 0.4 a=0.4 ): +0.002 on validation text, +0.05 on test text;
•
SmolLM2 at a = 0.5 a=0.5 and 0.3: at most +0.007 on both; at a = 0.2 a=0.2 , measured but never trained: +0.04.

The lost gradient carries over across loop counts; ρ \rho does not. Table 2 shows the three models we measured at several loop counts. Once R R is well above k k , the lost gradient depends on k k and hardly on R R . From 32 to 64 loops it moved by at most 0.013 at k ≤ 8 k\leq 8 on Parcae-140m and by at most 0.011 on Parcae-370m, a pre-registered check (S10, bar 0.03). On the same 16 sequences, Huginn’s moved by up to 0.034 from 32 to 48 loops, just over the same bar in a supportive check, and by up to 0.030 from 32 to 64. The fitted ρ \rho moves more: from 0.81 to 0.91 on Parcae-370m between 16 and 64 loops, from 0.77 to 0.87 on Parcae-140m between 8 and 64, and from 0.85 to 0.91 on Huginn. The rise is not universal: on the fixed-5-loop Part II model, ρ \rho falls from 0.85 at 5 loops to 0.79 at 10 (Table ). So a lost-gradient curve measured at one loop count carries over to longer ones, and a fitted ρ \rho does not. The first is what a settled forward pass predicts: the last k k loops see the same states at any loop count, and the far loops added at longer loop counts contribute little even though they fade more slowly. The carry-over held to 128 loops on Parcae-140m (no change at k ≤ 8 k\leq 8 ; S14); on Parcae-370m it held to 96 loops, and at 128 its value at k = 8 k=8 had moved by 0.032.

How close is close. For the unblended points at Table 1 ’s loop counts (23 points on six models), the lost gradient lies between 18% below and 13% above ρ k \rho^{k} , and within 0.07 of it (counting points with ρ k > 0.05 \rho^{k}>0.05 ). At these loop counts the rule has model-specific offsets of that size: Huginn slightly above it, Parcae-1.3B and the SmolLM2 conversions slightly below. At the other loop counts we measured (51 points), the lost gradient falls up to 61% below ρ k \rho^{k} (Parcae-370m at 64 loops, k = 16 k=16 ), and already at 32 loops Parcae-370m falls 21–29% below at k = 4 k=4 –16. For 29 of the 68 points with per-sequence data, ρ k \rho^{k} lies outside the bootstrap 95% interval of the median (half-widths 0.01–0.19, median 0.04). We set no threshold in advance; below, “on the line” means inside the range at Table 1 ’s loop counts. These points pool Table 1 ’s two protocols (the starred rows use a probe over part of the loop), so the range is not a like-for-like comparison between models.
Why ρ k \rho^{k} lands close, and when it does not. Three parts of the account were pre-registered: two here (S9, S10), and the cause of the drift in the next paragraph (S14). First, near the output successive loops’ contributions point in different directions, and this turning is in the backward signal itself, not in how each loop maps it to the weights (S9, on Parcae-140m, Parcae-370m and Huginn). Second, the fitted ρ \rho follows the far loops, which fade more slowly than the loops that carry the lost gradient, so ρ k \rho^{k} overshoots more as the loop count grows: at 64 loops, ρ k \rho^{k} at k = 4 k=4 is 1.34 [1.19, 1.47] and 1.46 [1.14, 1.65] times the lost gradient on Parcae-140m and -370m (bootstrap 95% intervals; S10). Aicher et al. (2019, Sec. 5.4) saw a related far-fade effect in recurrent networks and suggested that slowly decaying directions that do not affect the parameter gradient be ignored, leaving this to future work; the per-loop contributions do that weighting (Appendix ). The rest of the account, an exact identity that re-describes how the dropped sum shrinks and turns, and two checks that failed or were uninformative, is in Appendix .
The drift itself has a simple cause. The backward signal near the output is the same at every loop count; adding loops only lengthens the stretch over which ρ \rho is fitted. Fitting the first 32 or 48 loops of each 64-loop profile reproduces the ρ \rho of the separate 32- and 48-loop runs (median per-sequence difference at most 0.005; the medians of ρ \rho themselves differ by up to 0.009). If the signal is a fast near-output mode plus a slow far mode, the fitted rate has a closed form (Appendix ). The two-mode curve, fitted on the 64-loop profiles, predicted ρ \rho at 96 and 128 loops on Parcae-140m before they were measured: 0.891 [0.875, 0.904] and 0.900 [0.875, 0.910], against 0.898 and 0.908 measured, with the paired rise from 64 to 128 loops above zero (+0.039 [+0.009, +0.047]; S14). That supports the extrapolation more than the specific mechanism: a trend linear in log(loop count), fitted to the 16–64-loop medians after the fact, also lands inside both intervals (0.888 and 0.903), while a straight line in the loop count does not (0.921 and 0.969). A ρ \rho fitted on a fixed window of loops next to the output would not drift by construction; we did not test whether its ρ k \rho^{k} is a closer summary. The evidence for the window account itself is the window check above.
The dial of Section 2 fills the gap between the two clusters (crosses in Figure 2 ), with mixed results. Blended Parcae-140m ( R = 32 R=32 –64, ρ \rho 0.87–0.94) sits on the line, within about 0.03 at every k k . Blended SmolLM2 (A) ( R = 8 R=8 , ρ \rho 0.62–0.82) falls below it, by 0.10–0.22 at a = 0.3 a=0.3 and 0.2, and below both exact finite-loop forms (Appendix A ), so the short loop count does not explain it; we did not measure why.

Where the rule needs care. On Ouro the backward signal grows: about fourfold across the last loop and by 1.2–1.4 times per loop before that, for a fitted ρ \rho of 1.88. So ρ k \rho^{k} grows and the simple rule is meaningless. Ouro normalises its state between loops in its released code ( Guo et al., 2026 , App. D.1 ), so the growth could have reflected the state’s size rather than its sensitivity. It does not, or not only: weighting each loop’s backward signal by the size of its state still gives a fitted growth of 1.66 per loop, and 1.45 on the state before normalisation (S15; median over sequences; every sequence grows on both, the smallest at 1.41 and 1.16). With only 4 loops the measurement barely constrains ρ \rho , and we take one thing from Ouro: its backward signal grows, and truncation throws away most of the gradient of its final output. The same holds for the loss Ouro trains on, a weighted sum of every loop’s output loss: without its entropy term, keeping 1, 2 or 3 of 4 loops loses 0.94, 0.85 or 0.73 of its gradient, slightly more than for the final output alone (0.92, 0.81, 0.71; S15). None of this measures training cost. We did not train Ouro with truncation, because it lay outside the range our cost curves were fitted on (Appendix ), and under Adam a large lost gradient can cost little (Section 5 ). We did not record the exit distribution, so we cannot say how much of that objective’s weight falls on the last loop. Others report that Ouro’s loop does not settle in the forward pass either ( Huang et al., 2026 , Sec. 1 ); those results concern the forward pass, and their link to a growing backward signal is only suggestive. Its accuracy falls past four loops ( Yang et al., 2026 ), and reusing one key-value cache slot across loop steps drops its accuracy from 78% to 0.2%, while Huginn is unaffected ( Schwethelm et al., 2026b , Table 4 ). The warning applies to single sequences too: even at Table 1 ’s loop counts a few sequences have ρ i > 1 \rho_{i}>1 (3 of 32 on Parcae-1.3B, up to 1.41; one of 16 on Parcae-140m and on Parcae-370m), and one Parcae-370m sequence loses nearly all of its gradient at every loop count we measured. The rule also fails for a loop that overshoots (Appendix ). A loop’s largest stretch factor (the largest singular value of its Jacobian) is no guide: about 14 for Huginn and 30 for Parcae-1.3B, whose signals shrink, so a bound built from it says nothing useful here.

It falls, on held-out text too (S12, S13). With full backprop, the per-sequence lost gradient fell by 0.18–0.22 on Parcae-140m, 0.07–0.16 on the Part II models and at most 0.03 on SmolLM2, at k = 1 k=1 , 2 and 4. By the pre-set comparison, the lost share of the clipped update averaged over 64 sequences at step 300 lay 0.06–0.27 below the trained model’s per-sequence median on Parcae and both Part II models. On those models, then, the frozen-model medians of Tables 2 and overstate what truncation removes from the update at step 300 of 600, the only later stage we measured. The measured sequences had been trained on about once by then, so we repeated the measurement on GSM8K test problems, which no run trains on: as many as the split allowed, 56 batches of 8 on Parcae and 48 on the Part II models (on Parcae the repeated fine-tune used a pre-listed backup seed, Appendix ). There the fall was 90–102% as large on average over k k (Parcae 0.20 / 0.22 / 0.21; the two Part II models 0.12–0.14 at k = 1 k=1 and 2). So the fall is not the model fitting the sequences it saw. Those problems are the same kind of text as the fine-tuning data, though, and we did not test other text.
Where it falls (S15, S16). The lost gradient is over all parameters, so its denominator includes gradient that gets no credit through the loop at all, in the layers after it and the read-out, and a growing share of that would lower it with no change in the loop. Leaving it out, | g − g k | / | g − g 0 | |g-g_{k}|/|g-g_{0}| with g 0 g_{0} the gradient at k = 0 k=0 , the loop-only held-out fall was 0.80 times the all-parameter one on Parcae and 0.72 and 0.75 times on the Part II models (averaged over k = 1 k=1 , 2, 4; the two statistics are related by a product, not a sum, so this split is approximate). That locates the fall in the loop’s part of the gradient, not its cause: a change after the loop alters the backward signal that enters it. So we ran a second fine-tune with no loop credit ( k = 0 k=0 , the baseline of Section 5 ), which leaves the loop’s weights exactly as they were and trains only the layers after the loop and the read-out. Its loop-only lost gradient also fell, by 0.48 times the full-backprop fall on Parcae (90% interval over sequences 0.38–0.53) and 0.50 times on the model with a sampled loop count (0.38–0.59), but by only 0.14 times on the fixed-5-loop model (0.04–0.25). So training only outside the loop reproduces about half of the fall on two models and little of it on the third. This compares two runs, not two parts of one. In the full run the outer layers adapt alongside a changing loop, and the k = 0 k=0 run was clipped on only 3–5% of steps against 97–100%. The intervals cover sequences only; a second Parcae seed gave 0.44, and the Part II models have one seed each. On Parcae the token embedding, tied to the read-out, also trained, so the loop’s input changed even though its weights did not.
It falls under truncated training too (S15). A fine-tune with the same recipe, data order and learning rate that backpropagates through only the last 2 loops lowered the held-out lost gradient by 0.19 on Parcae (full backprop: 0.20) and by 0.10 on the model with a sampled loop count and 0.07 on the fixed-5-loop model (full backprop: 0.11 on both), averaged over k k . On the loop-only statistic the truncated fall was 1.00, 0.80 and 0.51 times the full-backprop one (Parcae, sampled loop count, fixed 5 loops), and on the fixed-5-loop model at k = 4 k=4 it did not fall at all (0.400 to 0.399). The truncated gradient is smaller and was clipped on only 13–60% of steps, so the two runs’ updates differ in size.
Averaging matters little once training is under way. At step 300 the averaged, clipped value was within 0.05 of the per-sequence median on every model and k k . On the trained models averaging mattered more, and not in one direction (Appendix ). Without clipping, a few sequences with very large gradients made the averaged gradient, the expected-gradient quantity of Aicher et al., lose 100% at every k k on Parcae; training clipped 99–100% of steps in all four fine-tunes, which limits those sequences’ pull.
5 What the lost gradient says about training cost
Setup. We fine-tuned trained looped models on GSM8K question-and-answer text for 600 steps, with batches of 8 × \times 512 tokens and AdamW ( Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019 ), in three kinds of run: backprop through all R R loops, through only the last k k , and a baseline in which the loop gets no gradient. The training cost of truncation is the fraction of full backprop’s benefit that is lost:
cost = loss with k loops − loss with all loops loss with no loop gradient − loss with all loops . \text{cost}=\frac{\text{loss with $k$ loops}-\text{loss with all loops}}{\text{loss with no loop gradient}-\text{loss with all loops}}.
So 0 means truncation lost nothing, and 1 means it was as bad as not training the loop. The runs being compared see the same data for the same number of steps, so this is the quality lost per training step, not at equal compute; in pretraining at fixed compute, the compute saved by truncation paid off when the loop count was fixed ( Schwethelm et al., 2026a ; Xiong et al., 2026 ), but full backprop through fewer loops did better when the loop count could also be chosen ( Xiong et al., 2026 , Sec. 5 ); we tested neither. All truncated and full-backprop runs of a model use one learning rate, chosen for full backprop; we did not tune it per k k . The effects are small in absolute terms: on Parcae-140m the baseline was 0.18 nats worse than full backprop, and truncation cost 0.005 to 0.016 nats in the settings scored below. Each number averages 5 seeds (where the exclusion rule of Appendix B removed an unstable run, a pre-listed backup seed replaced it).
Within one model, the lost gradient is a relabelling of k k . On a given model the lost gradient at each k k barely depends on the loop count (Section 3 ). So without an intervention, a rule using the lost gradient and a rule using k k alone make the same predictions, and our first study found exactly that: the cost followed the lost gradient’s ordering, but not separably from k k (S1). The question that matters is across models.

Across models (S11). Different models do pay different costs at the same k k : keeping one loop costs 22% of the loop’s benefit on Parcae-140m and 1% on SmolLM2 (A), whose loop fades much faster. A measurement of the loop could explain such differences, but these two models cannot test it: any measure of how fast the loop fades predicts near-zero cost on SmolLM2, as S6’s pre-registration said in advance (Figure 3 ). The test needs new models on which the rules disagree before training. We ran it on the two Part II models of Huang et al. (2026) (about 304M parameters counting embeddings, their scale S; 5 loops; Appendix ), with four predictions locked before training. Two use the frozen models’ measurements and a curve fitted on Parcae before these models were measured: the lost-gradient curve of S6, and a curve in the direction of the update after Adam’s rescaling (below). Two need no measurement: Parcae’s own cost at the same k k (“ k k alone”), and SmolLM2’s. At k = 1 k=1 the lost-gradient curve predicted 0.14 on the model with a sampled loop count and 0.18 on the one with a fixed 5 loops, the direction-after-Adam curve 0.26 and 0.41, and the two rules without a measurement 0.22 (Parcae) and 0.01 (SmolLM2) on both.
The lost-gradient curve did no better than Parcae’s own cost at the same k k : average error 0.037 against 0.036 over the six settings ( k = 1 k=1 , 2 and 4 on both models; Table ), so by the pre-set rule “ k k alone” was as good. The direction-after-Adam curve did worse (0.051), and the two curves tied on the three settings inside both curves’ fitting ranges (0.026 against 0.028). SmolLM2’s near-zero costs, the other rule without a measurement, were far off (0.116), so the curve beat one of the two such rules fixed in advance and tied the other. The test had little power: a win needed the curve’s average error to be half that of “ k k alone” on six settings from two models, and a seed bootstrap puts the difference at − - 0.0015 to +0.0026 (Appendix ). After the fact: at the same k k , the fixed-5-loop model, whose loop fades more slowly at its 5 loops (Table ), cost 1.5–2.1 times as much as the other (0.29 against 0.16 at k = 1 k=1 ). No rule based on k k alone can produce that difference; Parcae’s cost scored well by falling between the two. Both measured curves ordered the two models correctly (predicted ratios 1.3, 1.7, 2.2 for the lost-gradient curve and 1.6, 1.9, 2.0 for the direction-after-Adam curve, against 1.8, 2.1, 1.5 observed at k = 1 k=1 , 2, 4), but the lost-gradient curve under-predicted the fixed-5-loop model’s costs at k = 1 k=1 and 2 by about a third, and the direction-after-Adam curve over-predicted both models’ costs there by 32–59%. With one pair of models, getting the order right is weak evidence.

A reading found after the fact is consistent with S6. At k = 1 k=1 the dial mostly shrinks the truncated gradient instead of turning it (Appendix ), and Adam divides each weight’s update by that weight’s own recent gradient size, so it largely undoes a consistent shrink. At k = 2 k=2 and 4 on Parcae the dial also turned the direction after Adam’s rescaling, and the cost rose. For SGD, a relative bias δ < 1 \delta<1 multiplies the convergence bound by 1 / ( 1 − δ ) 1/(1-\delta) whatever its direction ( Aicher et al., 2019 , Thm. 2 ); our SGD runs could not check that half (Appendix ). A curve in the direction after Adam fitted both models after the fact (Appendix ), but a test within Parcae was uninformative (S8, Appendix ), and on the Part II models it did worse than “ k k alone” (S11).
What is still open. S11 compared one pair of small models at a single loop count. Whether the lost gradient carries cost information across a wider range of models, for instance in how it orders them, needs more models than we trained. The Part II models cannot test the reading above, because blending at k = 1 k=1 does not act as a pure shrink on them (Appendix ). And since the lost gradient falls during fine-tuning (S12, S13), a frozen-model measurement is at best a proxy for what truncation removes during training.
6 Related work
Truncated backprop. Truncated backpropagation through time is standard for recurrent networks. Aicher et al. (2019) bound its bias geometrically (Thm. 1), estimate the decay rate from late lags (Sec. 4.2), choose the truncation length adaptively, note that a few slowly decaying directions inflate the estimated rate (Sec. 5.4), and price relative bias in SGD (Thm. 2). Tallec & Ollivier (2017) remove the bias by randomising the truncation length and reweighting the backward pass, at the price of extra variance. Ajalloeian & Stich (2020) analyse SGD with relative gradient bias. In small recursive reasoning models truncation can even help: on ARC-AGI, leaving the first 2–3 of 8 loops without gradient raised pass@1 ( Gao et al., 2025 , Table 3 ), and on algorithmic tasks an intermediate gradient horizon generalised best ( Shakhvalieva et al., 2026 , Table 8 ). We measure the depth-recurrence version of this bias in looped transformers and test whether it predicts training cost.
Fixed-point models. For implicit and equilibrium models, a short or one-step gradient often trains well when the loop is contractive ( Fung et al., 2022 ; Geng et al., 2021 ). Liao et al. (2018, Prop. 3) and Movahedi et al. (2026, Prop. 1) bound the truncation error geometrically; Movahedi et al. state it for looped transformers but do not measure it, and their Theorem 3 (damping removes oscillation around the fixed point) underlies our Appendix . In bilevel optimisation, Shaban et al. (2019) bound the error of truncated backprop by the slowest mode, and Vicol et al. (2022) note that truncation diminishes the weight on slow, low-curvature directions. Our dial is the damped iteration behind Geng et al. ’s phantom gradients.
Looped language models. Huginn ( Geiping et al., 2025 ) and Parcae ( Prairie et al., 2026 ) choose their backprop depth by hand; Ouro ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) states none and apparently backpropagates through all of its loops (Table 1 ). McLeish et al. (2026) convert pretrained models into looped ones.
•
Parcae ( Prairie et al., 2026 , App. I) reports training cost against k k .
•
HRM-Text ( Wang et al., 2026 , App. C.1 ) reports that backpropagating through more loops amplifies the product of loop Jacobians and brings rare, very large gradient spikes, which fits the growing backward signal we see in Ouro.
•
Bansal et al. (2022) run a random number of iterations without gradient and then backpropagate through a random number of further ones, a form of truncated backprop. Together with a connection that re-injects the input, this lets recurrent networks run for many more iterations at test time without degrading.
•
Schwethelm et al. (2026a) pretrain 116 models at equal total depth and fit how much one loop is worth compared with an unshared block. Backpropagating through the last half of the loops, as Parcae does, saves about 30% of the training compute per token; at fixed compute the extra tokens lower validation loss, but each loop is worth less, which they suggest likely reflects early loops no longer receiving an accurate learning signal.
•
Huang et al. (2026) pretrain looped models from scratch and report training cost against the backprop window (a 400M model: +15% perplexity with one loop, within 1% from four), the agreement between truncated and full gradients at two single settings (cosine 0.978 for the last 10 of 16 loops), and that, with the input re-injected at every loop, truncated training makes the loop settle in the forward pass (without input injection it does not; their App. C.3).
•
Xiong et al. (2026) fit a scaling law over nearly 600 pretraining runs that vary the loop count and the backprop window separately: at a fixed loop count truncation saves compute, but when both are chosen, full backprop through fewer loops is best, and the loss depends far more on the backprop window than on the loop count (fitted exponents 1.02 and 0.24).
•
Yang et al. (2026) stabilise Ouro’s loop past its training depth with a Jacobian penalty; Guo et al. (2026) score a partial forward step H + α ⁡ ( F ⁡ ( H ) − H ) H+\alpha(F(H)-H) on frozen Ouro and Huginn, which at α = 0.25 \alpha=0.25 is our dial applied once.
What we add: exact measurements of the lost gradient on nine looped language models (seven published, two our own conversions), and of how fine-tuning changes it; the finding that the curve barely changes with the loop count while the fitted rate drifts (an effect known for recurrent networks, measured here in a pre-registered test); and pre-registered tests of what it predicts for training cost, which did not separate it from rules that need no measurement.
7 Limitations and takeaways
Limitations. The evidence on training cost is thin and mostly negative. No experiment here separates the lost gradient from a rule that needs no measurement: within a model, “ k k alone” does as well without the dial (S1) and a ⋅ k a\cdot k nearly as well with it (S5); across models, the prediction failed under blending (S6) and, on two new models, did no better than Parcae’s own cost at the same k k (S11). S11 used one pair of small models at 5 loops, with the token embedding frozen, so it cannot rule out a cost signal across a wider range of models. The explanation of S6’s failure was found after the fact. Two choices may also favour truncation’s apparent cost either way: the learning rate was tuned for full backprop and not per k k , and S11’s “ k k alone” rule took Parcae’s cost at the same k k (of 32 loops) for models run at 5 loops, rather than matching the share of loops kept.
The scope is narrow. All runs were 600-step fine-tunes, compared at the same number of steps rather than the same compute. ρ \rho needs a trained model, so it is no help when choosing k k for pretraining unless it is tracked during training, which we did not do; S2 and S12 show that ρ \rho and the lost gradient both move during fine-tuning. S13’s held-out problems come from the fine-tuning dataset, so we did not test other text. S12 and S13 follow the full-backprop run; S15 follows one truncated run ( k = 2 k=2 ) per model on three models and S16 one run with no loop credit, and other k k and later stages of truncated runs are unmeasured. Every model in Table 1 with ρ < 1 \rho<1 was trained with truncated backprop, and the one apparently trained through all its loops (Ouro) has ρ > 1 \rho>1 . Training through all loops does not by itself make the signal grow, though: the two Part II models, trained through nearly all or all of their loops, have ρ \rho of 0.80 and 0.85 (Appendix ). Ouro differs from every model whose signal shrinks in a way that may matter more: it does not re-inject the input at each loop ( Zhu et al., 2025 , Eq. 1 ). Its prompt reaches later loops only through the loop itself, so a loop that shrank everything it passed back would also erase the prompt. This argues against ρ \rho well below 1, though not for ρ \rho as large as 1.88. Ouro also trains on a weighted loss at every loop, so its early loops get gradient from their own outputs (under such a loss a loop’s own learning signal scales with its exit probability, and an early collapse of the exit gate can starve later loops ( Marchenko et al., 2026 )), and it normalises the state between loops (Section 3 ), which the Part II models do not. We tested none of these as causes; S15 shows only that the growth is not a matter of the state’s size, and that the weighted loss loses as much gradient as the final output’s. Huang et al. (2026, App. C.3) show causally, for the forward pass, that truncated training makes a loop that re-injects its input settle (without input injection it does not, which is Ouro’s case); our backward-pass pattern is consistent with that but only observed. SmolLM2’s loops are also short: conversion A’s unblended loop settles in about two passes, and for conversion B a version trained without ever looping did as well as the looped one.
The account of why ρ k \rho^{k} lands close (Appendix ) rests on per-loop contributions of three frozen models; beyond its pre-registered parts (S9, S10, S14) it re-describes the data with an exact identity. On measurement, the starred rows of Table 1 come from a different probe, and the lost gradient varies with the text by up to 0.12 (Section 3 ). Parcae and Huginn start the loop from a random state, drawn once per sequence with a fixed seed; we did not measure how much the lost gradient varies across draws for a fixed sequence. Last, bugs: an earlier study (not reported here) was scored and then invalidated by a precision bug that silently cut the gradient to the looped weights. Every later run checks its truncated gradient in the training precision. A second bug, in one diagnostic, was caught before scoring (Appendix ).
Takeaways.
•
To check a loop for a growing backward signal, or to see what a given k k throws away on a given checkpoint, measure the lost gradient directly. Compare models only under one protocol: the number moves with the text (by up to 0.12), the probe and the checkpoint. One full backward pass per held-out sequence on a trained model gives it for every k k (for 32 sequences of Huginn-3.5B on one T4, 52 minutes at 8 loops and 2.6 hours at 64, including the truncated comparisons), and once the loop count is well above k k the curve measured at one loop count carries over to longer ones (to 64 loops on all three models measured, and to 128 on Parcae-140m).
•
Measure it during training too. On three of four models it fell by 0.07–0.22 within 300 full-backprop fine-tuning steps, on held-out problems from the fine-tuning dataset nearly as much as on the ones trained on, and not just through the read-out’s share of the gradient; a fine-tune through only the last 2 loops lowered it too.
•
ρ \rho is a summary, not a shortcut. It needs the same full backward pass as the exact curve and saves only memory (the signal’s size at each loop instead of a parameter-sized buffer per k k ). Its main use is flagging a growing signal, for single sequences as well as on average; on Ouro, keeping 2 of 4 loops still loses 81% of the final output’s gradient (85% of its training objective’s), though we did not measure what that costs Ouro in training. As an approximation, ρ k \rho^{k} was within about 20% of the lost gradient at the loop counts of Table 1 (an observed range, which depends on the loop counts) and overstates it at longer loop counts.
•
In every setting we trained, small lost gradients came with small costs (Figure 3 , Table ); we set no threshold. Above that, we could not show it to be a guide to cost: a curve fitted on Parcae did no better on two other models than assuming Parcae’s cost at the same k k , and those two models’ costs differed by up to a factor of two. So measure the cost with your own model and optimiser. Li et al. (2026) reach the same conclusion for memory gradients in policies trained through a differentiable simulator: gradient norms measured on trained policies did not predict which cut cost more, and short training runs did. Least certain of all: which part of the gradient is lost may matter more than how much. Under Adam, a truncated gradient that was smaller but pointed nearly the same way after Adam’s rescaling cost almost nothing in our runs, and one that pointed elsewhere did not. That is a lead found after the fact, and a cost curve built on it did not transfer to the Part II models.

Appendix A The rule
Write the full gradient over the loop’s parameters as g = ∑ j c j g=\sum_{j}c_{j} , where c j c_{j} is the contribution of the loop j j steps before the output, and keeping k k loops gives g k = ∑ j < k c j g_{k}=\sum_{j<k}c_{j} . Over all parameters, the layers after the loop add the same term to both, while the layers before it get gradient only through the loops. If the backward signal shrinks by ρ \rho per loop, | c j | ∝ ρ j |c_{j}|\propto\rho^{j} .
•
Aligned contributions: lost gradient = ( ρ k − ρ R ) / ( 1 − ρ R ) (\rho^{k}-\rho^{R})/(1-\rho^{R}) .
•
Mutually orthogonal contributions: ( ρ 2 ​ k − ρ 2 ​ R ) / ( 1 − ρ 2 ​ R ) \sqrt{(\rho^{2k}-\rho^{2R})/(1-\rho^{2R})} .
•
In between: both forms are ≈ \approx ρ k \rho^{k} for ρ < 1 \rho<1 and R ≫ k R\gg k . When no two contributions point against each other, the decay rate is exactly ρ \rho and only a constant factor is uncertain: for R ≫ k R\gg k the lost gradient lies within a factor ( 1 + ρ ) / ( 1 − ρ ) \sqrt{(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)} of ρ k \rho^{k} either way, about 3.5 at ρ = 0.85 \rho=0.85 . Proof: write s j = | c j | s_{j}=|c_{j}| , S k = ∑ j ≥ k s j S_{k}=\sum_{j\geq k}s_{j} and Q k = ∑ j ≥ k s j 2 Q_{k}=\sum_{j\geq k}s_{j}^{2} . With no negative inner products, any set A of loops has ∑ A s j 2 ≤ | ∑ A c j | 2 ≤ ( ∑ A s j ) 2 \sum_{A}s_{j}^{2}\leq|\sum_{A}c_{j}|^{2}\leq(\sum_{A}s_{j})^{2} . Applied to the dropped loops and to all loops, this gives Q k / S 0 ≤ lost gradient ≤ S k / Q 0 \sqrt{Q_{k}}/S_{0}\leq\text{lost gradient}\leq S_{k}/\sqrt{Q_{0}} , and with s j = ρ j s_{j}=\rho^{j} and R → ∞ R\to\infty the factor above. Neither bound is attained at finite k k ; both are approached as k k grows. At ρ = 0.85 \rho=0.85 and k = 4 k=4 the bounds are 0.15 and 1.83. The most extreme arrangements we found (by construction and random search, not proved optimal) give 0.24–0.91, against ρ 4 = 0.52 \rho^{4}=0.52 : the dropped contributions all parallel and the kept ones orthogonal to everything give S k / Q 0 − Q k + S k 2 S_{k}/\sqrt{Q_{0}-Q_{k}+S_{k}^{2}} = 0.91; the dropped contributions mutually orthogonal and every kept one parallel to their sum give Q k / ( S 0 − S k + Q k ) \sqrt{Q_{k}}/(S_{0}-S_{k}+\sqrt{Q_{k}}) = 0.24.
•
Measured-profile forms: when the signal size s j s_{j} is measured loop by loop instead of assumed geometric, the aligned and orthogonal forms become ∑ j ≥ k s j / ∑ j s j \sum_{j\geq k}s_{j}/\sum_{j}s_{j} and ∑ j ≥ k s j 2 / ∑ j s j 2 \sqrt{\sum_{j\geq k}s_{j}^{2}/\sum_{j}s_{j}^{2}} . Appendix compares them with the measured lost gradient. In all-parameter measurements the gradient of the layers after the loop also enters the denominator. Huginn and Parcae-1.3B are measured over loop parameters only, where it is absent, and they lie above both forms too.
•
Uneven angles: the two forms assume every pair of contributions meets at the same angle. When the angles differ, the lost gradient can lie outside both forms with no cancellation at all. Call the coherence of a set of contributions the size of their sum divided by the sum of their sizes. Then, exactly, the lost gradient equals the aligned form on the measured sizes, ∑ j ≥ k | c j | / ∑ j | c j | \sum_{j\geq k}|c_{j}|\,/\,\sum_{j}|c_{j}| , times the coherence of the dropped loops divided by that of all loops. It lies above that form exactly when the dropped loops add up more coherently than all loops together: when they line up with each other more than the kept ones do, or when the kept and the dropped loops point in different directions. It lies below it in the reverse case. Without cancellation, the lost gradient lies between ∑ j ≥ k | c j | 2 / ∑ j | c j | \sqrt{\sum_{j\geq k}|c_{j}|^{2}}\,/\,\sum_{j}|c_{j}| and ∑ j ≥ k | c j | / ∑ j | c j | 2 \sum_{j\geq k}|c_{j}|\,/\,\sqrt{\sum_{j}|c_{j}|^{2}} , a much wider range (computed with the signal sizes standing in for | c j c_{j} |): 0.17–0.91 for Huginn at R = 8 R=8 , k = 4 k=4 , and 0.16–0.80 for blended SmolLM2 (A) at a = 0.2 a=0.2 . For example, kept loops all pointing one way and dropped loops pointing in unrelated directions give about 0.23 for the latter, its measured value. So a point outside both forms does not by itself show cancellation.
•
Tail sums: write D k D_{k} for the sum of the contributions of loops k k and beyond, so that over the loop’s parameters the lost gradient is | D k | / | D 0 | |D_{k}|/|D_{0}| , and each contribution is the difference of two successive sums, c k = D k − D k + 1 c_{k}=D_{k}-D_{k+1} . For a loop that has settled, the tail sums are the remainder terms of a Neumann series ( Liao et al., 2018 , App., eq. 9 ). If the sum shrinks by a factor r per loop while turning by an angle θ k \theta_{k} , then | c k | = | D k | ​ 1 + r 2 − 2 r cos θ k |c_{k}|=|D_{k}|\sqrt{1+r^{2}-2r\cos\theta_{k}} . Turning inflates the per-loop sizes without changing what is lost. Each contribution can be computed exactly from one backward pass: loop k’s recorded backward signal pushed through that loop’s weight Jacobian, as in Section 2 .
•
When the rule is exact. Write each contribution as c j = ρ j ​ b j c_{j}=\rho^{j}b_{j} , with the geometric shrink taken out. Then D k = ρ k ​ ∑ i < R − k ρ i ​ b i + k D_{k}=\rho^{k}\sum_{i<R-k}\rho^{i}b_{i+k} and D 0 = ∑ i < R ρ i ​ b i D_{0}=\sum_{i<R}\rho^{i}b_{i} . Two cases make the lost gradient ρ k \rho^{k} for R ≫ k R\gg k (in the second case its root-mean-square version), however much the contributions turn.
–
A fixed rotation: if b j + 1 = Q ​ b j b_{j+1}=Qb_{j} for an orthogonal Q, the first sum is Q k Q^{k} applied to the second with its last k k terms removed. Both sums tend to the same size as R R grows, so the lost gradient tends to ρ k \rho^{k} in every sequence.
–
Stationary statistics: if the b j b_{j} are random and the expected inner product of b i b_{i} and b j b_{j} depends only on the lag j − - i, the expected squared size of ∑ i < N ρ i ​ b i + k \sum_{i<N}\rho^{i}b_{i+k} is ∑ i , i ′ < N ρ i + i ′ ​ C ​ ( i ′ − i ) \sum_{i,i^{\prime}<N}\rho^{i+i^{\prime}}C(i^{\prime}-i) , with C the expected inner product at each lag, which does not depend on k k . So 𝔼 ​ | D k | 2 / 𝔼 ​ | D 0 | 2 \mathbb{E}|D_{k}|^{2}/\mathbb{E}|D_{0}|^{2} tends to ρ 2 ​ k \rho^{2k} , with corrections of order ρ R − k \rho^{R-k} . This holds for the ratio of expectations, not for each sequence or for the median over sequences that we report.
•
ρ > 1 \rho>1 : both finite-R forms still hold. For Ouro (median ρ = 1.88 \rho=1.88 , R = 4 R=4 ) the medians over sequences of each sequence’s forms are 0.92 (aligned) and 0.99 (orthogonal) at k = 1 k=1 and 0.78 and 0.96 at k = 2 k=2 .
Blending, x ← ( 1 − a ) ​ x + a ​ F ​ ( x ) x\leftarrow(1-a)\,x+a\,F(x) , maps each eigenvalue μ \mu of the loop’s Jacobian to 1 − a + a ​ μ 1-a+a\mu . It leaves each mode’s weight, and the unlimited-loop (implicit) gradient, unchanged. Over-blending ( a > 1 a>1 ) can push negative eigenvalues towards − - 1. Such a mode can then set the decay rate of the backward signal, and so ρ \rho , while carrying little weight in the gradient (its weight is divided by 1 + | μ \mu |), and its alternating terms partly cancel in the first-order cost of truncation. We infer that this is what happens in Appendix ; we did not measure the per-loop contributions.
The aligned form is the ratio of two geometric sums. The orthogonal form follows because mutually orthogonal contributions satisfy | ∑ c j | 2 = ∑ | c j | 2 |\sum c_{j}|^{2}=\sum|c_{j}|^{2} . The bounds are proved above. All formulas are checked numerically on small linear loops ( code/law1_toy_check.py in the code release); further results (mixtures of modes, damping) are in the supplementary material ( results_docs/law1_derivation_formal.md in the code release).
