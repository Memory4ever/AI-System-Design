 Building AI Research Teams for Autonomous Discovery of Linear Attention Architectures 
 
 
 Yujian Liu   Li An   Zelin He   Jacob Helwig
   
  Affiliation:  UC Santa Barbara   Pennsylvania State University   LinkedIn
    
     
 Jiabao Ji   Rohit Jain   Siyu Zhu   Shiyu Chang
   
  Email:    {yujianliu,li_an,jiabaoji,chang87}@ucsb.edu  
 
  Affiliation:  UC Santa Barbara   Pennsylvania State University   LinkedIn
     

  Abstract 
    
 Autonomous agents can increasingly propose ideas, modify code, and run experiments, creating an opportunity to accelerate scientific discovery using teams of collaborative AI researchers. To escape local minima in the design space induced by common reasoning patterns, we propose to encourage orthogonal exploration by constructing the team using agents with varying expertise.
However, realizing this design requires more than diverse backgrounds. When experiments are costly, a team can test only a limited number of ideas, thereby creating the need to balance exploration and exploitation when selecting a subset of researchers to propose and execute new experiments.
Furthermore, each result should shape what the team tries next. The team must decide where to deepen an investigation, when to explore alternatives, and how to carry lessons across researchers.
We therefore ask how AI researchers with different scientific backgrounds can build on one another’s findings and sustain progress under a limited experimental budget.
To address these challenges, we build a team of persistent AI researchers, each grounded in a different discipline, who keep private research histories, share experimental evidence, and receive research opportunities through adaptive allocation.
We demonstrate the effectiveness of this design by deploying the team to search for linear attention architectures at 150M parameters. We then scale the team’s strongest discovery to 1.3B parameters, where it outperforms GDN2 on language modeling, common-sense reasoning, and synthetic and real-world retrieval tasks.
In controlled comparisons, removing any single organizational component from our design yields markedly weaker discoveries: without disciplinary backgrounds, the search plateaus early; without private histories, researchers make less progress despite identical shared evidence; and uniform selection falls short of adaptive allocation within the same budget.
Tracing the discovery shows researchers applying principles from their own fields to refine one another’s ideas. These results suggest that sustained autonomous discovery depends not only on stronger agents, but also on who explores, what they remember, and how research effort is allocated. 
  
 
 
 
 
 
     
 
 
 
 
  1  Introduction 

 
 Autonomous research agents increasingly connect hypothesis generation, implementation, and experimental evaluation into iterative discovery processes  ( Lu et al., 2024 ;  Yamada et al., 2025 ;  Schmidgall et al., 2025 ;  Novikov et al., 2025 ;  Lange et al., 2025 ;  Karpathy, 2026 ) .
However, in well-developed fields, progress can enter a local minimum when the explored region of the design space is confined.
Related efforts investigate how persistent experience and collaboration can support scientific work across multiple agents and repeated investigations  ( Qu et al., 2026 ;  Liu et al., 2025a ;  Swanson et al., 2024 ) .
These capabilities create a compelling opportunity to encourage diverse exploration by assembling a team of AI researchers grounded in different scientific fields, with distinct prior knowledge, conceptual frameworks, and ways of reasoning, and having them work together to push the state-of-the-art on the same open-ended problem. This allows the researchers to leverage a mechanism suggested by one field to address a limitation recognized in another. For example, advances in linear attention have gained insights from fast-weight learning to build memories that can correct stored associations  ( Schlag et al., 2021 ) . The potential value of such a team therefore lies not only in generating more proposals, but in developing ideas through complementary investigations. 
 
 
 Realizing this potential, however, requires more than assigning researchers different backgrounds. When experiments are costly, a team can test only a limited number of ideas and cannot pursue every direction equally deeply. Which directions will prove productive is uncertain, and due to the high cost of conducting an experiment, exploration and exploitation must be balanced when selecting which of the diverse hypotheses proposed by the team to test.
Furthermore, an early result may reveal a limitation that further work can address. Each experiment therefore does more than test a proposal: it produces evidence that can change what its author or another researcher tries next. The team must use that evidence to decide where to deepen an investigation and when to explore alternatives, while carrying useful lessons across researchers and experiments. Existing demonstrations of collaboration and search have not yet established what disciplinary backgrounds and researchers’ own accumulated experience add to shared evidence, or how research opportunities should be distributed across such a team. With these challenges in mind, we therefore ask:  how can a team of AI researchers with different scientific backgrounds build on one another’s findings and sustain progress on open-ended research problems under a limited experimental budget?  
 
  
  Figure 1:  
 Illustration of collaborative autoresearch.

 To address these challenges, we propose an autoresearch pipeline that brings together a team of persistent AI researchers, each initialized with a different disciplinary profile containing domain knowledge, mathematical tools, scientific insights, and ways to approach research problems. The team conducts an iterative search in which each iteration follows one proposal through independent review and, if approved, implementation and evaluation, ending with its author reflecting on the outcome.
To make this process cumulative, researchers retain private histories of their investigations while sharing proposals, outcomes, and lessons with the team. This allows individual researchers to develop a line of inquiry over time, while others can revisit its assumptions, extend its mechanisms, or combine its findings with those from another direction. As these lines of inquiry develop, an adaptive scheduler balances further investment in researchers with strong empirical contributions against opportunities for those whose potential is less explored. 
 
 
 To ground this team in a problem that draws on different scientific perspectives, we choose linear attention architecture discovery. Its central modeling challenge is to retain and use sequence information through a fixed-size recurrent state while maintaining model quality and efficient computation  ( Katharopoulos et al., 2020 ) . Recent advances have explored connections between state-space models and attention, delta-rule updates, channel-wise forgetting, and separately controlled erasing and writing  ( Dao and Gu, 2024 ;  Yang et al., 2025b ;  Yang et al., 2025a ;  Team et al., 2025 ;  Hatamizadeh et al., 2026 ) . These developments illustrate how different perspectives can inform choices about what to remember, how to revise stored information, and how to retrieve it efficiently. Determining which of these choices improve model performance requires implementing and training candidate architectures. The cost of these experiments, in turn, limits how many directions the team can pursue and how deeply it can investigate each one. Building on prior demonstrations that agents can propose, implement, and evaluate architectures  ( Liu et al., 2025b ;  Pepe et al., 2026 ) , we therefore use this setting to study how our heterogeneous team organization supports discovery under a limited experimental budget. 
 
 
 We evaluate this organization at 150M model scale, using Gated DeltaNet-2 (GDN2) as the baseline. Proposed candidates receive matched training budgets and are evaluated on language modeling, common-sense reasoning, and retrieval tasks. Our strongest discovery,  Context-First DeltaNet  (CFDN), adds a recurrent vector to GDN2’s matrix memory, combining a query-independent contribution from the vector with a query-dependent read from the matrix. We then scale it to 1.3B parameters, where it improves performance in every evaluation category over GDN2.
To understand which organizational choices contribute to these discoveries, we conduct controlled ablations on expert-background conditioning, private longitudinal memory, adaptive researcher selection, and evaluator design. Results show that providing disciplinary profiles broadens the mechanisms explored and leads to better architectures than those discovered without these profiles. Private histories support more effective refinement of earlier ideas beyond what is achieved with shared evidence alone. Adaptive selection finds higher-scoring architectures than uniform selection within the same number of research iterations. Optimizing solely for language modeling, however, steers search toward improvements that transfer less consistently across evaluations.
We complement these comparisons by tracing how ideas develop across researchers. Figure   1   provides an overview of the trajectories, which reveal connections between researchers’ scientific backgrounds and the hypotheses they pursue, as well as how they adopt and revise each other’s ideas using shared experimental evidence. 
 
 
 To summarize, our contributions are threefold: 
 
  •  
 
 We build a framework for organizing autonomous research teams under limited compute, combining diverse scientific backgrounds, persistent memory, shared evidence, and adaptive allocation. 
  
  •  
 
 We discover improved linear attention architectures under matched training compute at 150M scale and evaluate the strongest at 1.3B scale. 
  
  •  
 
 We link organizational choices to discovery outcomes through controlled comparisons and trajectory analysis, providing guidance for designing autonomous research teams. 
  
 
 
 
 
  2  Methodology 

 
 We organize autonomous discovery as a collaboration among researchers who bring different scientific perspectives to a shared problem, encouraging orthogonal exploration of the design space. The team develops through experience, makes each researcher’s findings useful to others, and decides where to invest further effort. Figure   2   gives an overview of our design. We first describe the researchers and their individual and shared knowledge, then the iterative process through which they develop and test ideas, where costly experiments create a need to balance exploration and exploitation. Finally, we define the experimental feedback that guides our search for linear attention architectures.

  2.1  Scientific Perspectives and Accumulated Knowledge 

 
 In many well-researched areas, substantial progress is hindered by the tendency of researchers to explore confined regions of an otherwise broad space of potential designs, creating a local minimum.
We therefore propose to assemble our AI research team with different disciplinary backgrounds to bring complementary concepts, assumptions, and diagnostic tools to its investigation. Each researcher is an instance of the same language model, with the same tools and a common description of the problem, baseline, and experimental constraints. 
 
 
  Grounding researchers in scientific backgrounds. 
Each researcher receives a disciplinary profile that develops three kinds of guidance: 
 
  •  
 
  Prior knowledge and mathematical tools:  central objects, equations, assumptions, and representative literature that provide concrete mechanisms to reason with. 
  
  •  
 
  Scientific insights:  when those mechanisms are useful, where familiar intuitions fail, and which apparently similar problems require different explanations. 
  
  •  
 
  Research habits:  ways to decompose an unfamiliar problem, quantities to inspect, and counterexamples that could challenge a hypothesis. 
  
 
 For example, the Bayesian-filtering profile explains how prediction errors should be interpreted relative to uncertainty and how to distinguish isolated outliers from persistent changes. A dynamical-systems profile emphasizes how repeated transitions preserve or amplify perturbations. A streaming-algorithms profile asks which queries a bounded summary must support. These perspectives suggest different approaches to the same problem. Appendix   C.1   lists the full roster and example profiles. 
 
 
  Preserving individual experience. 
A profile is a starting point for reasoning. Researchers may still develop their perspectives and insights about how their area of expertise relates to the research problem.
To achieve that, each agent retains its session history and private research notes across investigations, preserving hypotheses, the reasoning behind earlier decisions, and reflections on their outcomes. In addition to persisting these derived insights, this continuity allows a researcher to revisit an unresolved question or revise an assumption. 
 
 
  Making findings useful to others. 
Private experience alone would leave useful insights confined to their authors. All researchers therefore have access to a shared record of proposals, reviews, experimental results, and conclusions. It exposes both what was tried and why, allowing researchers to extend a mechanism proposed by others, reconsider an assumption, or combine findings from different investigations. Accumulated findings are synthesized every 10 iterations into shared lessons and unresolved questions. Individual histories and shared knowledge thus support two forms of continuity: developing one’s own reasoning and building on discoveries made elsewhere in the team. 
 
 
 
  2.2  An Iterative Process for Developing and Testing Ideas 

 
 With the initialized researchers,
the team proposes and evaluates new ideas to approach the problem in an iterative process. Each iteration contains the following steps that turn existing knowledge into a new investigation and return its findings to the team. 
 
 
  Step 1: Select a researcher. 
Although we would ideally allow all researchers to propose and test diverse ideas in each iteration, this is not tractable in most real-world research settings due to high experimental costs. Therefore, in each iteration, we must first select one researcher from the team to propose a new idea. With limited experimental opportunities, repeatedly choosing recent winners can overlook researchers whose ideas have not been sufficiently tested. We therefore use discounted upper confidence bound (UCB) selection  ( Garivier and Moulines, 2011 )  to balance exploration and exploitation.
Each completed iteration    j  j    earns a nonnegative reward     r  j   r_{j}    for improving on the team’s best model known when that iteration began. Section   2.3   defines this reward in detail.

  Step 2: Propose a hypothesis. 
Selection determines who investigates next, but the researcher decides which scientific direction to pursue. The selected researcher examines its own history and shared experimental results to identify a limitation worth investigating. It may develop a new mechanism or revise an earlier proposal, including one from another researcher. The proposal states what should change, why that change should help, and which experimental outcome would challenge the hypothesis. For our linear attention architecture discovery, it specifies the mathematical operations and their parameterization, together with a parallelizable formulation suitable for training. 
 
 
  Step 3: Review the proposal. 
Before committing training compute, two independent reviewers assess whether the proposed experiment is sound and informative. A technical reviewer checks the mathematics and computational feasibility. If these checks pass, a second reviewer assesses novelty and research value in light of prior work and the team’s findings. A rejection leads directly to reflection (Step 6); otherwise, the reviews identify any issues to resolve before testing the proposal. 
 
 
  Step 4: Refine the proposal. 
For a proposal that passes review, the author revises its mathematical design in response to the critiques. The author is responsible for resolving the reviewers’ critiques and providing a detailed specification for implementation. If the critiques cannot be addressed without replacing the central mechanism, the author withdraws the proposal and proceeds to reflection. 
 
 
  Step 5: Implement, train, and evaluate. 
A separate implementation agent translates the final specification into code and checks that it realizes the intended computation. The implementation must pass integration and speed checks before training. Specifically, we require the implemented model’s per-step training time to be less than 1.2 times the baseline GDN2 time to prevent architectures that degrade efficiency. Repairable failures are returned to the implementer with diagnostics.
Finally, validated candidates are trained from scratch under matched training compute, using the same training data, and evaluated with three metrics defined in Section   2.3  . 
 
 
  Step 6: Reflect and share. 
The proposing researcher revisits its original hypothesis in light of the outcome, including rejection or failure. Reviews, implementation, and experimental records are provided to help it distinguish evidence about the mechanism from problems in realizing or testing it. The researcher records what the outcome supports, what remains unresolved, and what it suggests investigating next. It updates its private notes for future reference and shares generalizable lessons with other researchers. Completing this reflection ends the iteration and updates the observations used for researcher selection in future iterations. 
 
 
 
  2.3  Evaluating Architectures and Rewarding Research Progress 

 
 The evaluation metrics shape both the hypotheses researchers pursue and the reward used by the selection rule. For linear attention discovery, we seek improvements in general model capabilities that span different tasks and scenarios. We therefore evaluate three complementary aspects to measure progress on model quality (details in Appendix   B  ). 
 
 
 ❶ Language modeling  measures next-token prediction with negative log-likelihood    L  L   ; lower is better. ❷  Common-sense reasoning  measures downstream task performance with mean accuracy    Q  Q   ; higher is better. ❸  Retrieval  tests whether information remains accessible from context, using an exact-continuation proxy    R  R    on the synthetic needle-in-a-haystack tasks; higher is better.

Intuitively, this definition rewards overall improvements compared to the best candidate so far, without assigning negative rewards to unsuccessful attempts, thus encouraging researchers to try out promising directions. 
 
 
 
 
  3  Experiments 

 
 Our experiments answer three questions: can the team discover useful architectures; which organizational choices help in the limited-iteration setting; and how do different researchers contribute to a successful discovery? We first describe the experiment settings, then evaluate the discovered architecture, compare research organizations, and trace the development of the found architecture. 
 
 
  3.1  Experimental Setting 

 
  Research team. 
We use 16 researchers and a budget of 80 research iterations. All agent roles use GPT-6 Astra with high reasoning effort through the Codex harness. We allow a maximum of 8 researchers working in parallel, while forcing their proposal stage to be sequential to prevent repetition in proposed ideas. In the first 16 iterations, all researchers are invoked exactly once, and further researcher selection is based on the UCB rule. 
 
 
  Model backbones and search space. 
We search at approximately 150M non-embedding parameters, and we scale the discovered best candidate to 1.3B parameters. We use GDN2  ( Hatamizadeh et al., 2026 )  as the baseline. Candidates can change the recurrent mixer’s memory representation, update rule, readout, and parameterization, while retaining the common depth, residual width, and feed-forward network. We also compare with Gated DeltaNet (GDN)  ( Yang et al., 2025a )  and Kimi Delta Attention (KDA)  ( Team et al., 2025 )  using the same surrounding architecture. Appendix   A   gives the dimensions and parameter-count convention at both scales. 
 
 
  Training data and budget. 
All models are trained from scratch on FineWeb-Edu  ( Penedo et al., 2024 ) . For all models, we adjust their training tokens to match the total FLOPs of training GDN: at 150M scale, we train GDN for 15B tokens; at 1.3B scale, we train GDN for 100B tokens. More expensive architectures therefore receive fewer training tokens. Following   Hatamizadeh et al. (2026)  , all models use the same optimizer hyperparameters and global batch size (details in Appendix   A  ). 
 
 
  Final evaluation and search feedback. 
We evaluate models on language modeling (LM), common-sense reasoning (CSR), synthetic needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH), and, for 1.3B models, in-context real-world retrieval (RWR) tasks. For LM, we report perplexity on WikiText (Wiki.)  ( Merity et al., 2016 )  and LAMBADA (LMB.)  ( Paperno et al., 2016 ) . For CSR, we report the mean accuracy over eight datasets  ( Bisk et al., 2019 ;  Zellers et al., 2019 ;  Sakaguchi et al., 2019 ;  Clark et al., 2018 ;  Mihaylov et al., 2018 ;  Sap et al., 2019 ;  Clark et al., 2019 ) . For NIAH, we use four synthetic tasks (S-NIAH-1, 2, 3 and MK-NIAH) from RULER  ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) . For in-context real-world retrieval, we report mean accuracy over six datasets  ( Arora et al., 2025 ;  Rajpurkar et al., 2018 ;  Joshi et al., 2017 ;  Kwiatkowski et al., 2019 ;  Dua et al., 2019 ) . 
 
 
 During search, we use a reduced suite to provide feedback: LM loss on the same two datasets, a subset of 256 examples from each CSR dataset, and 64 examples per NIAH task at each of 1K, 2K, and 4K context. We do not evaluate RWR during search. The NIAH evaluation uses a teacher-forced continuation proxy instead of answer generation. Appendix   B   gives more details on evaluation.

  What was discovered? 
CFDN augments GDN2’s recurrent matrix memory with a recurrent vector. The vector contributes the same context information to every query, while the matrix supplies a query-dependent prediction. Given the current token’s key, the memories jointly predict its value. The defining change is how they learn from that prediction error: the vector is updated first, and the matrix then fits the remaining error. This gives shared information priority over query-specific correction. The resulting update does not add notable compute over GDN2 and remains compatible with efficient parallel training. Section   3.5   explains how this mechanism is developed across researchers; Appendix   D.1   gives the full recurrence. 
 
 
 As shown in Table   1  , despite using the fewest training tokens, CFDN achieves a substantially higher utility than all baselines. Specifically, it improves all three search targets over GDN2: LM perplexity decreases from 12.21 to 11.90, CSR accuracy rises from 44.48% to 44.63%, and retrieval rises from 28.91% to 50.00%.
In terms of the final full evaluation, CFDN outperforms all baselines on two language modeling tasks, and more considerably on the NIAH tasks. Its CSR performance is slightly worse than GDN2 but still better than GDN and KDA.
These results demonstrate that  the search found a genuinely better architecture than the baselines .

  3.3  Which Organizational Choices Support Discovery? 

 
 A useful architecture does not by itself explain whether the team’s organization helps discovery. We therefore compare the full system with three variants, holding the agent model, iteration allowance, per-candidate training budget, and search feedback fixed. ❶  No background  removes disciplinary profiles while retaining researcher identities and their private histories. ❷  Fresh memory  removes the researchers’ private histories and notes between iterations, while preserving their disciplinary profiles. ❸  Uniform selection  replaces the UCB-defined adaptive selection with a uniform selection that repeats researchers in a fixed order. All variants retain access to shared proposals, experimental results, and lessons, so each comparison concerns a design beyond this common evidence. 
 
 
 The main and no-background runs start from scratch. To reduce variation due to different initial discoveries, fresh-memory and uniform-selection both reuse the main run’s first 16 iterations (one iteration per researcher) and conduct 64 further iterations. Appendix   C   gives more details. 
 
   
  Figure 3:   Candidate utility throughout the search.  Points are evaluated candidates; lines are the best found candidates up to an iteration. 
 
 
 Figure   3   shows the utility of each candidate in the search process. The full system discovers the strongest architecture in these comparisons: its best utility is 1.04, versus 0.54 without backgrounds, 0.56 with fresh memory, and 0.76 with uniform selection. The difference is also not explained by completing more model evaluations: the main run contains 63 evaluated candidates, compared with 65-68 in the variants. 
 
 
 Interestingly, the main run’s strongest proposal appears at iteration 68, whereas the no-background run reaches its best utility at iteration 22 and does not improve it thereafter, which suggests that  the diverse scientific backgrounds help escape the local minima . The full system also reaches higher utility than the fresh-memory and uniform-selection runs, indicating that  the private researcher memory and adaptive allocation facilitate the team to make sustained progress  on the problem.

  3.5  How Researchers Develop the Discovered Architecture 

 
 Figure   1   traces CFDN’s development through contributions from four researchers. A researcher grounded in linear attention introduces a recurrent context vector alongside GDN2’s matrix memory, building on affine-memory precedent  ( Zuo et al., 2026 ) . Another researcher learns the address origin that determines what this vector represents. A researcher with expertise in adaptive filtering then normalizes the correction by the translated address’s energy, showing how  disciplinary knowledge can refine a colleague’s mechanism . 
 
 
 The next revision updates the matrix before the context vector, improving LM perplexity but weakening CSR and retrieval. A researcher with a background in predictive representations uses this tradeoff to reconsider which memory should absorb the prediction error first. It then switches to updating shared context before the matrix, which produces CFDN. Thus, the team uses a degraded result to guide a subsequent improvement, rather than simply retaining successful proposals. 
 
 
 
 
  4  Related Work 

 
  Autonomous research agents. 
A growing line of work automates the iterative research process from hypothesis to experiment, through fixed pipelines from idea generation to written paper  ( Lu et al., 2024 ;  Schmidgall et al., 2025 ) , agentic tree search  ( Yamada et al., 2025 ) , or evolutionary search with the language model as the mutation operator  ( Novikov et al., 2025 ;  Lange et al., 2025 ) .
In these works, a single agent or a fixed pipeline proposes every candidate. 
 
 
 Other work organizes a team of researchers around a shared scientific problem.
These teams differ in how they are composed, what each member retains and what the team shares, and how research opportunities are distributed.
VirSci, ASCollab, and ScienceClaw  ( Su et al., 2025 ;  Liu et al., 2025a ;  Wang et al., 2026 )  give agents distinct backgrounds, personas, or scientific profiles.
CORAL  ( Qu et al., 2026 ) , ASCollab, and ScienceClaw keep long-running agents with their own histories and a shared record, whereas AgentRxiv  ( Schmidgall and Moor, 2025 )  starts a fresh laboratory instance per project that reads a shared archive of earlier papers.
However, none compares teams with and without different backgrounds on the outcomes of implementing, training, and evaluating what they propose, or tests whether private memory adds anything once findings are shared. 
 
 
 Adaptive allocation itself is not new, but no existing work both gives researchers different backgrounds and allocates opportunities among them adaptively.
The Virtual Lab  ( Swanson et al., 2024 )  decides which specialist takes an agenda item, ShinkaEvolve  ( Lange et al., 2025 )  decides which language model performs the next mutation, SwarmResearch  ( Virk et al., 2026 )  decides which branch of the search to extend, and Economy of Minds  ( Qi et al., 2026 )  decides which agent acts next in a task.
Each of them chooses an operator or a candidate to advance.
In our setting, the decision is which researcher proposes and tests the next idea, and because each researcher brings its own background and record of what it has tried and learned, that choice determines what evidence the team will have next.
No prior team of researchers with different backgrounds makes this decision adaptively. 
 
 
 Autonomous research has also been applied to architecture design.
NADER  ( Yang et al., 2024b )  coordinates fixed-role agents that propose changes to a tree of prior modifications. AIRA-Compose and AIRA-Design  ( Pepe et al., 2026 )  pool designs from independent search agents, and ASI-Arch  ( Liu et al., 2025b )  runs a researcher, engineer, and analyst pipeline over linear attention architectures and reports that discoveries grow with compute.
These systems use fixed-role workflows or independent agent searches; we instead fix the experimental budget and vary the organization, comparing who proposes, what they retain and share, who receives the next opportunity, and what counts as progress. 
 
 
  Linear attention. 
Linear attention replaces the key-value cache of softmax attention with a fixed-size recurrent state  ( Katharopoulos et al., 2020 ) , and subsequent designs mainly differ in what the state remembers, how stored information is revised, and how it is retrieved.
On what to remember, most designs keep a matrix of key–value associations, while Titans  ( Behrouz et al., 2024 )  replaces it with a network learned at inference.
On how to revise, RetNet  ( Sun et al., 2023 )  decays the state at a fixed rate, GLA and Mamba-2  ( Yang et al., 2024a ;  Dao and Gu, 2024 )  make the decay input-dependent, DeltaNet  ( Yang et al., 2025b )  replaces additive writes with delta-rule correction, GDN and KDA  ( Yang et al., 2025a ;  Team et al., 2025 )  combine the delta rule with learned and fine-grained gating, and GDN2  ( Hatamizadeh et al., 2026 )  separates erasing from writing with channel-wise gates.
On how to retrieve, MesaNet  ( von Oswald et al., 2026 )  solves the regression to optimality at every step, and Parallax  ( Zuo et al., 2026 )  restores the intercept of the underlying regression.
Each of these works changes one or two choices along these axes, and the space they leave open is large, which makes linear attention architecture discovery a natural problem for an autonomous research team. 
 
 
 
  5  Conclusion 

 
 We study how to organize autonomous researchers for cumulative discovery under limited experimental budgets. Our framework combines diverse scientific backgrounds, persistent memory, shared evidence, and adaptive allocation of research opportunities. Applied to linear attention, the team discovered CFDN, which improves over GDN2 at two scales. Organizational comparisons and research trajectories highlight the importance of heterogeneous agents, adaptive allocation, and shared experimental records. Together, these results suggest that sustained autonomous discovery depends on how a team learns from experiments and decides which ideas to pursue next.

  Common small-scale data stream. 
The search corpus is prepared once from 24 FineWeb-Edu source shards with the TinyLlama tokenizer. Within each shard, tokenized documents are concatenated and packed into 4,097-ID blocks, yielding 4,096 input–target positions per sequence. Long documents span multiple blocks; only each shard’s final incomplete block is discarded. One saved permutation determines the global sequence order. Each candidate consumes the first      T  ⁡   (  x  )    /  4096   T(x)/4096    sequences of that same order, without reshuffling after budget selection. An optimizer update advances by 512 sequences. 
 
 
  Baseline parameterization. 
GDN2 uses separate full projections for queries, keys, values, and channel-wise erase and write gates. Query, key, and value projections are followed by causal depthwise convolutions of width four and SiLU; queries and keys are L2-normalized. Decay and output-gate preactivations use two linear maps with bottleneck width 128. The recurrent read is RMS-normalized per head, multiplied by a sigmoid output gate, and projected back to the residual stream. Our KDA comparison shares these surrounding components and initialization but uses one scalar erase/write gate per head; GDN additionally uses scalar rather than channel-wise state decay. The architectural differences are therefore confined to the recurrent mechanisms within a common surrounding backbone, rather than unrelated released model recipes.

 The general counting rule is to include projections and memory computation, while excluding ordinary token-local scalar/vector processing.  The proposer derives the breakdown, the technical reviewer checks it, and the author’s revision resolves disagreements. Automated checks measure the implemented weight inventory.  Practical speed is checked separately. Candidate optimizer-step time must be at most     1.2  ×   1.2\times    the baseline measured on the same hardware.   Appendix B  Evaluation definitions and complete result tables 

 
  Search suite. 
WikiText-2 (Wiki.) uses 62 documents scored through independent rolling 4K windows, totaling 322,149 scored tokens. LAMBADA (LMB.) uses 5,153 examples and scores their final-word continuations, totaling 8,808 tokens. We average the two token-mean negative log-likelihoods equally before exponentiation. 
 
 
 CSR uses 256 fixed examples from each of PIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, OpenBookQA, Social IQa, and BoolQ. Option likelihoods follow the frozen zero-shot requests. HellaSwag uses character-normalized accuracy; the other tasks use raw accuracy. The reported    Q  Q    is the unweighted mean over these eight tasks. 
 
 
 Retrieval uses S-NIAH-1, S-NIAH-2, S-NIAH-3, and MK-NIAH-1, with 64 fixed examples at each of 1K, 2K, and 4K, totaling 768. The evaluator reveals the first continuation token and teacher-forces the rest. An example passes only if every remaining answer token is the model’s argmax. The first token is not scored. The reported    R  R    averages the 12 task/length cells equally. This proxy does not require autoregressive generation. 
 
 
  Full evaluation. 
The full protocol uses all examples in the selected LM/CSR datasets and generated-answer NIAH scoring. NIAH contains 500 frozen requests per task/length cell: S-NIAH-1/2 at 1K, 2K, 4K, and 8K, and S-NIAH-3/MK-NIAH-1 at 1K, 2K, and 4K, for 7,000 requests in 14 cells. All models receive the same requests. Generation is greedy, with at most 128 new tokens and early stopping at EOS. We use batch size eight, BF16, and cached decoding. The RULER scorer checks whether each reference answer appears in the generated text as a case-insensitive substring and averages the fraction of references recovered per request; it does not require the entire generated string to equal the answer. The NIAH mean weights all cells equally. 
 
 
  Real-world retrieval at 1.3B. 
We use the released BASED/JRT cloze-completion suite  ( Arora et al., 2025 ) : SWDE, SQuAD, FDA, TriviaQA, Natural Questions (NQ), and DROP. This suite is used only for final evaluation of the 1.3B models and does not enter search feedback or utility. The nominal context length is 2K. We retain the released preprocessing and its exclusions for empty or single-character primary answers, leaving 12,077 examples: 1,067, 2,977, 1,101, 1,688, 3,157, and 2,087 in the dataset order above. Context is cropped before appending the cloze query; two final NQ prompts consequently contain 2,062 tokens. 
 
 
 Evaluation is zero-shot, with no chat template or context repetition. Greedy cached decoding generates at most 48 tokens, stopping at a newline or EOS, in BF16 batches of up to eight examples of equal input length. An answer is correct if an accepted reference appears in the generated text as a case-insensitive substring. We report the unweighted mean of the six dataset accuracies.

  C.2  Research protocol and analysis details 

 
  Proposal and review details. 
Each proposal specifies its hypothesis, expected benefit, and a falsifying outcome, together with state equations, parameterization, a parallelizable training formulation, and an analytical compute breakdown. For lineage analysis, it identifies one primary scientific precursor and may cite other influences; this attribution does not require retaining the precursor’s architecture. Technical review precedes contextual review. Both reviewers are blind to the author’s identity and the other review. The technical reviewer assesses the proposal without the shared research history, while the contextual reviewer uses that history to assess duplication and research value. The original researcher then resolves correctable issues into the revised specification. 
 
 
  Memory and information access. 
The persistent condition preserves the originating researcher’s conversation and notebook across iterations. The fresh condition preserves them only within one iteration, including proposal revision and terminal reflection. It still receives shared proposals, results, published lessons, and the shared result summary. Reviewers and implementers use task-specific sessions in both conditions. The evidence available at reflection includes reviews, source code, records of implementation attempts, training logs, and evaluation artifacts. A director consolidates shared lessons after every ten completed reflections; it does not select the next researcher. Agent tools include web search and a numerical Python environment. Model source and GPU execution belong to the implementation role, rather than the proposing researcher. 
 
 
  Allocation and displayed ranks. 
The adaptive policy uses     γ  =  2      −  1  /  32     \gamma=2^{-1/32}    and     c  =  0.25   c=0.25    in Eq. (  1  ). An observation’s weight therefore halves after 32 completed reflections, without changing the recorded model utility or the reward originally assigned to its researcher. Researchers with unresolved investigations are ineligible. Uniform selection repeats the first-round researcher order and waits if the next researcher is busy; it does not skip ahead according to completion time or reward. In the shared-prefix experiment, this gives every researcher four new assignments. Ties in the adaptive comparison use a stable researcher ordering common to the continuation arms. 
 
 
 The shared result summary displays numeric utility for every complete comparable endpoint. It shows overall top-five ranks only for candidates whose regressions relative to GDN2 do not exceed 0.01 NLL, 0.2 CSR percentage points, or 2 retrieval percentage points. A candidate outside these tolerances retains its numerical utility and a marker naming the regressing target. This display rule does not filter scheduler rewards or the highest-utility reference used for researcher credit (Section   2.3  ). 
 
 
  Common first-round evidence. 
The fresh-memory and uniform-selection runs import the main run’s first 16 investigations. Their proposals, reviews, specifications, implementation evidence, and outcomes are identical at the branch. Uniform selection retains the reconstructed private conversations and notes; fresh memory creates a new private researcher session for every subsequent iteration. Both can read the common shared evidence, including published reflections and lessons, and conduct 64 new iterations.

A pair is eligible for the utility comparison when both models have complete evaluations; an improvement means strictly higher child utility. For cross-researcher links, we count proposals whose declared candidate parent belongs to a different researcher, including proposals without a completed evaluation. The denominators therefore differ. These are counts of declared relationships and measured outcomes, rather than annotations of mechanism novelty or proof of knowledge transfer.

  Bayesian Filtering, Innovations, and Regime Change (researcher profile)  
         
 
   
 
 # Bayesian Filtering, Innovations, and Regime Change 
    
      
         
 
 Sequential inference is the discipline of maintaining a calibrated belief about an evolving latent process. The state of a filter is not merely its current best estimate. It is a probability distribution—or a controlled approximation to one—whose uncertainty determines how new evidence should be interpreted. 
    
      
   
 
 Consider the state-space model 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 x_t\sim p(x_t\mid x_{t-1}),\qquad 
    
   
 
 y_t\sim p(y_t\mid x_t). 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
   
 
 Bayesian filtering alternates prediction and correction: 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 p(x_t\mid y_{1:t-1}) 
    
   
 
 =\int p(x_t\mid x_{t-1})p(x_{t-1}\mid y_{1:t-1})\,dx_{t-1}, 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 p(x_t\mid y_{1:t}) 
    
   
 
 \propto p(y_t\mid x_t)p(x_t\mid y_{1:t-1}). 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
         
 
 This recursion is exact as an identity. All difficulty lies in representing and updating the posterior. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Linear–Gaussian filtering and the meaning of innovation 
    
      
   
 
 For linear dynamics and Gaussian noise, 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 x_t=F_tx_{t-1}+w_t,\quad w_t\sim\mathcal N(0,Q_t), 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 y_t=H_tx_t+v_t,\quad v_t\sim\mathcal N(0,R_t), 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
         
 
 the posterior remains Gaussian. After predicting mean \(m_tˆ-\) and covariance \(P_tˆ-\), the innovation and its covariance are 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 \nu_t=y_t-H_tm_tˆ-,\qquad 
    
   
 
 C_t=H_tP_tˆ-H_tˆ\top+R_t. 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
         
 
 The Kalman gain \(K_t=P_tˆ-H_tˆ\top C_tˆ{-1}\) balances model confidence against measurement confidence, and the corrected mean is \(m_t=m_tˆ-+K_t\nu_t\). 
    
      
         
 
 The key quantity is the **normalized innovation**, not the raw residual. A residual of magnitude ten is unremarkable if predicted uncertainty is one hundred and alarming if uncertainty is one tenth. Innovation whiteness is also a diagnostic: persistent temporal structure in normalized innovations signals a misspecified dynamics model, observation model, or noise covariance. 
    
      
         
 
 Covariance is computational state, not decorative metadata. Discarding it forces a fixed response to evidence and prevents principled fusion of measurements with different reliability. Square-root filters propagate factors of \(P\) for numerical stability. Information-form filters propagate precision \(J=Pˆ{-1}\) and information vector \(h=Jm\), making independent measurement contributions additive. Choice of form should follow sparsity and conditioning. 
    
      
         
 
 Consistency can be tested statistically. Under the assumed model, the normalized innovation squared 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 \operatorname{NIS}_t=\nu_tˆ\top C_tˆ{-1}\nu_t 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
         
 
 has a chi-squared reference distribution with dimension equal to the observation dimension. A sequence of values that is systematically too large indicates overconfidence or mismatch; values that are systematically too small indicate excessive uncertainty. Temporal dependence must be checked separately with innovation-whiteness tests. Coverage and whiteness reveal failures that root-mean-square error cannot. 
    
      
         
 
 Even algebraically equivalent covariance updates differ in finite precision. The compact expression \(P_t=(I-K_tH_t)P_tˆ-\) can lose symmetry or positive semidefiniteness. The Joseph form, 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 P_t=(I-K_tH_t)P_tˆ-(I-K_tH_t)ˆ\top+K_tR_tK_tˆ\top, 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
         
 
 is more robust, and square-root implementations are preferable when conditioning is severe. Numerical representation is part of the inferential method because an invalid covariance implies invalid confidence. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Beyond a single Gaussian 
    
      
         
 
 Extended Kalman filtering linearizes nonlinear dynamics locally and can become inconsistent when curvature is strong. Sigma-point methods propagate a designed set of points through the nonlinear map, improving moment approximation without explicit Jacobians but still reducing the posterior to a Gaussian. 
    
      
   
 
 Particle filters retain weighted samples: 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 p(x_t\mid y_{1:t})\approx 
    
   
 
 \sum_{i=1}ˆ{N}w_tˆ{(i)}\delta(x_t-x_tˆ{(i)}). 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
         
 
 They can represent multimodality and non-Gaussian tails, but importance weights degenerate over time. Resampling removes low-weight particles yet causes impoverishment and destroys ancestry diversity. The importance-sampling distribution is therefore central: sampling only from the transition prior wastes particles when the observation is informative. Differentiable or learned filters do not abolish these issues; they can simply hide miscalibration inside an expressive learned sampler. 
    
      
         
 
 Rao–Blackwellization is often preferable to brute-force particles. If part of the model is conditionally linear–Gaussian, integrate that part analytically and sample only the discrete or nonlinear component. Expert design exploits conditional structure before increasing particle count. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Robustness is a modeling decision 
    
      
         
 
 An unexpectedly large innovation admits several explanations: the observation 
    
         
 
 may be corrupted, the latent state may have moved farther than expected, the 
    
   
 
 dynamics may have changed regime, or the posterior may simply have been 
    
         
 
 overconfident. A robust filter should not collapse these explanations into one 
    
         
 
 generic downweighting rule. Each implies a different posterior and a different 
    
   
 
 prediction for the next observation. 
    
      
         
 
 Heavy-tailed likelihoods, such as Student-(t) observation models, reduce the 
    
   
 
 leverage of isolated outliers. Mixture likelihoods can carry an explicit 
    
         
 
 contamination indicator. Adaptive process noise can accommodate gradual drift, 
    
   
 
 while switching dynamics or run-length uncertainty represent persistent 
    
         
 
 structural change. These mechanisms are not interchangeable. An overly heavy 
    
         
 
 tail can explain away a genuine change point forever; inflated process noise 
    
         
 
 can preserve tracking error at the cost of uninformative uncertainty; a reset 
    
   
 
 rule can mistake one sensor fault for a new regime. 
    
      
         
 
 I distinguish these cases through persistence and cross-channel structure. A 
    
         
 
 single-channel spike with immediate recovery supports contamination. Coherent 
    
         
 
 innovations across several measurements support latent motion or model error. 
    
         
 
 A sustained change in the distribution of normalized innovations supports a 
    
   
 
 regime hypothesis. Posterior predictive checks should reproduce not only 
    
         
 
 marginal residual magnitudes but also their temporal dependence, direction, 
    
   
 
 and coincidence across sensors. 
    
      
   
 
 Robustness must remain calibrated. A method that never issues a confident 
    
         
 
 posterior is difficult to falsify but not useful. I report coverage conditioned 
    
         
 
 on nominal periods, outliers, and transitions, together with detection delay 
    
         
 
 and false-alarm rate. This makes explicit whether apparent resilience comes 
    
         
 
 from correct hypothesis management or from indiscriminately broad intervals. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Switching models and hypothesis management 
    
      
         
 
 Abruptly changing systems require uncertainty over both continuous state and discrete regime. If a mode \(z_t\) follows a Markov chain and each mode has its own dynamics, exact filtering yields a mixture whose number of components grows exponentially. The interacting multiple-model method controls this explosion by mixing, filtering within each mode, and merging hypotheses at every step. 
    
      
         
 
 This is an important general lesson: bounded inference requires a deliberate **hypothesis management policy**. Pruning, merging, and moment matching are not implementation afterthoughts; they determine which alternative explanations survive. A mode with low current probability may need preservation if it predicts a high-consequence future or is difficult to recover after deletion. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Change points as uncertainty over run length 
    
      
         
 
 Bayesian online change-point detection introduces the run length \(r_t\), the number of observations since the most recent change. A hazard function \(H(r)\) specifies the prior probability of a change after a run of length \(r\). The posterior update combines two possibilities: 
    
      
   
 
 \[ 
    
   
 
 p(r_t=0, y_{1:t}) 
    
   
 
 =\sum_{r_{t-1}} 
    
   
 
 p(r_{t-1},y_{1:t-1})H(r_{t-1}) 
    
   
 
 p(y_t\mid r_{t-1}), 
    
   
 
 \] 
    
      
   
 
 and growth transitions \(r_t=r_{t-1}+1\) weighted by \(1-H(r_{t-1})\). 
    
      
         
 
 This is more informative than a reset score. It maintains uncertainty over when the current regime began, and each run-length hypothesis carries its own sufficient statistics. The hazard encodes duration assumptions; a constant hazard implies a geometric regime duration and is often adopted without justification. 
    
      
         
 
 Exact run-length support grows by one at every observation. A bounded implementation must prune, merge, or aggregate old hypotheses, and this approximation determines the longest change history the detector can express. Keeping only the most probable run length turns posterior inference into brittle winner-take-all segmentation. A small tail mass spread over many long runs can matter even when no individual run is likely. 
    
      
         
 
 Outliers and change points are easily confused. A robust observation model should explain isolated anomalies without resetting the regime, whereas sustained predictive failure should transfer mass toward short run lengths. Heavy-tailed likelihoods, explicit outlier indicators, and hierarchical hazards help separate these phenomena. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Event-triggered inference 
    
      
         
 
 In sensing and networked control, communication or correction can be triggered only when the innovation is informative enough to justify its cost. Crucially, silence can itself be evidence: if a sensor transmits only when a residual leaves a known region, no transmission tells the estimator that the residual remained inside that region. Treating skipped updates as missing at random loses information and biases uncertainty. 
    
      
         
 
 Event policies should therefore be designed jointly with the estimator. A threshold on \(\nu_tˆ\top C_tˆ{-1}\nu_t\) has a statistical interpretation, while a threshold on raw magnitude does not. Resource-aware filtering asks for an optimal trade between posterior risk, sensing cost, latency, and energy. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Filtering, smoothing, and decision delay 
    
      
         
 
 Filtering estimates \(x_t\) from \(y_{1:t}\); smoothing permits later observations. Fixed-lag smoothing occupies a useful middle ground: it delays commitment by \(L\) steps and revises recent states using \(y_{t+1:t+L}\). This is often more valuable than making a much larger causal filter, especially near regime boundaries. 
    
      
         
 
 The correct comparison therefore fixes decision latency. Zero-lag detection, fixed-lag revision, and retrospective segmentation answer different questions. A change-point method should also state whether it estimates the first affected sample, the time at which evidence became sufficient, or the time at which an alarm was issued. 
    
      
   
 
 ## Failure modes 
    
      
         
 
 - **Overconfidence:** underestimated \(Q\) or \(R\) produces brittle gains and poor coverage. 
    
         
 
 - **Model mismatch:** a precise posterior under the wrong model is worse than an honest broad posterior. 
    
         
 
 - **Innovation misuse:** large raw residuals are treated as changes without uncertainty normalization. 
    
         
 
 - **Particle collapse:** a nominally multimodal filter effectively contains one duplicated hypothesis. 
    
         
 
 - **Premature merging:** distinct modes with similar current means have incompatible futures. 
    
         
 
 - **Hazard misspecification:** expected regime duration silently dictates detection latency. 
    
         
 
 - **Numerical loss of positive definiteness:** naïve covariance updates accumulate roundoff error. 
    
         
 
 - **Retrospective leakage:** smoothing results are reported as if available to an online filter. 
    
      
   
 
 ## An expert workflow for sequential inference 
    
      
         
 
 When a sequential estimator fails, I first decide which problem it is failing 
    
         
 
 to solve. State estimation asks where the process is within a trusted model. 
    
         
 
 Robust inference asks whether an observation is an outlier. Model selection 
    
         
 
 asks which dynamics explain the data. Change-point detection asks whether the 
    
         
 
 old regime has ended. These explanations can produce the same large residual, 
    
   
 
 but they imply different updates. 
    
      
   
 
 My operating sequence is: 
    
      
         
 
 1. **Write the belief state.** I state which variables are uncertain and which 
    
   
 
    approximation represents their joint posterior: moments, information 
    
         
 
    parameters, particles, regime mixtures, run lengths, or a factorization. 
    
   
 
    A point estimate is acceptable only when uncertainty cannot affect any 
    
   
 
    subsequent decision. 
    
         
 
 2. **Predict before observing.** The transition model produces a predictive 
    
         
 
    distribution, not just a predicted mean. Its spread is the reference against 
    
   
 
    which the next observation becomes ordinary or surprising. 
    
         
 
 3. **Correct by relative evidence.** I inspect likelihood ratios or normalized 
    
   
 
    innovations. Raw residual magnitude has no context. With Gaussian 
    
         
 
    observations, \(\nu_tˆ\top C_tˆ{-1}\nu_t\) is the natural scalar diagnostic; 
    
         
 
    for non-Gaussian models, posterior predictive checks play the same role. 
    
         
 
 4. **Keep explanations alive when their futures differ.** A low-probability 
    
         
 
    regime, run length, or particle family should not be merged merely because 
    
         
 
    its present mean resembles the winner. Pruning is a resource decision about 
    
         
 
    tail mass, recoverability, and future consequence. Conditional analytic 
    
   
 
    structure should be Rao–Blackwellized before particles are multiplied. 
    
   
 
 5. **Interpret persistence.** One unlikely observation may call for a 
    
   
 
    heavy-tailed likelihood or outlier indicator. A sequence of structured 
    
         
 
    innovations suggests dynamics mismatch or regime change. The hazard model 
    
         
 
    states how readily persistent failure becomes a reset; it should never be 
    
   
 
    an invisible constant. 
    
   
 
 6. **Choose commitment latency.** Filtering, fixed-lag smoothing, and 
    
         
 
    retrospective segmentation permit different evidence. I compare methods at 
    
   
 
    equal delay and distinguish the first affected time, the time evidence 
    
   
 
    became sufficient, and the alarm time. 
    
         
 
 7. **Audit numerical and statistical consistency.** I preserve symmetry and 
    
   
 
    positive semidefiniteness with square-root or Joseph-form updates when 
    
         
 
    needed. I check posterior coverage, normalized-innovation distributions, 
    
   
 
    innovation whiteness, effective particle diversity, and recovery after 
    
   
 
    mismatch—not point error alone. 
    
      
         
 
 Missingness and silence require an observation model too. If a sensor reports 
    
         
 
 only outside a threshold region, no report truncates the residual distribution 
    
         
 
 and is informative. If observations are missing for external reasons, treating 
    
         
 
 the gap as evidence is wrong. Sensing, communication, and inference policies 
    
   
 
 must therefore be specified jointly whenever reporting depends on latent 
    
   
 
 state or innovation. 
    
      
   
 
 To falsify an estimator, I vary noise scale, inject isolated heavy-tailed 
    
   
 
 outliers, switch among regimes with similar current means but different 
    
   
 
 dynamics, alter regime duration, and withhold informative observations. I 
    
         
 
 check whether uncertainty broadens before errors grow, whether alternative 
    
         
 
 hypotheses survive long enough to recover, and whether apparent calibration 
    
   
 
 comes from inflating \(Q\) or \(R\) to conceal model-form error. Learned 
    
         
 
 dynamics, observation models, and samplers are useful only if their flexibility 
    
   
 
 does not make these checks vacuous; epistemic uncertainty and irreducible 
    
   
 
 observation noise should not be collapsed into one convenient variance. 
    
      
         
 
 The unresolved resource problem is hypothesis management. Exact belief support 
    
   
 
 usually grows, while hard winner-take-all truncation is brittle. Pruning, 
    
         
 
 merging, moment matching, and learned sampling mechanisms should be judged by 
    
   
 
 which future distinctions they erase and how their lost tail mass is 
    
   
 
 accounted for. 
    
      
         
 
 The defining principle is that an observation has no fixed importance. Its 
    
   
 
 importance is the likelihood ratio it induces relative to the current 
    
   
 
 predictive belief.

  Appendix D  Architecture details 

 
  D.1  The discovered context-first recurrence 

 
 We describe Context-First DeltaNet (CFDN), introduced in Sections   3.2   and   3.5  . GDN2 stores information in a recurrent matrix and retrieves it through a query-dependent read. CFDN adds a recurrent context vector that contributes the same additive term to every query. This gives the memory two ways to represent information: a shared context contribution and an address-dependent contribution from the matrix. 
 
 
 The central idea is how these two memories learn from a prediction error. At each token, they jointly predict the gated value. CFDN corrects the context vector first, then updates the matrix using the error that remains. This gives shared context priority and encourages the matrix to represent corrections beyond what context already explains. 
 
 
  Correct context before fitting the remaining error. 
For one head, let      𝐒  t   ∈   ℝ    d  k   ×   d  v      \mathbf{S}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d_{v}}    be the matrix memory and      𝐦  t   ∈   ℝ   d  v     \mathbf{m}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}    the context vector, both initialized to zero. Let      𝐪  t   ,    𝐤  t   ∈   ℝ   d  k      \mathbf{q}_{t},\mathbf{k}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}    be the normalized query and key,      𝐯  t   ∈   ℝ   d  v     \mathbf{v}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}    the value, and      𝐛  t   ∈    (  0  ,  1  )    d  k     \mathbf{b}_{t}\in(0,1)^{d_{k}}    and      𝐰  t   ∈    (  0  ,  1  )    d  v     \mathbf{w}_{t}\in(0,1)^{d_{v}}    the sigmoid erase and write gates.

 The first line measures what the two memories fail to predict together. The second assigns part of that error to shared context. The third recomputes the error after this context correction, while keeping the matrix fixed. Only then does the fourth line update the matrix. 
 
 
 Equivalently, the error passed to the matrix is
