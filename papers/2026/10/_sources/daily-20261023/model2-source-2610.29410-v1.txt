  Abstract
 Preference optimization can improve every ranking metric while building a hidden, shared representation shift that the relative loss barely sees, one that can eventually destroy generation. We trace this failure to the  common-mode offset , a shared directional shift in the model’s hidden states that appears across token positions, prompts, and both chosen and rejected responses. Because it moves chosen and rejected representations together, the relative ranking loss barely reacts to its accumulation. As this offset grows, it projects onto the output layer to form a  vocabulary well , elevating a single token’s probability until it dominates decoding and causes generation collapse. We demonstrate the causal role of this mechanism through targeted interventions. The isolated offset alone reconstructs the vocabulary well, injecting the learned offset into an untreated base model recreates the collapse where norm-matched random directions do not, and masking this common component from the loss prevents the well in our flagship setting. Under the per-token ranking loss we study, the same collapse appears in 14 of 17 base-model lineages and under full-parameter fine-tuning. Moreover, the offset is not merely an artifact of collapse, since it is already present in textbook DPO and SimPO runs that form no well. Textbook DPO keeps it weakly aligned with any single vocabulary direction even with three times our base budget. Trained from base models for that budget, however, full-parameter SimPO drives it into collapse on both Llama-2 and Llama-3.1, and the same kind of offset grows, without a well, from a public SFT checkpoint under the official SimPO recipe. Across 17 UltraFeedback runs, the better a run ranks held-out responses, the less often two public reward models prefer its samples to the base model’s. These findings expose a blind spot in standard ranking metrics and introduce a representation-level signal that can warn of generation collapse before it occurs.
  1  Introduction
 Preference optimization has become the standard paradigm for adapting pretrained language models toward human-preferred responses  ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ) , ranging from pairwise Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 )  to listwise objectives based on Plackett–Luce ranking  ( Plackett, 1975 ;  Luce, 2005 ;  Liu et al., 2025 ) . Recent pipelines push this ranking signal further, with repeated rounds of preference optimization on continually refreshed preference data  ( Yuan et al., 2024 ;  Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) . However, recent work reveals a growing disconnect between successful ranking optimization and healthy text generation  ( Rafailov et al., 2024 ) .   Razin et al. (2025)   identify  likelihood displacement , where the absolute likelihood of the chosen response can plummet even as its relative preference margin improves.   Ren & Sutherland (2025)   show that suppressing rejected tokens can undesirably squeeze probability mass toward tokens the base model already favored.   Yuan et al. (2025)   mathematically explain why chosen and rejected likelihoods move together via  gradient entanglement . While these studies illuminate how probabilities shift during ranking, the terminal state of this dynamic remains largely unexplained. How does this movement manifest inside the model, why does it eventually fixate on a seemingly irrelevant token, and why do standard ranking metrics look healthy while generation breaks down?

 We find that preference training induces a surprisingly simple internal anomaly. The final hidden states begin to drift by a shared vector across token positions, prompts, and both chosen and rejected responses. We term this shared movement the  common-mode offset . Because this offset moves the scores of chosen and rejected responses by nearly the same amount, a ranking loss that optimizes their relative difference has almost no incentive to penalize it. Consequently, this shared shift can keep growing in the background, masked by improving preference margins.
 This hidden shift becomes destructive because the output layer projects these hidden states into vocabulary logits. When the offset pushes a single token’s logit far higher than the tokens the base model would predict, it creates a  vocabulary well . Once this well becomes deep enough to dictate decoding, the model enters  generation collapse , uncontrollably repeating that single token. Importantly, the well need not be a rare token with an unusually large output-vector norm, since the same mechanism produces a well on the ordinary token  Conf  on UltraFeedback  ( Cui et al., 2024 ) .
 Causal interventions confirm that this offset causes the well. Keeping only its common component reconstructs the well, injecting it into an untreated base model recreates the collapse where norm-matched random directions do not, and centering the rejected-side loss to hide it prevents the well in the flagship setting (§  5  ). The offset moves both chosen and rejected scores by tens of nats but their margin by less than 3 nat in an average list, which is why held-out ranking improves while generation collapses (Figure   1  ). Across 17 UltraFeedback runs, the better a run ranks held-out responses, the less often two public reward models prefer its samples to the base model’s (§  8  ).
 This vulnerability is not an artifact of one unusual hyperparameter setting. Our main objective is the ranking term of PRO  ( Song et al., 2024 ) , and we observe the same collapse signature in 14 of 17 base-model lineages and under full-parameter fine-tuning (§  4  ). Moreover, the common-mode offset grows under every objective we test, including textbook DPO and SimPO, and the objectives differ in how hard they push it. We measure this push by the loss’s  demand , the per-token margin beyond which it stops pushing. Textbook DPO at its usual setting has a low demand, so its offset stays weakly aligned with any vocabulary direction and generation stays intact. This protection does not come from the reference model, since adding a reference model to the per-token objective does not prevent the well (§  6  ). SimPO lies between the two. Trained from base models for three times our base budget, full-parameter SimPO drives the offset into collapse, and the offset also grows, without forming a well, from a public SFT checkpoint under the official SimPO recipe.
 Therefore, generation collapse is not an inevitability of preference optimization, but the extreme terminal state of a hidden dynamic that is active long before generation visibly fails. Hiding the offset from the loss and penalizing it prevents collapse up to three times the base budget in the four base models we tested.
 Prior work showed that preference optimization can move probability mass where it should not go and couples chosen and rejected updates. We link these probabilistic symptoms to one translation vector in the final hidden state that the loss barely sees, show that it causes the well, and give a necessary condition for its onset. This exposes a blind spot in ranking metrics.
  2  Related Work
  Likelihood displacement, squeezing, and gradient entanglement.    Razin et al. (2025)   demonstrate that DPO training can decrease the chosen response’s absolute likelihood and predict  when  a dataset risks it from the similarity of the responses’ hidden embeddings.   Yuan et al. (2025)   prove that margin-based losses entangle the gradients of chosen and rejected log-probabilities, explaining  why  the two tend to move in tandem. While these works map the symptoms in probability space, we isolate in representation space the  common-mode offset  of the final hidden state, which produces the well. Furthermore,   Ren & Sutherland (2025)   show that the negative gradient on rejected tokens “squeezes” probability mass toward tokens the base model is already confident about. The vocabulary wells we discover are different, since the base model initially ranks our well tokens far below its preferred tokens, typically by 17 to 39 nat, until the offset inflates their logits to dominate decoding.
  Regularizing absolute likelihood.  DPO-Positive  ( Pal et al., 2024 ) , an explicit NLL term  ( Pang et al., 2024 ) , and N-DPO, a length-normalized regularization toward the reference  ( Im et al., 2026 ) , constrain scalar probabilities rather than the shared direction in the hidden state. In our experiments, an NLL term on the chosen response does not prevent the well (§  7  ).
  Unembedding geometry and representation anisotropy.  Pretrained embeddings and hidden states share a common mean, a few dominant directions, and outlier dimensions  ( Mu & Viswanath, 2018 ;  Ethayarajh, 2019 ;  Biś et al., 2021 ;  Gao et al., 2019 ;  Puccetti et al., 2022 ;  Macocco et al., 2025 ;  Sun et al., 2024 ;  Stolfo et al., 2024 ) , whereas the common-mode offset is built during preference  training . Functionally, it resembles activation steering  ( Rimsky et al., 2024 ) , and DPO is known to change behavior by shifting the residual stream  ( Lee et al., 2024 ) , but here preference optimization grows the steering vector on its own until generation collapses. Many of our severe wells emerge from under-trained tokens  ( Land & Bartolo, 2024 ) , which merely provide a path of least resistance for the offset, and decoding-time corrections  ( Ding et al., 2026 )  leave the training-time cause in place. Appendix   A   gives more detail.

 A nearly unconstrained translation.
 Every ranking objective above depends only on differences between scores. Adding the same amount to the scores of all responses of a prompt therefore leaves the loss unchanged. This invariance makes rewards under the Bradley–Terry  ( Bradley & Terry, 1952 )  and Plackett–Luce models identifiable only up to a prompt-dependent constant  ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) . A shift of the hidden states shared by every position does not add exactly the same amount to every score, since its effect on each token depends on the token and its context. To first order, however, the objective sees such a shift only through its component along one direction, a weighted difference of the responses’ mean score gradients (Appendix   B.17  ). Whether a learned shift lies mostly outside that direction is an empirical question, which §  5   answers by measurement.

 Three stages of failure.
  Likelihood displacement  is the routine erosion of absolute log-likelihoods for both chosen and rejected responses and, in isolation, is not pathological.  Vocabulary well formation  is the intermediate state, in which the probability mass released by the displacement concentrates on one or a few tokens or token families; it can be measured before any text is sampled.  Generation collapse  is the terminal state, in which these tokens dominate decoding. We count a run as  collapsed  once it reaches either state, that is, once one token takes most of the released mass or most of its samples degenerate, by repeating text, ending nearly empty, or cycling through the forms of one word. Released mass can also spread thinly across the vocabulary without forming a well. We call the resulting loss of sample quality  diffuse degradation , measure it by the base model’s per-token negative log-likelihood of the samples, and do not count it as collapse.
 Measurement and configuration.
  Mass flow  is computed under teacher forcing on rejected responses. At every position we track the probability mass surrendered relative to the base model, the  released mass , the share of it absorbed by the top-1 token, the  capture , and the share leaking beyond the 10,000th-ranked vocabulary item, the  tail ; a well has  nucleated  once it captures the majority of the released mass.  Degeneration  scores sampled generations for    n  n   -gram repetition  ( Holtzman et al., 2020 ;  Welleck et al., 2020 ) , together with a word-stem ring rate and the base model’s per-token negative log-likelihood of the samples, which detects non-repeating degeneration that standard repetition counts overlook (Appendix   B.14  ).  Held-out ranking  is evaluated on 600 unseen lists, or on 300 of them for a few runs, by top-1 accuracy, pairwise accuracy, and the  margin  between the chosen response’s mean log-probability and that of the highest-ranked rejected response (Appendix   B.2  ). Unless stated otherwise, we fine-tune a rank-32 LoRA adapter with a learning rate of     10   −  5    10^{-5}    and an effective batch size of 32; full-parameter runs use a learning rate of     10   −  6    10^{-6}   . A  base budget  is three passes over the training data, which is 504 steps on the driving lists and about three quarters of one epoch of the official SimPO training data on UltraFeedback. Extended runs operate at fixed multiples of this budget under a schedule of the same form, and a base model’s  onset budget  is the smallest tested multiple at which its untreated run collapses. Offsets and mass flow use 80 rejected training responses unless stated otherwise.
  4  Ranking Improves While Generation Collapses
 We first train Llama-2-7B  ( Touvron et al., 2023b )  with a LoRA adapter under sequential     K  =  4   K=4    Plackett–Luce ranking on the driving lists. The lists were built for the Llama-2-based driving agent of   Li et al. (2025)  . On 600 unseen lists, top-1 accuracy reaches 0.995, and pairwise accuracy stays above 0.94. Generation, however, fails. Within 504 steps, 89% of the probability mass released relative to the base model concentrates on a single token,  textt , which the base model ranked deep in its vocabulary tail, and almost every sampled generation degenerates into repeating it. By every ranking metric the objective has succeeded, yet the output distribution has collapsed.
 We replicate this training across 17 base-model lineages from five tokenizer families, spanning 0.5B to 14B parameters. Fourteen of the 17 collapse within six times the base budget (Table   1  ; Appendix Table   4  ), including TinyLlama-1.1B and the Qwen2.5 models from 0.5B to 3B. Twelve form a single-token well, and in Qwen2.5-14B and Gemma-2-9B most samples cycle through the forms of one word,  collision  or  crash . Phi-3-mini and Gemma-2-2B, tested only up to three times the budget, stay below this criterion, although 39% of Phi-3-mini’s samples repeat  speed . The clear exception is Llama-3.2-3B. Its rejected responses fall by over 30 nat per token, yet its released mass stays on ordinary tokens and its samples stay nearly as fluent as the base model’s (§  6  ).

 The offset is shared across positions, prompts, and both responses.
 We computed the offset separately for each of 900 rejected responses and compared each with their global mean. Across prompts, the per-response offsets align with their mean with median cosines above 0.97 on each of the six runs we measured. Across the two sides of a preference pair, the chosen and rejected offsets exhibit cosines of 0.83 to 0.996. The shift is therefore a single geometric translation that moves both sides of every pair together (Figure   1  b). Furthermore, this shared vector points at the well, since for single-token wells the well token has the largest entry of the projected vector     W  ​   b  ¯    W{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    (Figure   2  b).
 The loss barely sees the offset.
 We added the trained offset to the base model’s hidden states and recomputed the scores of chosen and rejected responses on held-out lists. In the four collapsing runs we evaluated, the offset lowers both scores by 15 to 65 nat per token but moves the margin between them by only 0.2 to 2.7 nat in an average list (Appendix Table   8  ). Its effect on both scores is 10 to 270 times its effect on their difference, and its cosine with the gradient of that difference in the base model is at most 0.16. The objective therefore observes a healthy margin, while the log-probabilities underneath fall by tens of nats. A    z  z   -loss  ( Chowdhery et al., 2023 ;  Team OLMo et al., 2024 )  that compresses the log-partition function 2.5-fold still leaves 65 to 66% of the released mass in the well (Appendix   B.8  ). Little in the loss therefore holds the offset back, and fine-tuning alone already builds one. A supervised control without the ranking term grows an offset of norm 56 shared by chosen and rejected responses (cosine 0.99), yet it lifts no token by more than 3.4 (Appendix   B.11  ).
 Keeping, hiding, adding, or removing the offset controls the well.
 We test the offset’s causal role in four ways. The first adds     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    alone or     r  t   {\color[rgb]{0.7852,0.3906,0.1758}r_{t}}    alone to the base hidden state. On untreated runs,     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    alone makes the well the top token at nearly every rejected position, more often than the full trained state, while     r  t   {\color[rgb]{0.7852,0.3906,0.1758}r_{t}}    alone rarely does (Figure   2  c). The residual therefore partly works against the well. The same holds under full-parameter fine-tuning, where the fine-tuned unembedding rows alone almost never lift the well (Appendix   B.10  ).
 The second test hides the offset from the loss.  Centering  computes the rejected-side ranking loss on      h  t   −   stopgrad  ​   (   b  ¯   )     h_{t}-\text{stopgrad}({\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}})   . The rejected scores then no longer change when the offset grows, so pointing     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    at one vocabulary row no longer lowers them. The gradient can still move the offset, since it treats the subtracted mean as a constant. On the Llama-2 flagship, centering keeps most of the offset’s norm but turns its projection away from the vocabulary, and no well forms.
 The third test transplants the trained offset into an untreated base model. This  injection  adds     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    to the final hidden state at every position, causing the well token to dominate generation on every held-out prompt. Stripping only the well’s row component from     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    removes that well, but generation instead collapses onto a different single token, whereas random or orthogonal directions of the same norm produce no well. The well therefore follows the offset rather than any particular token. The fourth test removes the offset from a model that has already collapsed. Subtracting half of its logit bias     W  ​   b  ¯    W{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    from the untreated flagship model at decoding time lowers the share of degenerate greedy samples on 60 held-out prompts from 100% to 0%, and the chosen response regains 8.7 nat per token (Appendix   B.7  ). Keeping only the offset reconstructs the well, hiding it prevents the well, adding it creates one, and removing it stops the collapse, making the offset a controllable cause of collapse.

 A well forms only after the offset’s gain passes the base gap.
 The formation of a well depends on a logit ratio. Let     m  t   m_{t}    be the  base gap , which measures how much the base model prefers its own top token over the candidate well token at position    t  t   , and let     g  t   g_{t}    be the logit advantage that     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    adds to that well token. In the offset-only state, the well overtakes the base model’s top token where the  dose       D  t   =    g  t   /   m  t     D_{t}=g_{t}/m_{t}    exceeds one (Figure   3  a). Scaling the injected offset confirms this threshold, since a quarter-strength     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    fails to trigger the well while a half-strength one pushes     D  t   D_{t}    above one and puts the well on top at nearly all rejected positions. We also tracked the dose along natural training runs (Figure   3  b; Appendix Figure   9   shows each lineage). Across all 510 snapshots, the well never tops a majority of rejected positions while the median dose stays below one, so in our untreated runs a well needs a dose above one, but a dose above one does not guarantee a well. In Llama-3.2-3B the median dose reaches 2.75 on the ordinary subword  e . Yet the trained model puts  e  on top at only 29% of rejected positions, against 80 and 83% for the wells of Llama-3.1 and Llama-2, and its samples stay nearly as fluent as the base model’s.
 Wells tend to land on anomalous vocabulary rows.
 Because the same     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    projects more strongly onto rows with large norms, rare tokens with large unembedding rows gain the most. In the adapter runs on the driving data, most single-token wells sit in the top 1% of their base model’s row norms and never occur in WikiText-103  ( Merity et al., 2017 ) , whereas in Llama-3.2-3B the highest-norm rows belong to common subwords. High row norms are not required, however. On UltraFeedback Llama-2 collapses onto the ordinary token  Conf , which the offset alone still puts on top at nearly every position (Figure   2  c). Row geometry makes some tokens more exposed, but in untreated runs the offset is what lifts them.
 Different losses push the offset by very different amounts.
 We trained Llama-2-7B on the UltraFeedback pairs with our per-token ranking loss, the same loss with a reference model, SimPO, and textbook DPO, each at its usual setting and, except the plain per-token loss, at three times the budget. We also ran SimPO with full-parameter fine-tuning on Llama-2-7B and Llama-3.1-8B. Table   2   and Appendix Figure   11   report each run’s demand, offset norm, dose, capture, share of degenerate samples, and win rate over the starting model under Skywork-Reward-V2  ( Liu et al., 2026 ) .
 Only the per-token ranking loss collapses at the usual budget, and adding a reference model does not stop it. SimPO forms no well at its usual setting, although its samples still degrade, and it passes the threshold with three times the budget under full-parameter fine-tuning on both Llama-2 and Llama-3.1 (Figure   3  c). Textbook DPO stays far below the threshold even with three times the budget, because its offset stays short and spreads across the vocabulary.

 Between these extremes, SimPO within its published range grows an offset about as long as the collapsing run’s, but a quiet one whose dose remains below one, so no well forms. Nevertheless, its generation quality degrades. The released mass spreads deep into the vocabulary instead of settling on one token, and the samples devolve into strings of unrelated words that standard repetition counts fail to detect, while the reward model prefers the base model’s sample on about nine in ten prompts. However, adding the SimPO offset alone to the base model does not reproduce this diffuse form (Appendix   B.13  ). This diffuse degradation is therefore a second route to damage, separate from the well. The missing fine-tuning stage alone does not explain this damage, since textbook DPO from the same base model wins against it on about two thirds of prompts.
 Under a three-fold budget, however, the offset grows louder, concentrating its projection onto a narrow set of vocabulary rows. Its dose keeps rising, and under full-parameter fine-tuning it passes one on both Llama-2 and Llama-3.1. By the end of training, 80 to 86% of samples degenerate, and held-out ranking is the best of any UltraFeedback run on that base.
 We also trained SimPO from its authors’ public SFT checkpoint of Llama-3-8B with their hyperparameters (Appendix   B.12  ). From this start, no well forms within three times the base budget, and the median dose of every token stays below 0.4. The offset nonetheless grows from a norm of 33 at the base budget to 77 at three times the budget. It stays quiet, lifting no token by more than 4.3 nat, against about 18 nat for SimPO from the base models at the same budget. Meanwhile the SFT model’s per-token negative log-likelihood of the samples rises from 1.35 to 3.86. Two public reward models agree, preferring the one-fold run’s samples to the SFT model’s on 63 to 65% of held-out prompts but the three-fold run’s on only 33 to 34%. Supervised fine-tuning enlarges the base gaps of the candidate wells by at most 22%, so by the dose it should delay the well rather than prevent it.
 Supervised fine-tuning already produces an offset of the same form, and the preference loss sets where it points and how far it grows (Appendix   B.11  ). Small    β  \beta    and listwise objectives therefore do not invent a new failure mode; they push harder on a shift that fine-tuning already produces.
  7  Controlling the Offset
 Centering hides the offset from the loss but leaves most of its norm within the model. Our recipe therefore keeps centering and adds two penalty terms that act on the offset itself, used alone or together, and we call the combination  both . A norm penalty      λ  norm   ​   ‖   b  ¯   ‖    \lambda_{\text{norm}}\|{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}\|    shrinks the offset, and a loudness penalty      λ  loud   ⋅  κ  log   ∑  v    exp    (    (  W   b  ¯   )   v   /  κ  )    \lambda_{\text{loud}}\cdot\kappa\log\sum_{v}\exp\!\big((W{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}})_{v}/\kappa\big)    flattens its vocabulary projection without necessarily shrinking its norm. On the driving lists the recipe also keeps the NLL term of weight 1.0, so it differs from the NLL-only baseline only by offset control. Offset control matters most for objectives worth keeping despite a high demand. Listwise ranking, for example, also supervises the order among the rejected responses, which pairwise objectives never see, and on held-out driving lists it keeps that order at 0.82 to 0.92 while pairwise ranking inverts it (Appendix   B.6  ).
 Cross-family validation up to three times the base budget.

No run forms a well. The largest share of the released mass, at most 20%, goes to an ordinary token such as the period, the tail stays at or below 5.5%, and sampled degeneration remains at or below about 12%. By contrast, at the same three-fold budget on Llama-2, the NLL-only baseline, N-DPO, and DPO-Positive leave 71 to 81% of the released mass in a single well (Table   3  ). The cost to ranking performance is small, since held-out pairwise accuracy settles between 0.94 and 0.96. On Llama-2, Mistral, and Qwen2.5-7B, this is within 2 points of a run of the same base and budget without offset control. Controlling the offset is therefore enough to prevent collapse up to three times the base budget, at almost no cost to ranking.   8  What Ranking Metrics Miss
 An external judge disagrees with the ranking metrics.
 Held-out pairwise accuracy levels off at 0.93 to 0.97 within 300 to 500 steps, treated or not, while the margin keeps growing. The untreated run with the largest held-out margin typically collapses, in line with the over-optimization of direct alignment algorithms  ( Rafailov et al., 2024 )  (Appendix Figure   13  ). We evaluated one sample for each of 300 held-out UltraFeedback prompts using two public reward models, Skywork-Reward-V2  ( Liu et al., 2026 )  and ArmoRM  ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , across 17 Llama-2 runs (Appendix Table   7   and Figure   12  a). Across these runs, the better a run’s held-out pairwise accuracy, the less often the external judge prefers its sample to the base model’s, with a Spearman correlation of     −  0.78   -0.78    for Skywork-Reward-V2 and     −  0.79   -0.79    for ArmoRM. The runs that rank best move the most probability away from the base model’s likely tokens, and the judge tracks how much stays there. The share of mass remaining on the base model’s ten most likely tokens correlates with the win rate at     +  0.74   +0.74    and     +  0.87   +0.87   . Since the base models of these runs are not instruction-tuned, we use the win rate only to compare runs. By this measure, the recipe with an NLL term of weight 1.0 matches textbook DPO, winning 65% of prompts against 66 to 68%, as does the NLL-only baseline at 64%.
 The offset warns before the well forms.
 In seven of the nine lineages with snapshots every 50 steps, the eventual well enters the eight largest entries of     W  ​   b  ¯    W{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    before it takes over, up to 1,200 steps ahead, even while held-out ranking has long saturated (Appendix Figure   12  b). Tracking     ‖   b  ¯   ‖   \|{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}\|   , its loudness, and the leading entries of     W  ​   b  ¯    W{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    during training therefore offers an early signal that standard ranking metrics do not give, although we have read it only from completed runs.
  9  Conclusion
 Preference optimization can collapse generation while held-out ranking stays high. We traced this failure to a common-mode offset, a shift of the final hidden state shared across positions and across chosen and rejected responses, which the ranking objective barely penalizes. A well forms only after the offset lifts one token above the tokens the base model would otherwise predict. Different ranking losses turn the same preference pressure into very different doses. Hiding the offset from the loss and penalizing it prevents collapse up to three times the base budget. Across runs, held-out ranking accuracy even anticorrelates with an external judge (    ρ  =   −  0.78    \rho=-0.78   ). Ranking accuracy and preference margin measure only what the loss constrains, so the offset deserves monitoring.
 Limitations.
 Our evidence spans 17 base-model lineages, two datasets, and adapter and full-parameter training, with two further seeds for the flagship settings and one seed elsewhere (Appendix   B.16  ). The penalty strengths are calibrated on the driving data, the six-fold result covers the OLMo family only, and apart from the SFT-start runs the demand analysis starts from base models.
 Reproducibility Statement
 Training configurations (base models, LoRA rank, learning rate, batch composition, and the form of every penalty term), the exposure-ordering and offset-decomposition measurement code, and the held-out ranking probe are described in §  3  –§  7   and Appendix   B  ; code with all penalty coefficients and the training and held-out preference data will be released upon publication. Held-out ranking numbers are computed on 600 lists, or on 300 of them where stated, whose prompts and chosen responses do not appear in any training split; the corpus counts in §  6   use the full training text and the WikiText-103 training split.

  Appendix A  Extended Related Work
  Iterated preference optimization.  Recent pipelines repeat preference optimization over several rounds on continually refreshed preference data  ( Yuan et al., 2024 ;  Grattafiori et al., 2024 ) , although sampling more responses per prompt does not always help  ( Xiao et al., 2025 ) .
  Likelihood displacement, squeezing, and gradient entanglement.  Recent studies have documented the probabilistic symptoms of preference optimization, but from different angles.   Razin et al. (2025)   demonstrate that DPO training can decrease the chosen response’s absolute likelihood. They trace this to the unembedding matrix’s geometry, using a centered hidden-embedding similarity score to predict  when  a dataset risks spilling rejected updates onto chosen ones.   Yuan et al. (2025)   prove that margin-based losses entangle the gradients of chosen and rejected log-probabilities, explaining  why  the two tend to move in tandem. While these works map the symptoms in probability space, we isolate in representation space the  common-mode offset  of the final hidden state, which produces the well. Furthermore,   Ren & Sutherland (2025)   show that the negative gradient on rejected tokens “squeezes” probability mass toward tokens the base model is already confident about. Pushing down the probability of unwanted tokens is also how unlikelihood training reduces repetition  ( Welleck et al., 2020 ) , whereas in preference optimization the push on rejected responses can end in repetition. The vocabulary wells we discover represent a different phenomenon, since the base model initially ranks our well tokens far below its preferred tokens, typically by 17 to 39 nat. It is the accumulation of the common-mode offset that inflates the logits of these low-confidence tokens, until one of them overcomes this gap and dominates decoding at most positions.
  Regularizing absolute likelihood.  Several methods anchor the absolute probabilities that a relative ranking loss leaves free. DPO-Positive  ( Pal et al., 2024 )  penalizes the chosen log-probability if it falls below its reference value;   Pang et al. (2024)   inject an explicit NLL term for reasoning tasks; and   Im et al. (2026)   regularize the length-normalized log-probability of the full response set toward the reference. However, because these interventions constrain the scalar probabilities rather than the underlying vector representations, they do not directly constrain the shared direction in the hidden state. In our experiments, an NLL term on the chosen response delays the growth of the common-mode offset without eliminating it (Figure   5  ). When it does prevent immediate collapse, the vocabulary well relocates to positions  after  the desired answer, where the NLL term exerts no constraint.
  Unembedding geometry and representation anisotropy.  Prior work has mapped the geometry of pretrained representations. Word embeddings and contextual hidden states are anisotropic, sharing a common mean and a few dominant directions  ( Mu & Viswanath, 2018 ;  Ethayarajh, 2019 ;  Biś et al., 2021 ) , and standard training often squeezes them into a narrow cone  ( Gao et al., 2019 ) . Furthermore, specific outlier dimensions are known to track token frequency  ( Puccetti et al., 2022 ;  Macocco et al., 2025 ) , encode massive activations  ( Sun et al., 2024 ) , or regulate output entropy  ( Stolfo et al., 2024 ) . However, these works describe the geometry of a  pretrained  model. The common-mode offset we study is constructed during preference  training . It does not reshape the entire embedding space into a cone; instead, it acts as a single, shared displacement vector added to the hidden states, which increases the inner product with one specific vocabulary row. Functionally, this resembles activation steering, where adding a fixed vector at every position predictably alters a model’s behavior  ( Rimsky et al., 2024 ) . Mechanistic studies of DPO also find that it changes behavior by shifting the residual stream, bypassing rather than removing capabilities the model already has  ( Lee et al., 2024 ) . The key distinction is that preference optimization grows this steering vector on its own in the background, and the behavior it ultimately produces is generation collapse. Finally,   Land & Bartolo (2024)   identify “under-trained” tokens with near-zero frequencies in base models. While many of the severe vocabulary wells in our experiments happen to emerge from this vulnerable population, these fragile tokens do not spontaneously cause collapse; they merely provide a path of least resistance for the common-mode offset. Decoding-time corrections that subtract unconditional logit terms  ( Ding et al., 2026 )  act after the fact and leave the training-time cause in place.

  B.2  Setup and Measurement Details
 On the driving lists,    β  \beta    is fixed per scene type, 0.12 for the two thirds of lists in normal driving and 0.18 to 0.40 for the other scene types; on UltraFeedback,     β  =  0.12   \beta=0.12   . SimPO’s target margin    γ  \gamma    is subtracted from the top response’s scaled score at each stage. Because a list of length    K  K    yields     K  −  1   K-1    comparisons, the response ranked    k  k   -th is rejected in     k  −  1   k-1    of them, a quantity we call its  exposure . In the flagship run the rejected responses fall in order of their exposure, so the more often a response serves as a negative baseline, the steeper its drop.
 We evaluate each trained run along four axes, since no single metric captures the whole failure.  Mass flow  is defined in §  3  .  Degeneration  combines standard metrics for    n  n   -gram repetition  ( Holtzman et al., 2020 ;  Welleck et al., 2020 ) , non-ASCII runs, and abnormal length with two measures of our own. A word-stem ring rate catches token-family wells, which we call  word-family rings , and the base model’s per-token negative log-likelihood of the samples catches degeneration that does not repeat.  Held-out ranking  uses 600 unseen lists of a prompt and its responses, or 300 of them for the Mistral runs, the six-fold OLMo-2 runs, and the training trajectories. Top-1 accuracy is the fraction of lists whose chosen response scores highest, and pairwise accuracy is the fraction of response pairs within a list that the model orders correctly. Kendall’s    τ  \tau     ( Kendall, 1938 )  is computed over the unseen rejected responses, and the margin is the chosen response’s mean log-probability minus that of the highest-ranked rejected response.  Offset geometry  covers the norm     ‖   b  ¯   ‖   \|{\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}\|   , the loudness, and, for candidate well tokens, their unembedding row-norm percentile and their counts in the training text and in the     10  8   10^{8}   -token WikiText-103 corpus  ( Merity et al., 2017 ) . For centering,     b  ¯   {\color[rgb]{0.2031,0.5313,0.2813}\bar{b}}    is computed on the current batch against the adapter-free forward pass. Collapse and ranking dynamics are measured across all lineages, while the computationally intensive analyses of §  5   and §  6   use the representative subsets named there.

  B.4  Routes of the Released Mass at Extended Budget
 At six times the base budget, each penalty on the OLMo family closes one route for the released mass, and the mass takes the next one (Figure   5  ). Penalizing loudness alone moves the well to another token of the same token family. Adding the norm penalty removes the well but sends about half of the released mass to punctuation and premature end-of-text tokens. Applying the loudness penalty at every position, rather than only to the mean offset, does not close this route either.   B.6  A Listwise Advantage Orthogonal to Collapse
  Figure 7:  (a) On 600 held-out lists, pairwise     K  =  2   K{=}2    training orders the unseen rejected responses below chance (dashed line), with or without offset control (blue), while every sequential listwise     K  =  4   K{=}4    run orders them better than the base models.  Held-out evaluation reveals one respect in which listwise and pairwise objectives differ, independent of collapse. Beyond whether the single best response is identified, we ask whether a model preserves the graded ordering among the  unseen rejected  responses of a held-out list. Pairwise training never supervises this ordering, since it only compares the top response against one alternative. On the driving lists, pairwise ranking (    K  =  2   K{=}2   ) inverts the graded ordering, with ordering accuracy of 0.17 to 0.27 and Kendall    τ  \tau    of     −  0.46   -0.46    to     −  0.66   -0.66   , while every sequential listwise     K  =  4   K{=}4    run preserves it, at 0.82 to 0.92 (Figure   7  ). All-pairs and     K  =  3   K{=}3    training fall in between at about 0.58, and SFT alone falls slightly below chance, at 0.415. On UltraFeedback the effect does not appear, and pairwise training instead slightly improves the ordering of rejected responses over the base model. This listwise benefit is therefore domain-dependent and orthogonal to the collapse mechanism of §  5  . It does not correlate with whether a run nucleates a well, and offset-controlled runs keep whichever ordering their underlying objective produces.

 One alternative hypothesis is that collapse is a symptom of the output distribution’s absolute scale drifting upward, since a softmax is unchanged when the same constant is added to every logit. We test it by penalizing     log  Z  =  log   ∑  v   exp   (   z  v   )    \log Z=\log\sum_{v}\exp(z_{v})   , a    z  z   -loss as in   Chowdhery et al. At this coefficient the    z  z   -loss gradient reaches 19 to 43% of the ranking-gradient norm, yet collapse persists. We treat this as supporting rather than conclusive evidence, because a raw gradient-norm ratio is not the same as its realized contribution to the optimizer update. Together with §  5  , the result indicates that the operative quantity is not the overall output scale but the offset’s projection onto the vocabulary, which a    z  z   -loss constrains only indirectly and incompletely.

  B.14  Shapes of Well That Standard Rulers Miss
 We compared the standard    n  n   -gram repetition ruler with our mass-flow and word-stem measurements on every run. The standard ruler correctly flags the flagship run’s repetition loop, in which 96% of samples degenerate, but it misses three other shapes of well. A  latent well  appears only after the visible answer ends, meaning greedy decoding within a length-matched budget never reaches it. A  non-repeating well  keeps changing its surface form, for example a code-path continuation, while still capturing most of the released mass. The ruler then reports little repetition despite severe collapse. A  word-family ring  lacks a single dominant token, because a cluster of low-frequency, morphologically related tokens takes the released mass together. A ruler that counts single tokens therefore underreports the failure. Furthermore, these standard rulers miss the diffuse degradation from §  6  , which leaves no repetitive patterns to count but is exposed by evaluating the base model’s likelihood of the generated samples. Any conventional ruler used in isolation can therefore pass a model that the offset and mass-flow measurements from §  3   reveal has already collapsed.

 These measurements support claims of the main text, grouped by section.
  The offset is shared (§  5  ).  Recomputed on held-out text with phrasing the model never saw, the flagship offset keeps a cosine of 0.85 with the original.
  Dose threshold (§  6  ).  When the well takes over along natural training runs, it goes from under 10% of the rejected positions to a majority within 100 to 300 steps (Figure   9  b).
  Anomalous rows (§  6  ).  For every single-token well, we looked up the norm of its unembedding row and counted how often the token occurs in our training text and in WikiText-103. Most single-token wells of the adapter runs on the driving data never appear in our training text. In 13 bases, the highest-norm row belongs to a rare token, and its gain     g  t   g_{t}    can overtake base gaps of 17 to 39 nat. Under full-parameter fine-tuning on UltraFeedback, the Llama family collapses onto the bare space token, and there too the offset alone makes the well the top token at nearly every position.
  Losses and demand (§  6  ).  On the driving lists, the per-token loss with a reference model collapses onto  textt  at the base budget and onto a rare Cyrillic token at the three-fold budget. Per-token training at    β  \beta    of 1 and 10, whose demand is also low, degrades in the same diffuse way (Figure   10  ). With three times the budget, the SimPO offset concentrates its projection on a few vocabulary rows even where it becomes shorter. By the end of full-parameter training, its leading token takes 27 to 45% of the released mass (Table   2  ).
  Ranking cost of the recipe (§  7  ).  Eight base models have treated runs in our held-out evaluation. We compare each treated run with the untreated run of the same base and budget, or with the NLL-only run where no untreated run was scored. Every treated run but one lies between 1.8 points below and 0.1 points above its comparison run. The exception is the loudness variant of the recipe on Gemma-2-9B at the base budget, which barely lowers the rejected responses in training and scores 3.1 points below the untreated run, while the combined penalty on the same base scores 0.3 points below it.
  Margin along training (§  8  ).  The margin reaches three or four times its value at the point where ranking saturates. At a matched margin of 12 to 16 nat, Llama-2 runs without offset control have already channeled 42 to 51% of the released mass into a single well, against 8 to 20% for offset-controlled runs (Figure   13  ). The margin at which capture takes off also differs by base model, from 13 to 27 nat on six-fold OLMo-3 to 44 to 59 nat on OLMo-2, so the margin cannot serve as a stopping criterion.
  B.16  Seeds and Scope
 Both untreated seeds of the flagship settings collapse onto the same well as the first,  textt  on the driving lists and  Conf  on UltraFeedback. Their offset norms lie within 3% of the first seed’s, and their dose crosses one no later than the well takes over. Both treated seeds stay free of a well, with 10 to 12% residual degeneration and unchanged held-out ranking. Adapter rank from 8 to 128 and attention-only adapters change when a well forms rather than whether. Across these ranks, offset norms lie between 80 and 117 on Llama-2 and Llama-3.1, and rank 8 on Llama-2 nucleates the same well at three-fold budget. The demand analysis of §  6   covers pairwise UltraFeedback training under per-token averaging with    β  \beta    from 0.05 to 10, under textbook DPO with    β  \beta    from 0.1 to 0.0003, and under SimPO with    β  \beta    and    γ  \gamma    from its published tuning range. These runs use our adapter and full-parameter protocols for three or nine epochs.

 Three remarks bound the statement. The constraint is first-order and local, since the softmax is nonlinear and minibatch gradients differ from the expectation, so the complement is weakly rather than exactly unconstrained.
