Abstract
Force-sensitive manipulation requires contact and load information that is not provided explicitly by
visual observations.
We present CW-WAM, a world action model that jointly predicts video, motion, and contact
wrench. Inspired by hybrid motion–force control, it contains a unified
interaction module that treats both motion and contact wrench as modeling targets and predicts time-aligned
motion–wrench chunks as control references.
A pair-causal attention mechanism structures the motion–wrench dependencies, while a long fixed-length
wrench memory retains past interaction information. The predicted wrench chunk acts as a
force reference for a 20-Hz outer-loop admittance controller within a slow–fast control system
that maintains force feedback and smooth motion between model queries. CW-WAM improves contact
prediction and increases the insertion success rate from 25% to 70% for square pegs with 1-mm clearance per side
and from 40% to 95% for power adapters, compared with WAM without wrench input.
It also improves lifting-height accuracy across different loads.
Our framework with an admittance controller reduces force-reference RMSE
from 4.8 to 2.7 N for peg insertion and from 2.0 to 1.3 N for wiping, respectively,
with smaller force fluctuations during successful execution.

 I Introduction
World action models (WAMs) jointly predict future visual observations and actions,
providing coherent motion plans for manipulation  [ 1 ] .
Yet a square peg jammed during insertion may appear fully seated, and
visually similar objects may differ substantially in weight. Such force-sensitive
tasks require physical interaction information that wrist force–torque sensing can provide.
Force feedback improves contact-rich manipulation by complementing visual
observations  [ 2 , 3 ] and enabling rapid motion correction  [ 4 ] .
Joint action–wrench prediction extends these benefits: predicted forces guide
learned feedback correction  [ 5 ] or help evaluate contact
outcomes  [ 6 ] . These advances motivate WAMs that predict the wrench associated with
each action and use it to guide execution.
Contact depends on commanded motion, prior interaction, and the environment.
To capture these dependencies, we design an interaction module inspired
by hybrid motion–force control.
The module pairs past commands with measured wrenches
(Fig.  ) and predicts future motion and wrench
using separate tokens, prediction heads, and training losses.
High-rate wrench encoding captures contact changes within motion intervals, while
longer memory retains earlier wrench measurements.
CW-WAM integrates this module into a DreamZero-style video–action
backbone  [ 1 ] .
During execution, paired predictions provide nominal motion and
an expected wrench reference for admittance feedback.
A slow–fast system applies corrections and tracks continuous motion between model
queries. During contact, this keeps force feedback active while the model
computes the next motion–wrench chunk.
Our work makes three contributions:
 •
 Structured action–wrench modeling. We introduce a unified
interaction module with paired motion–wrench history,
separate future output streams and modality-specific losses.
Across four real-robot tasks, CW-WAM achieves the strongest overall
performance among the evaluated methods.
It improves insertion and release success, tracks force more accurately
in peg insertion and wiping, and adapts motion to sensed load.
 •
 Predicted wrench references for feedback. We use the predicted
wrench trajectory to guide admittance corrections without requiring a separately
trained corrective policy. During successful trials, this feedback reduces
the error between predicted and measured forces and lowers contact-force variation.
 •
 A slow–fast execution system. We combine low-rate CW-WAM inference
with faster admittance feedback and continuous trajectory tracking.
The admittance controller computes pose corrections at 20 Hz.
The corrected motion waypoints
are then interpolated into smooth trajectories for a 1-kHz robot controller.
We demonstrate this interface on four real-robot tasks.
 II Related Work
Visuomotor policies and WAMs
Visuomotor policies generate actions from visual or language-conditioned
observations  [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] .
Alongside direct action generation, world models support policy learning
from imagined trajectories  [ 11 ] .
Video-based approaches recover actions
from generated videos  [ 12 , 13 , 14 ] , while world action
models such as DreamZero jointly model video and actions  [ 1 ] .
Cosmos Policy uses predicted states and values for planning  [ 15 ] ,
and Fast-WAM omits future-video generation at inference  [ 16 ] .
Building on this foundation, CW-WAM incorporates interaction history
and wrench prediction.
Contact-aware policies and action–wrench prediction
Contact-rich manipulation requires interaction signals that vision
does not explicitly provide.
Prior work incorporates tactile feedback for reactive correction  [ 4 ]
or visual–tactile–action modeling  [ 17 , 18 , 19 ] .
ForceVLA and ManipForce condition actions on F/T
observations  [ 20 , 3 ] , while FoAR modulates force feedback using
predicted contact  [ 2 ] . Gripper-force feedback also supports
learning delicate grasping  [ 21 ] .
Beyond using force as input, FAWAM and Facet-0 jointly predict actions and wrenches.
They use the predicted wrenches in a learned residual corrector and
an action–wrench critic, respectively  [ 5 , 6 ] .
CW-WAM pairs past motion commands with measured wrenches and retains
longer wrench histories to capture prior contact.
It predicts future motion and wrench using separate tokens, prediction
heads, and losses. The predicted wrenches then serve as references
for admittance feedback control, without a learned corrective policy.
Slow–fast execution and contact feedback
Contact can change within an action chunk, requiring a fast response.
To respond between planning updates, slow planners can guide learned
residual or reflex policies  [ 22 , 23 ] , while learned motion
targets and compliance parameters guide high-rate force
control  [ 24 , 25 ] .
Force Policy combines global visual planning with fast local force
control  [ 26 ] .
In CW-WAM, predicted wrench trajectories guide online admittance correction
between model queries.
 III Structured Contact-Wrench World Action Modeling
 III-A Problem formulation and shared backbone
CW-WAM jointly predicts future video, motion, and contact wrench for
motion–force control. At observation time t , it models
 p_{\theta}(Z_{t}^{+},A_{t},W_{t}\mid V_{t},s_{t},\ell,\mathcal{H}_{t}^{AW},\mathcal{H}_{t}^{W}).
 (1)
Here V_{t} , s_{t} , and \ell denote RGB observations, robot state, and
the language instruction, respectively; \mathcal{H}_{t}^{AW} and \mathcal{H}_{t}^{W} provide recent
action–wrench history and longer wrench memory. Outputs are video
latents Z_{t}^{+} , motion targets A_{t}=(a_{t,i})_{i=1}^{H} , and wrenches
 W_{t}=(w_{t,i})_{i=1}^{H} , with hats denoting predictions.
Training pairs each motion target with the compensated wrench at
the same timestamp.
Each action a_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{10} contains position,
continuous 6-D rotation  [ 27 ] , and gripper position.
Position and rotation specify a delta from the tool center point (TCP)
pose at observation time t , converted to an absolute base-frame pose.
Each predicted wrench \hat{w}_{t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6} represents
bias-corrected, tool-gravity-compensated force and torque
in the TCP frame defined by its paired predicted pose,
with torque referenced to the TCP origin.
CW-WAM extends DreamZero  [ 1 ] using a Wan2.2-TI2V-5B
DiT  [ 28 ] . A VAE encodes three RGB views as a 160\times 320
composite; an MLP encodes robot state. Video, state, history, and
future action–wrench tokens share self-attention, conditioned on
OpenCLIP image and UMT5 language features through cross-attention.
 III-B Interaction history and paired prediction
The interaction module pairs past motion commands with measured
wrenches from the same time interval. Recent history spans H_{s} intervals at rate
 f_{h} , with boundaries b_{i}=t-(H_{s}-i)/f_{h} . Each interval (b_{i-1},b_{i}] associates up to
 K_{w} high-rate wrench samples with a motion-target token.
A temporal wrench encoder processes each sample’s 6-D value, relative
sampling time, and inter-sample interval. Validity masks exclude padding
from attention and pooling. The action encoder produces a separate token,
and both tokens receive the same history-interval encoding:
 \mathcal{H}_{t}^{AW}=[A_{1}^{-},W_{1}^{-},\ldots,A_{H_{s}}^{-},W_{H_{s}}^{-}].
 (2)
Long-term wrench memory (LTM) extends history to H_{\ell} wrench-only
intervals using the same encoder with additional window-time and history
embeddings. It retains preceding payload tension or sustained resistance.
Both histories overlap and end at the current observation.
Future motion and wrench tokens share the Transformer but use separate
encoders, prediction heads, and losses. Pair-causal (PC) attention uses a
lower-triangular mask over [A_{1}^{+},W_{1}^{+},\ldots,A_{H}^{+},W_{H}^{+}] .
Thus, W_{i}^{+} attends to A_{i}^{+} , but not conversely; both attend to earlier
pairs. All future tokens update jointly at each denoising step.
 III-C Training and inference
Training uses flow matching with normalized action and wrench targets.
For each modality m\in\{V,A,W\} , target x^{m} is mixed with independent
noise \epsilon^{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I) at a shared noise
level \sigma within each block:
 \displaystyle x_{\sigma}^{m}
 \displaystyle=(1-\sigma)x^{m}+\sigma\epsilon^{m},\qquad u^{m}=\epsilon^{m}-x^{m},
 (3)
 \displaystyle\mathcal{L}
 \displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\{V,A,W\}}\lambda_{m}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\omega(\sigma)\operatorname{MSE}_{M_{m}}(v_{\theta}^{m},u^{m})\right].
 (4)
Here v_{\theta}^{m} predicts flow velocity, \omega(\sigma) weights
noise levels, and M_{m} masks padded targets. Valid-channel normalization
averages motion and wrench losses over their 10 and 6 valid channels,
respectively, with \lambda_{m}=1 for all modalities.
Teacher forcing supplies clean preceding video context to noisy current
blocks  [ 1 ] .
We train the DiT and modality projectors for 50k steps with task-balanced
sampling, keeping VAE, OpenCLIP, and UMT5 frozen. We use H=H_{s}=24 ,
 H_{\ell}=150 , f_{h}=15 Hz, and K_{w}=16 : 1.6 s of recent history and
10 s of wrench memory.
At inference, four Euler steps jointly generate two video latent
frames and 24 action–wrench pairs spanning 1.6 s, while observed
latents remain fixed. Control uses the paired predictions without
decoding video to RGB.

 IV Slow–Fast Motion–Force Control
We apply task-weighted admittance corrections to nominal motion
at 20 Hz, with joint-impedance tracking at 1 kHz.
 IV-A Admittance control using predicted wrenches
We use the predicted wrench as a feedback reference to maintain
the interaction expected along the jointly predicted motion.
Predicted motion provides the nominal TCP pose
 (p^{\mathrm{nom}},R^{\mathrm{nom}}) in the robot base frame.
We express the selected predicted wrench and the measured wrench
in this nominal TCP frame, with moments about its origin,
and compute the admittance correction in the same frame  [ 29 , 30 ] :
 \displaystyle e_{w}
 \displaystyle=\hat{w}(\phi^{w})-w^{\mathrm{meas}},
 (5)
 \displaystyle M\Delta\ddot{x}+C\Delta\dot{x}+K_{a}\Delta x
 \displaystyle=-S(e_{w}+k_{I}\eta),
 (6)
 \displaystyle\dot{\eta}
 \displaystyle=e_{w}-\lambda\eta.
 (7)
Here \phi^{w} selects the progress-aligned predicted wrench as described in the next subsection.
 \Delta x=[\Delta p^{\top},\Delta\theta^{\top}]^{\top} contains translation and rotation offsets.
 M , C , and K_{a} specify virtual inertia, damping, and restoring stiffness.
The diagonal feedback-weighting matrix S weights the wrench-error components.
 \eta accumulates wrench error with decay rate \lambda , and
 k_{I} controls its contribution.
Integral accumulation pauses when correction is inactive or
would increase a saturated offset.
At 20 Hz, semi-implicit Euler integration updates velocity
first and then the offset, subject to acceleration, velocity, and displacement limits.
The corrected base-frame pose is:
 \displaystyle p^{\mathrm{cmd}}
 \displaystyle=p^{\mathrm{nom}}+R^{\mathrm{nom}}\Delta p,
 (8)
 \displaystyle R^{\mathrm{cmd}}
 \displaystyle=R^{\mathrm{nom}}\exp([\Delta\theta]_{\times}).
 (9)
Inverse kinematics then maps this corrected absolute pose to joint-position commands.
The feedback-weighting matrix S reflects each task’s contact constraints
(Table  I ). For peg insertion, smaller F_{z} and \tau_{z} weights
favor lateral and tilt corrections while reducing changes to nominal insertion motion.
Adapter insertion uses larger axial-force and torque weights. Wiping enables only F_{z}
feedback, leaving tangential motion and orientation to the nominal trajectory.

 IV-B Continuous action-chunk execution
On a single NVIDIA RTX 5090, four-step model queries typically take
0.5–0.6 s, including observation processing, inference, and postprocessing,
but excluding network communication. Chunks execute sequentially
(Fig.  3 ). While awaiting the next prediction, the robot
holds the final nominal pose with admittance active during contact.
An adaptive-duration C^{2} bridge joins each new chunk to the current
commanded pose, matching velocity and acceleration.
At 20 Hz, we interpolate nominal poses with C^{2} continuity and
predicted wrenches linearly. All experiments select the wrench reference
by matching the measured TCP pose to the planned motion trajectory.
Matched progress \phi^{w} cannot move backward or exceed scheduled
progress \phi , accommodating execution lag while preserving reference order.
Every 50 ms, we replan a 0.5-s piecewise-quintic joint trajectory,
matching the active trajectory’s position, velocity, and acceleration for
 C^{2} continuity. A 1-kHz joint-impedance controller tracks it with gravity
and friction compensation.
 V Experiments
We evaluate prediction accuracy through open-loop ablations,
force-sensitive manipulation through real-robot tasks, and execution
quality with and without predicted-wrench admittance.
 V-A Tasks and data collection
We select four tasks to test contact-guided alignment, precise seating, force regulation,
and adaptation to payload (Fig.  , left).
 Square peg insertion. The robot inserts a 38-mm square peg into a 40-mm hole
with 1-mm clearance per side and an insertion depth of about 50 mm. The tight clearance and
long engagement make misalignment and jamming challenging. Demonstrations include smooth
first-attempt insertion, failed attempts followed by recovery, and light
jamming followed by alignment. Insertion success requires full seating;
release success additionally requires the peg to remain seated after gripper opening.
 Power-adapter insertion. The robot grasps a loosely placed adapter and seats it
in another socket. This tests precise positioning and insertion with contact.
Compared with the long peg, the adapter is less prone to jamming once insertion begins.
Demonstrations therefore emphasize successful first-attempt insertion with minimal lateral
adjustment. The success criteria are the same as for peg insertion.
 Whiteboard wiping. The robot picks up an eraser and removes a marker trace
through sustained surface contact. The traces vary in orientation and curvature,
requiring both spatial coverage and sustained force regulation. Success requires complete marker
removal after automatic eraser pickup. We also quantify ink removal in additional
patterned-wiping recordings and evaluate contact-force accuracy separately.
 Spring-scale transport. The robot grasps a spring scale attached to a box
and transfers the box between bins. The boxes weigh either 30 g or 830 g but look identical. The heavier box stretches
the spring farther, requiring a higher lift to clear the rim. This tests whether contact
and load information guide motion beyond visual appearance. Success requires rim clearance,
transport, and placement in the destination bin.
Training uses task-specific language instructions and 450 demonstrations:
213 peg insertions, 97 adapter insertions, 64 whiteboard wipes,
and 76 spring-scale transports.
Data are collected by bilateral teleoperation with two 7-DoF Franka arms.
Leader joint positions command the follower, whose external-joint-torque estimates
provide force feedback to the operator. A wrist-mounted LinkTouch six-axis F/T sensor
records wrench at 150 Hz. We remove sensor bias and calibrated tool gravity, transform
the wrench to the TCP frame with moment about the TCP origin, and apply a causal
second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 10-Hz cutoff. The resulting wrench
represents external contact and grasped-payload loads. Two RealSense D405 cameras and
one D455 provide three RGB views. Motion commands, robot state, and images are recorded
at 15 Hz and aligned with the wrench stream by timestamps.
Training uses eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs and AdamW with a global
batch size of 8, a peak learning rate of 10^{-5} , and weight
decay of 10^{-5} . We use 5% linear warmup followed by cosine decay.
 V-B Model variants
 WAM has no wrench input or output. WAM + Wrench encodes pooled
wrench history and predicts concatenated 16-D action–wrench vectors.
It uses the current robot state without explicit past motion-command tokens.
 CW-WAM w/o LTM introduces the interaction module: explicit paired command
and wrench history, separate future streams, and PC attention.
Its recent history pairs 24 motion commands with wrench intervals over 1.6 s.
Full CW-WAM adds LTM.
 CW-WAM w/o LTM and PC replaces only PC with bidirectional
attention, isolating attention structure within the no-LTM module.
All variants share training data, backbone initialization, and a 50k-step
schedule with task-balanced sampling. WAM omits wrench loss.
WAM + Wrench uses either padded-width normalization, dividing masked
squared-error sums by 32, or CW-WAM’s valid-channel normalization
(Sec.  III-C ), with architecture and other settings unchanged.
The latter tests the interaction module under the same loss normalization.
PC and LTM ablations retain the CW-WAM objective: PC is tested without
LTM, and LTM with PC retained.

 V-E Task completion and contact behavior
The interaction module improves insertion and release (Fig.  4 ). Relative
to WAM + Wrench, CW-WAM w/o LTM raises adapter insertion from 9/20 to 16/20 and release from
0/20 to 13/20. Peg release improves from 1/20 to 6/20, with insertion at 7/20 versus 8/20.

force magnitudes across successful trials. Thick blue and green curves show CW-WAM
without and with admittance, respectively. The black curve and gray band show the
demonstration reference. Curves are aligned by contact phase, with low-force

Adapter unloading includes only trials with successful release.
Longer wrench memory improves both insertion tasks: peg insertion rises from 7/20 to 14/20 and
release from 6/20 to 11/20; adapter insertion rises from 16/20 to 19/20 and release from 13/20
to 16/20. It also lowers force-reference error in adapter insertion and wiping, demonstrating
the task value of longer interaction context (Fig.  4 ).
In successful peg trials, CW-WAM reduces position-tracking RMSE from 12.5 to 2.8 mm and
force-reference RMSE from 15.2 to 4.8 N relative to WAM + Wrench (Fig.  4 ).
Force profiles also show less variation during search and seating
(Fig.  5 (b)).
For adapter insertion, CW-WAM improves completion and reduces position-tracking RMSE from 25.3 to 8.8 mm.
Its higher force-reference RMSE and contact-force variation relative to WAM + Wrench (Figs.  4 and 5 (c))
are consistent with the observation that most successful WAM + Wrench trials rely on precise, one-shot alignment without
adjustment, followed by full insertion. In contrast, CW-WAM’s learned search and firm insertion
behavior improves insertion success while producing higher contact forces.
In the whiteboard-wiping task, all methods remove the ink in 20/20 trials with simple patterns. For more complicated
patterns, such as circles and letters, the average removal rate is 85.9% across the six selected trace patterns.
For contact-force quality, force-reference RMSE and contact-force variation both decrease from WAM + Wrench to CW-WAM with admittance,
showing more accurate force tracking and smoother contact (Figs.  4 and 5 (a)).
 V-F Load-dependent lifting
CW-WAM reproduces load-dependent lifting heights more accurately than the other models,
even when the boxes look identical.
Its median peak heights are 423.6 and 481.8 mm for light and
heavy loads, respectively,
close to the demonstration medians of 420.9 and 492.7 mm (Fig.  6 ). CW-WAM w/o LTM shows similar lifting behavior

lift for heavier loads. WAM + Wrench gives an intermediate response at 431.3 and
467.9 mm: its absolute differences from the demonstration medians are 10.4 and
24.8 mm, compared with 2.7 and 10.9 mm for CW-WAM.

 VI Conclusion and Discussion
In this work, we presented CW-WAM, a world action model that jointly
predicts video, motion, and contact wrench. The interaction module
combines paired action–wrench history, separate prediction streams,
and longer wrench memory. Across four real-robot tasks, CW-WAM
improves insertion and release success rates and adapts the lifting
height to different loads. Compared with the WAM baseline without
wrench input, the peg insertion success rate increases from 25%
to 70%, and the adapter insertion success rate increases from
40% to 95%.
The slow–fast execution system uses predicted wrenches as references
for admittance feedback control between model queries. During
successful execution, this feedback mechanism reduces force-reference RMSE
from 4.8 N to 2.7 N for peg insertion and from 2.0 N to 1.3 N for
wiping, while also reducing contact-force variation.
Our evaluation covers four tasks within the demonstration
distribution, using manually tuned feedback gains and predefined
compliant directions. Future work will examine how controller
settings affect performance and explore learning additional contact
parameters such as the feedback-weighting matrix and contact frame,
alongside motion and wrench prediction for end-to-end
closed-loop control. We will also evaluate generalization to new
objects and tasks.
