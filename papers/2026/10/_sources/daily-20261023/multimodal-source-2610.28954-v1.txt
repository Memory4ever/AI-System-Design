Abstract
Coarse-to-real (C2R) video generation converts coarse animation, simulation, or
game-rendered videos into photorealistic videos while preserving scene layout,
camera motion, and dynamics. Training such models requires aligned (coarse,
realistic) video pairs, which are expensive to create and confine prior
systems to narrow synthetic domains. We exploit a simple asymmetry: paired
videos are hard to obtain, but paired real-to-coarse images are easy to construct
with existing editing and 3D reconstruction tools. Using these image pairs, we
train the DINO-R2C model to transfer realistic images to coarse images in DINO
feature space, and apply it frame-wise to ordinary real videos to
synthesize pseudo-coarse videos. The C2R# model then learns to generate each
real video from its pseudo-coarse counterpart, free from needing (coarse,
realistic) video pairs. At inference, the DINO-R2C model is discarded and actual
coarse videos drive the C2R# model directly. Thus, our pair-free training scales with any
real-video corpora rather than with paired synthetic videos.
We will release the model and project webpage at https://gonzalognogales.github.io/coarse2real-sharp/ .

 1 Introduction
 Figure 1: The C2R# model transforms game renders (top), geometry blockouts (middle),
and stylized animations (bottom) into photorealistic videos.
Coarse-to-real (C2R) video generation uses coarse videos, such as
game renders, geometry blockouts, or stylized animations, as the structural
control for photorealistic video generation. The coarse input specifies scene
layout, camera motion, and dynamics, while a modern video model produces the
final visual appearance.
A major limitation of existing coarse-to-real systems is that they need paired
training data. The original C2R paper  ( Gomez-Nogales et al., 2026 ) 
introduces a model that learns controllability from paired synthetic (coarse, realistic)
videos. Such aligned video pairs are expensive to create, and the cost
multiplies with every additional coarse modality, so paired-video supervision is
difficult to scale.
Our key observation is that this bottleneck is asymmetric. Paired coarse-to-real
videos are expensive, but paired real-to-coarse images are cheap. Modern image editing
models and 3D fitting methods can turn diverse realistic images into aligned
coarse counterparts. Image-level supervision is therefore highly accessible.
However, using an image generator such as GPT-Image 2  ( OpenAI, 2026 ) 
to edit every frame of a large video corpus into its coarse counterpart would
incur substantial generation costs.
We exploit this asymmetry with an image-level real-to-coarse bridge, the DINO-R2C model.
Trained on (coarse, realistic) image pairs, it predicts the DINO
features  ( Siméoni et al., 2025 ) of the coarse counterpart of a realistic
image. Applied to every frame of an ordinary real video, it synthesizes a
pseudo-coarse video that is paired with its source. We train the
 C2R# model to generate the real video from this pseudo-coarse control video. At
inference, the DINO-R2C model is discarded and an actual coarse video drives the
 C2R# model directly.
Using the image-level bridge poses two challenges. First, the DINO-R2C model is an image model, so its per-frame
outputs are not temporally smooth. As a result, we apply camera augmentation during its training
to reduce this jitter, and the C2R# model learns to tolerate the rest because its
target is always the temporally coherent real video. Second, the predicted
features can leak cues of the realistic input that no actual coarse video
contains. Therefore, we block the leak by decoding the prediction to pixels with a
frozen RAE decoder  ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) and re-encoding those pixels
before they serve as control.
This formulation moves the expensive part of supervision from videos to
images. Once the DINO-R2C model is trained for a coarse modality, any real-video
collection becomes coarse-to-real training data, while supporting a new modality
requires only new image pairs rather than a new paired-video dataset.
 2 Related Work
Generative rendering from coarse structure.
Turning cheap renders into realistic imagery predates diffusion models:
 Richter et al. (2022) enhance game frames with a G-buffer-conditioned
network trained adversarially against unpaired photographs, and
vid2vid  ( Wang et al., 2018 ) learns paired label-to-video synthesis. Recent
work conditions image or video diffusion models on G-buffers
 ( Beisswenger et al., 2025 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) , animated
meshes  ( Cai et al., 2024 ) , physics-engine
states  ( Lin et al., 2026 ) , or multimodal spatial
maps  ( Abu Alhaija et al., 2025 ) , and RealMaster  ( Cohen-Bar et al., 2026 ) 
lifts rendered videos to photorealism by propagating enhanced keyframes to build
its own paired videos. NVIDIA’s DLSS 5  ( NVIDIA, 2026 ) conditions a
real-time generative renderer on rendered frames and engine motion vectors
to enhance photorealistic appearance. All of these obtain supervision from inside a rendering
pipeline, from per-scene synthesis, or from authored video pairs. We assume only
a coarse RGB video at test time and no paired videos at all.
The original C2R paper.
Our direct predecessor  ( Gomez-Nogales et al., 2026 ) conditions a Wan2.1
backbone on DINOv3 features of the control video through a lightweight adapter.
Its control pathway is trained mostly on unpaired real videos reconstructed
from their own DINO features, with a small set of paired synthetic (coarse,
realistic) videos anchoring controllability. Controllability therefore rests
on scarce, domain-limited video pairs, and the adapter sees mostly features of
real videos in training but features of coarse videos at test time. Our
pair-free C2R framework keeps the objective and control interface but removes
paired videos from training.
Controllable video diffusion.
Video diffusion and flow-matching models can be guided by depth, edges,
segmentation, or sparse keyframes through ControlNet-style
branches  ( Zhang et al., 2023 ; Esser et al., 2023 ; Guo et al., 2024 ; Lin et al., 2025 ) ,
by unified context blocks  ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Wan, 2025 ) , or by warped
noise that carries motion  ( Burgert et al., 2025 ) . For image generation,
LooseControl  ( Bhat et al., 2024 ) generalizes ControlNet’s depth
conditioning to loosely specified scene boundaries and 3D object boxes.
Instruction-based
video editors such as Editto  ( Bai et al., 2025 ) are trained on large
synthetic edit datasets and can perform sim-to-real edits, which is why we
compare against them. Explicit structural controls preserve geometry strongly
but can over-constrain generation or leak the appearance of the control
input; as in the original C2R paper, the C2R# model conditions on implicit DINO
features, which carry layout and semantics while leaving appearance to the
video model.
Self-supervised features as a cross-domain bridge.
DINO features  ( Oquab et al., 2024 ; Siméoni et al., 2025 ) are dense and robust
to appearance change, and several works exploit this for domain transfer.
Concurrently, Control-DINO  ( Dominici et al., 2026 ) conditions an
image-to-video model on DINOv3 features for stylization and video-from-3D,
and Liu et al. (2026) use self-supervised features as an appearance-invariant
bridge for unpaired image translation. Earlier unpaired video translation
relied on cycle consistency  ( Zhu et al., 2017 ) with pseudo-supervision
from synthetic motion  ( Wang et al., 2022 ) , while
InstructPix2Pix  ( Brooks et al., 2023 ) and Ditto  ( Bai et al., 2025 ) 
showed that strong image models can synthesize the paired data an editor
needs. We combine both ideas: image models provide cheap (coarse, realistic)
image pairs, an real-to-coarse model learned in DINO space and decoded through an RAE
decoder  ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) lifts them to videos, and the resulting
pseudo-paired videos supervise the coarse-to-real generator.

 3.1 Problem Setup
At inference time, the user provides an actual coarse video v^{\mathrm{c}} 
from categories such as game renders, geometry blockouts, or stylized animations, together
with a text prompt. We seek a generator that produces a realistic video while
preserving the scene layout, camera motion, and dynamics of v^{\mathrm{c}} .
For training, we assume access to ordinary real videos v^{\mathrm{r}} and to
aligned (coarse, realistic) image pairs, but to no paired
 (v^{\mathrm{c}},v^{\mathrm{r}}) videos. Throughout, superscripts
 \mathrm{r} and \mathrm{c} denote realistic and coarse data.
Our pair-free C2R framework has two models (Figure  2 ).
The DINO-R2C model (Section  3.2 ) learns real-to-coarse image transfer from
the image pairs and is then applied to every frame of the real videos,
producing pseudo-paired videos (Section  3.3 ). The C2R# model
(Section  3.4 ) is trained on these pseudo-paired videos to generate
the real video from its pseudo-coarse counterpart; at inference it is driven
directly by actual coarse videos.
 3.2 Image-Level Real-to-Coarse Bridge: the DINO-R2C 
 Figure 3: Example (coarse, realistic) image pairs. A realistic image (left) is paired with coarse
images in four modalities (right):
Coarser geometry, Coarse geometry,
Coarser Anime, and Coarse Anime.
Image pairs.
We define a coarse modality as one visual form of coarse input that the
 C2R# model should support, and use the four modalities shown in
Figure  3 . Each coarse image preserves the subjects, layout,
pose, and camera of its realistic counterpart. We generate realistic source
images with GPT-Image 2  ( OpenAI, 2026 ) and create their coarse
counterparts through instruction-guided editing with the same model or 3D
reconstruction and simplification. All images are produced
at 1280\times 720 , with 14,000 aligned (coarse, realistic) image pairs per
modality (Appendix  A.1 ). These image pairs are the only paired
supervision in our framework.
Model and objective.
Let E be a frozen DINO encoder (DINOv3-L/16  ( Siméoni et al., 2025 ) ) that
maps an image to a dense feature grid. For each coarse modality m , the
 DINO-R2C model learns a mapping S_{m} from a realistic image to the DINO features of
its coarse counterpart,
 S_{m}(I^{\mathrm{r}})\approx E(I^{\mathrm{c}}_{m}),\qquad S_{m}=H_{m}\circ B, 
 (1) 
where B is a trainable DINOv3-L backbone shared across all four modalities
and H_{m} is one of four modality-specific residual MLP heads. We learn the
mapping in DINO feature space rather than in
RGB because the self-supervised representation captures scene structure and
layout while being relatively insensitive to the large appearance gap between
realistic and coarse images; empirically, training also converges more easily
in DINO space. To supervise the prediction in pixel space as well, a frozen RAE
decoder D   ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) decodes it to a coarse image
 \hat{I}^{\mathrm{c}}_{m}=D(S_{m}(I^{\mathrm{r}})) . The training objective is
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{R2C}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{feat}}\big\|S_{m}(I^{\mathrm{r}})-E(I^{\mathrm{c}}_{m})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{rgb}}\big\|\hat{I}^{\mathrm{c}}_{m}-I^{\mathrm{c}}_{m}\big\|_{1}+\lambda_{\mathrm{LPIPS}}\,\mathrm{LPIPS}\big(\hat{I}^{\mathrm{c}}_{m},I^{\mathrm{c}}_{m}\big), 
 (2) 
a DINO feature reconstruction loss, an RGB reconstruction loss, and the LPIPS
perceptual loss  ( Zhang et al., 2018 ) , with
 \lambda_{\mathrm{feat}}=1.0 , \lambda_{\mathrm{rgb}}=1.1 , and
 \lambda_{\mathrm{LPIPS}}=0.82 .
Camera augmentation.
The DINO-R2C model is trained on image pairs but applied to every frame of a video,
where consecutive frames differ by small viewpoint changes. To close this
image-to-video gap, we apply the same random camera homography (crop,
translation, rotation, shear, and perspective) to a realistic image and its
coarse counterpart during training, so that the learned mapping is stable under
the viewpoint changes seen between frames. Section  4.2 ablates
the augmentation strength, and Appendix  A.3 gives the exact
bounds.
 3.3 Constructing Pseudo-Paired Videos
Applying the DINO-R2C model to every frame of a real video v^{\mathrm{r}} yields a
pseudo-coarse video that remains paired with v^{\mathrm{r}} . Because each
frame is synthesized independently, the pseudo-coarse video carries some
temporal jitter. We use it only as the control video and always use the
temporally coherent real video as the target, which teaches the C2R# model to
tolerate minor jitter in the control video while generating temporally
consistent output.
Decoding to RGB and re-encoding.
The features S_{m}(v^{\mathrm{r}}) predicted by the DINO-R2C model already live in
DINO space and could condition the C2R# model directly. However, they retain
hidden cues of the realistic input that are absent when an actual coarse video
is encoded at inference, a leakage that creates a train/test mismatch
(Section  4.3 ). We therefore decode the prediction to coarse
pixels with the frozen RAE decoder, which discards information that cannot be
expressed as a coarse image, and re-encode those pixels with the same frozen
encoder E used at inference. The training control features are thus
 C_{\mathrm{train}}=E\big(D(S_{m}(v^{\mathrm{r}}))\big), 
 (3) 
computed per frame and on the fly during C2R# training.
 3.4 Coarse-to-Real Video Generation: the C2R# 
Architecture.
The C2R# model builds on a frozen pretrained text-to-video diffusion backbone,
Wan2.2 T2V-A14B  ( Wan, 2025 ) , whose VAE, text encoder, and denoiser all
remain frozen. The denoiser is a mixture of two experts routed by diffusion
timestep, so we train one control adapter per expert, active only when its
expert is. Each control adapter takes the per-frame DINO control features C ,
projects them to an intermediate width, applies residual space-time convolution
blocks with timestep conditioning, compresses time to the VAE latent frame
rate, and maps the result to the denoiser width through a zero-initialized
output head. At our operating resolution the DINO feature grid aligns
one-to-one with the backbone’s spatial token grid, so no learned spatial
upsampling is needed. A zero-initialized fusion adapter then adds the control
features after the frozen patch embedding, so at initialization the model is
identical to the pretrained backbone. Architectural and training details are
given in Appendix  A.5 .
Training objective.
For a target real video v^{\mathrm{r}} with latent
 z_{0}=\mathrm{VAE}(v^{\mathrm{r}}) , we sample a flow timestep \sigma and
Gaussian noise \epsilon , form z_{\sigma}=(1-\sigma)z_{0}+\sigma\epsilon ,
and minimize the rectified-flow velocity loss  ( Liu et al., 2023 ) 
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\big\|G\big(z_{\sigma},\sigma,e_{\mathrm{text}};\,A(C_{\mathrm{train}},\sigma)\big)-(\epsilon-z_{0})\big\|_{2}^{2}, 
 (4) 
where G is the frozen denoiser, e_{\mathrm{text}} is the text embedding,
and A denotes the trainable adapters, the only parameters that are updated.
We use no control dropout: with a frozen backbone, steps without control
provide no gradient to the adapters.
Inference.
The user provides an actual coarse video v^{\mathrm{c}} and a text prompt.
The frozen encoder produces C_{\mathrm{test}}=E(v^{\mathrm{c}}) , which
conditions the same adapters and backbone used in training; generation starts
from Gaussian noise, and the frozen VAE decodes the result. Neither the
 DINO-R2C model nor the RAE decoder is used at inference.
 4 Experiments
We evaluate the DINO-R2C model (Section  4.2 ) and the C2R# model
(Sections  4.3 and  4.4 ). Because real-world coarse
videos have no photorealistic ground truth, the C2R# model is evaluated with
reference-free quality, control-alignment, and VLM-judged realism metrics on
three evaluation sets of 100 actual coarse videos each: game renders,
geometry blockouts, and stylized animations. We report aggregate results
across all 300 videos. Dataset construction and metric definitions are given
in Appendices  B.1 and  B.3 .
 4.1 Training data.
We train a shared four-modality DINO-R2C model on 14,000 image pairs per modality:
coarser geometry, coarse geometry, coarser anime, and coarse anime.
GPT-Image 2  ( OpenAI, 2026 ) generates the realistic images and edits
them into coarse geometry and both anime modalities; coarser geometry targets
are rendered from reconstructed and simplified 3D scenes.
Images are generated at 1280\times 720 using the procedure in
Appendix  A.1 ; larger training sets yielded only marginal improvements.
For C2R# training, the DINO-R2C model synthesizes pseudo-coarse controls on
the fly for 389,854 real videos from DL3DV  ( Ling et al., 2024 ) ,
 SEKAI_HQ , and SEKAI_DRONE   ( Li et al., 2026b ) .
The adapted VACE 2.2  ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) and Editto
Sim2Real  ( Bai et al., 2025 ) baselines use exactly the same real videos and
pseudo-coarse controls (Appendices  A.4 and  B.2 ).
 4.2 DINO-R2C 
Protocol.
We evaluate each DINO-R2C model on 1,000 held-out images and 1,000 real
videos. Because each coarse modality has a different target distribution,
values are not comparable across modalities. All directly comparable ablations
target the Coarse geometry modality and vary the camera-augmentation strength
(none, 0.5\times , 1\times , 2\times ) and the inclusion of LPIPS
(Table  1 ; image ablations are in
Appendix  B.5 , Table  3 ).
Image metrics compare predictions with the paired coarse target. Video
metrics assess the stability of the pseudo-coarse video produced by applying the
model to each frame of a real video.
Video stability metrics.
We estimate motion with RAFT-Small  ( Teed and Deng, 2020 ) .
Perceptual flicker measures motion-aligned appearance changes using
LPIPS  ( Zhang et al., 2018 ) ;
smoothed-frame flicker measures broad color and shape changes using L1 on
blurred frames. Both are divided by the corresponding source-video error,
summarized by the median within each video, and averaged across videos.
Motion error measures disagreement between estimated source and output motion
vectors, while motion magnitude ratio indicates whether the output moves too
little or too much. Boundary stability measures the overlap of motion-aligned
outlines, and content consistency measures similarity between consecutive
DINOv3 frame features  ( Siméoni et al., 2025 ) .

In Table  1 , the selected recipe achieves the
lowest smoothed-frame flicker and motion error, highest boundary

It ranks second in perceptual flicker.
Its motion magnitude ratio of 1.0797 indicates approximately
 8\% higher estimated motion magnitude than the source;
combined with the lowest motion error, this supports stronger

With LPIPS enabled, increasing augmentation from

 4.3 C2R# Ablations and Efficiency
Effect of the RGB bottleneck.
Without RAE decoding, C2R# model is trained directly on DINO-R2C model features
predicted from real videos. These features may carry hidden realistic
information that is unavailable when actual coarse videos are encoded at
inference, creating a training–inference distribution shift. Decoding through
the RAE acts as an information bottleneck that removes such hidden cues before
the coarse pixels are re-encoded by DINO. Figure  4 
shows the failure without this bottleneck.
This illustrative variant is excluded from quantitative comparisons.

Results.
 C2R# leads DOVER technical and aesthetic quality and all three VEFX
dimensions, ranking second in background consistency and VLM-judged realism. Relative to
the model from the original C2R paper, the C2R# model improves nine of the ten
reported metrics, including spatial correspondence, local-flow
agreement, and camera-pose error. The model from the original C2R paper retains
the higher realism mean (75.50 versus 74.17).
The C2R# model exceeds every external baseline on this realism score,
with Editto Global the closest at 66.77.
The results also show a quality–control tradeoff. The adapted
Editto model achieves the lowest DINO displacement, while default
VACE 2.2 leads local-flow agreement and camera-pose accuracy.
Thus, the quality gains of the C2R# model do not imply the strongest
preservation under every control metric. Additional plausible detail and secondary
motion may contribute to source–output disagreement.

Transfer of pseudo-paired training data.
Adapting Editto Sim2Real with our generated pairs improves all
ten reported aggregate metrics, including realism from 52.62
to 58.52. The effect on VACE 2.2 is mixed: adaptation improves
DINO spatial correspondence and VEFX edit exclusivity, while
reducing the remaining reported scores. These results support
the utility of pseudo-paired supervision beyond the C2R# model,
while showing that its benefit depends on the recipient model
and adaptation configuration.

 5 Limitations and Discussion
Our method relies on pseudo-coarse videos generated by DINO-R2C model to approximate
the coarse inputs encountered at inference. Performance may therefore degrade
when the gap between pseudo-coarse and actual coarse videos becomes large.
Evaluation on actual coarse videos is also challenging because paired realistic
ground truth is unavailable, requiring reference-free metrics and human
judgment.
Our current implementation is constrained by the quality of the
non-commercial backbone model and training data available. The frozen video prior also introduces a trade-off between realism and exact
control alignment. When the input contains abrupt pose or motion changes,
 C2R# model may adjust the motion pace or synthesize intermediate states rather
than following the discontinuity exactly. This can produce a more natural and
