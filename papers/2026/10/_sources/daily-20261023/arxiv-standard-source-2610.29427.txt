Abstract
Recent advances in multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL) have substantially improved the performance of LLM agents on complex, long-horizon interactive tasks.
However, despite progress in fine-grained credit assignment, policy optimization
still applies these learning signals through response-specific probability
ratios.
Different textual responses can induce the same executable effect, yet credit
differences arising from subsequent outcomes remain tied to their individual
textual realizations.
We identify this mismatch as credit–update misalignment .
Our analysis reveals that credit–text pairing directly shapes policy-update
directions, even when the policy context and executable effect are held fixed.
Motivated by this finding, we propose E ffect- M arginal
P olicy O ptimization (EMPO), which aligns policy optimization
with execution-level equivalence through an effect-level surrogate that couples
updates across alternative textual realizations.
EMPO preserves the original credit signals, integrates with both trajectory-
level and step-level credit estimators, and requires no additional critics or
rollouts.
Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban show that EMPO outperforms strong baselines in both task performance and learning efficiency.

1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as interactive agents for
embodied decision making, computer use, tool use, and coding.
Unlike single-turn reasoning, these tasks require agents to repeatedly perceive,
reason, and act over extended interaction horizons, often under partial observability,
sparse feedback, and delayed rewards.
Reinforcement learning (RL), which directly improves policies through environment
feedback, has therefore become an important paradigm for post-training long-horizon
LLM agents.
Credit assignment remains a central challenge in long-horizon agent RL, where
delayed outcomes must supervise many intermediate decisions.
PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) typically relies on a learned value function for
advantage estimation, while group-based methods such as
GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) replace the critic with relative comparisons among
sampled trajectories.
Recent work further improves the granularity and reliability of credit through
local state comparisons, hierarchical or graph-structured attribution, and
action-level calibration
( Feng et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2026 ; Cheng et al., 2026 ) .
Despite their different constructions, these methods share the goal of providing
more informative credit for individual decisions along a trajectory.
Yet credit estimation is only one part of policy learning, as the resulting signal
must still be translated into a policy update.
An LLM agent generates a textual response containing reasoning and an eventual
action, while an execution interface maps that response to the command that
actually affects the environment.
The mapping from textual responses to executable effects is generally many-to-one.
Responses that differ in reasoning, wording, or length may still induce the same
immediate executable effect.
Standard PPO-style objectives nevertheless retain realization-specific probability
ratios, so credit differences among execution-equivalent responses are translated
into distinct response-level probability updates.
As illustrated in Figure 1 , textual variation can
therefore introduce substantial update variation even when the resulting
environment-level decision is unchanged.
We refer to this mismatch between execution-level equivalence and
realization-specific credit–gradient pairing as
credit–update misalignment .
Credit assignment determines the learning signal associated with a sampled decision,
whereas policy optimization determines how that signal is coupled to changes in
the model distribution.
We find that conventional response-wise optimization contains a within-effect covariance term coupling supplied credit with realization-specific response scores.
The covariance term captures the sample-wise credit–gradient pairing retained by
independent response-level optimization, revealing an update-level source of
variation that is distinct from the quality of the underlying credit estimator.
Figure 1: Execution-equivalent responses may receive different credit and undergo divergent response-wise policy updates despite inducing the same immediate effect. Such within-effect variation can propagate into inconsistent ratio changes and unstable policy updates over training.
Based on this view, we propose
E ffect- M arginal
P olicy O ptimization (EMPO) , which reorganizes policy
updates around executable effects rather than individual textual realizations.
EMPO identifies locally comparable responses that induce the same canonical effect
and shares their length-normalized policy ratios during optimization while retaining
the original advantage assigned to each response.
This factorizes effect-conditioned policy change from realization-specific
credit pairing without modifying the underlying credit estimator.
EMPO operates entirely on existing rollout data and introduces no additional critic,
auxiliary model, reward model, or environment interaction, making it applicable to
both trajectory-level and fine-grained credit estimators.
We evaluate EMPO on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ; Yao et al., 2022 ; Schrader, 2018 ) with both GRPO and GiGPO,
covering trajectory-level and step-level credit estimation.
Across environments and model scales, EMPO improves the overall task performance
of the corresponding base algorithms.
Controlled analyses further connect the optimization objective to its intended
mechanism.
Effect-ratio sharing changes within-cell gradient coupling and leads to measurable
differences in response-probability updates after a controlled policy step, while
introducing only small additional actor-side computation.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
(1) We identify credit–update misalignment , a policy-optimization
problem arising when execution-equivalent textual responses retain realization-specific credit–gradient pairing, and characterize it
through an exact decomposition of the response-wise update.
(2) We propose E ffect- M arginal
P olicy O ptimization (EMPO) , which shares
policy ratios across locally comparable, execution-equivalent responses while
preserving the original advantages and requiring no additional rollouts, critics,
or auxiliary models.
(3) We validate EMPO across three long-horizon agent environments, multiple model
scales, and both trajectory-level and step-level credit estimators, with controlled
analyses connecting the proposed objective to gradient coupling and realized policy
updates.
2 Related Work
Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents.
Reinforcement learning has become a central post-training paradigm for improving
the reasoning and decision-making capabilities of large language models.
PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) remains a foundation, while critic-free and group-relative methods such as
RLOO ( Ahmadian et al., 2024 ) , GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) ,
REINFORCE++ ( Hu, 2026 ) , and
DAPO ( Yu et al., 2025 ) improve the scalability and stability of LLM reinforcement
learning.
More recently, GSPO ( Zheng et al., 2025 ) moves importance weighting and clipping
from the token level to the sequence level.
Building on these advances, RL has increasingly been extended from single-turn
reasoning to agents that interact repeatedly with external environments.
RAGEN ( Wang et al., 2025 ) studies multi-turn RL for reasoning agents in interactive
environments, while Search-R1 ( Jin et al., 2025 ) ,
WebAgent-R1 ( Wei et al., 2025b ) , and ToolRL ( Qian et al., 2025 )
apply RL to search, web interaction, and tool use, respectively.
Related efforts extend RL to software-engineering tasks
( Wei et al., 2025a ) and develop general interfaces for training heterogeneous
agent workflows ( Luo et al., 2025 ) .
As agentic training moves toward longer interaction horizons, recent methods also
address challenges specific to multi-turn optimization, including adaptive
exploration ( Wang et al., 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ) and training stability
( Li et al., 2026b ) .
These studies establish RL as a practical framework for improving LLM agents through interaction tasks.
Credit Assignment in Long-Horizon Decision Making.
Credit assignment is a longstanding challenge in reinforcement learning, where
delayed outcomes must be attributed to the intermediate decisions that produced
them.
Classical approaches reduce this difficulty through temporal value estimation,
such as generalized advantage estimation ( Schulman et al., 2018 ) , reward shaping,
or explicit redistribution of delayed returns
( Arjona-Medina et al., 2019 ) .
For LLM agents, one line of work provides denser intermediate supervision through
process-level reward or verification
( Choudhury, 2025 ; Ma et al., 2026 ) .
Another line extracts finer-grained credit directly from sampled interaction
trajectories without requiring an additional critic.
Turn-level advantage estimation assigns localized credit across multi-turn tool
interactions ( Wei et al., 2026 ) , while
GiGPO ( Feng et al., 2025 ) constructs step-level comparison groups from recurring
environment states.
HGPO ( He et al., 2026 ) further accounts for historical-context
consistency when constructing these comparisons.
Other approaches exploit richer rollout structures.
SALT ( Li et al., 2026a ) derives step-level advantages from trajectory graphs,
Tree-GRPO ( Ding and Ye, 2026 ) obtains local supervision from tree-structured
rollouts, and GraphGPO ( Cheng et al., 2026 ) estimates progress using a shared
state-transition graph.
Recent work further studies the reliability and granularity of these estimates.
ECPO ( Li et al., 2026c ) aggregates returns over canonical actions and calibrates
low-support step-level credit, while GACA ( Liang et al., 2026 ) adaptively balances
trajectory- and step-level advantages according to policy uncertainty.
These methods primarily improve what credit is assigned, or how it is estimated,
densified, and calibrated. Our work studies a complementary problem at the
policy-update stage. EMPO preserves the underlying credit signal and instead
changes how it is translated into policy updates when multiple textual responses
share the same executable effect.
3 Preliminaries
Long-Horizon LLM Agent RL.
We consider an LLM agent interacting with an environment over T T steps to complete
a task specified by an instruction x x .
At interaction step t t , the environment provides an observation o t o_{t} , which may
only partially reveal its underlying state.
The agent constructs a decision context c t c_{t} from the task instruction and the
available interaction history, and generates a textual response
y t ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ c t ) y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t}) .
An execution interface maps the generated response to an environment action a t a_{t} ,
after which the environment transitions and returns a reward r t r_{t} together with
the next observation o t + 1 o_{t+1} .
A complete episode produces an interaction trajectory
τ = { ( c t , y t , a t , r t ) } t = 1 T \tau=\{(c_{t},y_{t},a_{t},r_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T} .
Depending on the observability of the environment, this interaction can be viewed
as an MDP or POMDP; our formulation does not require the agent to access the
underlying environment state.
Group-Based Policy Optimization.
For each task, group-based RL methods sample multiple interaction trajectories and estimate relative advantages from the resulting rollout group.
Let A ^ i , t \hat{A}_{i,t} denote the advantage assigned to step t t of trajectory τ i \tau_{i} by an arbitrary advantage estimator.
The estimated advantages are then used to optimize the LLM policy.
Our work focuses on how these credit signals are translated into policy updates, while leaving the rollout procedure, reward function, and underlying advantage estimator unchanged.
Figure 2:
Overview of Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization (EMPO).
EMPO groups locally comparable responses with the same canonical execution effect
and aggregates their response-level policy ratios into a shared effect ratio.
The shared ratio is combined with the original advantages to coordinate policy
updates across execution-equivalent responses.
4 Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization
We introduce Effect-Marginal Policy Optimization (EMPO), illustrated in
Figure 2 .
EMPO operates at the policy-update stage, organizing probability changes across
execution-equivalent responses while preserving the credit estimates supplied by base algorithm.
We first characterize credit–update misalignment in conventional response-wise
optimization, then derive a policy direction associated with an executable effect,
and finally instantiate this direction in a PPO-style policy objective.
4.1 Credit–Update Misalignment
At each interaction step, an LLM policy generates a textual response
y ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ c ) y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c) under decision context c c .
A fixed execution interface maps the response to an executed effect
e = ψ ⁡ ( c , y ) , e=\psi(c,y),
(1)
such as a parsed environment action or a structured tool call.
Responses with different reasoning traces, wording, or generation lengths may
therefore induce the same executable effect.
We consider a local group K K containing responses with comparable decision
contexts and the same executed effect.
Let n = | K | n=|K| and let A ^ i \hat{A}_{i} denote the advantage assigned to response i i by
the base algorithm.
As policy changes are compared and aggregated across textual realizations with
potentially different generation lengths, raw sequence-level changes introduce a
length-dependent scale.
We therefore characterize each response on a per-token scale.

4.2 Effect-Conditioned Policy Direction
We next characterize the policy direction associated with an executable effect.
For a fixed decision context c c , the response policy induces a distribution over
executed effects:
π θ E ( e ∣ c ) = ∑ y : ψ ⁡ ( c , y ) = e π θ ( y ∣ c ) . \pi_{\theta}^{E}(e\mid c)=\sum_{y:\psi(c,y)=e}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid c).
(5)

∇ θ log π θ E ( e ∣ c ) = 𝔼 Y ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ c , e ) [ ∇ θ log π θ ( Y ∣ c ) ] . \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{E}(e\mid c)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c,e)}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(Y\mid c)\right].

The policy direction of an executable effect is therefore determined jointly by
its alternative textual realizations.
Guided by this identity, we use the mean policy direction
s ¯ K \bar{s}_{K} for locally comparable responses sharing the same effect.
The corresponding factorized local update is

(7)
Compared with Eq. 3 , this form replaces sample-matched
credit–direction products with cross-response combinations within the same
context–effect group.
EMPO implements this factorization through the policy ratio while retaining the
advantages supplied by the base algorithm.
This confines the intervention to the policy-update stage and allows the underlying
credit estimator to remain unchanged.

5 Experiments
We first evaluate the task performance and learning efficiency of EMPO
on long-horizon agent benchmarks (Section 5.2 ).
We then ablate the three operations underlying the effect-marginal
update (Section 5.3 ).
Finally, we examine the resulting policy-update structure
(Section 5.4 ) and characterize the effect cells available
for ratio sharing throughout training
(Section 5.5 ).
5.1 Experimental Setup
Tasks.
We evaluate EMPO on three long-horizon agent benchmarks:
ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) , WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) , and
Sokoban ( Schrader, 2018 ) .
ALFWorld evaluates multi-step decision making in household environments and
contains 3,827 task instances across six categories:
Pick & Place, Examine in Light, Clean & Place, Heat & Place,
Cool & Place, and Pick Two & Place.
WebShop requires agents to search, navigate, and purchase products in a
simulated shopping website containing over 1.1 million products and 12,000
user instructions.
Sokoban evaluates sequential visual planning through box-pushing puzzles.
We report success rate (SR) on all three benchmarks and additionally report
the average task score on WebShop to capture partial task completion.
Baselines.
We compare against three groups of baselines:
(1) closed-source LLMs, including GPT-4o ( Hurst et al., 2024 ) and
Gemini-2.5-Pro ( Comanici et al., 2025 ) ;
(2) prompting-based agents, including direct prompting with Qwen2.5-Instruct,
ReAct ( Yao et al., 2023 ) , and Reflexion ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) ;
and (3) reinforcement learning methods, including
PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) ,
RLOO ( Ahmadian et al., 2024 ) ,
GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , and
GiGPO ( Feng et al., 2025 ) .
We instantiate EMPO on GRPO and GiGPO, two representative RL algorithms
with trajectory-level and step-level advantage estimation, yielding EMPO-G and EMPO-GiG.
Training details.
We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
( Qwen et al., 2025 ) for ALFWorld and WebShop, and
Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct ( Bai et al., 2025 ) for Sokoban.
For each base algorithm and its EMPO counterpart, we use the same model
initialization, rollout configuration, training budget, and hyperparameter
settings.
The rollout group size is set to 8 for all group-based RL experiments. We train and evaluate each main setting with three random seeds and report mean ± \pm standard deviation.
Full training details are provided in
Appendix D .

5.2 Task Performance and Learning Efficiency
Table 1 presents the main results on ALFWorld and WebShop.
EMPO consistently improves the aggregate performance of both GRPO and
GiGPO across model scales, covering trajectory-level and step-level
advantage estimation.
With Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, EMPO-G improves the overall ALFWorld
success rate from 69.5% to 83.3% and the WebShop success rate from
62.8% to 70.3%.
EMPO-GiG further improves the corresponding GiGPO results from 90.1%
to 94.0% on ALFWorld and from 73.8% to 77.3% on WebShop.
The gains persist with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, where both EMPO variants achieve stronger overall performance on ALFWorld and WebShop.
We additionally evaluate EMPO on Sokoban with
Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct.
As shown in Table 3 , EMPO-G improves the success rate
from 72.7% to 84.4%, while EMPO-GiG improves it from 75.0% to
81.3%.
Together, the results across ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban demonstrate
consistent gains across text-based interaction and visual planning,
different model scales, and different advantage-estimation granularities.
Beyond final task performance, EMPO also improves learning efficiency.
Table 3 reports the training steps required
to first reach a 60% success rate ( TTT 60 \mathrm{TTT}_{60} ).
Across all eight evaluated model–algorithm–environment settings,
EMPO reduces TTT 60 \mathrm{TTT}_{60} , with the largest reduction reaching
36.5%.
The corresponding learning curves are provided in
Appendix E.4 .
5.3 Ablation Study
We ablate the three operations underlying EMPO, including length
normalization, effect-based grouping, and ratio sharing.
As shown in Table 4 , all three components
contribute to the effectiveness of the effect-marginal update.
Removing length normalization makes response-level ratios increasingly
sensitive to generation length, allowing accumulated token-level
probability changes in longer responses to exert a larger influence on
the shared ratio.
Disabling ratio sharing preserves the normalized response ratio but
restores realization-specific policy directions, weakening coordinated
updates among execution-equivalent responses.
Replacing effect-based cells with arbitrary groups couples responses
without preserving executable-effect consistency, introducing
interference between policy updates associated with different effects.
These results show that length normalization calibrates policy
changes across responses, effect-based grouping determines an
appropriate coupling structure, and ratio sharing realizes coordinated
policy updates within each effect cell.

6 Conclusion
We presented E ffect- M arginal
P olicy O ptimization (EMPO) for long-horizon LLM agent reinforcement learning. We identified credit–update misalignment, where execution-equivalent textual responses remain associated with realization-specific policy updates, and characterized its effect through an exact decomposition of the response-wise update.
EMPO addresses this mismatch by sharing length-normalized policy ratios
among locally comparable responses with the same executable effect while
preserving the advantages provided by the base algorithm.
Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban show that EMPO consistently
improves both task performance and learning efficiency across different
model scales and advantage estimators.
Further analysis shows that effect-level ratio sharing induces more
structured within-cell gradient coupling and more coordinated policy
updates among execution-equivalent responses.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of effect-marginal policy
optimization for long-horizon LLM agent training.

Appendix B Limitations
EMPO relies on identifying comparable interaction contexts and canonical executable effects. These are
readily available in the structured-action environments considered here, but may require more careful
construction for less structured tool interfaces. Because effect cells are formed from sampled
responses, their coverage also varies across tasks and training stages, responses outside eligible cells
retain the base objective.
Extending effect-cell construction and evaluation to a wider range of agent interfaces is a natural
direction for future work.

Appendix D Experimental Details
This section provides additional details on the baselines,
environments, and training configurations used in our experiments.
D.1 Baselines
ReAct.
ReAct ( Yao et al., 2023 ) interleaves natural-language reasoning
and environment actions, conditioning each decision on the
observations returned by previous interactions.
Reflexion.
Reflexion ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) augments subsequent attempts
with natural-language reflections generated from unsuccessful
trajectories.
It provides an inference-time self-improvement baseline without
parameter updates.
PPO.
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
( Schulman et al., 2017 )
optimizes a clipped policy-gradient objective and uses a learned
value function for advantage estimation.
We include PPO as an actor–critic baseline.
RLOO.
REINFORCE Leave-One-Out (RLOO)
( Ahmadian et al., 2024 ) replaces the learned critic with a
leave-one-out Monte Carlo baseline.
For K K trajectories with returns { R i } i = 1 K \{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K} ,
A ^ i RLOO = R i − 1 K − 1 ​ ∑ j ≠ i R j . \hat{A}_{i}^{\mathrm{RLOO}}=R_{i}-\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}R_{j}.
GRPO.
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
( Shao et al., 2024 )
normalizes returns among trajectories sampled for the same task.
For a trajectory group 𝒢 \mathcal{G} ,
A ^ i GRPO = R i − μ 𝒢 σ 𝒢 + ε , \hat{A}_{i}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}=\frac{R_{i}-\mu_{\mathcal{G}}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}+\varepsilon},
where μ 𝒢 \mu_{\mathcal{G}} and σ 𝒢 \sigma_{\mathcal{G}} are the group
mean and standard deviation.
The resulting advantages are optimized with a PPO-style clipped
objective without a learned critic.
GiGPO.
GiGPO ( Feng et al., 2025 ) augments the trajectory-level
group-relative advantage with a step-level relative advantage
computed among interactions sharing comparable decision contexts:
A ^ i , t GiGPO = A ^ i traj + ω ​ A ^ i , t step . \hat{A}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{GiGPO}}=\hat{A}_{i}^{\mathrm{traj}}+\omega\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{step}}.
We follow the original implementation with ω = 1 \omega=1 and
discount factor γ = 0.95 \gamma=0.95 .
D.2 Environments
ALFWorld.
ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) is a text-based embodied environment
derived from ALFRED.
Each episode specifies a household task that must be completed
through a sequence of textual actions.
The benchmark contains six task categories: Pick & Place,
Examine in Light, Clean & Place, Heat & Place, Cool & Place,
and Pick Two & Place.
WebShop.
WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) evaluates web-based decision making
in a simulated shopping environment.
The agent searches and navigates product pages, selects product
options, and completes a purchase according to a natural-language
instruction.
The environment contains more than 1.1 million products and
12,000 user instructions.
Sokoban.
We use procedurally generated Sokoban instances to evaluate EMPO
in a visual planning setting.
The agent observes an RGB rendering of a 6 × 6 6\times 6 board with
one box and outputs movement actions to push the box onto the
target location.
Boards are generated with a room-generation search depth of 30,
and each episode is limited to 15 interactions.
D.3 Training Metrics
Policy-gradient loss.
We report the clipped surrogate loss used to optimize the actor.
Its value is determined jointly by the estimated advantages, policy
ratios, and clipping behavior, and therefore reflects the optimization
signal encountered by the policy during training.
KL divergence.
We track the token-level KL divergence between the current policy and
the fixed reference policy.
This quantity measures the accumulated deviation of the learned policy
from its initialization over the course of training.
Policy-ratio clip fraction.
The clip fraction measures the proportion of policy-update terms for
which the likelihood ratio falls outside the PPO clipping interval.
It characterizes how frequently proposed probability changes are
constrained by the clipped surrogate objective.
For EMPO, this statistic is computed using the effective ratios defined
in Eq. 14 .
Training reward and success rate.
We report the mean episodic reward and the fraction of successful
episodes in each rollout batch to track on-policy task progress during
training.
The main experimental results are evaluated separately on the
corresponding validation sets.
Token entropy.
Token entropy is computed from the next-token distribution along
sampled response prefixes and averaged over valid response tokens.
It measures the diversity of the model’s token-level policy throughout
training.
Gradient norm.
We record the norm of the actor gradient before gradient clipping.
This statistic characterizes the scale of the proposed parameter update
and is used to monitor optimization dynamics throughout training.
D.4 Training Details
We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for
ALFWorld and WebShop, and Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct for Sokoban.
All models are optimized with AdamW using an actor learning rate
of 10 − 6 10^{-6} and weight decay of 0.01 0.01 .
The critic learning rate for PPO is 10 − 5 10^{-5} .
We use a PPO clipping parameter of 0.2 0.2 , dual clipping of 3.0 3.0 ,
a KL-loss coefficient of 0.01 0.01 , an entropy coefficient of
0.001 0.001 , and gradient clipping at 1.0 1.0 .
Rollouts are sampled with temperature 1.0 1.0 , while evaluation
uses temperature 0.4 0.4 .
We set top- p p to 1.0 1.0 and disable top- k k filtering.
The rollout group size is eight for all group-based methods.
Unless otherwise stated, models are trained for 150 iterations
and evaluated every five iterations using 128 episodes.
The maximum response length is 512 tokens in all environments.

E.4 Policy Adaptation and Response Diversity
We further examine how effect-marginal optimization shapes policy
adaptation throughout training.
Figures 6
and 7 report token entropy,
KL divergence to the reference policy, and gradient norm for
GRPO, GiGPO, and their EMPO variants.
Across both ALFWorld and WebShop, EMPO maintains higher token entropy
during the later stages of training while exhibiting continued policy
movement, as reflected by the KL divergence and gradient norm.
The three quantities together reveal a characteristic optimization
pattern: the policy continues to adapt while retaining a less
concentrated token distribution.
This pattern appears consistently for both trajectory-level GRPO and
step-level GiGPO, indicating that it is preserved across different
advantage-estimation granularities.
This training behavior connects naturally to the effect-marginal
update in Section 4 .
In response-wise optimization, each credit signal is paired with its
realization-specific policy direction, allowing within-cell credit
variation to directly shape the probability of individual textual
realizations.
EMPO couples execution-equivalent responses through a shared
effect-level ratio, so their policy changes are organized around a
common effect-conditioned direction.
The resulting entropy dynamics provide a distribution-level view of
the coordinated updates observed in the policy-update analysis in
Section 5.4 .
