Abstract
Diffusion-based video generation models (VGMs) can produce visually plausible videos, yet their camera ego-motion often drifts over long horizons. Camera ego-motion determines how the viewpoint changes between frames and is essential for maintaining consistent geometry and trajectories.
To address this problem, we propose CausalAlign , a causal alignment framework with two components.
First, we diagnose how precise ego motion is represented, used, and retained within VGMs using linear probes and causal interventions, revealing a rise-and-fall pattern during denoising.
Second, we strengthen ego-motion state in VGM by aligning the representations at the identified layers to a 3D foundation model through delta-token space. This space encodes the changes between consecutive frames, allowing the alignment to focus on ego-motion dynamics rather than static scene content.
With Wan2.2 fine-tuned on DL3DV and RealEstate10K, CausalAlign can largely improve motion accuracy, geometric consistency, and temporal smoothness, while preserving visual quality. For example, it improves geometric consistency by more than 20% relative to the base model.
Moreover, with the fine-tuned VGM, CausalAlign improves the success rate of world action models (WAMs) by 10 points on mobile manipulation tasks of RoboCasa365 benchmark.
Overall, CausalAlign improves ego-motion consistency in diffusion-based VGMs and yields noticeable gains in embodied models.

 1 Introduction
Recent advances in diffusion-based video generation models (VGMs) have led to remarkable models such as Wan2.2  ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) and Cosmos3  ( NVIDIA Cosmos Team, 2026 ) .
Trained on Internet-scale collections of 2D videos, these models can synthesize visually compelling content  ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026 ) .
However, trained to model 2D appearance and local motion statistics, standard VGMs struggle to capture 3D camera trajectories and to control viewpoint changes consistently across frames  ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Kupyn et al., 2025 ) . We refer to these cross-frame viewpoint changes as camera ego-motion.
As shown in Figure   1 (left panel), prior work has attempted to address this challenge by introducing explicit 3D priors or preference alignment  ( Wang et al., 2024b ; Ren et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Bahmani et al., 2025 ) , yet generating videos with geometrically consistent camera trajectories remains an open problem.
At the same time, recent work shows that VGMs can implicitly estimate camera poses from their internal representations, even when their generated trajectories are imperfect  ( Bahmani et al., 2025 ) . This raises a more fundamental question: how is precise ego-motion represented, used, and retained within VGMs? 
This question becomes increasingly important when VGMs are integrated into world action models (WAMs) for robotic mobile manipulation: visually plausible yet geometrically inconsistent predictions of the future can induce systematic errors in downstream action prediction.

As the first step, we perform analysis for understanding the ego-motion information within VGMs.
Inspired by prior works  ( Alain and Bengio, 2016 ; Meng et al., 2022 ) , we adopt linear probing to measure the internal representations of VGMs and causal intervention to reveal the mechanisms of VGMs.
Specifically, for linear probing, we define “decodability” as the amount of ego-motion information. Meanwhile, for causal intervention, we define “causal effect” as the extent to which the interventions on VGM can change the generation.
Our analysis results demonstrate that both measurements exhibit the same trend, that is, increasing first and then declining, across both denoising timesteps and denoising Transformer layers.
These results suggest that ego-motion state emerges as the denoising progresses, however, fades away at the late timesteps and the last few Transformer layers at each step.
Therefore, ego-motion is already learned and represented in VGMs, increasingly used at intermediate stages, yet not well retained at later stages. We refer to this progressive pattern as “geometric state forgetting” .
Motivated by this diagnosis, our goal is to preserve and strengthen the ego-motion state within VGMs without compromising the quality of visual generation.
To this end, we propose CausalAlign , a framework with two components: causal diagnosis and delta-token alignment, as shown in Figure  1 (middle panel).
First, causal diagnosis leverages linear probing and causal intervention to identify where ego-motion is represented, used, and forgotten within a VGM.
Second, delta-token alignment supervises the internal ego-motion state at the positions identified as forgetting.
There are two key challenges in our alignment: how to represent ego-motion with the hidden states in VGMs , and how to provide meaningful geometry supervision for such representations .
For the first challenge, we adopt the idea of delta tokens, which was initially designed for improving the efficiency of the world model  ( Kerssies et al., 2026 ) . We adapt this idea to measure the ego-motion via the difference between two consecutive frames. Delta tokens serve as a natural carrier for motion information while reducing redundant static scene content.
For the second challenge, we construct the delta-token space by leveraging the pretrained 3D foundation model. These 3D models are capable of estimating camera poses, depth, and 3D structure, and thus can provide high-quality geometric supervision. A learnable projector then maps consecutive hidden-state transitions into this teacher delta-token space, explicitly supervising ego-motion state of the VGM.
By aligning these projected tokens with the corresponding teacher delta tokens at the identified layers, CausalAlign transfers geometric knowledge to the locations where ego-motion information starts to fade.
With our CausalAlign method, we fine-tune the VGM on the widely-adopted datasets, DL3DV  ( Ling et al., 2024 ) and RealEstate10K  ( Zhou et al., 2018 ) .
To evaluate the effects of CausalAlign on VGMs, we establish a comprehensive evaluation protocol with three aspects: visual quality, ego-motion consistency, and geometric state retention.
Visual quality is measured with VBench-I2V  ( Huang et al., 2026 ) and complementary metrics.
Ego-motion consistency is evaluated through motion magnitude, geometric consistency, and temporal dynamics, while geometric-state retention is assessed through probe decodability and causal intervention effects.
CausalAlign preserves visual quality while improving camera ego-motion consistency, as shown in Figure  1 (right panel).
For example, it reduces reprojection error by 22.6% and improves trajectory smoothness by 23.9% relative to the base model.
It also alters the decodability and causal-effect patterns from rise-and-fall to rise-and-maintain , supporting the improved retention of ego-motion state.
Moreover, we observe that the improved ego-motion state can facilitate downstream applications, such as WAMs for embodied tasks. Specifically, our fine-tuned VGM raises success rate on RoboCasa365  ( Nasiriany et al., 2026 ) from 43.5% to 54.2% . These results validate our method with causal diagnosis and delta-token alignment.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
(1) We identify a pattern called “geometric state forgetting” which reveals the ego-motion state in diffusion-based VGMs. Through linear probing and causal intervention, our analysis reveals that ego-motion information is progressively represented and used during the denoising process, yet not retained at the late stages of denoising.
(2) We propose CausalAlign , a causal alignment method that aims to preserve ego-motion state at the VGM layers identified by our causal analysis. Our technical innovation lies in adapting the idea of delta tokens to strengthen ego-motion consistency via 3D foundation models.
(3) Extensive experiments and comprehensive metrics validate the benefits of our method. It largely improves ego-motion consistency, better preserves ego-motion state during denoising, and offers remarkable gains for WAM in robotic tasks.
 2 Related Work
 Video diffusion and world action models. 
Video diffusion extends image denoising to joint spatiotemporal generation  ( Ho et al., 2022 ) , while open backbones such as Wan have broadened its use  ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) .
Unlike image generation, VGMs require preserving temporal correspondences and scene geometry across multiple frames  ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Ren et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) .
These priors increasingly serve as action-conditioned simulators or policies: Genie and IRASim learn controllable future dynamics, while VideoVLA, DreamZero, and LingBot-VA couple observations with executable actions; Fast-WAM shows that video co-training can improve action representations without test-time video decoding  ( Bruce et al., 2024 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ; Shen et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026a ; Yuan et al., 2026 ) .
Therefore, ego-motion consistency is internally related to WAM and erroneous camera motion can corrupt the predicted actions.
Rather than proposing another WAM, CausalAlign restores and strengthens the ego-motion state of the VGM to facilitate WAM performance.
Our method elicits the knowledge that has been learned during VGM pretraining, providing an alternative route for stronger WAMs.
Feed-forward 3D foundation models.
Feed-forward 3D reconstruction has progressed from point-map predictors such as DUSt3R and VGGT  ( Wang et al., 2024a ; Wang et al., 2025a ) to streaming
models with temporal state. CUT3R recurrently maintains a metric-scale scene representation  ( Wang et al., 2025b ) , while LingBot-Map organizes streaming context into anchor, pose-reference, and trajectory memories and demonstrates improved trajectory and pose accuracy over VGGT  ( Chen et al., 2026a ) . These models provide camera and scene representations that can serve as supervision for video generation. We thus adopt LingBot-Map as the teacher model and study how its ego-motion state can be transferred to a video generator.
Geometry-aware alignment and post-training.
As summarized in Figure  1 , existing geometry-aware adaptation methods can be categorized into three types.
External 3D cache methods such as GEN3C and Voyager use point cloud or world memories for controllable camera motion, yet require additional geometric memory and may propagate cache errors  ( Ren et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025b ) .
Preference alignment methods such as VideoGPA and Epipolar-DPO supervise generated videos through preference or epipolar feedback, without directly improving the VGM’s internal geometric state  ( Du et al., 2026 ; Kupyn et al., 2025 ) .
Direct alignment methods, including JOG3R, Geometry Forcing, and FantasyWorld, transfer geometric features into the generator but do not explicitly target at effective timestep and layer locations  ( Huang et al., 2025a ; Wu et al., 2026 ; Dai et al., 2026 ) .
Complementary WAM methods leverage 3D, 4D, or action-grounded supervision to improve action representations without requiring auxiliary teachers at deployment  ( Li et al., 2026b ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026 ; Fu et al., 2026 ; Qiu et al., 2026 ) .
CausalAlign instead improves the internal ego-motion state of VGMs through delta-token space from 3D foundation models, at the locations identified by causal diagnosis.
 Figure 2: Three stages of CausalAlign training. CausalAlign transfers ego-motion knowledge from a 3D teacher to the VGM through three-stage training. In Stage 1, consecutive features from a streaming 3D foundation model are encoded into a teacher delta-token space that captures inter-frame geometric transitions. In Stage 2, a projector maps VGM DiT features into the same delta-token space and is frozen after training, using a small subset of the training split. In Stage 3, with full training split, LoRA adapters are optimized only at the identified VGM layers using delta tokens, while the projector remains frozen.
 3 Method
 3.1 Method Overview
CausalAlign combines causal diagnosis and delta-token alignment to preserve ego-motion representations in diffusion-based VGMs.
We first conduct causal diagnosis to identify the layers and the denoising timesteps where ego-motion information fades.
Guided by the causal diagnosis, we align the VGM using delta tokens in three stages. First, we learn delta tokens from a frozen 3D foundation model. Second, we train projectors to map the temporal transitions of VGM features into this space. Finally, we use these tokens to supervise LoRA updates at the identified layers and denoising timesteps, while keeping the original VGM backbone, teacher, and projectors frozen.
In this way, CausalAlign strengthens the geometric knowledge encoded in the VGM without introducing additional computation during video generation.
 3.2 Causal Diagnosis of Ego-Motion States
 Decodability. 
Let \mathbf{h}_{t}^{\ell,s} denote the spatially aggregated VGM state at
frame t , layer \ell , and noise-level index s . A shared operator
 \Gamma constructs the consecutive-frame transition feature
 \mathbf{u}_{t,\ell,s}=\Gamma\!\left(\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\ell,s},\mathbf{h}_{t+1}^{\ell,s}\right). 
A linear probe predicts the relative camera motion
 \widehat{\bm{\xi}}_{t,\ell,s}=\mathbf{W}_{\ell,s}\mathbf{u}_{t,\ell,s}+\mathbf{b}_{\ell,s},\qquad\bm{\xi}_{t}=\operatorname{Log}\!\left(\mathbf{T}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_{t+1}\right), 
 (1) 
where \mathbf{T}_{t}\in\mathrm{SE}(3) is the ground-truth
camera-to-world pose and \operatorname{Log} maps the relative pose to
a six-dimensional motion vector. The held-out score
 D_{\ell,s} measures how well ego-motion is decodable at location
 (\ell,s) ; decodability alone does not establish causal use
  ( Meng et al., 2022 ; Geiger et al., 2021 ) .
 Causal intervention and target selection. 
At c=(\ell,s) , we keep the prompt, initial noise, and random seed fixed
and intervene on the temporal hidden state using
 \mathcal{A}=\{\textsc{Mask},\textsc{Shuffle},\textsc{Replace},\textsc{Inject}\}. 
These interventions remove temporal information, disrupt its order,
replace it with another trajectory, or insert a pose-matched donor,
respectively.
For diagnostic clip d , let
 \mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{ref},d}_{1:T} be its ground-truth
camera-to-world trajectory from the corresponding real video. A frozen
pose estimator produces \widehat{\mathbf{T}}(Y) from a generated video
 Y , and we define
 E_{\mathrm{traj}}(Y;d)=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{traj}}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbf{T}}(Y),\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{ref},d}_{1:T}\right). 
 (2) 
The reference trajectory is used only for evaluation and is not provided
as a generation condition. For intervention a\in\mathcal{A} , the
norm-matched random-control-adjusted effect is
 \overline{\mathcal{C}}_{c}^{(a)}=\eta_{a}\,\mathbb{E}_{d}\left[E_{\mathrm{traj}}\!\left(Y_{c}^{(a)};d\right)-E_{\mathrm{traj}}\!\left(Y_{c}^{(\mathrm{rand},a)};d\right)\right], 
 (3) 
where \eta_{a}=+1 for the disruptive interventions and
 \eta_{a}=-1 for Inject , so that larger values indicate stronger
causal effects. The aggregate causal score is
 C_{\ell,s}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{(\ell,s)}^{(a)}. 
 (4) 
Averaging D_{\ell,s} and C_{\ell,s} over the complementary axis
gives layer profiles ( A=\mathrm{L} ) and timestep profiles
( A=\mathrm{S} ). We combine them as
 J^{A}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathcal{N}(D^{A})+\mathcal{N}(C^{A})\right],\qquad A\in\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{S}\}, 
 (5) 
where \mathcal{N} independently min–max normalizes each profile.
The target sets \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tar}} and
 \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}} are selected at locations with the largest
positive decline rates of J^{A} . The corresponding normalized timestep
declines provide the weights \omega_{\ell,s} used by the alignment
losses and shared across selected layers.
 3.3 Delta-Token Alignment
 Stage 1: learning teacher delta tokens. 
For a T -frame training video \mathbf{x}_{1:T} , the frozen 3D foundation model \Phi_{\mathrm{T}} extracts geometric states \mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}=\Phi_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) . A trainable encoder E_{\Delta} maps states to a delta token:
 \bm{\delta}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}=E_{\Delta}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}},\mathbf{z}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Delta}}, 
 (6) 
where d_{\Delta} is the token dimension; superscripts \mathrm{T} and \mathrm{V} denote teacher and VGM representations. A transition decoder reconstructs the next state from the current state and token, a temporal predictor forecasts the next token, and a camera decoder D_{\mathrm{cam}} predicts relative pose. These auxiliary tasks encourage the token to retain transition information rather than merely compressing the teacher state. Denoting their predictions by \widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}} , \widehat{\bm{\delta}}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}} , and \widehat{\Delta\mathbf{T}}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}} , we train the encoder and auxiliary modules with
 \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{T}}={} 
 \displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\|\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathbf{z}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{temp}}\|\widehat{\bm{\delta}}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}}-\bm{\delta}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}^{2}\right] 
 (7) 
 \displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{pose}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\ell_{\mathrm{pose}}(\widehat{\Delta\mathbf{T}}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}},\Delta\mathbf{T}_{t})\right]. 
Here, \Delta\mathbf{T}_{t}=\mathbf{T}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_{t+1} is the ground-truth relative pose used only for supervision, and \ell_{\mathrm{pose}} combines geodesic rotation distance with normalized SmoothL1 translation error. The expectation averages valid training-frame positions; \lambda_{\mathrm{temp}} and \lambda_{\mathrm{pose}}^{\mathrm{T}} weight temporal and pose supervision. These objectives preserve transition structure and anchor tokens to camera motion. We then freeze the encoder and auxiliary modules; decoder architectures and pose-loss details are provided in the appendix.
 Stage 2: learning VGM-side projectors. 
With the VGM and teacher modules frozen, layer-specific projectors Q_{\ell} map corresponding VGM features to teacher space tokens \bm{\delta}_{t,\ell,s}^{\mathrm{V}}=Q_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}_{t,\ell,s}) . We minimize
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{map}}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tar}}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}}\omega_{\ell,s}\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\|\bm{\delta}_{t,\ell,s}^{\mathrm{V}}-\bm{\delta}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}^{2}\right], 
 (8) 
where \omega_{\ell,s} weights selected timesteps by their normalized positive diagnostic-score decline rates, shared across selected layers. The projectors are frozen after training.
 Stage 3: adapting the VGM. 
We update LoRA adapters  ( Hu et al., 2022 ) at selected layers.
For an adapted transition \widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t,\ell,s} , frozen projector produces
 \widetilde{\bm{\delta}}_{t,\ell,s}^{\mathrm{V}}=Q_{\ell}(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t,\ell,s}) .
We use two complementary objectives:
 \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tar}}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}}\omega_{\ell,s}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\delta}}_{t,\ell,s}^{\mathrm{V}}-\bm{\delta}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right], 
 (9) 
which aligns the adapted VGM transitions with the teacher delta tokens, and
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pose}}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tar}}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}}\omega_{\ell,s}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\ell_{\mathrm{pose}}\!\left(D_{\mathrm{cam}}\!\left(\widetilde{\bm{\delta}}_{t,\ell,s}^{\mathrm{V}}\right),\Delta\mathbf{T}_{t}\right)\right], 
 (10) 
which explicitly supervises the predicted relative camera motion.
The final adaptation objective is
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CausalAlign}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pose}}+\lambda_{\Delta}\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}, 
 (11) 
where \lambda_{\Delta} controls the strength of token alignment.
Gradients pass through the frozen projectors and camera decoder to the LoRA adapters, while all other parameters remain frozen.
The teacher is used only to provide training targets, and no external geometric cache is required during generation.
 3.4 Inference
At inference, CausalAlign follows the same video generation procedure as the original Wan2.2  ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) .
Given an initial frame and a text prompt, the adapted VGM generates a video through the standard denoising and decoding process.
All auxiliary modules used during training, including the 3D foundation model, are no longer needed.
Therefore, CausalAlign improves ego-motion consistency without requiring additional inputs or geometric computation during video generation.
 3.5 Application to World Action Models
Consistent ego-motion is important for mobile manipulation, where robots predict actions while their viewpoints change.
We thus investigate whether improving the ego-motion state of VGMs can also benefit action prediction of world action models.
Specifically, we initialize the video expert of Fast-WAM  ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) with our adapted Wan2.2 model.
For the action expert, we follow Fast-WAM and transform the parameters of the original Wan model to initialize ActionDiT.
We train both experts using the original objectives for flow matching:
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WAM}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{v}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}^{a}, 
 (12) 
where v and a denote the video and action experts, respectively.
The policy receives synchronized images from three camera views and predicts actions starting from the current observation.
Future video is used as a training target, while at inference, the policy predicts executable actions from the observed history without generating future video  ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) .

 4 Experiment
We first conduct causal diagnosis to identify where ego-motion information fades and examine whether CausalAlign improves its retention.
We then fine-tune the VGM with delta-token supervision at these locations and evaluate its ego-motion consistency, visual quality, and the benefits for embodied tasks.
Finally, we conduct ablation studies to validate our design choices.
 4.1 Causal Diagnosis and Target Selection
 Setup. 
We freeze Wan2.2  ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) and diagnose the hidden states across Transformer layers and across denoising steps.
At each location c=(\ell,s) , a linear readout predicts relative camera motion, yielding a decodability score \mathcal{D}_{\ell,s} .
We also apply the causal interventions introduced in Section  3.2 and measure their effects on the generated camera trajectory, yielding a causal effect score \mathcal{C}_{\ell,s} .
Figure  3 summarizes the resulting layer–timestep profiles.
 Diagnosis and target selection. 
Both geometric decodability and causal effect in the original Wan2.2 exhibit a consistent rise-and-fall pattern.
Their increase at intermediate locations indicates that the VGM forms and uses an ego-motion state, while their subsequent decline suggests that this state is not retained during later denoising.
By applying the target selection rule from Section  3.2 , we select layers 14, 10, 12, and 20 in descending order of their layer-wise decline rates.
Layer 14 is the primary target, while layers 10, 12, and 20 are auxiliary targets.
The same rule applies to denoising timesteps s\in\{398,799\} .
Both selected timesteps are used at all selected layers, with weights proportional to their positive temporal decline rates.
 The benefits of our method. After alignment, CausalAlign retains both probe decodability and causal effects at later denoising stages, changing the original rise-and-fall pattern into a rise-and-maintain pattern, as shown by the orange curve in Figure  3 .
The sustained causal effects indicate that ego-motion information remains influential during generation, rather than merely becoming more decodable.
 4.2 Experiments on the Comprehensive Evaluation
 Setup. 
We apply CausalAlign to Wan2.2-14B-I2V  ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) and train it on datasets DL3DV  ( Ling et al., 2024 ) and RealEstate10K  ( Zhou et al., 2018 ) .
We choose these datasets since they provide camera pose annotations and cover diverse scenes and camera movements, making them suitable for supervising and evaluating ego-motion consistency.
For each dataset, 90% of the data is used for training and 10% of the data is for evaluation.
As in Figure  2 , we first learn delta tokens from the geometric features of a frozen 3D foundation model using 30% of the training data.
We then train projectors to map features from the video generator into this delta-token space using 10% of the training data.
Finally, we fine-tune the generator with LoRA at the identified layers and denoising steps using the full training split. The 3D teacher and the projectors are needed only during training.
For evaluation, we use the same video length, resolution, frame rate, sampling settings, and random seeds across methods, and average results over multiple samples for each input.
Further details are provided in Appendix  B.2 .
 Baselines. 
We compare CausalAlign with the base model Wan2.2 and three groups of methods for improving geometric consistency.
 External 3D cache methods , including GEN3C  ( Ren et al., 2025 ) and Voyager  ( Huang et al., 2025b ) , control camera motion using explicit trajectories and 3D caches.
We follow their standard inference procedures by providing the reference camera trajectory and constructing a new 3D cache from the available input frames, without using future frames.
 Preference alignment methods , including VideoGPA  ( Du et al., 2026 ) and Epipolar-DPO  ( Kupyn et al., 2025 ) , improve generated videos through preference or epipolar feedback.
 Direct feature alignment methods , including JOG3R  ( Huang et al., 2025a ) , Geometry Forcing  ( Wu et al., 2026 ) , and FantasyWorld  ( Dai et al., 2026 ) , transfer geometric features into the generator.
For Wan2.2, CausalAlign, and methods without explicit camera control, we provide only the first frame and a text prompt.
 Metrics. 
We use the same frozen estimators  ( Wang et al., 2025a ) to recover camera poses, depth, optical flow, and visual features from every generated video.
Our evaluation covers three complementary aspects.
 Motion Magnitude measures how closely the mean translational and angular speeds match the corresponding means from real validation videos (Appendix  B.1 ).
 Geometric Consistency measures reprojection error  ( Triggs et al., 2000 ) , consistency between depth and optical flow in static regions  ( Luo et al., 2020 ) , and epipolar error  ( Lee and Civera, 2020 ) .
 Temporal Dynamics measures trajectory smoothness and jerk  ( Hogan and Sternad, 2009 ; Flash and Hogan, 1985 ) , capturing continuity of motion and abrupt changes in acceleration.
Since the first frame and a text prompt can admit several valid future camera trajectories, we measure intrinsic consistency rather than error against a single reference trajectory (Appendix  B.2 ).

 Comparison with Baseline Methods. 
As shown in Table  1 , CausalAlign improves all seven metrics over Wan2.2 and achieves the best results on six of seven metrics among the compared methods.
For example, it reduces reprojection error from 8.4 to 6.5 pixels (22.6%), raises trajectory smoothness from 0.71 to 0.88 (23.9%), and lowers jerk from 0.182 to 0.151.
Further, its proximity to real camera speeds improves for both translation (0.68 to 0.79) and rotation (0.70 to 0.89), while epipolar error decreases from 2.9 to 2.1 pixels.
FantasyWorld achieves a slightly lower error for consistency between depth and flow (5.4 versus 5.5 pixels), but CausalAlign leads on the other six metrics without requiring a camera trajectory.
These results suggest that causal alignment through delta tokens strengthens the generator’s ability to produce consistent camera motion and scene geometry.
The examples in Figure  4 further show less trajectory jitter and drift when the camera completes a loop, compared with Wan2.2.

Visual Quality Preservation.
Further, we examine whether stronger geometric supervision harms visual quality. Specifically, we evaluate CausalAlign and Wan2.2 on the same evaluation clips with identical prompts, sampling settings, and random seeds.
We report VBench-I2V quality, I2V score, and smoothness  ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2026 ) , together with FVD, CLIP, SSIM, and LPIPS  ( Unterthiner et al., 2018 ; Radford et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 2004 ; Zhang et al., 2018 ) .
As shown in Table  2 , CausalAlign preserves visual quality comparable to Wan2.2: VBench quality increases slightly from 0.805 to 0.806, I2V score remains 0.958, and VBench smoothness rises from 0.983 to 0.985.
Further, CLIP and SSIM improve slightly, LPIPS remains unchanged, and FVD changes only marginally (182.4 to 182.7).
These results indicate that our method can improve ego-motion consistency without sacrificing visual fidelity.

Application to World Action Models.
Given that CausalAlign improves the retention of ego-motion information in VGMs, we wonder whether this benefit can transfer to the embodied models which are built on top of VGMs.
We initialize the video expert of Fast-WAM with our fine-tuned Wan2.2 and evaluate the resulting policy on RoboCasa365 Target100  ( Nasiriany et al., 2026 ) .
Following Fast-WAM, the policy is pretrained on Human300 and then trained with 500 target demonstrations per task. The Atomic Seen evaluation includes 18 tasks with 50 rollouts each.
After 50k steps of fine-tuning, the policy takes images from three cameras and the current proprioceptive state as input. It predicts 32 actions in ten denoising steps and executes eight actions before replanning at 20 Hz.
As shown in Table  3 , CausalAlign + Fast-WAM raises average success from 43.5% to 54.2% (+10.7 percentage points) and success on Atomic Seen tasks from 59.1% to 78.1% (+19.0 points, 703/900 successful rollouts).
Further, success on Composite Seen tasks increases from 36.4% to 40.8%, and success on Composite Unseen tasks rises from 33.2% to 40.6%, suggesting that stronger ego-motion representations can benefit mobile manipulation.
Additional rollouts are shown in Appendix  B.5 .

 Semantic partitioning. 
We first investigate whether separating delta-token into motion, geometry, and visual information helps the model learn a more effective representation.
To this end, we compare our structured delta tokens, consisting of 64D ego-motion, 40D geometry, and 24D visual regions, with two unpartitioned 128D encoders.
All encoders receive the same supervision, and we use PCA to project the unpartitioned representations to the corresponding dimensions.
As shown in Table  4 (a), our structured representation outperforms both alternatives across all six metrics.
Further, the cross-probes in Appendix  C.1 show that each region performs best on its corresponding task.
These results suggest that explicitly separating different types of information helps the model learn more effective representations of ego-motion.
 Complete-space comparison. 
We next examine whether learning delta tokens provides advantages over directly using the raw features of a pretrained 3D foundation model.
We compare our 128D delta tokens with two representations from LingBot-Map: the concatenated features of consecutive frames (8192D) and their difference (4096D).
Both original representations retain their full dimensions.
As shown in Table  4 (b), our delta tokens outperform both alternatives across all six metrics, despite using substantially fewer dimensions.
For example, the rotation error decreases from 3.243 to 2.530 degrees compared with the original feature pair.
These results suggest that learning a compact representation of temporal changes is more effective than directly using the original pooled 3D features.
 5 Conclusion
We introduce CausalAlign , a causal alignment framework for ego-motion consistent video generation.
By linear probing and causal interventions, we show that video generation models already learned and used ego-motion state during denoising but did not retain it well at the late stages, a pattern we call “geometric state forgetting”.
CausalAlign targets at the locations where such forgetting is identified, and aligns ego-motion states with the delta-token space from a 3D foundation model.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that CausalAlign consistently improves ego-motion consistency and temporal dynamics while preserving visual quality. It mitigates geometric state forgetting by changing the pattern of ego-motion state from rise-and-fall to rise-and-maintain.
Further, our improved VGM largely raises the success rate of WAM on the mobile manipulation tasks of RoboCasa365.
We hope this work can inspire the research on preserving geometric states of generative video models and the research on enhancing embodied models.
