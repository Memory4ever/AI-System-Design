Abstract
World models turn visual representations into predictions and actions.
We argue that their visual foundation should combine physical-state information, native language alignment, and pixel reconstruction, so that a single representation can support prediction, instruction, and inspection.
However, existing encoders typically specialize in only one or two of these capabilities; we therefore introduce ALPS, a pretrained visual state encoder that unifies all three in a single set of patch tokens.
Using only still images and text, we pretrain a ViT-B/16 from scratch with language, reconstruction, and spatial correspondence objectives, and then refine it via cross-view self-distillation.
In a unified evaluation across fifteen public encoders, ALPS achieves the lowest aggregate LIBERO state-probe error among ViT-B models (matching the 3.5\times larger V-JEPA 2-L) while maintaining strong language alignment and accurate pixel reconstruction.
Downstream, frozen ALPS tokens reach the highest mean policy success on LIBERO (85.5%) when paired with our native text tower, match or exceed leading encoders on world-model planning across PushT, Wall, and Granular, and allow predicted future tokens to be decoded directly into pixels for visual inspection.
We hope this work helps establish visual pretraining as a shared foundation for understanding, prediction, and action in world models.
 1 Introduction
World models predict how a scene changes in response to actions and use these predictions to plan.
Many of them operate in the latent space of a frozen pretrained visual encoder  [ 91 , 6 , 4 ] .
The encoder therefore decides what the world model can see: only when its tokens encode where objects are and what state they are in can a downstream world model learn how that state changes from scarce interaction data.
Two complementary interfaces make such an encoder far more versatile.
With tokens aligned to language, instructions and goals stated in words can be related to the scene without a second visual encoder.
With tokens that decode back into images, both observed scenes and imagined futures can be rendered and inspected.
This raises a central question: how can we pretrain a single visual encoder that provides physical-state information, language alignment, and pixel reconstruction at once?
Existing public encoders typically specialize in only one or two of these capabilities.
Self-supervised and agglomerative models such as DINOv2, DINOv3, and C-RADIOv3 provide transferable spatial features  [ 53 , 66 , 30 ] , while predictive video encoders such as V-JEPA 2 learn spatiotemporal representations through latent prediction  [ 4 ] .
Meanwhile, image-text encoders such as CLIP, SigLIP 2, and PE-Core align vision with language  [ 61 , 69 , 7 ] , and reconstruction-based encoders preserve fine visual detail in pixels or latents  [ 29 , 86 ] .
Combining state information, language alignment, and pixel reconstruction in a single representation is not straightforward.
A refinement that improves the state information of an image-text encoder, for example, can substantially degrade its language alignment, as we show for SigLIP 2 and CLIP ( Sec.   5.2 ).
To resolve this tension, we pretrain our encoder in two stages ( Fig.   2 ).
The first stage establishes language alignment, pixel reconstruction, and spatial features in a shared representation.
We train a ViT-B/16 from scratch on image-text pairs with contrastive alignment, captioning, reconstruction, and dense correspondence objectives on the same patch tokens.
The second stage then strengthens state information through cross-view self-distillation, training the encoder to predict scene content across changes in crop, scale, and appearance.
From an augmented view, the student predicts a frozen first-stage teacher’s patch tokens at corresponding locations in a clean view.
Both stages train on still images and text alone, without video or interaction supervision.
We call the final encoder ALPS ( A L anguage-aligned, P ixel-renderable S tate encoder) and the intermediate encoder after the first stage ALPS (Stage 1).
We compare ALPS with fifteen public encoders, all frozen and evaluated under common protocols ( , Tab.   1 ).
ALPS achieves the lowest aggregate LIBERO state-probe error among ViT-B encoders, matching the 3.5\times larger V-JEPA 2-L, while retaining strong language alignment and accurate pixel reconstruction.
Cross-view refinement reduces state-probe error by 17% while changing image-text retrieval by at most 0.5 points, and also improves world-model prediction.
In contrast, the same refinement lowers ImageNet zero-shot accuracy and image-to-text retrieval by 4–12 points for SigLIP 2 and CLIP ( Fig.   5 ).
Frozen ALPS tokens achieve the highest mean policy success in our LIBERO comparison when paired with our native text tower, and support leading planning performance on PushT, Wall, and Granular ( Sec.   5.3 ).
Predicted future tokens can also be decoded into images for inspection.
Our contributions are as follows:
 • 
We formulate visual state pretraining for world models and establish a unified evaluation protocol spanning physical-state information, native language alignment, pixel reconstruction, and downstream control, planning, and prediction.
 • 
We introduce ALPS, pretrained from scratch on still images and text with joint language, reconstruction, and spatial objectives followed by cross-view self-distillation.
 • 
We demonstrate that one frozen representation can combine competitive performance across all three capabilities and serve as a shared visual foundation for robot policies and world models.
 2 Related Work
 Figure 2 : Two-stage visual state pretraining. 
Stage 1 trains an encoder E from scratch with four objectives on shared patch tokens.
Stage 2 initializes a student from E and keeps E frozen as the teacher.
Given student tokens from an augmented crop A and query tokens encoding position and relative scale, a predictor regresses standardized teacher tokens from a clean crop B .
Only patches of B whose centers lie inside A contribute to the loss.
After training, the predictor is discarded and the refined student serves as ALPS. 
Image-text and unified visual pretraining.
Contrastive image-text pretraining connects visual representations to language  [ 61 , 84 ] .
Subsequent approaches incorporate captioning supervision  [ 82 , 40 , 44 ] , self-distillation and masked prediction  [ 69 ] , spatial objectives  [ 49 , 12 ] , image reconstruction  [ 67 ] , or autoregressive patch prediction  [ 23 ] .
Meanwhile, unified tokenizers and multimodal models couple understanding with generation  [ 46 , 89 , 76 ] .
Other work instead adapts a frozen self-supervised backbone to language, as in dino.txt  [ 32 ] , or encodes the latents of a generative autoencoder, as in OpenVision 3  [ 86 ] .
Instead of adapting a frozen backbone or encoding autoencoder latents, our first stage jointly trains unnormalized patch tokens for language alignment, pixel reconstruction, and dense correspondence, providing a unified initialization for stage-two cross-view refinement.
Self-supervised and agglomerative representations.
Self-distillation  [ 13 , 92 , 53 , 66 ] , latent prediction  [ 3 , 18 ] , and multi-teacher distillation  [ 63 , 30 ] learn transferable patch representations.
To further strengthen dense spatial features, recent methods align region features with crop embeddings  [ 75 ] , match patch-neighborhood orderings across views  [ 55 ] , enforce locality alignment  [ 17 ] , apply spatial refinement as in Perception Encoder  [ 7 ] , or supervise predictions at multiple depths  [ 51 ] .
Without relying on external teachers, our second stage refines the encoder from its own frozen first-stage weights through geometry-conditioned cross-view self-distillation, sharpening state information while preserving native language alignment and pixel reconstruction.
Visual representations for robot learning.
Policies using pretrained visual representations can be competitive with those using ground-truth state inputs  [ 54 ] .
Robot representations have been learned from egocentric video  [ 52 ] , masked autoencoding  [ 62 , 48 ] , language supervision  [ 33 ] , foundation-model distillation  [ 65 ] , and neural rendering  [ 93 ] .
Comparative studies find no single dominant representation  [ 48 ] , show that general vision datasets can match egocentric video for visuomotor pretraining  [ 19 ] , and note that domain-specific pretraining does not guarantee robustness under visual shifts  [ 11 ] .
Pretrained on general-domain images and text, ALPS provides frozen state tokens and a native text tower that transfer directly to language-conditioned manipulation.
Representations for world models.
World models can learn a visual autoencoder before fitting latent dynamics  [ 27 ] , learn representations jointly with dynamics  [ 28 , 47 , 36 ] , or build on pretrained features  [ 6 ] .
In particular, DINO-WM plans over frozen DINOv2 tokens  [ 91 ] , V-JEPA 2 connects video pretraining to action-conditioned planning  [ 4 ] , and frozen features also support language-conditioned planning  [ 38 ] , whereas RepWAM learns visual and action tokenizers directly from robot data  [ 70 ] .
Generative world models instead simulate observations using learned tokenizers with autoregressive or diffusion dynamics  [ 50 , 9 , 1 ] , or via pixel-space diffusion  [ 2 ] .
Recent studies further show that off-the-shelf features do not automatically improve sample efficiency in model-based reinforcement learning  [ 64 ] , that pixel reconstruction fidelity alone is an unreliable proxy for recovering actions from latent transitions  [ 80 ] , and that static image encoders can outperform video encoders depending on downstream protocols  [ 68 ] .
We focus on pretraining the visual encoder whose frozen tokens serve as the state representation for downstream world models.
 3 Method
Let E map an image x to patch tokens z=E(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d} .
Downstream state probes, policies, world models, and pixel decoders operate directly on the frozen tokens z .
For language alignment, a linear projection W_{\text{img}} followed by average pooling and \ell_{2} normalization maps z to a global image embedding v=\text{norm}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{\text{img}}z_{i}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{D} , which is paired with a text encoder E_{\text{text}} .
 3.1 Stage 1: joint pretraining from scratch
Stage 1 builds a shared representation through complementary language, pixel, and spatial supervision ( Fig.   2 , top).
Contrastive alignment and captioning connect the tokens to language, reconstruction preserves visual detail, and dense correspondence supervises local spatial relationships across views.
Architecture.
The image encoder E is a ViT-B/16  [ 22 ] with fixed sine-cosine position embeddings.
It outputs the raw patch tokens z of the last transformer block without a final normalization layer.
We jointly train a text encoder and a caption decoder with the same width and depth as E ( Sec.   S1 ).
A lightweight convolutional decoder D maps z to pixels for the pretraining reconstruction objective, whereas all reported reconstructions use a separate evaluation decoder trained on frozen tokens ( Sec.   4 ).
Objectives.
Four objectives supervise the same patch tokens:
 \mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{itc}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}}+w_{\text{rec}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}+0.25\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{dense}}. 
 (1) 
 Contrastive alignment. 
Each image is paired with its web caption and one sentence from a longer synthetic caption.
We compute an InfoNCE loss  [ 61 ] between the mean-pooled, projected image tokens and each caption set, then average the two losses to obtain \mathcal{L}_{\text{itc}} .
Each loss uses its own softmax, so the two captions paired with the same image are never treated as negatives of each other.
 Captioning. 
The caption decoder autoregressively predicts the full synthetic caption, conditioned on the image tokens and preceding text  [ 82 ] .
 Reconstruction. 
The decoder D reconstructs x from both z and a noisy copy of z .
The objective combines \ell_{1} , structural similarity, and image gradient terms ( Sec.   S1 ).
We do not apply color augmentation to x , which directly serves as the reconstruction target.
 Dense correspondence. 
This objective directly supervises patch correspondence across crops, scales, and flips.
We form a second view g(x) via a random crop and flip of x , so the location of each patch in the original view is known.
A small projection head maps the tokens of both views to contrastive features.
For each patch of g(x) , the positive feature is sampled bilinearly from the projected token field of x at the corresponding location.
Features at other locations in the same image and from other images serve as negatives in an InfoNCE loss \mathcal{L}_{\text{dense}} ( Sec.   S1 ), encouraging local discriminability.
Following prior work on dense correspondence and pixel-level consistency  [ 71 , 77 ] , this term adds explicit cross-view patch supervision to global image-text alignment and single-image reconstruction.
Data.
We train on DataComp-1B  [ 24 ] using its web captions and synthetic captions generated by the LLaVA-LLaMA-3 captioner from Recap-DataComp-1B  [ 39 ] , conditioned on the original web caption  [ 44 ] .
Because the captioner uses a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14  [ 39 ] , knowledge from this image-text model reaches our encoder only through text; no external visual model supplies feature targets.
Training processes 12.8 billion samples at 128\times 128 resolution, followed by 1.0 billion samples at 224\times 224 resolution ( Sec.   S1 ).
After Stage 1, we retain the image encoder, text encoder, and image projection, while discarding the caption decoder, dense-correspondence projector, and D .

 3.2 Stage 2: cross-view self-distillation on single images
Stage 2 refines the shared representation through cross-view self-distillation ( Fig.   2 , bottom).
From an augmented crop, the student predicts the tokens produced by a frozen Stage 1 teacher for a clean crop of the same image.
Known crop geometry specifies the target locations, and supervision focuses on the overlap between views.
Teacher and student.
Both the frozen teacher and the trainable student are initialized from the Stage 1 encoder, and only the student encoder and predictor are updated.
Before training, we compute fixed per-channel statistics to standardize the teacher tokens throughout this stage.
For each image, we independently sample two views: the teacher receives a clean crop B covering 40% to 100% of the image, while the student receives with equal probability a local crop A (10% to 40%) or a global crop (40% to 100%) with photometric augmentation ( Sec.   S1 ).
Geometry queries.
Geometry queries specify where and at what scale the predictor should estimate teacher features.
A four-layer transformer predictor P reads the student tokens E(A) and produces one output for each query q_{i} associated with patch i of B .
In each layer, the queries first attend to one another and then to E(A) .
The crop boxes determine the position of each teacher patch center in the coordinate frame of A .
Each query encodes this position using a fixed sine-cosine embedding, measured in the patch units of A , and encodes the log side-length ratio of B to A through a learned embedding ( Tab.   S2 ).
The queries thus supply the geometric correspondence, while the student features provide the visual content used to predict the teacher tokens.
Training objective.
Let V denote the set of teacher patches whose centers fall inside the student crop A .
We minimize the mean squared error between the predicted features and the standardized teacher tokens over V :
 \mathcal{L}_{2}=\frac{1}{|V|\,d}\sum_{i\in V}\big\|P\big(E(A),q_{i}\big)-\bar{z}^{\,\text{teacher}}_{i}(B)\big\|^{2}. 
 (2) 
Patches outside V are excluded from the loss, and we resample the student crop if |V|<16 .
Unlike masked prediction of unseen regions, this geometry-conditioned objective trains local patch tokens to remain consistent across changes in crop context, scale, and appearance while staying aligned with the frozen Stage 1 teacher.
Data and output.
Stage 2 trains on 5 million single-image samples drawn from a curated collection of public image-editing datasets ( Sec.   S1 ).
These sources provide high-resolution, object-centric scenes well-suited for local cross-view crops, which we sample as individual images without edit-pair or instruction supervision.
After training, we discard the predictor and retain the refined student encoder as ALPS, paired directly with the unchanged Stage 1 text encoder and image projection.

Encoders.
We compare fifteen public encoders with our first-stage encoder and ALPS ( Tab.   1 ).
Fourteen of the public models are image encoders, while V-JEPA 2 ViT-L  [ 4 ] is a video encoder that takes the last 16 frames at 256\times 256 resolution and outputs tokens for the current frame.
Our primary comparison uses ViT-B image towers at 224\times 224 resolution (196 tokens for B/16 and 256 for B/14), taking each encoder’s last-layer patch tokens with its default normalization.
Alongside the ViT-B models, OpenVision 2, V-JEPA 2, and dino.txt  [ 32 ] serve as ViT-L references; for dino.txt, we extract the patch tokens after its language-aligned vision head.
State probe.
The state probe evaluates scene-state readout and action-conditioned prediction directly from frozen tokens ( Fig.   3 ).
Using demonstrations from LIBERO-Object and LIBERO-Spatial  [ 42 ] , a two-layer attentive probe (2.5M parameters) reads the tokens of the last 16 frames to estimate the movable objects’ 3D positions, orientations, and binary predicates against simulator ground truth.
In the readout setting, the probe estimates the current state from the frames alone; in the two prediction settings, it additionally receives the robot actions over the next 1 or 3 seconds to predict the future state at that horizon.
We report position error relative to our first-stage encoder, averaged across the six combinations of the two suites and three settings ( Tab.   S4 reports absolute errors in centimeters).
World models instead predict future tokens in latent space and are evaluated separately ( Sec.   5.3 ).
Language alignment.
We evaluate language alignment using each model’s native text tower, reporting ImageNet-1k  [ 20 ] zero-shot accuracy and Recall@1 for image-text retrieval on Flickr30k  [ 81 ] and COCO  [ 41 ] .
For encoders released without a paired text tower, these metrics are marked n/a, indicating the absence of a native text interface rather than a lack of semantic information in the features.
C-RADIOv3 uses the stronger of its two language adaptor heads, while ALPS uses its Stage 1 language interface without modification.
Pixel reconstruction.
To evaluate pixel reconstruction on equal footing, we train a separate lightweight convolutional adapter for each encoder that maps its frozen tokens to the latents of a frozen SDXL autoencoder  [ 57 ] for image decoding.
The adapter is trained on 19,000 ImageNet validation images and evaluated on 1,000 held-out images (one per class) using \ell_{1} error and LPIPS  [ 87 ] .
Although much larger generative decoders can reconstruct plausible images from DINOv2 or SigLIP 2 tokens  [ 90 ] , our protocol measures the visual detail directly recoverable by a lightweight probe, and ALPS maintains its advantage when paired with a stronger decoder ( Sec.   S2 ).
Control.
For visuomotor control, we train the LIBERO transformer policy  [ 42 ] on frozen visual tokens via behavior cloning with 50 demonstrations per task across four LIBERO suites.
Task instructions are embedded either by a frozen BERT model  [ 21 ] or by each encoder’s native text tower.
Each trained policy is evaluated three times over 50 rollouts per task ( Sec.   S2 ).
World-model prediction and planning.
We follow the DINO-WM protocol  [ 91 ] on PushT, Wall, PointMaze, Granular, and Rope, and LeWorldModel  [ 47 ] on Reacher.
On each task, we train an identical dynamics predictor over the frozen patch tokens of each encoder to support both cross-entropy planning and multi-step prediction evaluation ( Sec.   S2 ).

State.
ALPS achieves the lowest aggregate relative position error among the compared ViT-B encoders (.829), followed by C-RADIOv3 (.864) and DINOv3 (.870); together, these three models form a leading group ahead of PE-Spatial (.936).
ALPS also achieves the lowest error in each of the three individual settings (readout, +1 s, and +3 s) as well as on frames rendered at 256\times 256 resolution ( Tab.   S6 ).
While V-JEPA 2-L matches its aggregate error using 3.5\times as many parameters and video pretraining, the two models show complementary strengths: V-JEPA 2 reads out the current state more accurately (.741 vs. .842), whereas ALPS is more accurate at 3-second prediction (.813 vs. .904).
Compared with SigLIP 2 and PE-Core (1.160 and 1.170), ALPS reduces aggregate state-probe error by about 29%, and probing their intermediate layers narrows this gap without closing it ( Sec.   S2 ).
On moving objects, ALPS reaches a mean error of 3.22 cm across the four prediction settings, matching the 3.04–3.25 cm range of V-JEPA 2, DINOv3, and C-RADIOv3 ( Tab.   S5 ).
Language.
ALPS retains retrieval performance close to the strongest image-text encoders in our comparison.
It reaches 93.2/78.9 Recall@1 on Flickr30k and 70.2/51.9 on COCO (image-to-text/text-to-image), within 2 points of the best score on each of the four measures.
Its ImageNet zero-shot accuracy is 73.4, compared with 78.4 for PE-Core, the strongest ViT-B image-text encoder on this metric.
This larger 5.0-point gap on classification is expected when pretraining on descriptive synthetic captions, whose distribution differs from short ImageNet class prompts  [ 43 ] .

Pixels.
ALPS preserves fine visual detail that can be decoded directly from its tokens.
With the evaluation decoder, ALPS achieves an \ell_{1} error of .120 and an LPIPS of .372 ( Fig.   4 ), yielding a lower \ell_{1} error than every compared encoder not explicitly trained to reconstruct pixels or latents.
In particular, C-RADIOv3 has 34% higher \ell_{1} error (.161), DINOv3 has 63% higher error (.196), and the image-text encoders have more than twice the error.
Encoders pretrained to reconstruct pixels (MAE at .109 and VC-1 at .106) or autoencoder latents (OpenVision 3 at .104) reconstruct more accurately, but incur 14% to 22% higher state-probe error.
LPIPS gives a slightly different ordering: these three reconstruction-focused encoders score .243–.260, and C-RADIOv3 also edges out ALPS (.359, about 4% lower).
Under a stronger decoder configuration, however, ALPS maintains a lower \ell_{1} error than C-RADIOv3 (.107 vs. .141, about 24% lower) and overtakes it on LPIPS (.302 vs. .311; Tab.   S18 ).
All three at once.
ALPS combines leading state-probe performance, strong language alignment, and accurate pixel reconstruction in one frozen representation.
Other leading state encoders have weaker retrieval or no paired text tower, while encoders with comparable retrieval or lower reconstruction error have higher state-probe error ( ).
We use these interfaces for instruction-conditioned control and visual inspection of predicted world-model rollouts ( Sec.   5.3 ).
 5.2 Effect of cross-view refinement on state and language
Cross-view refinement moves our encoder into the leading state group: before refinement, ALPS (Stage 1) has higher state-probe error than seven public ViT-B encoders in Tab.   1 , whereas after refinement it achieves the lowest.
 Figure   5 compares the same Stage 2 recipe across our first-stage encoder, SigLIP 2-B/16, and CLIP-B/16, each trained for 5 million samples with its own frozen copy as the teacher and evaluated through its unchanged language head ( Tab.   S20 ).
For our encoder, refining on DataComp web images also reduces state-probe error, though less than our default curated collection (.886 vs. .829) under the same training budget, reflecting differences in both scene content and native resolution ( Sec.   S3 ).
All three encoders improve on state probing.
Stage 2 reduces our encoder’s aggregate state-probe error by 17% (14% to 19% across the six individual settings) and raises DAVIS video object segmentation from .456 to .533 ( Sec.   S2 ).
Similarly, SigLIP 2 improves from 1.160 to .893 on the state probe and from .503 to .552 on DAVIS, while CLIP improves from 1.194 to .898 and from .574 to .585, respectively.

Only our encoder keeps its language alignment.
For ALPS, the improvement in state accuracy comes with minimal change to the other capabilities: image-text retrieval shifts by at most 0.5 points, ImageNet accuracy changes by about 1 point, and reconstruction remains close to its first-stage level ( \ell_{1} .120 vs. .112 and LPIPS .372 vs. .365).
In contrast, the public encoders lose substantially more language performance.
SigLIP 2 drops by 4.3 points on ImageNet accuracy and by 7.5 and 11.4 points on Flickr30k and COCO image-to-text retrieval, while CLIP drops by 10.2, 10.9, and 11.8 points, respectively.
These results show that cross-view refinement can strengthen state information while preserving the other interfaces, but this balance depends critically on the encoder being refined.
Lowering the learning rate does not remove the language gap.
To test whether SigLIP 2 simply requires a smaller step size, we lower its encoder learning rate from 10^{-5} to 3\times 10^{-6} while keeping all other settings unchanged.
Its state-probe error then falls by 17% (from 1.160 to .959), matching the relative reduction of our encoder (from 1.000 to .829).
Yet it still loses more on every language metric: image-to-text Recall@1 falls by 2.3 points on Flickr30k and 2.9 on COCO, compared with 0.2 and 0.4 for ALPS ( Sec.   S5 ).
Under the default refinement schedule, ALPS also exhibits smaller drift in its patch tokens and image embeddings ( Sec.   S5 ).
A key structural difference lies in the language readout: ALPS linearly projects mean-pooled patch tokens, whereas SigLIP 2 uses nonlinear attention pooling and CLIP reads a class token.
Because linear projection commutes with average pooling, zero-mean local token updates can cancel out in the pooled image representation, even without an explicit language constraint in Stage 2.

 Reconstruction gives the pixel interface at a cost in state-probe error. 
We next test whether the learned representation supports downstream control, planning, and dynamics prediction.
Policies act directly on the encoded frames, while planners use a world model that predicts future tokens.
Policies.
ALPS achieves the highest mean policy success in our LIBERO evaluation ( Tab.   2 ), which tests four suites using shared BERT instructions in the upper block and each encoder’s native text tower in the lower block.
With BERT, ALPS reaches 83.4% mean success, outperforming spatial encoders such as C-RADIOv3 (78.2%), DINOv2 (78.4%), and DINOv3 (79.0%) as well as SigLIP 2 (81.4%).
Moreover, Stage 2 refinement raises LIBERO-Spatial success by 9.7 points, from 76.8% to 86.5% (per-suite breakdown in Sec.   S2 ).
The text tower as the instruction interface.
We replace BERT with projected embeddings from each encoder’s native text tower, scaled to the mean norm of BERT embeddings.
Mean success rises from 83.4% to 85.5% for ALPS, from 81.4% to 84.8% for SigLIP 2, from 78.2% to 81.8% for C-RADIOv3, and from 75.0% to 79.2% for CLIP.
Across three independent training runs per suite, ALPS gains an average of 3.1 percentage points with its native text tower ( Sec.   S2 ).
These gains, especially on the multi-step instructions in LIBERO-Long, likely benefit from vision-language alignment during pretraining and, for ALPS, joint training on detailed synthetic captions.
Planning.
ALPS leads or ties the best planning results on PushT, Wall, and Granular under the shared training protocols ( Tab.   3 ).
It reaches 90% success on PushT (tied with C-RADIOv3) and 92% on Wall (tied with DINOv2), while all six encoders solve every goal on PointMaze.
On Granular, refinement substantially lowers the Chamfer distance from .324 (Stage 1) to .176, ahead of DINOv3 and SigLIP 2.
On the remaining tasks, C-RADIOv3 achieves the lowest error on Rope (.558 vs. .750), and DINOv3 attains the highest success rate on Reacher (86.0% vs. 78.7%).
World-model prediction.
Refinement also improves the accuracy of predicted states across four tasks ( Tab.   4 ).
We evaluate five-step token predictions using state probes trained on real-frame tokens, with true-token readout as a reference ( Tab.   S16 ; protocol and rollout analysis in Sec.   S2 ).
Stage 2 reduces the first-stage encoder’s prediction error by 28% on PushT, 17% on Reacher, 13% on Granular, and 11% on Rope.
Compared with specialized spatial encoders, ALPS outperforms DINOv3 on PushT and matches its accuracy on Granular and Rope, while C-RADIOv3 and DINOv3 achieve lower error on Reacher.
On Wall, true-token readout error is already close to prediction error, leaving little room to differentiate the encoders.

On PushT, an evaluation decoder trained only on real-frame tokens ( Sec.   4 ) renders the world model’s five-step open-loop predictions without participating in dynamics training or planning ( Figs.   6 and  S15 ).
The rendered rollouts clearly expose predicted object motion and visual errors, with ALPS achieving lower five-step LPIPS (.020) than DINOv3 (.024), Stage 1 (.025), and SigLIP 2 (.031), behind only C-RADIOv3 (.018).
 5.4 Trade-offs between the first-stage objectives
State, language, and pixels.
The first-stage objectives contribute differently to the three capabilities.
We train each variant from scratch at one tenth of the Stage 1 schedule, then apply the same Stage 2 refinement ( Tab.   5 ).
All variants retain contrastive alignment, so the ablation tests the three auxiliary objectives.
The reconstruction loss is essential for the pixel interface: the two variants without it incur 72\% and 78\% higher \ell_{1} reconstruction error after Stage 1 (.194 and .200 vs. .113).
Removing reconstruction or captioning lowers state-probe error by 8.5\% and 4.3\% , respectively, indicating a capacity trade-off among the auxiliary tasks.
Across all variants, Stage 2 reduces state-probe error by 14\% to 19\% , largely preserves language performance, and changes \ell_{1} error by less than 4\% .
We therefore retain all objectives in the full recipe, trading a small amount of state-probe accuracy for accurate pixel decoding while still outperforming specialized ViT-B spatial encoders at scale ( Tab.   1 ).
Language retention across objectives.
Refinement also preserves language alignment even without the three auxiliary objectives.
For the contrastive-only variant, ImageNet zero-shot accuracy changes from 69.8 to 67.9, while image-to-text Recall@1 changes from 91.0 to 89.3 on Flickr30k and from 67.1 to 64.1 on COCO.
Thus, the auxiliary objectives are not required to retain strong language alignment in this reduced-budget setting.
Dense correspondence.
Dense correspondence directly supervises local patch matching across crops, scales, and flips ( Sec.   3.1 ).
Under the one-tenth budget of Tab.   5 , removing \mathcal{L}_{\text{dense}} has minimal impact on global and single-view metrics (changing aggregate state-probe error by less than 1\% , \ell_{1} error by under .001 , LPIPS negligibly, and retrieval by at most 1.1 points), as these benchmarks do not directly test cross-view patch correspondence.
When evaluated directly on cross-view patch matching ( Sec.   S4 ), however, removing \mathcal{L}_{\text{dense}} reduces matching accuracy by 6.0 points after Stage 1 (from 37.4% to 31.4%), and the full recipe retains a 2.6-point advantage even after Stage 2 refinement (40.8% vs. 38.2%).
 6 Conclusion
ALPS shows that a single frozen visual representation, pretrained on still images and text, can combine competitive state information, native language alignment, and pixel reconstruction.
Cross-view refinement strengthens state representations while preserving the language and pixel interfaces.
The same tokens support robot control, world-model prediction and planning, and visual inspection of predicted scenes.
These results suggest visual state pretraining as a shared foundation for robot policies and world models.
Limitations and future work.
We focus on a ViT-B/16 backbone and simulation benchmarks for controlled comparison across fifteen encoders, leaving larger model scales and real-robot deployment to future work.
On the training side, our recipe relies on a two-stage curriculum to balance reconstruction, language, and spatial state ( Sec.   5.4 ); folding cross-view refinement into a single training stage remains an open question.
Moreover, ALPS is pretrained without video supervision and is less sensitive to fine-grained temporal actions despite improving cross-frame correspondence on DAVIS ( Sec.   S2 ), which motivates extending Stage 2 to video and interaction data.
Finally, our downstream evaluations test language-conditioned control and world-model planning in separate settings; integrating state, language, and pixel decoding into a single language-guided, pixel-verifiable planner is a natural next step.
