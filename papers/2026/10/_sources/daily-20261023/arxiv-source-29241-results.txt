6.3 Tracing Visual Evidence and Improving Its Utilization
Having established that most errors occur despite successfully encoded visual evidence, we next ask two questions: where does this evidence become accessible within the model, and can we improve its utilization once it is encoded? VELens naturally supports both analyses: applying it to layers traces how evidence develops across the model, while its final prediction identifies errors in which the required evidence is encoded.
Visual Evidence Emerges Differently Across Attributes and Models.
Although VELens combines scores across all layers, we train per-layer classifiers using each layer’s evidence score separately to trace evidence accessibility with depth. Figure 11 shows that evidence generally strengthens toward upper layers, consistent with prior work finding semantic information increasingly accessible in middle-to-late layers ( Neo et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2025a ; Jiang et al., 2025b ) . However, trajectories vary across attributes and models. In Qwen, material evidence is already strong in early layers, whereas color and object evidence rise sharply near the end; in LLaVA, object evidence increases more gradually. These results indicate that visual evidence does not become accessible at a universal layer. Interventions aimed at improving visual utilization may therefore need to account for these layer-wise dynamics rather than targeting a fixed layer across models and concepts.
Evidence-Positive Errors Are More Easily Corrected.
Distinguishing visual-representation from visual-utilization failures is important, as they call for different interventions. Since our results identify visual utilization as the primary bottleneck, we test whether strengthening access to encoded visual evidence can recover incorrect answers. Guided by the absence of a universal evidence layer, we apply an attention intervention at every layer that increases attention from each text token to visual tokens inside the annotated target-object region (§ D.6 ). Because this requires a known object region, it serves as a proof of concept rather than a standalone correction method. Figure 5 shows an example where VELens detects the evidence and the intervention corrects the answer.

We apply the same intervention to evidence-positive errors, M ⁡ ( X , Y X ) = 1 M(X,Y_{X})=1 , and evidence-negative errors, M ⁡ ( X , Y X ) = 0 M(X,Y_{X})=0 , obtaining correction rates of 25.2% and 12%, respectively. The higher correction rate supports our diagnosis: the intervention is substantially more effective when the required evidence is encoded. This is encouraging because most errors are evidence-positive. It suggests that a large fraction of current errors may be addressed not by replacing the visual encoder, but by developing better mechanisms to locate, route,
and use evidence the model already represents.
7 Discussion
In this work, we proposed VELens, a rank-based probe that traces object and attribute visual evidence across the layers of a VLM. Using it to separate visual-representation from visual-utilization failures, we found that utilization failures account for the majority of errors in both models we studied, with conservative lower bounds of 77.5% for LLaVA and 61.0% for Qwen. Our analysis covers attribute questions over color, material, and shape. VELens itself, however, operates on vocabulary-space rankings and needs only a target concept and competing alternatives, so it naturally extends to other question types: attribute verification questions, for instance, are answered from evidence for the object and the queried value, both of which VELens already provides. More broadly, we see this work as a step toward interpretability of the concepts encoded in visual tokens, and we hope it can extend beyond the question types we study (§ E ) and guide model development rather than only describe model behavior: knowing whether the required evidence is encoded determines whether progress should come from a better visual encoder or from better mechanisms to locate, route, and use what the model already represents.
