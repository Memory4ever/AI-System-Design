One Axis, No Brake: Self-Knowledge Limits the Filtering of Harmful Peer Conformity in LLMs

Yibo Hu
Illinois Institute of Technology
yhu89@illinoistech.edu
Abstract

Multi-agent LLM systems are expected to be more reliable because agents can catch each other’s mistakes. But peer pressure cuts both ways: the same correction that fixes a wrong answer can overturn a right one. The tempting safeguard is a brake that keeps the beneficial revisions and blocks the harmful ones. We show this brake is hard to build, for a simple reason: a revision is harmful exactly when the original answer was right, so deciding whether to block it is the same as knowing whether the model was already correct. This turns the open-ended hunt for a brake into one measurable quantity, the model’s self-knowledge: any brake built from a deploy-time signal is a correctness probe in disguise, and self-knowledge is far from perfect (AUROC ≈0.64\approx 0.64–0.890.89 across six model families). We call this ceiling the wall. Even white-box steering of the model’s own correctness direction does not breach it: it changes how often the model revises, but harmful and beneficial revisions move together. At population scale the wall becomes the cliff: when most agents start wrong, debate amplifies the shared mistake into a confident, wrong consensus. In our multiple-choice societies, more agents, more model diversity, and a stronger member do not fix it. What helps is adding information before the revision, not filtering after it. Local agreement is not global correctness.111Code and data: https://github.com/yibo-hu-lab/wall-and-cliff

1 Introduction

Multi-agent LLM systems are increasingly deployed on the premise that conferring makes them more reliable: let several agents debate, review one another, or vote, and the group should catch mistakes a single model would miss. The premise has support (debate and self-consistency can raise accuracy (Du et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024)), and it now underwrites production pipelines for reasoning, code, and decision support.

But the same exchange can backfire: it can pull a model off a right answer as easily as it fixes a wrong one. Picture a model that answers a question correctly, sees three peers disagree, and revises to match them: the “correction” has made it worse. Call a revision harmful when it abandons a correct answer and beneficial when it fixes a wrong one. A system that wanted the benefit without the harm would keep the beneficial revisions and block the harmful ones.

A large literature documents that LLMs are swayed by users, peers, and majority pressure (Sharma et al., 2023; Perez et al., 2022; Cho et al., 2025), and that multi-agent debate can fail or even degrade accuracy (Wu et al., 2025; De Marzo et al., 2026). Most of it asks one of two things: whether models are influenced, and whether conferring improves aggregate accuracy. We ask the question a deployed system faces one step later: once revisions happen, can the system tell its harmful revisions from its beneficial ones and filter accordingly?

The answer is unexpectedly simple, and it is a constraint rather than a method. On the set of revisions, a revision is harmful exactly when the original answer was correct. So asking “is this revision harmful?” is, term for term, asking “was I right to begin with?” Any filter that runs on signals a model exposes at deploy time (its verbalized confidence, its logprobs, its hidden activations) is therefore a correctness probe (Figure 1). It can be no better at catching harmful revisions than the model is at knowing when it was right. And self-knowledge is far from perfect: across the six model families we test, the best deploy-time read of a model’s own correctness reaches AUROC only ≈0.64\approx 0.64–0.890.89, the same imperfect regime prior work reports for internal truth signals (Kadavath et al., 2022; Azaria and Mitchell, 2023). We call this ceiling the wall.

The wall is not the weakness of one signal. We hit it with three: a coverage-calibrated confidence gate, an engineered hidden-state probe, and (the sharpest test) causal activation steering along the model’s own correctness direction. Even with full white-box access, steering buys no selective brake: turning the knob changes how often a model revises, but harmful and beneficial revisions rise and fall together. Steering moves revision propensity, not revision valence.

The wall has a consequence the moment agents revise each other. The cliff is the wall at population scale, not a separate phenomenon. Because no agent can reliably brake its own unwarranted revision, a group whose members err in similar ways has no internal channel to recover, and its fate is set by where it starts. Groups that begin mostly correct converge to the truth; groups that begin mostly wrong (the hard items) amplify the shared mistake into a confident, wrong consensus. The collapse is difficulty-gated, and it resists the rescues one reaches for first: more agents, more model diversity, a stronger member, even a correct dissenting minority.

The design lesson follows directly. Multi-agent debate is a useful exploration mechanism (it surfaces candidates and covers ground a lone agent would not), but it is not a self-certifying safety mechanism: it cannot filter its own errors on the problems where filtering matters most. What helps is not a better post-hoc filter but adding information before or around the revision: an external verifier or retrieved evidence, decorrelated peers, or a pre-committed warrant for what would justify a change. In one line: post-hoc filtering of harmful peer revision is self-knowledge in disguise, and because self-knowledge is imperfect, multi-agent LLM systems can lock into confident, wrong consensus on hard items.

2 Setup

Protocol.

A model first answers a multiple-choice question on its own (round 1). It is then shown a short summary of how a panel of peers answered (reported as “kk chose (X)”, with or without the peers’ average confidence, and constructed to disagree with it) and answers again (round 2). The summary carries only the social signal: how many peers disagree and how confident they are. It adds no evidence or argument. We vary this channel to isolate the effect of social pressure itself, not persuasion by content. The revision set FF is the items on which the round-2 answer differs from round-1; we condition everything that follows on FF, since only a revision can be harmful or beneficial. We use four open models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, gemma-2-9B) on ARC-Challenge and TruthfulQA, plus a Qwen2.5 scale ladder (1.5B–72B) and Qwen2.5-3B, with targeted grids for the probe, steering, and population studies (Appendix G); self-knowledge AUROCs use n=200n{=}200 items per cell.

What we measure.

Let correct0\textit{correct}_{0} be round-1 correctness. On a revision, define its valence V=1−correct0V=1-\textit{correct}_{0}, so V=0V{=}0 marks a harmful revision (the model abandoned a correct answer) and V=1V{=}1 a beneficial one (it fixed a wrong answer). A deploy-time signal SS is anything available without ground truth: verbalized confidence, logprob margin or entropy, or hidden activations. A brake is a score g​(S)g(S) that ranks harmful revisions above beneficial ones on FF well enough to act on. Equivalently, it detects the harmful indicator H≡correct0H\equiv\textit{correct}_{0} (so H=1H{=}1 on harmful revisions), which we take as the positive class. We grade any candidate brake by how well it separates the two classes, measured by AUROC (area under the ROC curve; 11 = perfectly separable, 0.50.5 = chance). AUROC scores separability and is unchanged up to orientation (AUROC↔1−AUROC\mathrm{AUROC}\!\leftrightarrow\!1-\mathrm{AUROC}) by which class is called positive. 3 The Wall: Filtering Is Bounded by Self-Knowledge

3.1 Why filtering needs self-knowledge

A harmful revision is, by definition, a revision away from a correct answer, and a beneficial one a revision away from a wrong answer. So on the revisions, labeling a revision harmful is exactly labeling the initial answer correct.

Proposition 1 (Reduction).

On the revision set FF, valence is fixed by initial correctness, V=1−correct0V=1-\textit{correct}_{0}. Telling a harmful revision from a beneficial one with a score g​(S)g(S) is therefore the same discrimination as reading correct0\textit{correct}_{0} from g​(S)g(S), up to the orientation of the score, so supgAUROCV​(g​(S)∣F)=AUROCcorrect0∗​(S∣F)\sup_{g}\mathrm{AUROC}_{V}(g(S)\mid F)=\mathrm{AUROC}^{*}_{\textit{correct}_{0}}(S\mid F): separating harmful from beneficial revisions is the same problem as reading round-1 correctness, and is bounded by the model’s self-knowledge in SS.

The reduction is elementary, and that is its force: it collapses the open-ended search for a deploy-time brake (across confidence rules, hidden-state probes, and activation edits) into a single measurable quantity: how well the model knows its own correctness. The bound needs only that correctness be checkable, not multiple choice specifically; whether a model’s deploy-time signals stay below it on other checkable tasks is then an empirical question. What remains is empirical: how high that ceiling sits, and whether any signal reaches it. (Selection slack and a data-processing argument for causal interventions are in Appendix A.)

A concrete case from our runs makes the identity tangible. Asked why an ice cube melts in a hand, Qwen2.5-7B first answers correctly, “heat moves from her hand to the ice cube”; shown three peers who unanimously pick the reverse, it revises to their answer. To block this one harmful revision, a filter would have to recognize that the original answer was the correct one, that heat flows from warm to cold, which is precisely the correctness judgment of Proposition 1. A deploy-time signal that could reliably flag the flip would be one that already knew the model was right, so there is no shortcut around self-knowledge.

3.2 Every signal reaches the same ceiling

Figure 2 shows the wall directly: across models and datasets, telling a harmful revision from a beneficial one is no easier than reading one’s own correctness: seven of eight cells lie on or below the y=xy{=}x line, and the one exception (gemma/ARC) is a 35-revision cell whose gap CI spans 0 (per-cell values in Appendix B).

Three signals confirm it. A coverage-calibrated confidence gate catches ≈95%\approx 95\% of harmful revisions only by blocking most beneficial ones, and does no better than plain confidence, at any operating point, not just in AUROC (Figure 3; per-threshold curve and net-accuracy detail in Appendix B). A hidden-state probe, even when engineered (late-layer pressure-induced deltas), reaches AUROC 0.810.81–0.830.83 and does not exceed the ≈0.75\approx 0.75–0.830.83 self-knowledge band; an answer-content control confirms it reads genuine correctness rather than the answer change. A classifier trained on the model’s own free-text rationale for the revision does worse still. It separates harmful from beneficial revisions at chance (AUROC 0.460.46): the model’s stated reasoning carries no usable signal about whether its own revision was harmful. Two further checks stay under the same ceiling. A logistic-regression ensemble of the three deploy-time signals (verbalized confidence, logprob confidence, logprob entropy), scored by out-of-fold cross-validation, does not beat the best single one (mean Δ=−0.05\Delta=-0.05 AUROC). Sampling-based self-consistency uncertainty, a signal class outside the single-pass argument, reaches only AUROC ≈0.71\approx 0.71 (Llama-3.1-8B) and does not improve as the sample count grows to 2424 (Appendix B). The third signal, causal steering, is the sharpest test and the subject of §3.3.

3.3 White-box steering gives no clean brake

The sharpest test is causal. We steer activations along the model’s own correctness direction (the most direct way to make a model “trust itself” more or less) and ask whether that pulls harmful and beneficial revisions apart. It does not. In the deploy-relevant range, steering changes how often the model revises but not which revisions: the harmful and beneficial arms move together, and a pre-registered equivalence test confirms a gap below ±0.05\pm 0.05 (Figure 4; ranges and full grid in Appendix C). Pushing the knob harder does not recover a brake: extreme steering cuts harmful revisions only by cutting beneficial ones too. Steering moves revision propensity, not revision valence. The null is not an artifact of the linear direction: an independent sparse-autoencoder steering direction gives the same null on Llama-3.1-8B, on both ARC and TruthfulQA (Appendix C). White-box access, linear or SAE-feature, buys no selective brake. The same null holds at 32B on ARC: the two arms stay together in the deploy-relevant range (max |Δ​H−Δ​B|=0.07|\Delta H-\Delta B|=0.07 for |α|≤1|\alpha|\leq 1, accuracy flat; Appendix C). The beneficial arm is small there, so we read 32B as consistent with the null rather than as a clean equivalence test.

3.4 The wall is structural

The ceiling is not an artifact of scale, family, or quantization (Table 1). It stays well below 11 for all six families, including two full-precision held-out models never tuned on our stack. It does not rise across a 1.5B→\to72B scale ladder (plateau 0.730.73–0.830.83; at 72B, ARC 0.810.81 / TQA 0.670.67, three seeds each). Test-time reasoning does not lift it either (R1-distilled models reach 0.640.64–0.720.72 on ARC). Across the six families the self-knowledge AUROC spans 0.640.64 (Mistral/TQA) to 0.890.89 (Qwen-7B/ARC).

Beyond multiple choice.

The wall is not an artifact of the multiple-choice format. On two exact-match-checkable free-response tasks, GSM8K and the harder MATH, self-knowledge stays imperfect (best-signal AUROC 0.580.58–0.850.85 across two models each, always <1<1; Appendix D).

4 The Cliff: Populations Lock Into Confident-Wrong Consensus

The cliff is not a separate phenomenon: it is what the wall looks like once agents revise each other. Because an agent cannot reliably filter its own unwarranted revision, a group whose members err in similar ways has no internal way to recover, so the outcome depends sharply on where the group starts (Figure 5). Groups that start mostly correct converge to truth; groups that start mostly wrong (the hard items) amplify the shared mistake into a confident, wrong consensus (final-consensus accuracy ≈0.00\approx 0.00–0.320.32 vs. 0.710.71–1.001.00 on TruthfulQA; 0.000.00–0.360.36 vs. 0.940.94–1.001.00 on ARC). The gap is significant in every run, and confident-wrong lock-in covers 2525–55%55\% of hard items on TruthfulQA and 55–18%18\% on the easier ARC.

The cliff’s location is not arbitrary. A simple model, evaluated at each model’s measured calibration with no fit to these runs, places the separatrix at p∗∈[0.357,0.391]p^{*}\in[0.357,0.391] across the four models, which falls at the observed crossover (Figure 5, dashed line p∗p^{*}; Appendix E), so the population transition sits where the measured per-agent quantities predict, without any fit to the runs.

The wall is what leaves the cliff un-braked. A hypothetical selective brake (a correctness detector of quality q=AUROCq=\mathrm{AUROC} that blocks harmful revisions at its best operating point) must reach q≳0.96q\gtrsim 0.96 to avert the collapse, whereas a brake at the measured self-knowledge ceiling (q≤0.89q\leq 0.89) leaves confident-wrong lock-in on the hardest items at 0.410.41–0.980.98 (Appendix E). The cliff is the population-scale consequence of the same bounded self-knowledge.

4.1 The driver is majority dynamics, not confidence

Hiding vs. showing peer confidence makes no difference to the lock-in, on both datasets; Wu et al. (2025) report the same. So the driver is majority dynamics on shared misconceptions, not confidence weighting, and we treat the mean-field model as illustrative rather than a validated mechanism.

4.2 The collapse resists the obvious fixes

None of the natural rescues works (Table 2). The lock-in is flat from N=6N{=}6 to N=24N{=}24 agents. A heterogeneous society scores below its best member in all three seeds (0.600.60–0.66<0.710.66<0.71–0.770.77 on TruthfulQA; corroborated on ARC, Appendix E). A correct minority is outvoted 7171–100%100\% of the time on the qualifying items.

5 What Helps: Acting Before the Revision

Since no post-hoc gate separates harmful from beneficial revisions, the useful levers act before or around it. Decorrelating the peer set helps: a unanimous wrong panel multiplies the harmful-revision rate ≈2.2×\approx 2.2\times over a mixed panel (2.35×2.35\times on ARC, 1.99×1.99\times on TruthfulQA), so not manufacturing a fake consensus is a deployable rule.

A pre-registered warrant (committing in advance to what would justify a change) is a preliminary transparency lever: in an ARC audit it exposes a subset of harmful revisions whose stated reasons depart from that pre-commitment, a signal post-hoc text classifiers miss (Appendix F). Both work with the wall rather than against it.

6 Related Work

Conformity, sycophancy, and peer influence.

LLMs bend to social pressure. Trained with human feedback, assistants come to favor user-matching responses over truthful ones (Sharma et al., 2023), a tendency measurable at scale (Perez et al., 2022) that splits into accuracy-improving and accuracy-harming revisions (Fanous et al., 2025); internally it is a largely linear, sometimes deep-layer override of intact knowledge (Wang et al., 2025a), and “not one thing”: genuine and sycophantic agreement are causally separable (Vennemeyer et al., 2025). The same bending appears between agents, as herd behavior and inter-agent sycophancy (Cho et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025), and at the single-agent level a wrong consensus flips correct answers more readily than a correct consensus repairs wrong ones (Qu et al., 2026). This work establishes that models conform and characterizes sycophancy behaviorally and internally. We take the step a deployed system needs next: whether the harmful half of that conformity can be filtered out after the revision, and show that doing so reduces to reading one’s own correctness.

Multi-agent debate and collective decision-making.

Debate, self-consistency, and voting are widely reported to improve accuracy (Du et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), though the gains are contested: a single well-prompted agent can match a discussion (Wang et al., 2024), and debate can entrench rather than correct an error (Wu et al., 2025). At population scale, individually aligned agents settle into a stable, collectively misaligned consensus past a tipping point (De Marzo et al., 2026; Ashery et al., 2025), and system-level taxonomies trace such failures to interaction structure rather than any single bad agent (Cemri et al., 2025; Bisconti et al., 2025). Some conditions recover debate: answer diversity with calibrated confidence updates (Zhu et al., 2026), or human- inclusive rather than pure-LLM groups (Sheffer et al., 2025). These results establish that collectives help or fail. We supply the micro-to-macro mechanism: a per-agent self-knowledge ceiling becomes a group-level, difficulty-gated confident-wrong cliff that survives the rescues: more agents, member heterogeneity (distinct from the answer-initialization diversity of Zhu et al. (2026)), a stronger member, and a correct minority.

Calibration, self-knowledge, and uncertainty signals.

A line of work asks whether models know what they know. Internal P​(True)P(\text{True}) signals exist but degrade out of distribution (Kadavath et al., 2022); hidden states carry a truth signal readable at 7171–83%83\% (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023); a probe at the exact-answer token reads correctness more sharply on some tasks yet is skill-specific and does not transfer across them (Orgad et al., 2025); verbalized confidence is systematically overconfident and worst on hard items (Xiong et al., 2024); and models cannot reliably self-correct their reasoning without external feedback (Huang et al., 2024). These signals read correctness imperfectly, and that imperfection is exactly the wall. Because separating harmful from beneficial revisions is reading correctness (Proposition 1), every such signal is a candidate brake and every one inherits the same ceiling; none is an independent post-hoc channel.

Activation steering and internal control.

Steering activations along a learned direction can change targeted behavior (Panickssery et al., 2023), but is often unstable or non-selective: truthfulness steering needs adaptive intensity and clustered vectors (Wang et al., 2025b), multi-attribute steering needs sparse orthogonal vectors to avoid cross-talk (Nguyen et al., 2025), directions transfer across models (Wang et al., 2025c), and a single self-preference direction flips both legitimate and illegitimate cases (Roytburg et al., 2025). Where one assumed direction conflates two functions, decomposing it can restore selectivity (Zhang and Sun, 2025; Vennemeyer et al., 2025). We run the analogous test on the correctness axis and obtain a negative result: steering the model’s own correctness direction moves revision propensity but not valence (Δ​H≈Δ​B\Delta H\approx\Delta B), so even white-box access yields no selective brake. Post-hoc detectors of rationale–answer inconsistency (Fan and Li, 2025) face the same bound on the revision set unless they add a channel independent of the answer.

Social dynamics and information cascades.

Confident-wrong consensus has classical roots: DeGroot averaging (DeGroot, 1974), bounded-confidence opinion dynamics (Hegselmann and Krause, 2002), and limits on the wisdom of crowds under influence concentration (Golub and Jackson, 2010), with herd and cascade behavior now ported to LLM groups (Cho et al., 2025; Cau et al., 2025). In human conformity, people bend to a wrong unanimous majority but a single dissenting ally breaks the spell (Asch, 1955), and a committed minority past a critical mass flips a convention (Centola et al., 2018; Ashery et al., 2025); adversarial minorities steer LLM debates the same way, modulated by interaction topology (Kraidia et al., 2026; Bashir et al., 2025; Mehdizadeh and Hilbert, 2026). None of these carries a correctness or calibration axis, and the human brake is absent: in our LLM societies the lone correct dissenter is not Asch’s liberating ally but is outvoted, so the cliff is governed by initial accuracy and (mis)calibration, not by influence topology alone.

7 Discussion

What the wall does and does not say.

The wall is about post-hoc control: it says a deployed system cannot tell its harmful revisions from its beneficial ones using a signal it already has, because that judgment is the same as knowing whether it was right. It does not say conformity is irrational or that debate is useless. It says debate cannot manufacture correctness the participants do not already hold, and that the natural knob (be more or less swayed by peers) moves good and bad revisions together.

When do collectives help, then?

Exactly when a member opens an independent channel to the answer: re-deriving the problem from scratch, calling a tool or retriever, or checking verifiable evidence. These add information the group did not have, which is what the wall requires and what a post-hoc text filter cannot supply. This reframes a common design choice: on hard items, scaling the number or diversity of agents trades against the cliff, while adding one verifier with a real channel to ground truth is what changes the outcome.

The wall is highest where it hurts most.

The individual limit and the population catastrophe are coupled by difficulty. Self-knowledge is not uniform: verbalized confidence is most overconfident on hard items (Xiong et al., 2024), and correctness probes are skill-specific rather than general (Orgad et al., 2025), so the wall rises exactly on the questions a model is most likely to answer wrong. Those are the same items on which a group starts mostly wrong and locks in (§4). The post-hoc brake is therefore weakest precisely where the population is most vulnerable: individual self-doubt and collective lock-in peak on the same inputs, which is why a fix has to add an outside channel rather than tune the group’s internal dynamics.

Practical reading.

This is why the levers that survive the wall (§5) are design and transparency choices rather than detectors: the only two routes that move the bound are raising calibration, so the wall sits higher, and wiring in an external verifier. Agreement among agents is not evidence that they are right: without an independent channel to the answer, debate redistributes the uncertainty the agents already have rather than reducing it.

8 Limitations

The reduction is an identity, so the wall’s strength is empirical and the measured AUROCs are lower bounds: the robust claim is that separability is well below 11. The steering result is on ARC for the two adequately-powered families, and the strong-α\alpha asymmetry on Qwen-3B is not a usable brake because it does not spare beneficial revision (Appendix C). The society runs are small and mostly same-family, on multiple-choice items. The mean-field model is illustrative, and the warrant arm needs human validation.

Several axes are left to future work; the reduction predicts each stays bounded unless it adds an independent channel to the answer. These include deploy-time utility curves under asymmetric harmful/beneficial costs (beyond AUROC); open-ended and tool- or retrieval-augmented settings and alternative debate protocols (rounds, aggregation rules); broader cross-provider and base-vs-instruct heterogeneity in the society experiments; further post-hoc signals such as rationale–answer consistency (Fan and Li, 2025) or text classifiers on revision rationales; and adaptive or token-wise steering (Wang et al., 2025b; Nguyen et al., 2025), which the reduction subsumes as further functions of deploy-time signals.

9 Conclusion

Filtering harmful peer revisions while keeping beneficial ones is the same task as judging one’s own correctness, and is therefore limited by self-knowledge. Empirically that ceiling is well below 11 and is reached but not exceeded by every deploy-time signal we test, including causal white-box steering. At population scale this limit combines with correlated errors and majority revision into a difficulty-gated, confident-wrong cliff that the obvious rescues do not prevent. The reliable way to lower correctness-uncertainty is to add information the system does not already hold: better-calibrated self-knowledge, or an independent, more capable verifier, not a post-hoc filter on the conversation.

Appendix A The reduction: selection slack and causal interventions

Proposition 1 gives the on-flip identity. Two refinements. (i) Selection slack. The conditional ceiling AUROCcorrect0∗​(S∣F)\mathrm{AUROC}^{*}_{\textit{correct}_{0}}(S\mid F) equals the global self-knowledge AUROCcorrect0∗​(S)\mathrm{AUROC}^{*}_{\textit{correct}_{0}}(S) iff flip⟂correct0∣S\text{flip}\perp\textit{correct}_{0}\mid S; the empirically small difference is the only gap. (ii) Causal interventions. For any post-processing S′=T​(S,U)S^{\prime}=T(S,U) with U⟂(correct0,V)U\perp(\textit{correct}_{0},V) (including activation steering a↦a+α​da\mapsto a+\alpha d along the diff-in-means correctness direction), the chain correct0→S→S′\textit{correct}_{0}\to S\to S^{\prime} is Markov, so by the data-processing inequality (Cover and Thomas, 2006) AUROCV∗​(S′∣F)≤AUROCcorrect0∗​(S∣F)\mathrm{AUROC}^{*}_{V}(S^{\prime}\mid F)\leq\mathrm{AUROC}^{*}_{\textit{correct}_{0}}(S\mid F). We treat this as motivation only, since steering also changes which items flip; the white-box case is settled empirically in Appendix C.

Appendix E Population: dynamics and the mean-field model

The basin numbers (§4) pool 88 TruthfulQA runs and 88 ARC runs; the per-condition rescue results are in Table 2 (main text). The minority sets are small (n≈5n\approx 5–2121 qualifying items per run), which we read with that caveat. Mean-field model.

Consider N→∞N\to\infty agents adopting the confidence-weighted local-majority answer. Let μ\mu be the error-correlation (fraction of wrong agents on one shared distractor) and r=wd/wcr=w_{d}/w_{c} the confidence-ratio of wrong to correct agents. The accuracy map has an unstable interior fixed point (the basin boundary; stated as Proposition 2)

p∗=μ​r1+μ​r,G′​(p∗)>1,p^{*}=\frac{\mu r}{1+\mu r},\qquad G^{\prime}(p^{*})>1, (1)

so the population is bistable with separatrix p∗p^{*}. At r=1r{=}1 this is the calibration-blind DeGroot/Golub–Jackson regime; the calibration axis rr is the addition. We estimate μ\mu as the fraction of wrong agents concentrated on the single most common distractor (per item, averaged) and rr as the ratio of mean round-1 confidence of wrong to correct agents; both are read from the round-1 kernels and are not fit to the society outcomes. From these, r≈0.91r\approx 0.91–1.051.05 (mean 0.950.95); averaging, Eq. (1) (μ=0.61\mu{=}0.61) gives a measured separatrix p∗≈0.368p^{*}\approx 0.368 and a calibrated counterfactual ≈0.315\approx 0.315 (gap ≈+0.05\approx+0.05), and the simulation reproduces both with no refit (0.3500.350/0.2830.283, gap +0.07+0.07). Because showing vs. hiding peer confidence does not change the live outcome (§4.1), rr enters only as a structural property, not an operator signal; we therefore read it as a reduced-form stickiness asymmetry and treat the empirical transition as the result.

Proposition 2 (Bistability).

Under the kernel above, GG has the unstable interior fixed point p∗=μ​r/(1+μ​r)p^{*}=\mu r/(1+\mu r) with G′​(p∗)>1G^{\prime}(p^{*})>1; the dynamics are bistable with separatrix p∗p^{*}, undergoing a transcritical bifurcation as μ​r→0+\mu r\to 0^{+}.

The wall bounds the brake.

We extend the dynamics above with a hypothetical selective brake: a correctness detector of quality q=AUROCq=\mathrm{AUROC} blocks harmful revisions at its Youden-optimal operating point (binormal equal-variance ROC, true/false positive rate TPR​(q)=Φ​(Φ−1​(q)/2)\mathrm{TPR}(q)=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(q)/\sqrt{2}), FPR=1−TPR\mathrm{FPR}=1-\mathrm{TPR}; q=0.5q{=}0.5 recovers the un-braked cliff, q=1q{=}1 is an ideal brake) and sweep qq (Figure 6). On the hardest items (groups starting ≥\geq4:1 wrong), confident-wrong lock-in falls from 1.001.00 (no brake) to 0.980.98 at q=0.64q{=}0.64 and 0.410.41 at q=0.89q{=}0.89, reaching ≤0.05\leq 0.05 only at q≳0.96q\gtrsim 0.96; the effective separatrix moves 0.36→0.28→0.140.36\to 0.28\to 0.14 over the same range. A brake anywhere in the measured self-knowledge band (q∈[0.64,0.89]q\in[0.64,0.89]) leaves the cliff largely intact, so averting it requires a selectivity the wall rules out.
