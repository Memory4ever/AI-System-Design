OAK: Restart- and Age-Aware Scheduling for Distributed Machine Learning on Shared GPU Clusters

Khaled Aljbab    Amine Barrak
Abstract

Distributed machine learning increasingly runs on shared multi-tenant GPU clusters where contention and failures are routine. Goodput-driven schedulers maximise instantaneous throughput but treat accumulated waiting time and restart cost as second-class signals: long-waiting jobs are repeatedly deferred, and the minutes of checkpoint loading paid after each interruption are not folded back into allocation decisions.

We present OAK, a per-round mixed-integer linear scheduler whose composite utility adds two first-class terms to goodput: an age key that elevates jobs with high cumulative waiting time, and a decomposed restart factor that tracks productive training time and checkpoint overhead as separately measured quantities rather than a single aggregate ratio.

We evaluate OAK against four representative goodput- and fairness-driven baselines in a trace-driven simulator on a 12-GPU cluster across four data-parallel workloads with controlled Poisson failure injection, and reproduce the failure-mode improvement on a 4-V100 real-hardware cluster.

Under failure-free conditions OAK matches the strongest goodput-driven baseline within 4%4\%. Under failures at rate λ=0.1\lambda=0.1 it reduces mean job completion time (JCT) by 33.933.9–58.3%58.3\% and worst-case JCT by 5858–76%76\% over its goodput-driven foundation; the decomposed restart factor alone accounts for a 4040–57%57\% mean and 5252–76%76\% tail JCT improvement against an aggregate restart estimator used in prior goodput-driven schedulers. Per-round decision latency is 7.657.65 ms, three orders of magnitude faster than evolutionary-search alternatives at default settings.

I Introduction

Deep-learning training has become a dominant workload on shared multi-tenant GPU clusters [1], and its operational cost has grown with model scale. A modern training run can occupy dozens of GPUs for days or weeks, so the cluster scheduler’s per-round allocation decisions directly determine job completion time (JCT)—how quickly users get results—how predictable tail latency looks for jobs bound by service-level agreements (SLAs), and how much of the cluster’s purchased capacity becomes useful work. Scheduling distributed machine-learning (DML) workloads differs from scheduling traditional batch jobs in two ways that make it substantially harder. First, individual jobs are long-lived and checkpoint-coupled: preempting a job is not free; it pays a real cost in time-to-resume from the last checkpoint, and that cost grows with model size [2, 3]. Second, jobs are failure-prone: at the scale of contemporary DML training, hardware and software faults are routine rather than exceptional [4]. A scheduler that treats every running job as immortal will repeatedly absorb restart cost that better allocations could have avoided.

Production measurements quantify both pressures. The MLaaS-in-the-Wild study of large-scale GPU clusters [1] reports that contention and heterogeneity dominate real-world workloads, with a non-trivial fraction of GPU-time consumed by failed or restarted jobs. Failure-recovery work for large models [4, 2, 3] similarly documents that recovery overhead scales poorly with model size and that frequent interruption of long-running training is a first-class operational concern rather than an edge case.

Existing schedulers address parts of this problem but not the combination. Goodput-driven schedulers such as Sia [5] and Pollux [6] re-decide GPU allocations every round to maximise throughput, but they do not surface accumulated waiting time as a first-class signal, and Pollux’s restart-aware factor folds productive training time and checkpoint overhead into a single aggregate estimator rather than tracking them separately. Fairness-driven schedulers such as Gavel [7] and DRF [8] target finish-time fairness or dominant-resource fairness but do not internalise restart cost in their allocation objectives. Straggler-aware execution [9, 10, 11] addresses worker-level latency variability rather than the cluster-allocation question. None of these lines combines goodput, accumulated waiting, and measured restart cost in a single per-round objective.

We propose OAK, a composite-utility scheduler that addresses both gaps within Sia’s goodput-driven foundation. OAK adds two first-class signals to the per-round score: an age key that encodes accumulated queue waiting as a continuous bounded multiplier, and a decomposed restart factor that tracks productive training time and checkpoint overhead as separately measured quantities rather than collapsing them into a single ratio. The combined score is plugged into the same per-round mixed-integer linear program Sia already uses, keeping the optimiser linear.

This paper makes the following contributions:

  • •

    We integrate goodput, restart cost, and accumulated waiting time in a single per-round mixed-integer linear program (MILP) with a sub-10 ms decision time at the 12-GPU scale. The same composite utility delivers Sia-beating failure resilience at this latency, a combination none of the four baselines achieves.

  • •

    We track productive training time and checkpoint/restore overhead as separately measured quantities, in contrast to Pollux’s aggregate multiplicative estimator. The decomposition alone yields a 4040–57%57\% mean JCT reduction and a 5252–76%76\% tail JCT reduction at λ=0.1\lambda=0.1.

  • •

    We report a trace-driven simulator comparison (OAK, Sia, Pollux, Gavel, DRF) on a 12-GPU cluster across four data-parallel workloads, supplemented by a 4-V100 real-hardware validation.111Code, configurations, and traces for reproducing the results are available at https://sites.google.com/oakland.edu/oak-icpads2026/home.

II Related Work

II-A Cluster Schedulers for Deep-Learning Training

Cluster-level scheduling for deep-learning workloads has been studied along two axes: allocation efficiency and goodput- or fairness-driven optimisation. Allocation-efficient schedulers such as Optimus [12], Aryl [13], DL2 [14], Titan [15], and Lucid [16] target specific operational concerns (predictive scaling, elasticity, GPU utilisation) but do not jointly optimise goodput, waiting time, and restart cost. The goodput- and fairness-driven line of work is the closest to OAK and includes DRF [8], Gavel [7], Pollux [6], and Sia [5]. Production GPU-cluster measurements [1] motivate this line by showing that contention and heterogeneity dominate real-world workloads, so a single scheduler must reconcile efficiency and fairness rather than optimise either alone.

Sia [5] provides our architectural foundation: configuration-based, heterogeneity-aware allocation that re-decides each job’s GPU assignment every round to maximise goodput, formulated as a per-round mixed-integer linear program. Pollux [6] shares the goodput objective but solves the joint allocation through NSGA II evolutionary search and includes a multiplicative restart-aware factor max⁡(Ai−ni⋅Prestart,0)/(Ai+Prestart)\max(A_{i}-n_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}},0)/(A_{i}+P_{\mathrm{restart}}) that aggregates progress and overhead into a single estimator. Gavel [7] treats fairness through finish-time-fairness or max-sum-throughput policies but does not internalise restart cost or accumulated waiting time. DRF [8] allocates dominant-resource shares without a goodput model.

OAK extends this line by adding waiting time and restart cost as first-class signals inside a single per-round score (G⋅r)p⋅K(G\cdot r)^{p}\cdot K: the age key KiK_{i} encodes waiting time as a bounded multiplier, and the restart factor rir_{i} replaces Pollux’s aggregate estimator with directly measured progress and checkpoint time. Table I summarises these distinctions; none of the four baselines combines goodput with both signals in one objective.

II-B Restart Cost and Recovery in DML Training

A complementary line of systems work targets the cost of an individual restart by improving checkpoint and recovery mechanisms at the runtime layer. MoEtion [3] reduces checkpoint overhead for mixture-of-experts training, while MoC-System [17] and the asynchronous checkpointing approach for large language models [2] drive down the wall-clock cost of saving and restoring model state during training. At the failure-recovery layer, Unicron [4] targets large-model training by overlapping recovery with surviving-replica progress, and adaptive fault-tolerance schemes for cloud LLM training [18] combine partial reload with selective re-execution to amortise recovery cost.

A second related thread operates below the scheduler at the worker and network level. Straggler-aware execution [9, 10, 11] addresses latency variability among workers during a single training step rather than across allocation decisions. Network and transport designs for distributed training [19, 20, 21] optimise gradient synchronisation and in-network aggregation paths but leave the per-round allocation policy untouched.

These approaches are largely orthogonal to cluster scheduling: they reduce the cost paid for a single recovery event but do not feed restart-induced inefficiency back into the allocation objective. A scheduler that ignores accumulated checkpoint time will keep preempting the same job, paying the recovery cost repeatedly.

OAK is complementary to runtime-level checkpoint and recovery systems. Rather than reducing the cost of a single restart, the decomposed restart factor rir_{i} feeds accumulated checkpoint time back into the per-round score, steering the scheduler away from configurations that would generate further restart cost in the first place.

III Design of OAK

OAK is a scheduler for distributed machine learning (DML) clusters that adds two extensions to Sia’s existing design [5]: an age key that prioritises jobs with long accumulated waiting time, and a restart factor that penalises configurations whose effective progress is eroded by checkpoint and recovery overhead. Both terms are folded into the same per-round scoring function alongside goodput, so the scheduler weighs efficiency, waiting time, and restart cost together in one decision.

Relationship to Sia.

OAK extends Sia rather than replacing it. The only thing OAK changes is the per-pair (job, configuration) score that the ILP maximises: it adds an age-key term KiK_{i} and a restart-factor term rir_{i} to the goodput term Gi​jG_{ij} that Sia already uses. Every other Sia component is reused without modification:

  • •

    The per-job set of candidate configurations CiC_{i} (replica counts and GPU types Sia considers feasible for job ii);

  • •

    The trace-based goodput estimate Gi​jG_{ij} that predicts each (job, configuration) throughput;

  • •

    The placement step that, after the optimiser decides how many GPUs each job receives, maps that count to specific GPU IDs on specific nodes;

  • •

    The optimisation back-end, the same mixed-integer linear program is built with the CVXPY modelling library [22] and solved with the GLPK_MI integer solver, both of which are open source and standard in the operations-research toolchain.

Because OAK and Sia differ only in the score, any JCT difference between them in our experiments can be attributed to the age key and the restart factor.

III-A Scheduling Model and Notation

OAK schedules in rounds. Round tt begins when a fixed timer fires (every 60 s in our experiments) or when the active job set changes (a job arrives, completes, or is preempted). At the start of each round the scheduler reads the current cluster state, picks an allocation, and applies it until the next round.

We use the following notation throughout.

  • •

    Cluster. G={g1,…,gm}G=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\} is the set of GPU types present in the cluster, and RgR_{g} is the number of GPUs of type gg. Each GPU is assigned to at most one job per round (no time-sharing within a round).

  • •

    Active jobs. JtJ_{t} is the set of jobs active at round tt — those that have been submitted but have not yet completed.

  • •

    Configurations. For each job ii, the candidate set CiC_{i} lists the configurations the job can run. A configuration j∈Cij\in C_{i} specifies a target GPU type, a replica count, and the per-type GPU demand dg​(i,j)d_{g}(i,j).

Before the round-level optimisation runs, OAK filters CiC_{i} to drop configurations that exceed per-GPU memory budgets or violate workload-specific constraints. As a result, the per-pair score Ui​jU_{ij} and the ILP structure depend only on the surviving configurations; they do not depend on which jobs happen to be active in a given round.

Two per-job temporal counters drive OAK’s new utility terms:

  • •

    Tqueue,i​(t)T_{\mathrm{queue},i}(t): how long job ii has been waiting without GPUs. Used by the age key to boost long-waiting jobs.

  • •

    Tckpt,i​(t)T_{\mathrm{ckpt},i}(t): how long job ii has spent in checkpoint or restore. Used by the restart factor to penalise jobs whose progress is eroded by overhead.

The job’s age is Ai​(t)A_{i}(t), and its productive training time is Tprogress,i​(t)=Ai​(t)−Tckpt,i​(t)T_{\mathrm{progress},i}(t)=A_{i}(t)-T_{\mathrm{ckpt},i}(t).

III-B Composite Utility

For each pair (job ii, feasible configuration jj), OAK assigns a score

Ui​j​(t)=(Gi​j⋅ri​(t))p⋅Ki​(t),U_{ij}(t)=\big(G_{ij}\cdot r_{i}(t)\big)^{\,p}\cdot K_{i}(t), (1)

where Gi​jG_{ij} is the goodput estimate inherited from Sia, ri​(t)∈(0,1]r_{i}(t)\in(0,1] is the restart factor (down-weights jobs eroded by checkpoint and recovery overhead), and Ki​(t)≥1K_{i}(t)\geq 1 is the age key (boosts long-waiting jobs).

The fairness exponent pp shapes how much additional goodput contributes to the score: at p=0.5p=0.5 (used throughout) doubling goodput multiplies the score by only 2\sqrt{2}, so the scheduler prefers high-goodput allocations without chasing them aggressively. This produces soft fairness rather than strict max-min behaviour. Negative values of pp cause the score (Gi​j⋅ri)p(G_{ij}\cdot r_{i})^{p} to diverge when ri→0r_{i}\to 0 (a job dominated by checkpoint time), so we restrict p≥0p\geq 0 here and leave strictly fair regimes for future work. Assigning separate exponents to Gi​jG_{ij} and rir_{i} is a natural generalisation; we couple them here because both express per-round execution efficiency, and leave independent shaping to future work.

The age key KiK_{i} sits outside the power: this keeps the waiting-time boost as a bounded multiplicative signal whose magnitude is independent of the goodput-shaping exponent pp. Placing KiK_{i} inside the power would couple its effect to pp and require re-tuning the age-key parameters every time pp changes.

Per-round optimisation.

At each round, OAK chooses binary variables xi​j​(t)∈{0,1}x_{ij}(t)\in\{0,1\}, where xi​j=1x_{ij}=1 means job ii is assigned configuration jj:

max{xi​j}\displaystyle\max_{\{x_{ij}\}}\qquad ∑i∈Jt∑j∈Cixi​j​Ui​j​(t)+μna​∑i∈Jt(1−∑j∈Cixi​j)\displaystyle\sum_{i\in J_{t}}\sum_{j\in C_{i}}x_{ij}\,U_{ij}(t)+\mu_{\mathrm{na}}\sum_{i\in J_{t}}\Big(1-\sum_{j\in C_{i}}x_{ij}\Big) (2)
s.t. ∑j∈Cixi​j≤1,\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{j\in C_{i}}x_{ij}\leq 1, ∀i∈Jt\displaystyle\forall i\in J_{t}
∑i,jxi​j​dg​(i,j)≤Rg,\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\,d_{g}(i,j)\leq R_{g}, ∀g∈G\displaystyle\forall g\in G

The constant μna=1.1\mu_{\mathrm{na}}=1.1 acts as a utility threshold for allocation. The solver commits GPUs to job ii only if at least one of its feasible utilities Ui​jU_{ij} exceeds μna\mu_{\mathrm{na}}; if every Ui​jU_{ij} for that job falls below the threshold, leaving the job idle is more attractive to the objective than any of its candidate allocations, because the unallocated branch contributes μna\mu_{\mathrm{na}} per idle job. The power transformation (Gi​j⋅ri)p(G_{ij}\cdot r_{i})^{p} from Eq. 1 is precomputed before the optimisation step, so the solver receives a purely linear integer program over |Jt|⋅maxi⁡|Ci||J_{t}|\cdot\max_{i}|C_{i}| binary variables.

III-C Restart Factor and Age Key

Restart factor.

OAK’s decomposed restart factor is

ri​(t)=max⁡(Tprogress,i​(t), 0)Tprogress,i​(t)+Tckpt,i​(t)+Prestart,i,r_{i}(t)=\frac{\max\!\big(T_{\mathrm{progress},i}(t),\,0\big)}{T_{\mathrm{progress},i}(t)+T_{\mathrm{ckpt},i}(t)+P_{\mathrm{restart},i}}, (3)

where Tprogress,i​(t)=Ai​(t)−Tckpt,i​(t)T_{\mathrm{progress},i}(t)=A_{i}(t)-T_{\mathrm{ckpt},i}(t) is the job’s productive training time (Section III-A) and Prestart,iP_{\mathrm{restart},i} is the per-application restart penalty (covering checkpoint loading, state reconstruction, and warm-up). Checkpoint and recovery time freezes the numerator (the job ages without training) while inflating the denominator through Tckpt,iT_{\mathrm{ckpt},i}; both effects lower rir_{i}. For freshly submitted jobs, where Ai​(t)<Prestart,iA_{i}(t)<P_{\mathrm{restart},i}, the formula is overridden to ri=1r_{i}=1 so that the score is not penalised before the job has had a chance to make progress. Note that rir_{i} penalises restart-heavy configurations, not users: a job repeatedly hit by infrastructure faults loses score through rir_{i}, but its queue waiting simultaneously accumulates in KiK_{i}, which partially offsets the penalty. Distinguishing user-caused from system-caused failures in the score is left to future work.

Contrast with Pollux.

Pollux [6] down-weights restart-heavy jobs through an aggregate factor that estimates the time lost to restarts as ni⋅Prestartn_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}} (restart count times a constant penalty). The difference is precision: this approximation accumulates estimation error as restarts pile up, whereas OAK’s directly measured Tckpt,iT_{\mathrm{ckpt},i} remains exact at any failure rate. Section V-D shows the two formulas side by side and isolates the empirical effect of this design choice.

Age key.

The age key turns the queue-waiting counter Tqueue,i​(t)T_{\mathrm{queue},i}(t) into a multiplicative score boost that grows with waiting time but never exceeds a fixed cap:

Ki​(t)=min⁡(exp⁡(α​Tqueue,i​(t)),Kmax),K_{i}(t)=\min\!\Big(\exp\big(\alpha\,T_{\mathrm{queue},i}(t)\big),\;K_{\max}\Big), (4)

The exponential term raises a job’s score the longer it waits, while the upper bound KmaxK_{\max} prevents a single starved job from dominating the cluster. We use α=0.01\alpha=0.01 and Kmax=100K_{\max}=100 in all experiments. These defaults give waiting jobs a gradual priority boost without letting any single job dominate the cluster. The cap serves two purposes. First, it bounds the worst-case throughput cost of prioritising any single job: without a cap, a sufficiently aged job would dominate the round regardless of efficiency, and a user could exploit this by submitting jobs designed to accumulate waiting time. Second, Kmax=100K_{\max}=100 is deliberately above the values reached in our workloads: it acts as a robustness guard rather than an active tuning knob, so operators need not recalibrate it per workload. We use an exponential rather than linear aging law so that priority accelerates with starvation: a linear key raises long- and briefly-waiting jobs at the same rate, whereas the exponential form keeps briefly delayed jobs near Ki≈1K_{i}\approx 1 while sharply promoting chronically starved ones before the cap binds.

IV Methodology

IV-A Hardware and Simulator

All experiments run on a high performance computing cluster managed by SLURM. The cluster provides three GPU compute nodes (12 V100 GPUs in total); each node has 4 NVIDIA Tesla V100 16 GB GPUs with NVLink, 192 GB RAM, and 48 CPU cores at 2.10 GHz, connected via HDR100 InfiniBand at 100 Gbps.

The OAK simulator is built on the Sia simulator codebase [5] and models job arrivals, allocation decisions, goodput estimation, checkpoint and restore dynamics, and queue management within a configurable cluster. The simulator supports pluggable scheduling policies, which enables A/B comparisons between OAK and baselines under identical workload and cluster conditions. Every scheduler we compare against (OAK, Sia, Pollux, Gavel, DRF) runs a new scheduling round once every 60 seconds.

Goodput estimates Gi​jG_{ij} that feed every scheduler are trace driven: per application step time and scaling traces collected on AWS T4 GPUs are inherited from the Sia codebase, and the simulator interpolates between trace points for unobserved (batch size, replica count) combinations. All scheduler comparisons in this paper are paired (same workload, same submission times, same Poisson failure stream, same trace based Gi​jG_{ij} values), so the relative JCT differences reported below are invariant to the simulator’s absolute throughput calibration.

IV-B Workloads

Workloads are derived from real deep learning training profiles. They cover three job families: computer vision (CIFAR-10, ImageNet, YOLOv3), natural language processing (BERT), and speech (DeepSpeech2). Each job has a GPU demand (replica count), runtime dynamics taken from profiling traces, and per application checkpoint and restart behaviour modelled by the simulator. Job arrivals follow a time indexed submission process: each job has a submission timestamp and enters the queue at that time, with the trace timing chosen to induce overlap and contention so scheduling decisions have measurable impact.

We use four contention scenarios that escalate the queue pressure on the cluster: workload-small (4 jobs, light load, validates correctness), workload-medium (12 jobs, typical cluster usage), workload-congestion (24 jobs, resource scarcity), and workload-stress (40 jobs, the queue is consistently full).

IV-C Composite Utility Solver

At each scheduling round OAK solves a mixed integer linear program (MILP). The composite utility uses a power transformation Ui​jpU_{ij}^{p} with p=0.5p=0.5; we precompute the exponentiation as U~i​j=(Gi​j⋅ri⋅Ki)p\tilde{U}_{ij}=(G_{ij}\cdot r_{i}\cdot K_{i})^{p} in NumPy before the values reach the solver, so the solver itself sees a purely linear objective over binary allocation variables xi​j∈{0,1}x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}. The MILP is formulated in CVXPY [22] and solved with the GLPK_MI backend, with an automatic fallback to CBC if GLPK_MI errors. At our cluster scale (about 14 jobs, 10 configurations per job, 12 GPUs) per round solve times stay below 100 ms, well within the 60 second scheduling interval.

The simulator filters infeasible configurations (those exceeding GPU memory budgets or violating workload-specific resource constraints) before they reach the solver. These checks operate on the input set CiC_{i} (the feasible configurations for each job) rather than inside the optimisation, so the score and solver structure are unchanged.

IV-D Failure Injection and Restart Factor

Failures are injected as a Poisson process at rate λ\lambda per round per active job. When a job fails the simulator adds the per application restart penalty to a per job counter Tckpt,iT_{\mathrm{ckpt},i} that accumulates total time spent in checkpoint and restore. The simulator also tracks a queue waiting time Tqueue,iT_{\mathrm{queue},i} that grows when the job has no allocation and when it is allocated but not making training progress (i.e., during a checkpoint or restore). Both counters feed OAK’s restart factor and age key.

OAK’s restart factor extends the aggregate reallocation form used by Sia and Pollux, ri=max⁡(Ai−ni⋅Prestart,0)/(Ai+Prestart)r_{i}=\max(A_{i}-n_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}},0)/(A_{i}+P_{\mathrm{restart}}), which estimates the time lost across nin_{i} restarts as ni⋅Prestartn_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}}. OAK instead measures the loss directly: the simulator-tracked counter Tckpt,iT_{\mathrm{ckpt},i} replaces the ni⋅Prestartn_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}} estimate inside the decomposed factor of Section III-C, so the penalty remains exact at any failure rate. For newly submitted jobs (where Ai<PrestartA_{i}<P_{\mathrm{restart}}) we set ri=1r_{i}=1 so the score is not penalised before the job has had a chance to make progress.

The per-application restart penalties used throughout this paper are: CIFAR-10: 5050 s; DeepSpeech2: 2525 s; BERT: 120120 s; YOLOv3: 8080 s; ImageNet: 250250 s; NCF: 2525 s; GPT (pipeline-parallel): 3030 s. A 3030 s fallback is used for applications not in the map. These values are configured to reflect the relative recovery cost of each model family; Section V-A quantifies the scheduler’s sensitivity to this parameter.

IV-E Baselines

We compare OAK against four baseline schedulers, chosen to span the spectrum from pure fairness to pure efficiency:

  • •

    Sia [5]: the goodput optimised, heterogeneity aware ILP scheduler on which OAK is built (Section III). We run Sia with its default fairness exponent (p=0.5p=0.5).

  • •

    Pollux [6]: NSGA II multi objective optimiser jointly considering goodput and statistical efficiency. We run Pollux at its published default NSGA II settings (population 100100, generations 100100) throughout.

  • •

    Gavel [7]: a configurable, heterogeneity aware scheduler. We run Gavel with the finish-time fairness (FTF) policy, in which allocations are chosen by a linear program that minimises the maximum ratio of each job’s actual finish time to its proportional-share finish time. Gavel is deterministic.

  • •

    DRF [8]: Dominant Resource Fairness; allocates GPU shares so that each job receives an equal fraction of its dominant resource. We use the standard formulation with no throughput optimisation; DRF is deterministic.

IV-F Statistical Methodology

Each experiment is repeated across NN independent random seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation across the NN runs. We use cell to mean one specific combination of scheduler, workload, and failure rate; for example, OAK on workload-medium at λ=0.10\lambda=0.10 is one cell. The seed count NN varies by experiment:

  • •

    The main OAK vs Sia comparison across the four workloads uses N=10N=10 seeds per cell.

  • •

    The failure rate sweep on workload-medium and workload-congestion (the regime the paper focuses on) uses N=30N=30 seeds per cell, because each seed produces a different sequence of failure events and a larger sample is needed to estimate the mean reliably.

  • •

    Sensitivity sweeps and the four scheduler Pollux comparison use N=5N=5 seeds per cell, with N=10N=10 for Pollux to characterise its slightly higher seed-to-seed variance under failures.

In the four scheduler comparison, all schedulers share the same seed values. Seed 42 with OAK, seed 42 with Sia, and seed 42 with Pollux all receive the same job submission times and the same per round Poisson failure events.

V OAK Scheduler Evaluation

V-A Parameter Defaults and Calibration

V-A1 Calibration Sweep Methodology

OAK exposes four scheduler-level parameters: the fairness exponent pp, the age-key sensitivity α\alpha, the age-key cap KmaxK_{\max}, and the per-application restart penalty PrestartP_{\mathrm{restart}}. We calibrate each by sweeping it independently on workload-medium at λ=0.05\lambda=0.05 with the others held fixed (N=5N=5 seeds per cell). Figure 2 summarises the resulting sweeps.

V-A2 Sensitivity Findings

Three findings emerge from the sweep.

Insensitive parameters: pp, α\alpha, KmaxK_{\max}.

Mean JCT varies by less than 1.51.5% across the tested range for each of these three parameters, and the chosen defaults sit within 11% of the per-sweep minimum. The age-key sensitivity α\alpha saturates above 0.050.05 (no further effect at α≥0.05\alpha\geq 0.05); the age-key cap KmaxK_{\max} is rarely reached, since few jobs accumulate enough waiting time for KiK_{i} to approach Kmax=100K_{\max}=100 in our experiments.

The sensitive parameter: PrestartP_{\mathrm{restart}}.

PrestartP_{\mathrm{restart}} varies mean JCT by 1212% across the tested range (32,37732{,}377 s at 1010 s vs 36,24636{,}246 s at 120120 s). Lower values let OAK preempt more aggressively when failures are rare, trading some failure resilience for throughput. This sensitivity motivates a per-application lookup rather than a single fallback constant, so each job’s restart cost matches its actual recovery cost (Section IV-D).

Excluded value: p=0p=0.

p=0p=0 collapses the goodput-restart product to a constant and leaves only KiK_{i} in the score, eliminating the optimisation gradient on goodput. Non-degenerate pp is required for the composite utility to function as designed; we exclude p=0p=0 from the sweep.

V-A3 Selected Defaults

All experiments below use p=0.5p=0.5 (square-root shaping of the goodput-restart product: diminishing returns to additional goodput while keeping a non-zero gradient), α=0.01\alpha=0.01 (KiK_{i} reaches KmaxK_{\max} after roughly 460460 s of accumulated waiting, about eight rounds of zero progress), Kmax=100K_{\max}=100 (a meaningful boost for chronically waiting jobs, but bounded so a single starved job cannot dominate the round), and the per-application PrestartP_{\mathrm{restart}} table of Section IV-D, chosen per application because the sweep identifies PrestartP_{\mathrm{restart}} as the only sensitive parameter: a single constant would over-penalise short-recovery jobs (CIFAR-10, DeepSpeech2) or under-penalise long-recovery ones (BERT, ImageNet).

V-D Decomposed Restart Factor: Empirical Effect

The restart factor is OAK’s primary technical refinement over prior goodput driven schedulers. The contrast is clearest when the two formulations are seen side by side.

OAK’s decomposed factor measures productive training time and checkpoint/restore overhead as separate quantities tracked by the simulator:

riOAK​(t)=Tprogress,iTprogress,i+Tckpt,i+Prestart,i.r_{i}^{\mathrm{OAK}}(t)=\frac{T_{\mathrm{progress},i}}{T_{\mathrm{progress},i}+T_{\mathrm{ckpt},i}+P_{\mathrm{restart},i}}. (5)

Pollux’s aggregate factor instead estimates the time lost to restarts as ni⋅Prestartn_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}}, namely the restart count times a constant penalty:

riPollux​(t)=max⁡(Ai​(t)−ni⋅Prestart, 0)Ai​(t)+Prestart.r_{i}^{\mathrm{Pollux}}(t)=\frac{\max(A_{i}(t)-n_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}},\,0)}{A_{i}(t)+P_{\mathrm{restart}}}. (6)

Both formulas downweight jobs with high restart cost; the difference is precision. Pollux’s ni⋅Prestartn_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}} approximation accumulates estimation error as restarts pile up, while OAK’s tracked TckptT_{\mathrm{ckpt}} remains exact at any failure rate.

OAK vs Pollux: controlled A/B comparison.

To isolate the empirical effect of this single design choice, we run a controlled A/B comparison in which every other component of OAK is held constant (age key, goodput estimator, ILP solver, scheduling cadence, workloads) and only the restart factor formula is varied between Equations 5 and 6. The experiment covers two workloads, namely ckpt-variance (a mix of models with very different per restart costs) and medium, at two failure rates (λ=0.05\lambda=0.05 and λ=0.10\lambda=0.10), with N=10N=10 seeds per cell. Figure 5 reports mean and tail JCT for both formulations.

OAK’s decomposition consistently outperforms the aggregate formulation, and the gap widens with failure rate. Concretely, at λ=0.05\lambda=0.05 both formulas produce essentially the same mean JCT (within 33%) because few restarts happen and the estimation error in ni⋅Prestartn_{i}\cdot P_{\mathrm{restart}} is negligible; at λ=0.10\lambda=0.10, the gap opens to 4040% on ckpt-variance and 5757% on medium, where the aggregate formula’s accumulated estimation error materially affects the scheduler’s reallocation decisions. OAK’s decomposed formula is also far more consistent across random seeds than Pollux’s aggregate formula: its cross seed JCT standard deviation is 99 to 13×13\times smaller across both workloads and both failure rates. This consistency lets operators commit to tighter SLA bounds without over provisioning capacity to hedge against bad luck runs.

V-E Hybrid OAK: NSGA II as the OAK Scheduler

OAK and Pollux offer complementary strengths: OAK encodes restart cost explicitly through the decomposed factor and age key, while Pollux’s NSGA II absorbs failures incidentally through frequent reallocation. Hybrid OAK combines both by evaluating OAK’s score

Ui​j=(Gi​j⋅ri)p⋅KiU_{ij}=\big(G_{ij}\cdot r_{i}\big)^{p}\cdot K_{i}

inside the same NSGA II loop Pollux uses, in place of Pollux’s internal aggregate restart factor. The components keep their OAK meaning (Section III), with p=0.5p=0.5.

Setup.

Hybrid OAK uses NSGA II at population =20=20, generations =20=20, chosen to keep per round decision latency below one second. We sweep λ∈{0,0.05,0.10,0.15}\lambda\in\{0,0.05,0.10,0.15\} on the medium and congestion workloads with N=5N=5 seeds per cell, sharing the same simulator and Poisson failure stream as OAK and Sia.

Decision time vs JCT trade off.

We instrumented all schedulers to record per round wall clock decision time. OAK’s mean per-round solver time, measured with a 12-GPU configuration, is 7.657.65 ms (564 rounds across all simulated runs), nearly identical to Sia’s 7.537.53 ms (6,859 rounds); Pollux’s NSGA II based optimiser costs 9,9099{,}909 ms per round (344 rounds measured), making OAK 1,295×1{,}295\times faster per decision than Pollux. Hybrid OAK sits between the two regimes at ∼\sim285 ms per round, an order of magnitude above OAK but two orders of magnitude below Pollux at default.

On the JCT side, Hybrid OAK is markedly more resilient to failures than pure OAK and starts competing with Pollux across the sweep, with the contrast most pronounced at λ=0.15\lambda=0.15 where pure OAK collapses: on medium, pure OAK has mean JCT 78,94978{,}949 s with high cross seed variance (std 26,61926{,}619 s; 22 of 1010 seeds finish in ∼25,000\sim 25{,}000 s, 88 of 1010 collapse to ∼92,000\sim 92{,}000 s), while Hybrid OAK reaches 19,63419{,}634 s consistently (std 263263 s), within 55% of pure Pollux’s 18,65118{,}651 s; on congestion, pure OAK 77,14877{,}148 s vs Hybrid OAK 36,21536{,}215 s, a 5353% reduction.

Scheduler choice.

The implication is that OAK’s composite utility, (G⋅r)p⋅K(G\cdot r)^{p}\cdot K with the decomposed restart factor and age key, is a solver agnostic framework. The choice of optimiser (linear ILP vs NSGA II) is an orthogonal speed/quality knob: the ILP variant gives sub 10 ms decisions and is sufficient at low to moderate failure rates (λ≤0.10\lambda\leq 0.10); the NSGA II variant costs sub second per decision but extends the same composite utility’s reach into the high failure regime (λ=0.15\lambda=0.15) where ILP based stable allocation is fundamentally exposed. An operator can therefore select between OAK’s ILP variant (production deployments where decision latency dominates) and OAK’s NSGA II variant (research clusters or settings with extreme failure density) using the same scoring function.

VI Threats to Validity

Internal validity. Every scheduler comparison is paired (same workload, Poisson failure stream, trace-derived Gi​jG_{ij}, and seeds), so reported JCT differences are invariant to absolute throughput calibration; Section V-A bounds parameter sensitivity below 1.5% for pp, α\alpha, and KmaxK_{\max}.

External validity. Our simulated cluster is 12 GPUs and our real-hardware verification is 4 GPUs, while production clusters span hundreds to thousands; behaviour at that scale is untested. Evaluation is restricted to data-parallel jobs and Poisson failures; parameter-server, pipeline-parallel, mixed-GPU, and bursty-failure regimes are left to future work.

Construct validity. Most results are simulator based and abstract over NCCL overhead, CPU-bound data pipelines, and node-level failure correlation. The 4-V100 real-hardware experiment in Section V-F reproduces the simulator’s directional ordering and decision-time gap on physical hardware, though the small cluster size means we treat it as directional rather than quantitative. We also report tail (worst-case) JCT alongside the mean so SLA-relevant outcomes are visible.

VII Conclusion

Distributed deep-learning training on shared GPU clusters suffers from contention among long-lived jobs and routine failures, which goodput-driven schedulers treat as second-class signals. We presented OAK, a per-round mixed-integer linear scheduler whose composite utility (G⋅r)p⋅K(G\cdot r)^{p}\cdot K adds an age key KiK_{i} that encodes accumulated waiting as a bounded multiplier, and a decomposed restart factor rir_{i} that tracks productive training time and checkpoint overhead separately rather than collapsing them into a single ratio. Evaluated against Sia, Pollux, Gavel, and DRF in a trace-driven 12-GPU simulator under Poisson failure injection and reproduced on a 4-V100 real-hardware cluster, the age key compresses tail JCT on contention-heavy workloads, while the decomposed restart factor alone yields a 4040–57%57\% mean and 5252–76%76\% tail JCT improvement over an aggregate Pollux-style estimator at λ=0.1\lambda=0.1.

Hybrid OAK further shows the composite utility is solver-agnostic: plugged into a reduced-population NSGA II, it retains genetic search’s reallocation robustness in the high-failure regime at a fraction of Pollux’s per-round decision cost.

Future work targets scaling the real-hardware validation beyond 4 GPUs, extending the composite utility to pipeline-parallel regimes, and characterising Hybrid OAK’s population and iteration trade-off across failure densities.
