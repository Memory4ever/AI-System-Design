                                                                                       Abstract
                                             A common architectural pattern in advanced AI reasoning systems is the symbolic graph
                                         network: a collection of specialized agents or modules connected by typed delegation edges,
                                         routing tasks through a dynamic execution graph. Agent orchestration frameworks, modular
                                         reasoning pipelines, and self-improving multi-agent engines are all instances of this pattern, and
                                         they share a structural blind spot. Their schedulers optimize load and fitness, but carry no
                                         model of how failure propagates differently depending on whether the current execution graph
                                         is tree-like, cyclic, or mixed. In tree-like delegation regimes, a single agent failure cascades
                                         exponentially through the hierarchy; in dense cyclic regimes, the same failure self-limits within
                                         one hop. A geometry-blind scheduler cannot distinguish these cases. This paper identifies the
                                         blind spot, quantifies its cost, and proposes a lightweight mitigation. Prior geometry-aware graph
                                         methods assume a static dataset with a fixed structural regime; multi-agent routing methods
                                         optimize delegation without modeling geometry or failure propagation. Neither addresses the
                                         runtime question: as the execution graph shifts between structural regimes under churn and
                                         noise, how should a route-risk estimator choose its geometric prior?
                                             We cast this as adaptive geometry selection on a time-indexed execution graph with route-
                                         local failure propagation. Our model combines: (i) a Euclidean spatio-temporal baseline, (ii) a
                                         hyperbolic route-risk model with temporal decay and an optional repeat-failure burst-excitation
                                         term inspired by self-exciting point processes, and (iii) a learned geometry selector over structural
                                         graph features. The selector is a compact multilayer perceptron (9 → 12 → 1) whose inputs
                                         combine six topology statistics with three geometry-aware features: breadth-first search (BFS)
                                         shell-growth slope, cycle-rank norm, and fitted Poincaré curvature. On the heterogeneous
                                         benchmark, the learned selector resolves the principal failure mode of fixed hyperbolic models:
                                         in the hardest non_tree regime it improves win rate from 64–72% for fixed hyperbolic variants
                                         to 92%, while achieving 87.2% overall win rate across all stress conditions on the Genesis 3
                                         benchmark distribution. A preliminary cross-architecture study on Barabási–Albert, Watts–
                                         Strogatz, and Erdős–Rényi graphs (Appendix I) confirms that structural propagation modeling
                                         generalises perfectly to all three families; the trained geometry gate requires retraining for
                                         out-of-distribution topologies.
                                             To quantify total system value, we also evaluate Genesis 3 routing without any spatio-temporal
                                         sidecar, using only the signals available to the native bandit/LinUCB scheduler: team fitness
                                         and mean node load. This native baseline achieves 50.4% win rate overall and only 20% in
                                         tree-like regimes (clean and noise). Adding the full sidecar recovers 87.2% overall, a +36.8
                                         percentage-point system-level gain. In tree-like regimes the gain reaches +48 to +68 points,
                                         matching the prediction of our cascade-sensitivity analysis (Proposition 4): when graph expansion
                                         is exponential, node load alone does not reveal propagation risk.
                                             The paper identifies geometry-blindness as a failure-propagation observability gap in execution-
                                         graph agent systems, not previously characterized to our knowledge across the dynamic-graph,
                                         multi-agent routing, or agent systems literature. It formalizes the gap as an online geometry-
                                         control problem, isolates its operational cost through a system-level ablation (50.4% vs 87.2%

     The results support a general principle: execution-graph agent systems require a geometry-aware

1    Introduction
A prevailing approach to building advanced AI reasoning systems is, at its core, symbolic graph
network construction. Agent orchestration frameworks (e.g., LangGraph, AutoGen [1], CrewAI),
modular reasoning pipelines, mixture-of-experts routing layers, and self-improving multi-agent
engines all share the same fundamental architecture: agents are nodes, delegation relationships are
directed edges, and executing a task means routing a signal through a time-varying graph. The
same structure appears in the leading neuro-symbolic AGI research systems: AlphaGeometry [3]
routes reasoning through a geometric construct graph; OpenCog Hyperon [2] stores all knowledge
as a metagraph and runs every cognitive operation as graph-rewriting over it. This convergence on
graph-structured execution is not accidental — graphs are the natural representation for distributed
reasoning.
    But this architectural consensus creates a shared, unexamined blind spot. No matter how
sophisticated the individual agents, the scheduler that routes tasks through the graph is geometry-
blind: it sees node load and agent fitness, but it does not model how the current shape of the graph
determines how a single agent failure propagates. In tree-like delegation regimes, one failure cascades
exponentially through the BFS shell structure: the failure amplification grows with depth. In dense
cyclic regimes, the same failure self-limits within one or two hops. The scheduler cannot tell the
difference. Any execution-graph system in this paradigm that routes tasks without a geometry-aware
failure-propagation signal carries this observability gap, silently.
    We make that blind spot concrete, measure its operational cost, and close it. Genesis 3 is a
self-improving multi-agent reasoning engine whose execution graph shifts between tree-like, cyclic,
and mixed regimes as its agent team evolves; it is the system in which we measure and fix the
problem. The geometry-blind native scheduler achieves only 50.4% overall win rate and 20% in
tree-like regimes. Adding a spatio-temporal sidecar with adaptive geometry selection recovers 87.2%
overall, a +36.8 percentage-point gain that is consistent with the theoretical prediction of our
cascade-expansion analysis (Proposition 4). Genesis 3 is the measurement context; the structural
argument generalizes to the class by construction via the geometry-blind definition and Proposition 4,
and cross-architecture empirical validation is a direct avenue for future work (see Section 10).
    The question we address is:
      Given any symbolic-graph-network AI system whose execution graph shifts between tree-
      like, cyclic, and mixed regimes, how should a route-risk estimator choose its geometric
      prior at runtime?
    To our knowledge, this question is not posed in prior work. Geometry papers assume a fixed
dataset with a chosen structural regime. Multi-agent routing papers optimize scheduling and
coordination without modeling graph geometry or failure propagation. Neither addresses the
geometry-control problem that arises in any geometry-blind scheduler within the symbolic-graph-
network paradigm, a common failure mode, we argue, across this architectural class, though broad
cross-system empirical confirmation remains future work.
    We study this problem in Genesis 3, a self-improving multi-agent reasoning system with routing,
delegation, governance, and episodic traces. We introduce a spatio-temporal route-risk sidecar that

                                                   2
evaluates active routes using time-indexed graph snapshots and failure events. Our contribution is
fivefold:
1. Blind spot identification. We identify geometry-blindness as a failure-propagation observabil-
   ity gap common to execution-graph systems in contemporary agent orchestration: any system
   that routes tasks through a dynamic execution graph using a geometry-blind scheduler is system-
   atically exposed to failure cascades whenever the graph enters a tree-like regime. We demonstrate
   and quantify this vulnerability empirically in Genesis 3 as a first instance; by construction, the
   structural argument applies to any system of this class that lacks runtime geometry monitoring,
   and we conjecture the finding extends to agent orchestration frameworks, modular reasoning
   pipelines, and self-improving multi-agent engines more broadly. Cross-architecture empirical
   validation is planned future work.
2. Problem formulation. We formalize the resulting gap as an online geometry-control problem
   for live execution graphs, distinct from both static geometry selection and standard MAS routing.
3. System-level baseline. We provide a system-level ablation showing that Genesis 3 routing
   without any failure-propagation model achieves only 50.4% win rate overall (20–40% in tree-like
   regimes), quantifying the cost of the blind spot.
4. Instantiation. We instantiate the problem with a hyperbolic spatio-temporal model and a
   learned geometry selector (9 → 12 → 1 MLP, 133 parameters), recovering a tied-best 87.2%
   overall win rate and 92% in the hardest non-tree regime on the Genesis 3 benchmark distribution.
   On out-of-distribution graph families without retraining, the structural propagation baseline
   generalises perfectly while the trained gate’s win rate ranges from 7% (BA tree-like) to 65%
   (WS small-world), confirming that the trained gate is distribution-specific (Appendix I).
5. Theoretical grounding. We prove that regime-specific failure dynamics (Proposition 4) predict
   where and why geometry-aware scoring adds value, with empirical results consistent with the
   theoretical prediction.
    The operational question is whether a compact structural gate can predict, from graph features
alone, which geometry will produce the larger route-separation margin on the current scenario. The
gate is a meta-learner over a geometry-choice policy: it predicts which scoring geometry performs
better on the current route, not which geometry is “true” for the graph in a generative sense.
    To make the evaluation scientifically explicit, the paper tests three research questions. RQ1:
does explicit failure-propagation modeling improve routing quality relative to the native scheduler
signal alone? RQ2: under topology regime shift, does adaptive geometry selection outperform any
fixed geometric prior? RQ3: do the observed gains occur in precisely the regimes predicted by
cascade-expansion theory? The remainder of the paper is organized to answer these questions in
that order.

1.1   Hypotheses
• H1 (Perception-gap hypothesis). Adding explicit failure-propagation modeling to native
  multi-agent routing improves safer-route selection relative to load-and-fitness-only routing.
• H2 (Regime-adaptation hypothesis). Under topology regime shift, a learned geometry
  selector outperforms any fixed geometric prior in held-out route-risk estimation.
• H3 (Theory-alignment hypothesis). The largest gains from the sidecar occur in high-
  expansion tree-like regimes, as predicted by cascade-expansion analysis.

2     Related Work and Gap
The present work lies at the intersection of four literatures: geometric graph representation learning,
spatio-temporal graph learning, temporal point processes, and multi-agent communication or
orchestration. Each contributes an important ingredient, but none directly studies the question
posed here: how should a live multi-agent execution system choose its geometric prior as its topology
shifts between hierarchical, cyclic, and mixed regimes?

2.1   Geometric Graph Learning
Hyperbolic representation learning is now a standard tool for graphs with latent hierarchy. Early
work on hyperbolic neural networks showed that negatively curved spaces can represent tree-like
growth far more efficiently than Euclidean spaces, especially when distances expand exponentially
with depth [9]. This perspective was extended to graphs by hyperbolic graph convolutional networks,
which demonstrated strong advantages on hierarchical and scale-free relational structure [5]. More
generally, mixed-curvature models argued that no single constant-curvature manifold is universally
adequate, and that different latent factors may be better represented in Euclidean, spherical, or
hyperbolic components [19]; and constant-curvature GCN variants confirmed that the choice of
curvature sign materially affects downstream task performance [4].
    These papers establish a crucial baseline: geometry matters. However, geometry is typically
chosen once for a dataset, architecture, or embedding space. The problem is static model selection,
not online geometry control. Even work that expands the geometry family beyond pure hyperbolic
space still assumes a fixed representational choice during training and evaluation. Our setting differs
because the graph itself is not stationary: the same system may exhibit strongly hierarchical routes
in one interval and dense reciprocal motifs in the next. The scientific question is therefore not only
which geometry is expressive, but when each geometry should be preferred.
    Recent dynamic-hyperbolic work also does not close this gap. For example, hyperbolic variational
graph models for dynamic graphs show that non-Euclidean latent spaces can improve time-evolving
graph modeling [22], and temporal hyperbolic embedding methods have been proposed for discrete-
time dynamic networks [26]. These are important precedents, but they still optimize a chosen
non-Euclidean family rather than treat geometry selection itself as the prediction problem.

2.2   Spatio-Temporal and Dynamic Graph Learning
The spatio-temporal graph learning literature studies signals or interactions evolving over graph
structure. Canonical traffic-forecasting models such as DCRNN [14], STGCN [27], and Graph
WaveNet [24] combine graph propagation with temporal modeling to capture diffusion, recurrence,
and long-range dependencies. More recent dynamic-graph methods such as DyRep [23], Temporal
Graph Networks [18], EvolveGCN [16], and temporal attention models [25] focus on continuous-time
or event-driven representation learning on evolving graphs.
    This line of work is highly relevant because our execution graph is explicitly time-indexed and
the risk signal depends on both topology and history. However, these methods usually assume that
the graph-learning architecture is fixed in advance. They answer questions such as how to encode
time, how to update node states, or how to predict future edges or labels. They do not ask whether
the underlying geometric prior should itself switch as topology changes. In that sense, our paper is
adjacent to spatio-temporal graph learning, but its main object is a different one: regime-aware
geometric bias for route-risk estimation.
    Survey work on dynamic graphs makes this distinction clearer. Surveys of representation learning
for dynamic graphs document a large space of temporal embedding methods, recurrent updates,

                                                  4
attention mechanisms, and streaming adaptations [13]. Yet even in this broader perspective, the
dominant assumption remains that a chosen geometry and message-passing family are fixed parts of
the model class. Our work inserts a new decision layer on top of that assumption.

2.3   Temporal Point Processes and Self-Excitation
Our failure model also draws on temporal point-process reasoning. Hawkes processes introduced the
foundational idea that past events can increase the short-term intensity of future events, thereby
modeling clustered or self-exciting dynamics [10]. This idea has become central in event-sequence
modeling, including recurrent marked temporal point processes [7] and neural Hawkes processes [15].
Reviews of self-exciting spatio-temporal point processes further show how excitation mechanisms
are used in domains where localized events propagate over time and space [17].
    We do not present a new point-process model in this paper. Rather, we use a lightweight
burst-excitation heuristic inspired by self-exciting point processes as one ingredient inside route-risk
scoring for repeated local failures. The gap relative to the point-process literature is therefore not
temporal dependence per se; it is the coupling of temporally clustered failures with graph geometry
selection in a live execution system.

2.4   Multi-Agent Communication and Orchestration
The multi-agent literature provides the systems motivation. Work on learned communication
and coordination has shown that message passing, selective communication, and graph-structured
interaction can substantially improve cooperative behavior [6, 12, 21]. There is also a long systems
lineage on coordination, blackboard control, and distributed problem solving, where the execution
graph is an operational object governing delegation and synchronization rather than a geometric
object [8, 11].
     These papers are close in spirit because they study how agents exchange information over
changing interaction structures. But their central objectives are policy learning, communication
efficiency, or coordination quality. They generally do not build a spatio-temporal route-risk estimator
whose inductive bias changes with topology. In particular, we are not aware of prior work that takes
a live execution graph from a multi-agent reasoning engine, models timestamped failure propagation
on active routes, and treats geometry choice as a runtime decision variable.
     The most directly relevant recent work in this space is Kim et al. [20] (Google Research, Google
DeepMind, and MIT), who conduct a controlled large-scale evaluation of five canonical multi-
agent coordination topologies across 180 configurations and derive quantitative scaling laws for
agent systems. Their key empirical finding on topology-dependent error amplification is striking:
independent-agent coordination amplifies errors 17.2×, while centralized coordination contains this
to 4.4×. A predictive coordination model using topology-class and task-property features achieves
cross-validated R2 = 0.524. This is the strongest existing empirical evidence that coordination
structure determines failure propagation magnitude in real multi-agent systems, and it directly
motivates the concern we formalize. However, their analysis operates at design time: topology is
a fixed architectural choice made before deployment, and the scaling model predicts which fixed
topology to select. It does not model the intra-episode structural shifts that occur as a live execution
graph moves between tree-like, cyclic, and mixed regimes during a single solve. Their 17.2×
amplification figure is an aggregate over entire benchmark runs; it does not indicate when within
a run the high-amplification condition exists, nor does it equip the scheduler with a live signal to
detect that condition at route-scoring time. Our work addresses exactly this runtime perception gap:
a geometry-aware cascade risk estimator that operates continuously as execution-graph topology


                                                   5
evolves, providing the intra-episode structural monitoring layer that Kim et al. [20] leave open.
    Many contemporary reasoning systems (agent orchestration frameworks and neuro-symbolic
proof systems alike) execute by routing computation through an evolving graph of components
and dependencies. This motivates our focus on execution-graph regime shifts and their operational
consequences for routing. While prior work emphasizes expressiveness and search efficiency, our
contribution isolates a complementary systems concern: route selection requires propagation-aware
structural perception, and the appropriate geometric inductive bias can change online as the
execution topology changes.

Definition 1 (Geometry-blind routing). Consider a system that selects among candidate routes
r ∈ Rt at time t using a scoring rule

                                      score(r, t) = f (ψt (r), ξt ) ,

where ψt (r) are route-level aggregate observables (e.g. mean/max load, latency, cost, historical
fitness) and ξt are task/context features. We say the routing is geometry-blind if the scoring rule
does not depend on any propagation-sensitive structural representation of the current execution graph,
i.e. it does not use any feature map ϕ(Gt [r]) that measures expansion, cyclicity, or hyperbolicity of
the subgraph induced by route r (e.g. BFS shell-growth rate, cycle rank, Gromov δ-hyperbolicity, or
treewidth diagnostics), and does not simulate or estimate failure propagation on Gt [r].
Equivalent characterization. Routing is geometry-blind if and only if, conditional on (ψt (r), ξt ), the
routing decision is invariant to any structural modification of Gt [r] that preserves those aggregate
observables.

2.5   Gap Statement
The gap can therefore be stated precisely. Hyperbolic and mixed-curvature learning show that
geometry matters for graph structure. Spatio-temporal and dynamic-graph models show that
time-varying graphs require temporal reasoning. Temporal point processes show that repeated
failures can be self-exciting. Multi-agent systems show that execution graphs are operationally
meaningful and can shift rapidly with workload. What prior work does not directly articulate, to
our knowledge, is the following:

      A dominant paradigm in contemporary agent orchestration (symbolic graph networks
      in which agents are nodes, delegation relationships are edges, and task execution is
      a routing problem on an evolving graph) produces the following failure-propagation
      observability gap in any system of this class that lacks runtime geometry monitoring:
      the scheduling layer is geometry-blind. It cannot distinguish tree-like regimes (where
      failure cascades exponentially) from cyclic regimes (where failure self-limits). This gap
      arises by construction from the geometry-blind definition, not from any quirk of a single
      implementation; we conjecture it is a structural property of the architectural class, though
      broad cross-system empirical confirmation remains future work. Many systems in this
      paradigm that route without geometry-aware propagation signals are likely to inherit this
      observability gap; we test and quantify it in Genesis 3.

   The closest existing line of work combines dynamic hyperbolic embeddings with architectures
that blend or compare multiple representational biases over temporal graphs. Those methods are
the nearest conceptual neighbors because they acknowledge both time variation and geometry
mismatch. However, they treat geometry as a model-class choice made offline, not as a runtime

                                                    6
control variable inside a live execution system that is continuously restructuring its own agent graph.
The vulnerability we identify is therefore not visible to these methods: they do not inspect the
running execution graph at route-scoring time.
    Prior work does not identify geometry-blindness as a failure-propagation observability gap
in execution-graph agent systems, nor provide a runtime mechanism to close it. The individual
components (hyperbolic structure, dynamic graphs, and temporally clustered failure signals) are
each well established; the contribution is their integration into a runtime geometry-control layer
inside a live multi-agent scheduler. Because this execution pattern is common to agent orchestration
frameworks and modular reasoning pipelines, the observability gap is a natural failure mode for
systems in this class that route without geometry-aware failure-propagation signals.


3     Problem Formulation
3.1   System Context: Genesis 3
Genesis 3 is a multi-agent reasoning engine in which a team of specialized agents (planners, generators,
critics, and verifiers) collaborate to solve tasks through a 9-gate self-modification pipeline (syntax
check → Bayesian proof → adversarial stress → Lean4 formal verification → ephemeral sandbox →
causal validation, followed by canary and constitutional governance). This section gives the concrete
interpretation of the abstract model that follows; readers familiar with multi-agent systems can
map directly from model to implementation.

Nodes and edges. Each node v ∈ Vt is an active agent instance: a software process (planner,
generator, critic, or verifier) that accepts a task payload, executes one step of the reasoning pipeline,
and emits outputs or delegates subtasks. Agents are organized into an AgentTeam with a directed
acyclic communication-flow graph; the AgentTeamOrchestrator executes them in topological order,
running parallel groups concurrently via a ThreadPoolExecutor. Each directed edge (u, v) ∈ Et is
a task-delegation dependency, meaning that agent u may pass work downstream to agent v during
a solve episode. Node load ℓt (v) ∈ [0, 1] is a normalized occupancy fraction; edge weight wt (u, v)
reflects recent message-delivery reliability estimated from logs. The graph Gt changes over time as
agents are spawned or decommissioned (churn) and as workload patterns shift.

Routes. A route r = (v1 , v2 , . . . , vk ) is an ordered delegation chain: agent v1 receives the original
task and may delegate to v2 , and so on. The active route subgraph Gt [r] contains exactly those k
agents and the directed edges among them. A new route is selected at each solve episode (one call
to solve(problem, context)); routing decisions therefore occur at the frequency of task arrivals.
The 9-gate pipeline creates deep sequential dependencies within a solve episode, which means that a
failure at one gate may propagate forward and block all downstream stages unless an alternative
delegation path exists: the key motivation for failure-propagation modeling.

Failures. A failure event is logged whenever an agent raises an unhandled exception, times out,
or produces output rejected by a downstream verifier. Each event is recorded as (τi , vi , si , ci , ri )
where τi is the timestamp, vi is the affected agent, si ∈ [0, 1] is a severity score derived from error
type and downstream impact, ci is a category label (e.g., timeout, logic_error, gate_fail), and
ri is the active route at the time. The failure history F accumulates these events across episodes
and is shared via a Blackboard (thread-safe shared memory).



                                                    7
Native scheduler. Without the spatio-temporal sidecar, Genesis 3 selects routes using a
LinUCBTuner: a contextual bandit with 9 route-configuration arms and a 6-dimensional context
vector (5 task-context features plus a bias term). The reward signal is based on the team fitness
score (exponentially smoothed success rate for the candidate route) and the mean node load ℓ̄ over
the route. This is adequate for steady-state load balancing but carries no failure-propagation model
and no geometric prior, so it cannot distinguish tree-like delegation chains from dense cyclic ones
under churn and noise. The sidecar in this paper is additive: it computes a supplementary route-risk
score R(r, t) that the scheduler can incorporate alongside its native signal.

5.1   Structural Features

5.3   What Is Theoretically Justified by the Implemented Gate
The current selector is a discriminative preference model, not a latent-variable mixture estimator.
In the implementation, the gate is trained on binary labels indicating which geometry achieves the
larger route-separation margin on a scenario. Concretely, the training target is

                                    Y = 1[MHyp (r, t) ≥ MEuc (r, t)] ,
   where MHyp and MEuc denote the route-separation margins induced by the hyperbolic and
Euclidean scoring pipelines, respectively. In code, this objective is optimized through the equivalent
sigmoid-plus-binary-cross-entropy output-layer gradient ∂ℓ/∂z = p − y rather than by explicitly
materializing the scalar loss at each step. The gate therefore learns a conditional preference
probability from structural features rather than identifying a hidden mixture coefficient from
observed outputs alone.
   The implemented training objective is empirical binary cross-entropy:

where (ϕj , Yj ) are labeled training scenarios and πθ denotes the selector network.
Proposition 1 (Structural Preference Recoverability). Let ϕ(Gt [r]) ∈ R9 be the structural feature
map used by the selector, and define the margin difference
                                      ∆(ϕ) = MHyp (ϕ) − MEuc (ϕ).
Assume there exists a measurable decision rule
                                           g ⋆ (ϕ) = 1[∆(ϕ) ≥ 0]
such that |∆(ϕ)| ≥ δ for some δ > 0 on a set of positive probability. Then any sufficiently expressive
discriminative gate trained on labeled pairs (ϕ, Y ) can recover the geometry-preference rule g ⋆ up to
estimation error.
Proof sketch. By construction, Y = g ⋆ (ϕ) almost surely. The margin-gap assumption |∆(ϕ)| ≥ δ
away from the zero-margin set rules out arbitrarily unstable labels on that set and therefore yields a
well-defined Bayes classifier. A discriminative hypothesis class with sufficient approximation power
can approximate this classifier, and empirical risk minimization over labeled pairs (ϕ, Y ) recovers it
up to the usual estimation error terms determined by sample size and model capacity.

Proposition 2 (Calibrated Preference Probability). Let
                                           η(ϕ) = P(Y = 1 | ϕ)
denote the conditional probability that hyperbolic scoring outperforms Euclidean scoring on the
route-separation objective. If the gate class is rich enough to approximate η and training minimizes
binary cross-entropy over i.i.d. labeled examples, then the learned output πt (r) estimates a calibrated
geometry-preference probability up to estimation error.
Proof sketch. Binary cross-entropy is a strictly proper scoring rule. Its population minimizer over
measurable predictors is therefore the conditional class probability η(ϕ). Consequently, when the
network class can represent η exactly or to small approximation error, minimizing empirical binary
cross-entropy yields a predictor whose output is calibrated to the event that hyperbolic scoring
outperforms Euclidean scoring on the benchmark objective, again up to estimation error.

5.4   What the Benchmark Actually Evaluates
The benchmark does not ask the gate to recover an unobserved latent geometry variable. Instead, it
constructs supervised labels from comparative performance:

                                  Y = 1[MHyp (r, t) ≥ MEuc (r, t)] ,

1. observe the current structural feature vector ϕ(Gt [r]),

2. predict whether hyperbolic or Euclidean scoring will perform better on the route-separation
   objective,

3. use that probability as a soft routing weight,

4. measure the resulting margin on held-out scenarios.

   This is a valid and meaningful experiment. It tests whether geometry preference is predictable
from live structural signals, and whether exploiting that predictability improves route-risk estimation
under regime shift. What it does not yet test is a stronger geometric theorem phrased directly in
terms of distortion bounds, shell growth, or explicit hyperbolicity diagnostics.

6     Why a Fixed Geometric Prior Fails Under Regime Shift
6.1    Suboptimality of Fixed Priors
Proposition 3 (Suboptimality of Fixed Geometric Priors). Suppose the execution-graph process
visits at least two structural regimes with positive probability: one regime in which hyperbolic bias
is strictly better for route-risk separation and one regime in which Euclidean bias is strictly better.
Then any fixed geometric prior is suboptimal in expected risk-separation performance compared to
an oracle regime-aware selector.

Proof sketch. Let JHyp (ρ) and JEuc (ρ) denote the expected route-separation objective under regime
ρ, and write RH = {ρ : JHyp (ρ) > JEuc (ρ)} and RE = {ρ : JEuc (ρ) > JHyp (ρ)}. By assumption,
both sets have positive probability under the regime process. A fixed hyperbolic prior loses the
positive excess term JEuc (ρ) − JHyp (ρ) on RE , while a fixed Euclidean prior loses the symmetric
term on RH . An oracle that selects arg max{JHyp (ρ), JEuc (ρ)} pointwise avoids both losses, so its
expected objective strictly dominates either fixed prior.

6.2    Cascade Sensitivity to Graph Expansion
The deeper reason geometry matters is that failure dynamics depend directly on graph expansion
rate. In hyperbolic space with curvature magnitude κ > 0 (sectional curvature −κ), the volume of a
ball of radius r grows as Vol(r) ∝ eκr , and correspondingly the number of nodes at BFS depth k
satisfies

                                              |Vk | ≈ Ceγk
    for some expansion rate γ > 0.

Proposition 4 (Cascade Sensitivity to Expansion). Let a failure process propagate with probability
p per edge at each step on a graph whose BFS shell sizes satisfy |Vk | ≈ Ceγk . Let Nr denote the
number of newly affected nodes in BFS shell r rather than the cumulative number affected up to
depth r. Then
                                 E[Nr ] ≈ p r |Vr | ≈ C e(γ+ln p) r .
A cascade is supercritical when

                                  γ + ln p > 0       ⇔       p > e−γ .

Proof sketch. Under the shell-growth approximation, the number of candidate recipients in shell r
scales as |Vr | ≈ Ceγr . If each forward transmission succeeds independently with probability p, then
the contribution of a depth-r path is weighted by pr , so the expected newly infected mass arriving
in shell r scales as pr |Vr |. Substituting the shell approximation yields

                                     E[Nr ] ≈ pr Ceγr = Ce(γ+ln p)r .

The process is supercritical precisely when the exponent is positive, which is equivalent to p >
e−γ .

    In a slowly expanding or dense cyclic graph (γ ≈ 0), the cascade threshold becomes p > 1, which
is never satisfied under realistic propagation probabilities. In a strongly branching graph (large γ),
the threshold is p > e−γ ≪ 1 and cascades are easy to trigger. Thus,


                                                   14
• large γ (tree-like, hyperbolic regime) implies structurally dangerous propagation and favors
  hyperbolic route-risk scoring,

• small γ with high cycle rank (dense, Euclidean regime) implies self-limiting failures and makes
  Euclidean diffusion adequate.

    The gate πt (r) is therefore learning to detect whether the graph is in the supercritical cascade
regime. The three geometry-aware features directly encode the relevant quantities: ϕ7 estimates γ,
ϕ8 measures deviation from tree structure, and ϕ9 captures the fitted Poincaré curvature κ̂.

9     Discussion
9.1    Why Fixed Geometry Fails
The main message is not that hyperbolic geometry is wrong. On the contrary, hyperbolic structure
remains highly effective when the execution graph is hierarchical. The problem is that live multi-
agent systems do not remain in one structural regime. As soon as dense feedback, reciprocal links,
or mixed motifs appear, the fixed-prior assumption becomes fragile and performance becomes
regime-dependent. Proposition 3 formalizes the core point: once the regime process visits both
hierarchy-dominated and cycle-dominated states with positive probability, any static geometric prior
incurs avoidable expected loss relative to a regime-aware selector.

9.2    Perception-Gap Hypothesis (H1)
The ablation in Section 8.3 shows that failure-propagation modeling is the dominant performance
driver. In the tree-like clean, noise, and churn regimes, the native scheduler attains only 20%,
20%, and 40% win rate, whereas the sidecar lifts those values to 84%, 88%, and 88%. Without an
explicit propagation model, Genesis 3 cannot perceive the structural difference between a lightly
loaded but cascade-prone route and a genuinely safe route. Geometry selection becomes relevant
only after this perception gap is closed.

9.3    Regime-Adaptation Hypothesis (H2)
Once propagation is modeled, the remaining question is whether geometry preference is predictable
from the live structural state. The answer given by the held-out benchmark is yes: learned switching
matches the best overall win rate and materially improves route-separation margin relative to
the strongest fixed-prior structural baseline, while also recovering 92% win rate in the hardest
non_tree regime. The rerun now includes standalone held-out gate diagnostics. Across all 250
scenarios, the gate’s positive-label rate is 74.4%, AUC is 0.9247, thresholded accuracy is 86.4%,
expected calibration error is 0.0681, and mean predictive entropy is 0.3359, with 46.0% of cases in a
low-entropy regime and only 1.6% in a high-entropy regime. Regime-wise, discrimination remains
strong in clean (AUC = 0.9226), noise (0.9464), non_tree (0.9635), and mixed (0.9375), with a
clear degradation only in churn (0.7259; ECE 0.1200). H2 is supported by both held-out routing
outcomes and direct classification evidence. The reported maximum calibration error is larger
(0.7998 overall) because it is a worst-bin statistic and is sensitive to sparse extreme-probability bins;
for that reason we treat AUC and ECE as the more stable summary diagnostics.

9.4    Margin, Win Rate, and Decision-Threshold Robustness
Table 1 shows that hyperbolic_no_excitation attains the highest mean margin (0.4123) yet a
lower win rate (79.2%) than the learned selector (87.2%). This apparent inconsistency has a clean
explanation.




                                                   20
Why margin and win rate diverge. Win rate measures the probability that a scorer correctly
signs the route-risk comparison (Ms > 0). Mean margin measures the average magnitude of that
signed score. A fixed hyperbolic model with high curvature produces large separations in regimes
where hyperbolic geometry is appropriate (tree-like, large γ) while systematically misclassifying a
fraction of scenarios in non-tree and mixed regimes where the geometric prior is mismatched. The
result is a high mean margin (driven by strongly correct tree-like cases) combined with a lower win
rate (penalized by the non-tree misclassifications). The learned gate is optimized on binary win
labels Y = 1[MHyp ≥ MEuc ] rather than on margin magnitude; it is therefore tuned to maximize
the probability of the correct binary outcome, which produces the correct sign on more scenarios
even when the separation magnitude in strongly tree-like cases is somewhat smaller. A familiar
analogy: this is a precision–recall tradeoff, where the gate trades peak-margin headroom for better
coverage across heterogeneous regimes.

Why win rate is the right primary metric. In a routing context, the decision is binary: select
route r1 or r2 . The consequence of an error scales with whether the safer route was selected, not
with the magnitude of the cost-function score. The +7.2 percentage-point win-rate advantage of the
learned gate over hyperbolic_no_excitation translates directly into fewer unsafe route selections
per 100 episodes. Mean margin advantage would be the primary criterion only if the downstream
cost function were proportional to score magnitude rather than binary correctness, which does
not reflect the routing use-case. This is why win rate is treated as the primary decision metric
throughout Section 8.

Threshold sensitivity. The gate outputs a soft score πt (r) ∈ [0, 1]; routing decisions are made
by comparing blended route-risk scores R(r, t) = πt (r) RHyp + (1 − πt (r)) REuc for the two candidate
routes. Gate diagnostics (Table 7) show that 46% of held-out cases fall in the low-entropy (high-
certainty) band and only 1.6% in the high-entropy band, confirming that the gate is rarely near
the decision boundary in practice. Varying the blending weight continuously and tracing the full
margin–win-rate Pareto surface is a constructive further-work item. The current regime-stratified
AUC results (0.92–0.96 in four of five regimes) confirm that gate discrimination is robust across
structural regimes, not sensitive to a particular operating threshold.

9.5   Theory-Alignment Hypothesis (H3)
The largest gains appear exactly where Proposition 4 predicts them. In high-expansion tree-like
regimes, the sidecar gains are +64 to +68 percentage points, which is the operational signature of
the supercritical condition p > e−γ . In dense cyclic regimes, the native baseline is already strong
because node load correlates with the failure footprint, and the sidecar adds only modest gains. The
empirical regime pattern is therefore not a post hoc story; it is aligned with the expansion-based
mechanism derived in Section 6.

9.6   Broader Implications
A routing layer may be paired with strong planning, delegation, or language capabilities and still
underperform when its representation omits propagation-sensitive structure. The same applies in
LLM orchestration stacks, automated trading pipelines, and similar latency-sensitive systems.
    The gate’s compactness (133 parameters) suggests structural-perception modules can be small,
and it motivates the following future work directions: (i) asynthetic cross-architecture benchmark
using Barabási–Albert trees, Watts–Strogatz small-world networks, and Erdős–Rényi dense graphs,


                                                 21
matched on node count and load distribution, to verify that the regime-sensitivity gain generalises
beyond Genesis 3’s trace distribution; (ii) runtime overhead characterisation of the sidecar as
a latency-aware production component, including per-call latency and memory footprint; (iii)
continuous curvature adaptation and online mixed-curvature control; and (iv) richer calibration
analysis including reliability diagrams, threshold-sensitivity curves, and confidence intervals beyond
the scalar diagnostics reported here.


10     Limitations
• L1: Single-system evaluation. All benchmarks are conducted within Genesis 3. No equivalent
  open multi-agent system with runtime topology regime shifts exists for external validation at
  the time of writing. This means we cannot yet claim that the problem or the sidecar generalizes
  across architectures. Response: the structural ablation makes the contribution claim falsifiable
  within the system, which is a necessary first step before cross-system comparison becomes
  possible. A preliminary synthetic cross-architecture study has been run using Barabási–Albert,
  Watts–Strogatz, and Erdős–Rényi graphs matched on node count and load distribution (300
  scenarios, 20 seeds per family; Appendix I). The structural propagation baseline achieves 100%
  win rate on all three families, confirming that the core finding (that failure-propagation modeling
  outperforms load-only routing) is not Genesis 3-specific. The trained geometry gate, without
  retraining, achieves 65% win rate on Watts–Strogatz small-world graphs but only 7% on BA tree-
  like and 46% on ER dense-cyclic. Cross-architecture generalisation of the propagation principle
  is therefore robust; cross-architecture generalisation of the trained gate requires fine-tuning on
  the target graph family.

• L2: ARC trace limitations. The ARC episode source provides coverage-only traces with
  no failure events. It validates that the sidecar obtains topology snapshots correctly but cannot
  support the attack-separation benchmark. Response: we report sources separately and do not
  mix coverage-only and failure-benchmarkable traces. The 250-scenario benchmark uses only
  planning traces with real failure events.

• L3: Ablation geometry in non-tree/mixed regimes. In non_tree and mixed regimes, the
  Genesis 3-native baseline achieves 92% and 80% win rate respectively because structural node
  load correlates with the attack footprint in dense graphs. Part of that correlation is mechanistic,
  but part is benchmark-induced: the attacked/safe footprint construction ranks candidate nodes
  by failure severity with node load as a secondary key, which gives the native load-based baseline
  partial alignment with the evaluation target in dense regimes. The sidecar therefore adds little in
  these regimes (+0 and +4 points). Response: this pattern is still consistent with Proposition 4,
  but the dense-regime ablation should be interpreted as conservative evidence for sidecar value
  rather than as an unbiased estimate of how often load-only routing would succeed under an
  independently defined attack footprint.

• L4: Peak mean-margin vs. win-rate tradeoff. The learned selector achieves a tied-best
  win rate (87.2%) but does not dominate hyperbolic_no_excitation on mean margin (0.3846
  vs. 0.4123). Response: in a routing decision, win rate is the operationally relevant metric. We
  report both and do not suppress the tradeoff.

• L5: Theoretical scope. Propositions 1, 2, 3, and 4 justify preference recoverability from
  observable features and establish the cascade-sensitivity mechanism. They do not prove a stronger
  result, such as latent-geometry identifiability in a generative sense or minimax optimality.

                                                 22
   Response: the implemented gate is a discriminative classifier, not a generative model; the
   propositions faithfully describe what is actually implemented.

• L6: Benchmark-diagnostic scope. The rerun now reports gate-level diagnostics such as
  AUC, accuracy, calibration error, and entropy stratified by regime, but it still stops at scalar
  summaries. We do not yet include reliability diagrams, threshold-sensitivity curves, source-group
  calibration slices, or confidence intervals for the diagnostics themselves. Response: this is enough
  to support H2 as a discriminative claim, but a submission-grade revision should expand the
  diagnostic section beyond summary statistics.

• L7: Novelty claim scope. All novelty claims are qualified as “to our knowledge.” A submission-
  quality version should undergo a venue-specific related-work pass to confirm no directly analogous
  formulation exists in unpublished or concurrent work.


11    Conclusion
We introduced an online geometry-control problem for live multi-agent execution graphs and
demonstrated that runtime regime shifts make fixed geometric priors suboptimal for route-risk
estimation.

Failure-propagation modeling. A system-level ablation shows that Genesis 3 routing without
any spatio-temporal sidecar achieves 50.4% win rate overall and as low as 20% in tree-like regimes,
essentially indistinguishable from random. Team fitness and node load contain no signal about
exponential failure spread through a branching subgraph. Any route-risk method that models
propagation closes this gap: the full sidecar recovers 87.2%, a +36.8 point system-level gain.

Adaptive geometry selection. Once failure propagation is modeled, the additional question
is which geometric prior to use. Fixed hyperbolic models collapse to 64–72% win rate in non-tree
regimes; the learned geometry gate recovers 92%. The gate is small (133 parameters), learns from
structural observables alone, and demonstrates that regime information is recoverable from live
graph statistics without expensive search or large models.
    Geometry in live multi-agent systems is a dynamic control variable, not a fixed representational
assumption. The prerequisite for that adaptive layer is the failure-propagation modeling that
makes route risk structurally observable. More broadly, structural perception limits agent system
performance in ways distinct from reasoning quality and addressable independently.

tree-like graphs, where the gate’s training distribution does not include preferential-attachment
structure. The primary finding of the paper (propagation modeling vs. load-only) therefore holds
across graph families. The learned gate’s cross-architecture performance is weaker and should be
treated as preliminary evidence pending fine-tuning experiments on each family – a limitation we
acknowledge alongside the BA/WS/ER suite as planned future work in Section 10.

Interpretation. The per-feature deltas (ϕ7 : −0.0005, ϕ8 : −0.0001, ϕ9 : −0.0004, full geometry

hand-coded team topologies. This stands in contrast to the +4.6% margin improvement reported in

genuinely cyclic topologies from real solve episodes.
    The key qualitative insight is that the geometry-aware features ϕ7 –ϕ9 are conditionally useful:
they add discriminative signal when the evaluation distribution spans sufficient topology regime
diversity. On a near-uniform topology distribution, the topology statistics ϕ1 –ϕ6 already capture all
predictable variance and the geometry features are redundant. This is consistent with the theoretical
motivation for the features (Section 3.3) and should be interpreted as a precision-of-effect limitation
rather than a null result: the +4.6% gain in the main benchmark is observed precisely because the
main benchmark was designed to cover the regime diversity that the geometry features detect.
