38: 3.2 Representation-level Rebalancing with Feature Calibration Module
39: IMR introduces an imbalanced exposure pattern, where some modalities are observed far more often than others. This imbalance distorts the corresponding unimodal representations, weakening their semantic consistency and compromising cross-modal learning [ 34 ] . Here, we introduce Feature Calibration Module ( FCM ) to align modality-specific features that are distorted by uneven exposure under IMR. Instead of imputing missing signals, it leverages global contextual cues aggregated from available modalities to recalibrate unimodal embeddings.
40: Feature aggregation under IMR. Following [ 16 ] , we obtain modality-wise descriptors to represent the overall statistics of available features. Given the masked features x ~ i m \tilde{x}_{i}^{m} and binary indicators e i m e_{i}^{m} from the masking operator ℳ ⁡ ( x , 𝐫 ) \mathcal{M}(x;\mathbf{r}) , the unimodal global descriptor is computed by averaging available features:
41: x glob m = ∑ i = 1 N x ~ i m ε + ∑ i = 1 N e i m , x^{m}_{\textit{glob}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{x}^{m}_{i}}{\varepsilon+\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{m}_{i}},\vskip-3.0pt (7)
42: where x global m ∈ ℝ d m x^{m}_{\textit{global}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}} and ε \varepsilon is a smoothing hyper-parameter.
43: Cross-modal context extraction. The unimodal descriptors are concatenated and passed through a fully connected layer to capture shared cross-modal context:
44: x global = 𝐑𝐞𝐋𝐔 ⁡ ( 𝐅 global ​ ( [ x glob m 1 , x glob m 2 , … , x glob m M ] ) ) , x_{\textit{global}}=\mathbf{ReLU}\!\big(\mathbf{F}_{\textit{global}}([x^{m_{1}}_{\textit{glob}},x^{m_{2}}_{\textit{glob}},...,x^{m_{M}}_{\textit{glob}}])\big), (8)
45: where x global ∈ ℝ d global x_{\textit{global}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textit{global}}} encodes the global information jointly derived from all available modalities { m j } \{m_{j}\} . This representation is then projected to modality-specific calibration weights through independent fully connected layers:
46: w cal m = 𝐅 cal m ​ ( x global ) , w^{m}_{\textit{cal}}=\mathbf{F}^{m}_{\textit{cal}}(x_{\textit{global}}), (9)
47: where w cal m ∈ ℝ d m w^{m}_{\textit{cal}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}} represents the contextual calibration weight for modality m m .
48: Feature calibration. Each modality feature is adaptively recalibrated through excitation-based calibration weights derived from global contextual information [ 12 ] . These weights highlight the importance of each modality and help correct representation bias under IMR. Formally, a gating operation with sigmoid activation is applied to rescale each unimodal feature:
49: x ^ i m = ( 1 + σ ⁡ ( w cal m ) ) ⊙ x ~ i m , \hat{x}^{m}_{i}=(1+\sigma(w^{m}_{\textit{cal}}))\odot\tilde{x}^{m}_{i}, (10)
50: where x ^ i m ∈ ℝ d m \hat{x}^{m}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}} , ⊙ \odot and σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) denote element-wise multiplication and sigmoid function, respectively. Features of missing modalities remain as 0 → \vec{0} .
51: Although the recalibrated features x ^ i m \hat{x}^{m}_{i} differ in composition, they preserve dimensionality and seamlessly integrated into any backbone. This shared calibration mitigates IMR-induced representation discrepancies and provides a consistent feature basis for downstream optimization.
52: 3.3 Model-Agnostic Integration of Multimodal Emotion Recognition Backbone

141: B Theoretical Analysis
142: B.1 Gradient Imbalance under IMR
143: This section provides a formal justification for Eq. 6 in the main paper, showing how the imbalance in gradient magnitudes arises under the Imbalanced Missing Rate (IMR) condition.
144: Assumption 1 (Independent Missingness). Each modality m m is independently missing according to a Bernoulli variable e i m ∈ { 0 , 1 } e_{i}^{m}\in\{0,1\} with P ⁡ ( e i m = 1 ) = ( 1 − r m ) P(e_{i}^{m}\!=\!1)=(1-r_{m}) and P ⁡ ( e i m = 0 ) = r m , P(e_{i}^{m}=0)=r_{m}, , where r m ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) r_{m}\in[0,1) denotes the missing probability of modality m m . The missingness indicators { e i m } \{e_{i}^{m}\} are assumed independent of the target label Y Y (i.e., Missing Completely At Random-MCAR).
145: Lemma 1 (Expected Gradient Scaling under IMR). Under above Assumption, for a differentiable per-sample loss ℓ m = ℓ ⁡ ( f m ​ ( X m ) , Y ) \ell_{m}=\ell(f_{m}(X_{m}),Y) with finite variance, the expected gradient of modality m m with respect to its parameters θ m \theta_{m} satisfies:
146: 𝔼 ⁡ [ ∇ θ m ℒ IMR ] = ( 1 − r m ) ​ 𝔼 ​ [ ∇ θ m ℓ ​ ( f m ​ ( X m ) , Y ) ] . \mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}}\right]=(1-r_{m})\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\ell(f_{m}(X_{m}),Y)\right]. (28)
147: Proof. Let ℒ IMR \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}} denote the expected loss under the IMR distribution:
148: ℒ IMR = 𝔼 X , Y , e ​ [ ∑ m = 1 M e m ​ ℓ ​ ( f m ​ ( X m , θ m ) , Y ) ] , \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}}=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y,e}\!\left[\sum_{m=1}^{M}e^{m}\,\ell(f_{m}(X_{m};\theta_{m}),Y)\right], (29)
149: where f m ​ ( ⋅ , θ m ) f_{m}(\cdot;\theta_{m}) represents the modality-specific encoder or prediction branch parameterized by θ m \theta_{m} , and e m e^{m} indicates its availability. Taking the gradient with respect to θ m \theta_{m} yields:
150: ∇ θ m ℒ IMR = 𝔼 X , Y , e ​ [ e m ​ ∇ θ m ℓ ​ ( f m ​ ( X m , θ m ) , Y ) ] . \nabla_{\theta_{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}}=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y,e}\!\left[e^{m}\,\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\ell(f_{m}(X_{m};\theta_{m}),Y)\right]. (30)
151: By the linearity of expectation, we can separate the stochastic variable e m e^{m} :
152: 𝔼 ⁡ [ ∇ θ m ℒ IMR ] \displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}}\right] = 𝔼 e ​ [ e m ] ​ 𝔼 X , Y ​ [ ∇ θ m ℓ ​ ( f m ​ ( X m , θ m ) , Y ) ] . \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{e}\!\left[e^{m}\right]\,\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\ell(f_{m}(X_{m};\theta_{m}),Y)\right]. (31)
153: Since e m ∼ Bernoulli ⁡ ( 1 − r m ) e^{m}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-r_{m}) , we have 𝔼 ⁡ [ e m ] = 1 − r m \mathbb{E}[e^{m}]=1-r_{m} . Substituting this into Eq. 31 gives:
154: 𝔼 ⁡ [ ∇ θ m ℒ IMR ] = ( 1 − r m ) ​ 𝔼 ​ [ ∇ θ m ℓ ​ ( f m ​ ( X m , θ m ) , Y ) ] . \mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}}\right]=(1-r_{m})\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\ell(f_{m}(X_{m};\theta_{m}),Y)\right]. (32)
155: Eq. 32 shows that, in expectation, the gradient propagated to the parameters θ m \theta_{m} of each modality-specific encoder is linearly scaled by its availability ratio ( 1 − r m ) (1-r_{m}) .
156: Corollary 1 (Gradient Imbalance Effect). Taking the L 2 L_{2} norm of Eq. 32 yields
157: 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ ∇ θ m ℒ IMR ‖ ] = ( 1 − r m ) ​ 𝔼 ​ [ ‖ ∇ θ m ℓ ​ ( f m ​ ( X m , θ m ) , Y ) ‖ ] . \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IMR}}\|\right]=(1-r_{m})\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\ell(f_{m}(X_{m};\theta_{m}),Y)\|\right]. (33)
158: Therefore, modalities with higher missing rates ( r m r_{m} large) receive proportionally weaker gradient updates on their encoder parameters, causing slower convergence and optimization dominance by low-missing-rate modalities.
159: The above analysis reveals that, under IMR, each modality receives a gradient update scaled by its availability ratio ( 1 − r m ) (1-r_{m}) . This induces biased optimization dynamics where dominant modalities converge faster while rare ones lag behind. To counteract this imbalance, the Gradient Rebalancing Module (GRM) introduced later (Eq. 21 and Eq. 22 ) adaptively rescales the gradient flow through modulation coefficients μ m \mu_{m} that act as an inverse correction to the implicit scaling factor ( 1 − r m ) (1-r_{m}) . In essence, GRM restores equilibrium among modalities by dynamically adjusting both the magnitude and the direction of their gradients, thereby stabilizing multimodal optimization under heterogeneous missing conditions.
160: B.2 Gradient Rebalancing Rationale
161: Proposition 1. Building on Lemma 1 , which shows that the expected gradient magnitude of each modality is scaled by its availability ratio ( 1 − r m ) (1-r_{m}) , we explain the rationale behind the proposed Gradient Rebalancing Module (GRM).
162: Rationale. GRM compensates for the imbalance in gradient magnitudes through adaptive modulation of each modality’s update:
163: μ m \displaystyle\mu^{m} = ρ ​ ∑ m ′ ∈ { a , v , l } , m ′ ≠ m Δ KL m ′ ∑ m ′ ∈ { a , v , l } Δ KL m ′ , \displaystyle=\rho\,\frac{\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\{a,v,l\},\,m^{\prime}\neq m}\!\!\Delta^{m^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{KL}}}{\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\{a,v,l\}}\!\!\Delta^{m^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{KL}}}, (34) θ ( t + 1 ) ϕ m \displaystyle\theta^{\phi^{m}}_{(t+1)} = θ ( t ) ϕ m − α ​ μ m ​ ∂ ℒ task ∂ θ ( t ) ϕ m . \displaystyle=\theta^{\phi^{m}}_{(t)}-\alpha\,\mu^{m}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}}{\partial\theta^{\phi^{m}}_{(t)}}.
164: Here, Δ KL m \Delta^{m}_{\mathrm{KL}} quantifies the learning discrepancy of modality m m between its unimodal and fused distributions, and ρ \rho is a hyperparameter controlling the modulation intensity. A smaller μ m \mu^{m} indicates that modality m m is learning faster ( i.e. , larger Δ KL m \Delta^{m}_{\mathrm{KL}} ) and thus its gradient update is attenuated, whereas slower modalities are amplified. This mechanism counteracts the imbalance identified in Lemma 1 and encourages all modality-specific gradients to approach an equilibrium:
165: ‖ μ m ​ ∇ θ ϕ m ℒ task ‖ ≈ ‖ μ m ′ ​ ∇ θ ϕ m ′ ℒ task ‖ , ∀ m , m ′ . \big\|\mu^{m}\nabla_{\theta^{\phi^{m}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}\big\|\approx\big\|\mu^{m^{\prime}}\nabla_{\theta^{\phi^{m^{\prime}}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}\big\|,\quad\forall m,m^{\prime}. (35)
166: Here, ∇ θ ϕ m ℒ task \nabla_{\theta^{\phi^{m}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}} denotes the gradient of the task loss with respect to the parameters of the m m -th encoder, and ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| denotes the Euclidean norm measuring its magnitude.
167: Such modulation is consistent with previous analyses [ 29 , 8 ] showing that gradient reweighting based on learning discrepancy stabilizes multimodal training. GRM extends this idea to imbalanced missing-rate conditions, using KL-divergence as a continuous signal of learning disparity rather than a fixed prior ratio ( 1 − r m ) (1-r_{m}) .
