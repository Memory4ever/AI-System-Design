Abstract
With the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), new methods in Information Retrieval are available in which relevance is estimated directly through language understanding and reasoning, instead of embedding similarity. We argue that similarity is a short-sighted interpretation of relevance, and that LLM-Based Relevance Judgment Systems (LLM-RJS) (with reasoning) have potential to outperform Neural Embedding Retrieval Systems (NERS) by overcoming this limitation. Using the TREC-DL 2019 passage retrieval dataset, we compare various LLM-RJS with NERS, but observe no noticeable improvement.
Subsequently, we analyze the impact of reasoning by comparing LLM-RJS with and without reasoning. We find that human annotations also suffer from short-sightedness, and that false-positives in the reasoning LLM-RJS are primarily mistakes in annotations due to short-sightedness. We conclude that LLM-RJS do have the ability to address the short-sightedness limitation in NERS, but that this cannot be evaluated with standard annotated relevance datasets.
1 Introduction
Information Retrieval (IR) is the task of finding relevant information in a pool of numerous documents, where a document can be any retrievable unit like a webpage or text fragment. IR methods have constantly been evolving (Kazi et al., 2025). Vector Space Models (Salton, 1965; Salton et al., 1975) use sparse vector representations of term weights, where each weight represents the frequency of the term in the given document. Documents are ordered by relevance to the query, by the means of an ordering function, such as the BM25 (Robertson et al., 2009, Chapter 3.4). Modern IR methods rely on dense vector representations of text, known as “embeddings", which are learned by Neural Networks (Huang et al., 2013). We will refer to these methods as Neural Embeddings Retrieval Systems (NERS). In NERS, documents are ordered by their similarity with the query, which is given by a distance metric such as Euclidean Distance, or Cosine Similarity (Wang and Dong, 2020). Karpukhin et al., 2020 showed that NERS outperform sparse vector representation approaches.
Figure 1: Motivation for LLM-RJS. The query and document are not similar, so embedding similarity is too short-sighted for IR. The document is the correct answer, but requires reasoning about relevance to identify as a match.
More recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been proposed as a different approach to judging how relevant a document is to a query (MacAvaney and Soldaini, 2023; Faggioli et al., 2023; Abbasiantaeb et al., 2024). Alaofi et al., 2024 showed that LLMs’ relevance judgments are similar to those of humans. This has the potential to outperform NERS models, as NERS models are limited by retrieving documents that are similar to the query, but relevant documents are not always similar. An example of NERS being short-sighted is shown in Figure 1.
For the scope of this paper, we will call a system that uses an LLMs verbalized relevance score for matching an LLM-Based Relevance Judgment System (LLM-RJS). While LLM-RJS have been shown to be effective Alaofi et al. (2024), to the best of our knowledge a 1-to-1 comparison against NERS is missing.
Additionally, IR applications of reasoning models that are equipped with Chain of Thought (CoT) capabilities, such as GPT-o1 (Jaech et al., 2024) remain unexplored. As reasoning capabilities become cheaper and available in open models (Guo et al., 2025), examining its impact on document relevance judgments becomes increasingly important and broadly applicable.
We aim to build on Alaofi et al., 2024 by comparing LLM-RJS to NERS. Furthermore, we explore the effects of reasoning capabilities of LLMs in the task of document relevance estimation.
With this, we make the following contributions:
•
We evaluate NERS and multiple LLM-RJS models on the TREC-DL 2019 passage retrieval benchmark to compare their performances in relevance ranking. We see that top LLMs perform on par with NERS, but do not yield demonstrably better rankings.
•
We evaluate the impact of “reasoning effort” for LLM-RJS at directly estimating the relevance scores. We propose that this can help to overcome the short-sightedness of NERS. We find that adding reasoning results in higher relevance ratings that the human relevance scores, and that the LLM-RJS overall shows higher relevance ratings.
•
We explain the difference in relevance ratings as short-sightedness in the ground-truth annotations, showing examples of documents that have low relevance ratings in the human annotation, but that are highly relevant. From the 94 ‘false positives’, 89 are actually mistakes in the annotation because the passages are relevant but not similar.
•
We conclude that the empirical capabilities of zero-shot methods for Information Retrieval may be underestimated due to short-sighted annotations, making short-sighted similarity models appear better than more capable reasoning models. LLM-RJS have the ability to overcome the short-sighted limitation of NERS, but this does not lead to better metrics when evaluated on human annotations.

2 Related Work
We describe previous research on NERS and LLM-RJS models. While NERS stem from a long line of existing research in text embeddings Mikolov et al. (2013); Pennington et al. (2014), we specifically focus on embeddings from Neural Networks.
NERS
Neural Embedding Retrieval Systems (NERS) rely on pre-trained language models to create a dense vector representation of text. These language models are Neural Networks trained on some other such as Masked Language Modeling (Devlin et al., 2019) or Contrastive Learning Gao et al. (2021). The penulatimate layer of the Neural Network therefore has a dense representation of the semantic meaning of the text, capturing properties like synonymy Karpukhin et al. (2020).
NERS models work by computing the embedding representation of the query and the document, and evaluating the distance between them, typically through cosine similarity. When a document and a query describe similar concepts they get a higher similarity score. Document relevance is therefore ranked by similarity to the query.
We consider this a fundamental limitation of IR systems that rely on embedding similarities. While similarity between query and document is a good heuristic for relevance, it is not exactly the same. When the document answers the question but does not repeat content from the query (as the example in Figure 1), it is not similar, but can still be highly relevant. Even with ever-improving embedding models Trabelsi et al. (2021), this necessarily gives a limitation of NERS.
LLM-RJS
Large Language Model-Based Relevance Judgement Systems (LLM-RJS) instead use the generated answers from an LLM to establish relevance. In this work, the LLM is prompted with query and document, and instructed to quantify how “relevant” the document is in line with the human annotations, though many variations for prompting exist Arabzadeh and Clarke (2025).
Faggioli et al., 2023 found that LLMs can perform comparably to humans in identifying irrelevant information but struggled to consistently recognize relevant pieces of information. In their results GPT-3.5 showed a higher false negative rate than humans.
Evaluating the goodness of document relevance assigned by LLMs can be directly compared to human-labeled query-document pairs are used as the “ground truth". However, humans are not consistent in rating document relevance (Bailey et al., 2008). To evaluate LLMs fairly, Alaofi et al., 2024 posed the issue as “if
LLMs produce labels that agree with humans to the same extent
that humans agree with each other (human-to-human range), we can conclude that they are
sufficiently reliable for use". Their results show that larger LLMs, such as GPT-4, GPT-4o, and LLaMA70B give results in the human-to-human range. Only GPT-4 and GPT-4o were robust to the prompt variations. However, this perspective cannot show when LLMs might outperform humans in the ability to estimate relevance.
Because LLM-RJS systems are zero-shot models and are not trained as supervised learning systems on the human annotations, we should consider that it has the potential to outperform human annotations. Additionally, with reasoning models Wei et al. (2022), we can generate reasoning processes that show relevance in a way that has more depth than simple similarity scores, and might have more depth than the human labels.
3 Dataset
TREC (Text REtrieval Conference) datasets are commonly used in the IR field (MacAvaney and Soldaini, 2023; Faggioli et al., 2023; Alaofi et al., 2024). Similarly to Alaofi et al., 2024, we use queries and passages from the test set of the the passage retrieval task dataset of the Deep Learning Track of TREC 2019 (TREC-DL 2019) (Voorhees et al., 2020). TREC-DL 2019 contains triples of form (query, passage, relevance score).
The passage corpora of TREC-DL 2019 are derived from the MS MARCO dataset. MS MARCO is a large-scale dataset of real web search queries and web text originally sampled from Bing search logs. Queries in MS MARCO are real user queries and passages are extracted from web documents obtained via Bing search results. Table 1 gives some examples of (query, passage, relevance) triples.
The relevance scores of TREC-DL 2019 are evaluated by human assessors. The scores (Voorhees et al., 2020, Section 2.2) are integer values using a 4-point scale (0-3) where:
3.
Perfectly relevant: The passage is dedicated to the query and contains the exact answer.
2.
Highly relevant: The passage has some answer for the query, but the answer may be a bit unclear, or hidden amongst extraneous information.
1.
Related: The passage seems related to the query but does not answer it.
0.
Irrelevant: The passage has nothing to do with the query.
For both experiments, the LLMs are instructed to only output the relevance score of the (query, document) pair. Any response that could not be casted to an integer between 0 and 3 was considered a failed response. All others are successes. For each model, the (query, document) pairs were divided into successes and failures. The intersection of all successful pairs was kept for final analysis.

BM25 baseline:
BM25 is a probabilistic scoring function that ranks documents based on query term frequencies while balancing the effects of term frequency and document length.
The BM25 scoring function is defined as:
BM25⁡(d,q)=∑t∈qIDF⁡(t)​ft,d​(k1+1)ft,d+k1​(1−b+b​|d|avgdl)\mathrm{BM25}(d,q)=\sum_{t\in q}\mathrm{IDF}(t)\frac{f_{t,d}(k_{1}+1)}{f_{t,d}+k_{1}\left(1-b+b\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)}
where qq is the query, ft,df_{t,d} is the frequency of term tt in document dd, k1k_{1} controls the degree of term-frequency saturation, |d||d| is the length of document dd, avgdl is the average document length in the collection, and b∈[0,1]b\in[0,1] controls the strength of document length normalization.
The inverse document frequency (IDF) reduces the weight of common terms while increasing the importance of rarer terms across the collection:
IDF​(t)=log⁡N−nt+0.5nt+0.5\text{IDF}(t)=\log\frac{N-n_{t}+0.5}{n_{t}+0.5}
where NN is the total number of documents and ntn_{t} is the number of documents containing term tt.
Similarly to Voorhees et al., 2020, BM25 with the default parameters (k1=1.5k_{1}=1.5, b=0.75b=0.75) was chosen as a baseline ranking system.
The rankings yielded by both LLM-RJS and NERS will be compared to the one of BM25.
Model evaluation:
The relevance estimates from NERS and BM25 cannot be directly compared to ground truth relevance scores, due to different representations of relevance. Instead, the document rankings, i.e. the set of documents ordered by their relevance to the query, yielded by the different systems can be directly compared.
The Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) (Wang et al., 2013) is a measure of ranking quality, which we will use to compare the rankings obtained by different retrieval systems.
NDCG is defined as:
NDCG⁡(A)=DCG⁡(A)DCG⁡(I),\mathrm{NDCG}(A)=\frac{\mathrm{DCG}(A)}{\mathrm{DCG}(I)},
DCG⁡(R)=∑i=1Nh⁡(di)log⁡(i+1),\quad\mathrm{DCG}(R)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{h(d_{i})}{\log(i+1)},
where II is the ideal ranking and h⁡(di)h(d_{i}) is the relevance of document dd at position ii in the ranking. NDCG ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 corresponding to the ideal ranking induced by human relevance judgments.
NDCG@k is NDCG evaluated for the sub-ranking that consists of the first kk documents in the initial ranking.
Since each query yields a ranking, and the dataset consists of multiple queries, the average NDCG and NDCG@k with k=5k=5 and k=10k=10 over queries will be computed as final evaluation metrics.
4.1 Results and Discussion
For each query, all the passages in relation with it have been ranked, and the NDCG, NDCG@5 and NDCG@10 has been computed for the rank yielded by each model. Figure 3 shows the results of Experiment 1 as a violin plot with NDCG, NDCG@5 and NDCG@10 of all models.
The NERS and LLM-RJS models performed on par with previous models Hofstätter et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2023. These previous models are purposely trained dense retrievers, unlike the models analyzed by this paper, which are zero-shot. Note that the zero-shot models seem to outperform trained retrievers. The presented NDCG@10 of these previous models was reported in the respective papers and not implemented here.
For this experiment, only Llama3.1:8b, Llama3.2:1b and Sonnet 4.5 had any failures, out of which, Llama3.2:1bs and Sonnet 4.5 have been excluded for having failed more than 2/3 of all data points (lack of instruction compliance, see Appendix A. Table 5 provides an example of failure for each model.). The models failed to only output one integer between 0 and 3. Expectedly, one of the excluded models is Llama3.2:1b, the smallest model in the selection, with 8312/9260 failed responses. Surprisingly, the other one was Sonnet 4.5, the state-of-the-art (at the time of writing) of Anthropic, which failed to only give relevance scores for 7473/9260 data points. Llama3.1:8b only failed 84/9260 data points.
Figure 4: Overview of Experiment 2. The LLM estimates relevance of (query, document) pairs in "high reasoning" and "low reasoning" conditions. The mean absolute error between LLM and human assessment in the two conditions is used as evaluation metric.
Overall, NERS are still most proficient for ranking documents, and perform the task better for only a fraction of the cost, and a fraction of the time. The price per 1 million tokens strongly favors NERS, with Gemini Embeddings costing 0.15$ per 1 million tokens. On the other side, GPT-5.1 costs 1.25$ per 1 million input tokens and 10$ per 1 million output tokens. Albeit financially undesirable, LLMs do not leave much on the table, when it comes to ranking (query, document) pairs.
The closed-source models consistently outperform the open models. The bottom line is, if one is looking for the best option, Gemini Embeddings is the winning model. If, aside performance, running the retrieval system locally is also a consideration, nomic-embed-text:v1.5 is a viable option.

Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning Wei et al. (2022) has been shown to yield substantial improvement in LLM capabilities on a variety of tasks. It works by letting the LLM generate a longer series of tokens as “reasoning” before giving the final answer. This strategy has been shown to lead to better results in image Information Retrieval Wu et al. (2025), and is therefore also promising for having LLM-RJS outperform NERS models.
We prompt GPT-5.1 to estimate the relevance of (document, query) pairs with the prompt described in Appendix A. To compare the reasoning against the non-reasoning conditions we change the ‘‘reasoning effort’’33

Since the LLM-RJS can directly estimate prompt relevance under the same definitions as the human annotations, the relevance estimates are compared directly, instead of by the ranking. Figure 5 shows the confusion matrices of the non-reasoning and reasoning LLM-RJS relevance rating compared to human relevance ratings.
We observe reasonable agreement between the LLM-RJS systems and the human annotations, but find that in many cases the LLM-RJS assigns higher relevance ratings than the humans annotations. This is particularly visible in the large number of cases where the human rating has relevance 0 (irrelevant), while the LLM-RJS has relevance 1 (related). This difference increases for the reasoning model, which identifies yet more (document, query) pairs as related. These higher ratings are also reflected in the average relevance ratings, where the average human rating is 0.79, LLM-RJS without reasoning is 1.03, and with reasoning is 1.23.
Figure 5: Differences between human and LLM relevance ratings with and without reasoning for GPT 5.1. The LLMs overall are more likely to see relevance in documents, especially with reasoning.
Particularly interesting is that the reasoning configuration, which would be expected to perform better, has double the incidences where human ratings identify a pair as irrelevant (0), while the LLM-RJS identifies the pair as perfectly relevant.
Table 3 shows examples from these cases. Predominantly, the passages are actually highly relevant, but might not be immediately obvious. The “difference between a mcdouble and a double cheeseburger” is indeed one slice of cheese instead of two. This may not be obvious to human annotators or short-sighted NERS models that evaluate similarity, but in these cases the reasoning LLM-RJS outperforms the benchmark.
Overall the results from Experiment 2 show that the addition of reasoning allows the model to identify relevance that is not obvious, beyond the human annotations.
6 Conclusion
We proposed that the short-sightedness problem of NERS may be resolved with relevance reasoning LLM-RJS. Similiarity is not always sufficient for relevance. In Experiment 1 we evaluated whether these LLM-RJS outperform NERS on a ranking task, and do not find improvement. In Experiment 2 we added reasoning, and directly compared the LLM-RJS relevance rankings to the human rankings. This showed the the LLM-RJS was able to identify more documents as relevant than the human annotations. This suggests that the human annotations suffer from similar short-sightedness as the NERS systems, explaining why the LLM-RJS do not reach a noticeable improvement compared to NERS when evaluated on NDCG with short-sighted human annotations as ground truth.
Limitations
The primary limitation to LLM-RJS systems is the added cost compared to embedding models. The price-per-token of top performing LLMs is roughly 10x that of equivalent embedding models. Additionally, for NERS systems implemented at scale, each document only needs to be embedded once even for multiple queries, and all document embeddings can be stored in a vector database for efficient retrieval. The LLM-RJS system demonstrated here requires LLM inference for each (query, document) pair and therefore does not scale well. A solution may be found in CoT re-ranking Wu et al. (2025), where an embedding model is used to make a first selection of documents that are similar, and then the reasoning-LLM selects the relevant documents from the subset of similar documents. However, this only addresses the problem where similar documents are not relevant, but would still fail in cases as the example in Figure 1, where the document is relevant but not similar.
Additionally, we should consider that the short-sightedness in human annotations is not universal for all datasets. We have shown cases of short-sightedness in this dataset, but through expert annotators, instructions that support further research, and more time for annotations it is possible to construct relevance annotations that do not suffer from short-sightedness. Since LLM-RJS models do not need large training datasets, efforts can be focused on only creating test sets.
Future work
The presented results suggest, but do not definitively show that LLM-RJS models can outperform human annotations. To establish the performance difference between human relevance and LLM-RJS relevance re-annotation may be done where trained annotators compare existing labels and LLM-RJS relevance judgements and establish which is correct. This can be supported by additionally providing the reasoning chain from the LLM-RJS, to provide argumentation for the judgment.
We have established in this instance of an Information Retrieval a mismatch between similarity and relevance, and show this as a nearsightedness-bottleneck for embeddings. However, for a variety of different Information Retrieval tasks, a variety of retrieval requirements exist beyond ‘relevance’. In tasks further conditions apply such as preferring high-quality documents, while in other tasks specifically similarity might be preferred over relevance (e.g. for detecting copyright infringement). With advanced Information Retrieval capabilities of LLM-RJS models, soft requirements can be considered, and there are more possibilities to deviate from ‘similarity’ or requirements that can be quantified. Evaluating LLM-RJS against human annotations still appears to be the standard Arabzadeh and Clarke (2025), and efforts are needed to re-specify what the (sub)tasks of Information Retrieval problems are, and how LLM-RJS models can be evaluated.
In conclusion, we found that the short-sightedness motivation presented in the illustrative Figure 1 is a valid limitation of NERS models that can be addressed by LLM-RJS. However, as shown with the examples of short-sighted annotations in Table 3, human annotations suffer from the same short-sightedness limitation, they present as false-positives for a confusion matrix in Figure 5. Therefore, under a standard annotated benchmark evaluation as shown in Figure 3, they do not demonstrably outperform NERS, while they still could be giving better relevance judgments.

Appendix A Prompt
The prompt that was used for all LLMs was:
You are an automatic passage relevance judge for information retrieval.
Your task is to rate how relevant a single passage is to a given query.
Follow these instructions:
- Carefully read the query and the passage.
- Decide how relevant the passage is to the query only, ignoring any external knowledge you might have.
- Assign exactly one integer relevance score using this 4-point scale:
3 - Perfectly relevant:
The passage is clearly dedicated to the query and contains the exact answer or all key information needed to answer it. No important part of the answer is missing.
Example:
Query: "What is chocolate?"
Passage: "Chocolate is a food made by roasting and grinding cacao seeds."
Score: 3
2 - Highly relevant:
The passage contains some answer to the query, but the answer may be partially unclear, incomplete, indirect, or mixed with a lot of extraneous information. Still, it provides clear help in answering the query.
Example:
Query: "Labrador breed"
Passage: "Labradors are friendly dogs often used as guide animals."
Score: 2
(Passage is about Labradors but doesn’t define the breed fully.)
1 - Related:
The passage is about a topic clearly related to the query, but it does not actually answer the query. It may provide background, context, or tangential information only.
Example:
Query: "What is chocolate?"
Passage: "Cacao trees grow in tropical
climates."
Score: 1 (Related ingredient, but no definition of chocolate.)
0 - Irrelevant:
The passage has nothing to do with the query or is only extremely weakly related, and it does not help answer the query at all.
Example:
Query: "Labrador breed"
Passage: "The Eiffel Tower is in Paris."
Score: 0
Output format:
You must always return a single integer score between 0 and 3 and NOTHING else.
