Abstract
Preference-based alignment methods (e.g., RLHF, DPO) typically optimize a single scalar objective, implicitly averaging over heterogeneous human preferences. In practice, systematic annotator and user-group disagreement makes mean-reward maximization brittle and susceptible to proxy over-optimization. We propose Disagreement-Aware Alignment via Risk-Constrained Decoding (DARC), a retraining-free inference-time method that frames response selection as distributionally robust, risk-sensitive decision making. Given multiple preference samples or scalable disagreement proxies, DARC reranks candidates by maximizing a KL-robust (entropic) satisfaction objective, and provides simple deployment controls that cap or penalize the corresponding entropic risk premium relative to the mean, enabling explicit risk budgets without retraining. We provide theoretical characterization linking this decoding rule to principled pessimism and KL-based distributionally robust optimization. Experiments on alignment benchmarks show that DARC reduces disagreement and tail risk while maintaining competitive average quality under noisy, heterogeneous feedback.
Keywords: Machine Learning, ICML

1 Introduction
Preference data has become the dominant supervision signal for aligning large language models (Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020).
Most pipelines—RLHF with reward modeling and RL optimization (Christiano et al., 2017; Schulman et al., 2017),
offline preference objectives such as DPO and its refinements (Rafailov et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2024),
and reference-free single-stage variants such as ORPO (Hong et al., 2024)—share a common abstraction:
preferences are treated as noisy observations of a single latent scalar utility (e.g., Bradley–Terry) (Bradley and Terry, 1952).
This abstraction largely persists in newer reformulations such as KTO and IPO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024; Garg et al., 2025),
and even when reward models are made multi-dimensional via multi-head/objective designs (Wang et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024).
Yet, treating feedback as perturbations around a single scalar provides limited guidance for inference-time response selection under heterogeneous preferences (Hung et al., 2025),
and it also lacks a unifying robust-optimization account for common risk-penalized decoding heuristics.
However, real-world preferences are often heterogeneous rather than i.i.d. noise:
annotators disagree for systematic reasons (Zhang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).
Empirically, human ratings show substantial variance even on the raw Top-KK candidate pool (Appendix H.1),
suggesting that uncertainty is intrinsic rather than an artifact of our selection rule.
Under such plurality, maximizing the average reward μ^\hat{\mu} can be brittle (Casper et al., 2023),
and the issue is exacerbated by proxy over-optimization, which can improve an imperfect preference proxy while degrading the underlying target (Gao et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2024).
Recent work further shows that proxy misspecification can induce inference-time reward hacking:
as best-of-NN (a widely used decoding primitive (Sun et al., 2024)) or soft best-of-NN becomes greedier,
true utility can increase and then inevitably degrade (Huang et al., 2025a).
Best-of-Poisson and HedgeTune mitigate this effect by tuning inference-time parameters (Khalaf et al., 2025);
however, they primarily model risk through proxy–distortion tradeoffs under a single reward signal, rather than preference heterogeneity.
Closely related, pessimistic best-of-NN rules penalize atypical candidates via an auxiliary error model to mitigate reward hacking (Anonymous, 2025),
but they target distributional uncertainty of the reward model (e.g., atypicality/OOD) rather than disagreement-grounded risk across users.
In parallel, robustness has been pursued through training-time objectives such as robust DPO under noisy preferences (Wu et al., 2024),
which improve robustness via retraining and noise assumptions;
group-robust objectives that protect minority preference groups (Ramesh et al., 2024), which rely on access to group structure;
and uncertainty-aware reward modeling (Banerjee and Gopalan, 2024), which quantifies estimation uncertainty but does not by itself specify how to select responses under inference-time proxy shift (Ichihara et al., 2025).
Taken together, these lines suggest a common lesson:
when preferences are plural, the relevant object is not a deterministic score, but a random variable over users and annotation noise.
Yet, a principled inference-time selection rule that is explicitly risk-constrained under heterogeneous preferences remains underdeveloped.
We therefore study inference-time alignment under heterogeneous preferences through the lens of risk-constrained decision making (Chow et al., 2018; Tamar et al., 2015).
Given a fixed candidate set and noisy preference or reward scores, we derive a finite-sample pessimistic rule based on a lower confidence bound,
yielding high-probability guarantees for selecting a competitive response while controlling tail risk across prompts.
This leads to Disagreement-Aware Alignment via Risk-Constrained Decoding (DARC), an inference-time-only, retraining-free procedure that plugs into any LM and preference estimator.
DARC grounds risk in multi-annotator disagreement (Zhang et al., 2024) instantiated via validated proxy signals,
improving robustness on high-disagreement prompts (Fig. 1).
We include representative cases where Best-of-KK is polarizing or unstable, whereas DARC yields consistently preferred responses (Appendix H.10).
Beyond the statistical view, we give a distributionally robust optimization (DRO) characterization (Wiesemann et al., 2014; Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019),
viewing decoding as maximizing the worst-case expected satisfaction over local divergence neighborhoods (Namkoong and Duchi, 2016; Duchi et al., 2021).
This yields a practical KL-robust instantiation and situates widely used mean–dispersion scoring rules within the same DRO perspective,
clarifying the conditions under which they arise as principled risk-sensitive criteria (Duchi and Namkoong, 2019).
Contributions.
•
Method. We formulate inference-time alignment as risk-constrained decision making under heterogeneous preferences, with risk induced by preference uncertainty and annotator disagreement.
•
Theory. We connect LCB-based uniform pessimism to a KL-DRO view, yielding a closed-form entropic decoding objective and its constrained/penalized variants via an entropic risk premium.
•
Empirics. Across benchmarks, DARC improves disagreement and prompt-level tail risk with competitive mean quality; a dual-robust multi-scorer extension hedges scorer shift and proxy over-optimization, with a KL-regularized DRO interpretation over scorers.
2 Problem setup
Let ss denote a prompt (context) and let 𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s) be a realized candidate set produced by a fixed generator (e.g., sampling, beam variants, or a proposal model), with K:=|𝒴⁡(s)|K:=|\mathcal{Y}(s)|.
Conditioning on the candidate set.
The generator may be stochastic; throughout the analysis we condition on the realized 𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s).
All probabilities below are taken over the evaluation randomness (human or otherwise), holding 𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s) fixed.
Latent satisfaction under heterogeneous preferences.
For each (s,y)(s,y), let R⁡(s,y)∈ℝR(s,y)\in\mathbb{R} denote a (latent) user-satisfaction random variable capturing preference heterogeneity and evaluation noise, with mean μ⁡(s,y):=𝔼⁡[R⁡(s,y)]\mu(s,y):=\mathbb{E}[R(s,y)].
Intuitively, μ⁡(s,y)\mu(s,y) measures average quality.
KL-robust (entropic) value and risk premium.
For β>0\beta>0, define the entropic value
Vβ​(s,y):=−1β​log⁡𝔼⁡[exp⁡(−β​R​(s,y))],V_{\beta}(s,y):=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\big(-\beta\,R(s,y)\big)\right],
(1)
which is equivalent to a KL-based distributionally robust objective (Section 3.2).
We define the entropic risk premium.
RPβ​(s,y):=μ⁡(s,y)−Vβ​(s,y)≥0\mathrm{RP}_{\beta}(s,y):=\mu(s,y)-V_{\beta}(s,y)\geq 0
(2)
Decision problem (risk-aware decoding).
Conditioning on (s,𝒴⁡(s))(s,\mathcal{Y}(s)), each candidate y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s) induces an (unknown) satisfaction distribution over users/raters.
Our population objective is to select an output by solving
y⋆∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​Vβ​(s,y),y^{\star}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}V_{\beta}(s,y),
(3)
where entropic risk measure VβV_{\beta} is defined in (1).
We further consider explicit risk control via a budget or a penalty:
yτ⋆∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​Vβ​(s,y)s.t.RPβ​(s,y)≤τ,y^{\star}_{\tau}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}V_{\beta}(s,y)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathrm{RP}_{\beta}(s,y)\leq\tau,
(4)
or in penalized (Lagrangian) form
arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​Vβ​(s,y)−λ​RPβ​(s,y).\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}V_{\beta}(s,y)-\lambda\,\mathrm{RP}_{\beta}(s,y).
3 Guarantees via Lower Confidence Bounds
This section provides a statistical justification for disagreement-aware decoding by deriving high-probability lower confidence bounds (LCBs) on expected satisfaction under heterogeneous preferences.
To contextualize the resulting pessimistic rules, we also give complementary distributionally robust optimization (DRO) characterizations.
Scalar satisfaction samples (guarantee setting).
For each candidate y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s), we observe nn i.i.d. scalar satisfaction samples
{Ri​(s,y)}i=1n\{R_{i}(s,y)\}_{i=1}^{n} drawn from the (unknown) distribution of R⁡(s,y)R(s,y):
Ri​(s,y)​∼i.i.d.​R​(s,y),i=1,…,n.R_{i}(s,y)\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}R(s,y),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
(5)
In this regime, the empirical mean and standard deviation
μ^n​(s,y)\hat{\mu}_{n}(s,y) and σ^n​(s,y)\hat{\sigma}_{n}(s,y) estimate μ⁡(s,y)\mu(s,y) and σ⁡(s,y)\sigma(s,y).
Moreover, the plug-in estimator of (1) is
V^β​(s,y):=−1β​log⁡(1n​∑i=1nexp⁡(−β​Ri​(s,y))),\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y):=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(-\beta\,R_{i}(s,y)\big)\right),
(6)
with the empirical risk premium
RP^β​(s,y):=μ^n​(s,y)−V^β​(s,y)≥0.\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y):=\hat{\mu}_{n}(s,y)-\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y)\ \geq 0.
(7)
In practice, one may operate with approximate (proxy) estimates of these empirical quantities; the analysis below does not rely on proxy scores being independent, and any such approximation induces only an additive slack in the resulting LCB objective (Appendix A.12).
3.1 Estimation risk: uniform LCB and a mean–dispersion surrogate
Fix a prompt ss and condition on 𝒴=𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}(s) with K:=|𝒴|K:=|\mathcal{Y}|.
For readability, write Ri​(y)R_{i}(y) for Ri​(s,y)R_{i}(s,y), and μ⁡(y),σ⁡(y)\mu(y),\sigma(y) for μ⁡(s,y),σ⁡(s,y)\mu(s,y),\sigma(s,y).
For each y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y}, we observe nn independent satisfaction samples with mean μ⁡(y)\mu(y) and variance σ2​(y)\sigma^{2}(y), and denote the empirical mean and standard deviation by
μ^n​(y):=1n​∑i=1nRi​(y)\hat{\mu}_{n}(y):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(y) and
σ^n2​(y):=1n−1​∑i=1n(Ri​(y)−μ^n​(y))2\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(y):=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}(y)-\hat{\mu}_{n}(y))^{2}.
Bridge to pairwise preferences.
Our scalar-sample analysis can be viewed as operating on standard scalarizations of pairwise preferences (e.g., win-rate or fitted BT/Thurstone scores); see Appendix A.14. Empirically, our main conclusions are stable when replacing absolute scalar ratings with a pairwise scalarization against the base response (Table 6).
Remark 3.1 (Shared annotators across candidates).
Our guarantee requires independence across ii for each fixed candidate yy.
Annotator overlap across candidates may induce dependence between {(μ^n​(y),σ^n​(y))}y∈𝒴\{(\hat{\mu}_{n}(y),\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y))\}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}},
but the uniform guarantee below follows from per-candidate concentration with a union bound and does not require independence across candidates.
Assumption 3.2 (Bounded rewards).
For all y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y}, the satisfaction samples are almost surely bounded:
Ri​(y)∈[a,b]a.s.R_{i}(y)\in[a,b]\quad\text{a.s.}
(8)
Boundedness matches typical rating scales and can be enforced for proxy scores via truncation (Appendix H.1); an analogous LCB holds under sub-Gaussian noise (Appendix A.4).
Proposition 3.3 (Uniform LCB).
There exists an absolute constant c>0c>0 such that for any δ∈(0,1)\delta\in(0,1), with probability at least 1−δ1-\delta, simultaneously for all y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s),
μ⁡(y)≥μ^n​(y)−c​σ^n​(y)​log⁡(K/δ)n−c⁡(b−a)​log⁡(K/δ)n.\mu(y)\geq\hat{\mu}_{n}(y)-c\,\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}}-c\,(b-a)\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}.
(9)
We denote the right-hand side by LCBδ​(y)\mathrm{LCB}_{\delta}(y).
Proof. See Appendix A.2.
Remark 3.4 (Variance governs estimation hardness).
The LCB form (9) highlights a statistical driver of selection risk: the dominant estimation error scales with the standard deviation σ⁡(y)\sigma(y).
The lower-order term decays as O⁡(1/n)O(1/n), whereas the leading term scales as O⁡(σ⁡(y)/n)O(\sigma(y)/\sqrt{n}).
Consequently, low-disagreement candidates (small σ⁡(y)\sigma(y)) admit substantially tighter confidence bounds at the same sample size, while controversial candidates require many more samples to certify their mean.
Penalizing σ^n​(y)\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y) therefore has an identification rationale: it discourages selecting candidates whose true quality is intrinsically harder to verify from limited feedback.
Lower-tail interpretation.
Maximizing a lower confidence bound is a principled way to avoid candidates with poor lower-tail satisfaction even when μ^n​(y)\hat{\mu}_{n}(y) is high (Boucheron et al., 2013; Vershynin, 2018).
This is a statistical conservatism argument rather than an equivalence to coherent tail-risk measures such as CVaR (Artzner et al., 1999; Rockafellar et al., 2000).
See Appendix A.4 for details.
On constants and practical calibration.
The uniform LCB in (9) is obtained via standard concentration plus a union bound over KK candidates and is therefore conservative.
We do not claim tight constants; instead, the bound motivates the functional form
λδ∝log⁡(K/δ)n,\lambda_{\delta}\;\propto\;\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}},
(10)
and the lower-order term c⁡(b−a)​log⁡(K/δ)nc(b-a)\tfrac{\log(K/\delta)}{n} is uniform across candidates and does not affect arg⁡maxy\arg\max_{y} decisions.
In practice, we treat the coefficient as a risk-budget knob (optionally scaled by a factor α\alpha) and fix it via a small held-out calibration, while reporting sensitivity of the resulting trade-off. Combined with the σ^n​(y)\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y) factor in (9), the uncertainty penalty scales as O~​(σ^n​(y)​log⁡K/n)\widetilde{O}(\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\sqrt{\log K/n}), matching the intuition that more controversial candidates require larger risk budgets to be selected.
LCB decoding and a mean–dispersion surrogate.
We define the LCB decoder as
yLCB∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴​LCBδ​(y).y_{\mathrm{LCB}}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathrm{LCB}_{\delta}(y).
(11)
On the high-probability event of Proposition 3.3, this rule maximizes a valid lower bound on the true mean satisfaction μ⁡(y)\mu(y).
Crucially, under bounded ratings, maximizing LCBδ​(y)\mathrm{LCB}_{\delta}(y) is equivalent (up to a constant) to a mean–dispersion surrogate:
arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(μ^n​(y)−λ​σ^n​(y)).\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\big(\hat{\mu}_{n}(y)-\lambda\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\big).
We provide the derivation in Corollary A.2 (Appendix A.3) and discuss the theoretical connection between this σ\sigma-penalty (estimation uncertainty) and the entropic σ2\sigma^{2}-penalty (risk aversion) in Remark A.3.
3.2 Distributional risk: DRO characterizations of pessimistic value
KL-robust (entropic) decoding.
We first consider a relative-entropy (KL) robustification of expected satisfaction.
Let R∈ℝR\in\mathbb{R} be the (latent) satisfaction with reference distribution ℙ\mathbb{P}.
We consider worst-case distributions ℚ\mathbb{Q} over RR that are absolutely continuous w.r.t. ℙ\mathbb{P}.
For β>0\beta>0, define the KL-regularized robust value
RobβKL(ℙ;R):=infℚ≪ℙ{𝔼ℚ[R]+β−1DKL(ℚ∥ℙ)},\mathrm{Rob}^{\mathrm{KL}}_{\beta}(\mathbb{P};R):=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[R]+\beta^{-1}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})\Big\},
(12)
where ℚ≪ℙ\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P} ensures the KL term is well-defined.
This is standard in risk-sensitive control and large deviations (Dupuis and Ellis, 2011; Hansen and Sargent, 2011).
Theorem 3.5 (KL-robust value equals an entropic objective).
For any β>0\beta>0 and any ℙ\mathbb{P} such that 𝔼ℙ​[exp⁡(−β​R)]<∞\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\exp(-\beta R)]<\infty,
RobβKL​(ℙ,R)=−1β​log⁡𝔼R∼ℙ​[exp⁡(−β​R)].\mathrm{Rob}^{\mathrm{KL}}_{\beta}(\mathbb{P};R)\;=\;-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\exp(-\beta R)\right].
(13)
In particular, for the empirical rater distribution ℙ^ny\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{y},
RobβKL​(ℙ^ny,R)=−1β​log⁡(1n​∑i=1nexp⁡(−β​Ri​(y)))=V^β​(s,y)\mathrm{Rob}^{\mathrm{KL}}_{\beta}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{y};R)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\!\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(-\beta R_{i}(y))\big)=\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y).See Appendix A.5 for the detailed proof.
Relation to Expected Utility Theory (CARA).
Maximizing the entropic value in (13) is equivalent to maximizing expected utility under a constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) utility, u⁡(x)=−exp⁡(−β​x)u(x)=-\exp(-\beta x).
A useful invariance is translation equivariance: for any constant cc, Vβ​(R+c)=Vβ​(R)+cV_{\beta}(R+c)=V_{\beta}(R)+c, hence the risk premium μ​(R)−Vβ​(R)\mu(R)-V_{\beta}(R) is invariant to additive reward shifts.
This is desirable in RLHF-style pipelines where learned reward models are often only identifiable up to an affine transformation.
χ2\chi^{2}-DRO yields a mean–dispersion special case.
We now give a complementary robust-optimization view (Wiesemann et al., 2014; Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019):
under a χ2\chi^{2}-divergence ambiguity set around ℙ\mathbb{P} (Ben-Tal and Nemirovski, 2002),
the worst-case expected satisfaction admits a mean–dispersion form.
(Definitions and tightness conditions are deferred to Appendix B.)
Proposition 3.6 (χ2\chi^{2}-DRO robust mean admits a mean–dispersion form).
Let RR be square-integrable under ℙ\mathbb{P} with mean μℙ:=𝔼ℙ​[R]\mu_{\mathbb{P}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[R]
and variance σℙ2:=Varℙ​(R)\sigma_{\mathbb{P}}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{P}}(R).
For any ρ≥0\rho\geq 0,
infℚ∈𝒰ρ​(ℙ)𝔼ℚ​[R]≥μℙ−ρ​σℙ.\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P})}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[R]\;\geq\;\mu_{\mathbb{P}}\;-\;\sqrt{\rho}\,\sigma_{\mathbb{P}}.
(14)
Specializing to ℙ=ℙ^ny\mathbb{P}=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{y} yields the empirical closed form
infℚ∈𝒰ρ​(ℙ^ny)𝔼ℚ​[R]=μ^n​(y)−ρ​v^n​(y),\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rho}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{y})}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[R]=\widehat{\mu}_{n}(y)-\sqrt{\rho}\,\sqrt{\widehat{v}_{n}(y)},
(15)
whenever the extremal density is nonnegative on the empirical support. Otherwise, the mean–dispersion form remains a valid lower bound (Remark B.1), ensuring the rule remains pessimistic.
Proof and tightness conditions.
See Appendix B for the extremal density characterization and a sufficient nonnegativity regime (e.g., bounded ratings imply tightness for small ρ\rho).
A unified pessimistic value (LCB as calibrated DRO).
Define the candidate-wise pessimistic value
Vδ​(y):=μ^n​(y)−c​σ^n​(y)​log⁡(K/δ)n−c⁡(b−a)​log⁡(K/δ)n,V_{\delta}(y):=\widehat{\mu}_{n}(y)-c\,\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}}-c\,(b-a)\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n},
(16)
which is a uniform lower confidence bound (LCB) under Proposition 3.3 (Appendix A.2).
On the other hand, Proposition 3.6 implies that, up to the lower-order term in (16),
Vδ​(y)V_{\delta}(y) matches the χ2\chi^{2}-DRO robust mean of the empirical rater distribution with radius
ρδ≍log⁡(K/δ)n,\rho_{\delta}\asymp\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n},
(17)
since ρδ​σ^n​(y)≍σ^n​(y)​log⁡(K/δ)/n\sqrt{\rho_{\delta}}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\asymp\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)/n} (see Appendix C for a discussion distinguishing statistical estimation risk from intrinsic disagreement).
This yields a unified interpretation of disagreement-aware decoding as calibrated pessimism:a statistical LCB rule that is equivalent (up to lower-order terms) to optimizing a local DRO objective. Thus, disagreement-aware decoding can be viewed equivalently as
(i) maximizing a high-probability LCB on expected satisfaction (statistical pessimism),
or (ii) maximizing a distributionally robust worst-case expected satisfaction over a local χ2\chi^{2} neighborhood (adversarial robustness).
4 Disagreement-Aware Risk-Constrained Decoding
Motivated by the LCB and DRO characterization in §3–§3.2, we now present practical decoding rules that implement the KL-robust entropic objective (and its constrained/penalized variants) over a finite candidate set.
For completeness, we also include a second-moment pessimistic surrogate implied by the LCB analysis in (42) as a computationally convenient ablation.
4.1 From preference maximization to risk controls
Standard reward-based reranking selects the highest estimated mean,
ymean​(s)∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​μ^​(s,y),y_{\text{mean}}(s)\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\hat{\mu}(s,y),
(18)
which optimizes average preference but may favor brittle outputs when disagreement is large.
Risk-sensitive decoding (primary rule).
Our primary decoder selects the candidate with the largest scorer-robust entropic value:
yEntropic​(s)∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​V^β​(s,y),y_{\text{Entropic}}(s)\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y),
(19)
where V^β\widehat{V}_{\beta} is the entropic value defined in (6).
Risk-constrained decoding via an entropic risk premium.
We define explicit deployment knobs using the entropic risk premium
RP^β​(s,y)\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y) from (7).
We consider two standard forms:
yτ​(s)\displaystyle y_{\tau}(s)
∈argmaxy∈𝒴⁡(s)V^β(s,y)s.t.RP^β(s,y)≤τ,\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y)\leq\tau,
(20)
yλ​(s)\displaystyle y_{\lambda}(s)
∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​V^β​(s,y)−λ​RP^β​(s,y),\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y)-\lambda\,\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y),
(21)
where the penalty form is a Lagrangian relaxation.
If the feasible set {y∈𝒴⁡(s):RP^β​(s,y)≤τ}\{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s):\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y)\leq\tau\} is empty, we fall back to
𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s), consistent with Algorithm 1.
The constrained and penalized forms provide two equivalent ways to tune the reward–risk trade-off; their finite-candidate-set relationship is given in Appendix A.6.
Disagreement as a risk proxy: interpretation and limitations.
We use disagreement as a human-centric signal of preference heterogeneity, and implement risk sensitivity through the KL-robust entropic value (19) (a standard convex risk measure).
The mean–dispersion form arises only as a finite-sample surrogate (via LCB pessimism) or as a χ2\chi^{2}-DRO special case, rather than being the defining principle of our method.
Importantly, we do not claim that variance is a coherent risk measure (e.g., CVaR) (Artzner et al., 1999; Rockafellar et al., 2000) or that σ\sigma is equivalent to a tail-risk functional in full generality.
Instead, §3 shows that penalizing σ^\hat{\sigma} arises naturally from maximizing a statistically justified lower confidence bound (LCB) on μ⁡(s,y)\mu(s,y), yielding a principled pessimistic selection rule under finite data.
Moreover, §3.2 provides a complementary distributionally robust optimization characterization (Wiesemann et al., 2014; Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019).

4.2 Practical decoding: ϵ\epsilon-tie breaking
Proxy robustness.
When (μ^,σ^)(\hat{\mu},\hat{\sigma}) are obtained from scalable proxy scorers rather than i.i.d. human samples, a uniform proxy-error assumption yields a robust LCB guarantee; we defer the formal statement and proof to Appendix A.12.
ϵ\epsilon-rule as a constrained optimization.
Our ϵ\epsilon-tie-breaking is equivalent to
yϵ​(s)∈arg⁡miny∈𝒴⁡(s)​σ^​(s,y)s.t.V^β​(s,y)≥V^max​(s)−ϵ,y_{\epsilon}(s)\in\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\widehat{\sigma}(s,y)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y)\geq\widehat{V}_{\max}(s)-\epsilon,
(22)
where V^max​(s):=maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)⁡V^β​(s,y)\widehat{V}_{\max}(s):=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y).
Pareto view.
Eq. (22) selects a Pareto-optimal point of the robust-value–disagreement trade-off: among candidates whose robust value is within ϵ\epsilon of the best, it returns the least controversial one.
Moreover, when the chosen point is supported, the same solution can be written as a scalarization
arg⁡maxy⁡(V^β,γ​(s,y)−λϵ​σ^​(s,y))\arg\max_{y}\big(\widehat{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y)-\lambda_{\epsilon}\widehat{\sigma}(s,y)\big) for some λϵ≥0\lambda_{\epsilon}\geq 0;
formal statements and proofs are deferred to Appendix A.13.
Two-stage rule.
To avoid sacrificing entropic value excessively for marginal robustness, we use:
ℱϵ​(s)\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(s)
:={y∈𝒴⁡(s)∣V^β​(s,y)≥V^max​(s)−ϵ},\displaystyle:=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)\mid\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y)\geq\widehat{V}_{\max}(s)-\epsilon\},
(23)
yϵ​(s)\displaystyle y_{\epsilon}(s)
∈arg⁡miny∈ℱϵ​(s)​σ^​(s,y).\displaystyle\in\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(s)}\widehat{\sigma}(s,y).
This approximates the scalarized decoder maxy⁡(V^β​(s,y)−λ​σ^​(s,y))\max_{y}\big(\widehat{V}_{\beta}(s,y)-\lambda\,\widehat{\sigma}(s,y)\big) by enforcing near-optimality in entropic value and then selecting the least controversial candidate.
In practice, we tune all risk parameters (β,τ,ϵ\beta,\tau,\epsilon) on a held-out development set; detailed tuning protocols are provided in Appendix H.4.
4.3 Multi-scorer robustness
Scorer-robust decoding (multi-reward models).
In scalable proxy settings, satisfaction scores may come from learned reward models and be sensitive to model shift or proxy over-optimization.
We optionally hedge this scorer ambiguity by using a family of MM scorers indexed by m∈[M]m\in[M].
For each candidate y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s), scorer mm provides nn scalar samples {Rm,i​(s,y)}i=1n\{R_{m,i}(s,y)\}_{i=1}^{n} (e.g., via perturbations), from which we compute the scorer-specific entropic value V^β,m​(s,y)\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y) and risk premium RP^β,m​(s,y)\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta,m}(s,y).
Because reward scales can differ across scorers, we apply a per-prompt affine normalization (Appendix E).
We then aggregate scorer-specific entropic values using a soft worst-case operator:
V~β,γ​(s,y):=−1γ​log⁡(1M​∑m=1Mexp⁡(−γ​V^β,m​(s,y))),\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y):=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y)\big)\right),
(24)
and aggregate risk premia pessimistically by RP~β​(s,y):=maxm∈[M]⁡RP^β,m​(s,y)\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y):=\max_{m\in[M]}\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta,m}(s,y).
We decode by maximizing V~β,γ​(s,y)\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y) subject to RP~β​(s,y)≤τ\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y)\leq\tau (or its penalized form).
Proposition 4.1 (Scorer aggregation as KL-regularized DRO over scorers).
Let vm:=V^β,m​(s,y)v_{m}:=\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y) and 𝐯∈ℝM\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.
Define 𝐮=(1/M,…,1/M)\mathbf{u}=(1/M,\ldots,1/M) and the simplex ΔM:={𝐪∈ℝ+M:∑m=1Mqm=1}\Delta_{M}:=\{\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}_{+}:\sum_{m=1}^{M}q_{m}=1\}.
Then the soft worst-case aggregation (24) admits the variational form
V~β,γ(s,y)=inf𝐪∈ΔM{∑m=1Mqmvm+1γDKL(𝐪∥𝐮)}.\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y)=\inf_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta_{M}}\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}q_{m}v_{m}+\frac{1}{\gamma}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{u})\right\}.
(25)
Interpretation.
Eq. (25) shows that γ\gamma interpolates between averaging across scorers (γ→0\gamma\!\to\!0) and a worst-case scorer (γ→∞\gamma\!\to\!\infty), yielding a principled hedge against scorer shift. In particular, minm⁡vm≤V~β,γ​(s,y)≤1M​∑mvm\min_{m}v_{m}\leq\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y)\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}v_{m}, and V~β,γ​(s,y)\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y) is non-increasing in γ\gamma.
5 Experiment
5.1 Experimental Setup and Implementation Details
Candidate sets and evaluation protocol.
For each prompt ss, we generate a fixed candidate pool 𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s) of size KK (shared across all methods),
so differences arise solely from the inference-time selection rule.
In the human-grounded setting, for each y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s) we collect nn scalar satisfaction ratings
{ri​(s,y)}i=1n\{r_{i}(s,y)\}_{i=1}^{n} (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022),
and split annotators into disjoint selection and evaluation sets
ℐsel\mathcal{I}_{\text{sel}} and ℐeval\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}} to avoid selection–evaluation leakage.
All decoders operate on statistics computed from ℐsel\mathcal{I}_{\text{sel}}.
Our entropic decoder uses the empirical entropic value in the human-grounded setting (where M=1M=1 and V~β,γ=V^β\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}=\widehat{V}_{\beta}),
and uses the scorer-robust aggregated value V~β,γ​(s,y)\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y) (and RP~β​(s,y)\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y)) in proxy settings with multiple reward models.
Second-moment baselines use only (μ^sel​(s,y),σ^sel​(s,y))(\hat{\mu}_{\text{sel}}(s,y),\hat{\sigma}_{\text{sel}}(s,y)) (or their scorer-indexed analogues under proxies).
All reported metrics are computed on held-out ratings in ℐeval\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}} (definitions in Appendix H.3).
Estimating mean and disagreement.
We define (μ^,σ^)(\hat{\mu},\hat{\sigma}) as the mean and disagreement estimates, derived from either empirical samples or scorer proxies .
DARC is inference-time only, modifying the selection over 𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s) without retraining.
While our theory assumes multi-annotator feedback, we validate proxy uncertainty as a scalable approximation for human disagreement.
Proxies, baselines, and hyperparameters.
When multi-rater human scores are unavailable, we use scalable proxy scorers (e.g., ensembles / bootstrap reward models)
to obtain a proxy mean together with either (i) a score distribution for computing V^β\widehat{V}_{\beta}, or
(ii) a proxy disagreement diagnostic σ^proxy\hat{\sigma}_{\text{proxy}} (Kendall and Gal, 2017; Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017);
we validate proxy–human alignment via rank correlation and stratified analyses (Uma et al., 2021).
Inference-time baselines include mean Best-of-KK,
inference-time hedging methods for reward hacking (Best-of-Poisson and HedgeTune) (Khalaf et al., 2025),
pessimistic best-of-NN reranking via an auxiliary error/atypicality model (Caution) (Anonymous, 2025),
uncertainty-based reranking (e.g., MC-Dropout) (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016; Kendall and Gal, 2017),
reward-regularized decoding (RBoN) (Jinnai et al., 2024) and DeAL (top-kk lookahead reward-guided decoding) (Huang et al., 2025b);
training-time baselines include cDPO/rDPO (Mitchell, 2023; Chowdhury et al., 2024).
We report default settings and full tuning ranges (including our LCB- and DRO-instantiated decoding rules),
along with complete baseline configurations and implementation details,
in Appendix H.2 (see also Appendix F).
Inference overhead.
Disagreement estimation adds only a modest inference-time cost: in our implementation, using Naug=4/8/16N_{\text{aug}}=4/8/16 increases end-to-end latency by only ≈1.5%/2.0%/3.2%\approx 1.5\%/2.0\%/3.2\%, since candidate generation dominates runtime (Appendix H.6, Table 7).
Metrics (mean, risk, and tail).
For each prompt ss and candidate response yy, we evaluate rewards on a held-out rater set ℐeval\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}} and report the empirical mean μ^eval​(s,y)\hat{\mu}_{\text{eval}}(s,y).
As a scalable proxy for robustness, we compute a perturbation-sensitivity statistic σ^sel​(s,y)\hat{\sigma}_{\text{sel}}(s,y) as the standard deviation of rewards across NaugN_{\text{aug}} style-preserving perturbations of yy
(see Appendix H.13).
Human evaluation details are in Appendix I; the human mean is the average rating and the human variance is the sample variance across raters.
We summarize performance via Tradeoffeval​(s,y):=μ^eval​(s,y)−λ​σ^sel​(s,y)\mathrm{Tradeoff}_{\text{eval}}(s,y):=\hat{\mu}_{\text{eval}}(s,y)-\lambda\,\hat{\sigma}_{\text{sel}}(s,y), using the same λ\lambda for all methods (Appendix H.14).
We also report CVaR10%\mathrm{CVaR}_{10\%} over prompts (Chow et al., 2015); see Appendix H.3.

Automated proxy evaluation.
Table 1 reports MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) and AlpacaEval2.0 (Li et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2024) results under two instruction-tuned generators (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Dubey et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025)).
We evaluate each method by mean reward μ^\hat{\mu}, proxy disagreement risk σ^\hat{\sigma} (reward sensitivity to style-preserving perturbations), and a risk–reward tradeoff score Tradeoff=μ^−λ​σ^\mathrm{Tradeoff}=\hat{\mu}-\lambda\hat{\sigma} with the same λ\lambda across methods; we also report Len(tok) to control for length bias.
Across both generators, inference-time risk-aware selection improves robustness: DARC variants reduce σ^\hat{\sigma} while keeping μ^\hat{\mu} competitive, yielding higher Tradeoff\mathrm{Tradeoff} than mean-only Best-of-KK, with larger gains on the high-variance subset (top 20% prompts by baseline σ^\hat{\sigma}) and broadly stable Len(tok).
Training-time robust policies (cDPO/rDPO) exhibit different trade-offs, and DARC remains complementary: applying DARC-ϵ\epsilon as an inference-time plug-in can further reduce σ^\hat{\sigma} and improve Tradeoff\mathrm{Tradeoff} in several cases, supporting a modular view where training shapes the policy while DARC calibrates risk on a fixed candidate set.
Human-loop evaluation and tail robustness.
We then close the loop with multi-annotator ratings on MT-Bench.
Table 2 shows that DARC improves risk-sensitive criteria (Tradeoff and CVaR10%) while maintaining competitive mean satisfaction.
On the high-disagreement subset, disagreement-aware decoding yields substantial gains in tail robustness (CVaR10%) and conservative quality (Tradeoff), indicating that risk-controlled selection is particularly beneficial on genuinely controversial prompts.
Figure 3 demonstrates that Risk-adjusted satisfaction(Δ\DeltaTradeoff) scale positively with disagreement, rising steadily from Q1 to Q5, peaking in the most controversial buckets (Q5). Validity of the disagreement proxy.
We validate our perturbation-sensitivity proxy σ^\hat{\sigma} against human disagreement measured by multiple independent rater scores on the same (prompt, response) pair.
To avoid selection effects, we compute both proxy and human disagreement on the baseline candidate.
As summarized in Figure 4, proxy disagreement shows a statistically significant rank-level consistency with human disagreement and remains positively associated even after controlling for mean reward and response length (strong and common confounders for RM-based signals).
Moreover, the proxy serves as an effective risk filter for identifying prompts likely to exhibit preference heterogeneity, where risk-controlled decoding is designed to intervene: prompts identified as high-disagreement by the proxy substantially overlap with those identified by humans, far beyond random chance (Fig. 4).
While mismatch cases exist (App. H.11)—often reflecting surface-form sensitivity (FP) or orthogonal validity/completeness issues (FN)—this is consistent with using σ^\hat{\sigma} as a scalable screening signal to prioritize human verification rather than a fully calibrated estimator of disagreement magnitude.
Consistently, this signal effectively allocates risk control: bucketed analysis shows a monotonic increase in Δ​Tradeoff\Delta\mathrm{Tradeoff} from low- to high-disagreement groups when bucketing by either human or proxy disagreement (App. H.7).
We also stratify prompts by proxy–human disagreement alignment and find that, even in the worst-alignment bucket, DARC still achieves the best Tradeoff among all baselines (Appendix H.8, Table 8).

Scaling robustness.
To assess scale robustness, we additionally evaluate our inference-time decoding rules on a stronger generator, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, using the same candidate-pool protocol and metrics.
We observe consistent improvements over mean Best-of-KK in risk-sensitive criteria (Tradeoff and prompt-level CVaR), with gains again concentrating on high-disagreement prompts (Table 14).
Hyperparameter sensitivity and ablations.
Figure 2 summarizes a sensitivity study over the main knobs of DARC-ϵ\epsilon, reporting improvements over mean Best-of-KK in Δ​Tradeoff\Delta\mathrm{Tradeoff} and Δ​CVaR\Delta\mathrm{CVaR} on fixed candidate pools.
Increasing the candidate pool size KK improves both metrics with diminishing returns (Fig. 2a).
The risk sensitivity β\beta in the entropic robust value V^β\widehat{V}_{\beta} yields rapid robustness gains that plateau at moderate values (Fig. 2b).
Fixing β\beta, enlarging the near-optimal set via ϵ\epsilon further improves robustness up to saturation (with slight degradation when overly permissive) (Fig. 2c).
Finally, the perturbation budget NaugN_{\text{aug}} used to estimate proxy disagreement σ^\hat{\sigma} saturates quickly; a small budget (e.g., 44–88) suffices, keeping inference overhead modest (Fig. 2d).
6 Conclusion
We cast decoding-time alignment under heterogeneous preferences as risk-constrained decision making.
DARC uses a KL-robust (entropic) decoding rule with a conservative LCB/DRO interpretation, improving lower-tail outcomes while preserving mean quality.
Limitations include a finite candidate pool and scorer bias; perturbation-based disagreement proxies are scalable screening signals, not calibrated human-disagreement estimates.
Future work includes richer robustness signals and user/group-conditional risk control.
Impact Statement
This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning.
Our contributions focus on inference-time response selection under preference heterogeneity.
As with many machine learning methods, the proposed approach may exhibit uneven performance if the underlying proxies or learned scorers encode dataset- or population-specific biases.
We encourage reporting sensitivity to key design choices and auditing proxy/scorer behavior when the method is used in practice.
Consequently, the method should be used and evaluated with appropriate care in deployment.

A.1 A self-normalized (empirical-variance) concentration bound
We first record a standard empirical-Bernstein style inequality (a self-normalized concentration bound)
that controls the deviation of the empirical mean in terms of the empirical standard deviation.
This lemma is used to prove Proposition 3.3.
Lemma A.1 (Empirical Bernstein bound (bounded case)).
Let X1,…,XnX_{1},\dots,X_{n} be i.i.d. supported on [a,b][a,b] with mean μ\mu.
Let
μ^:=1n​∑i=1nXi,σ^2:=1n−1​∑i=1n(Xi−μ^)2.\hat{\mu}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\hat{\mu})^{2}.
Then for any δ∈(0,1)\delta\in(0,1), with probability at least 1−δ1-\delta,
μ≥μ^−2​σ^2​log⁡(2/δ)n−7​(b−a)​log⁡(2/δ)3​(n−1).\mu\geq\hat{\mu}-\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{\sigma}^{2}\log(2/\delta)}{n}}-\frac{7(b-a)\log(2/\delta)}{3(n-1)}.
(26)
Remark.
Lemma A.1 is a standard form of empirical Bernstein inequality.
We use it because it yields the same structure as (9):
a leading term of order σ^​log⁡(1/δ)/n\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n} and a lower-order term of order log⁡(1/δ)/n\log(1/\delta)/n.
For completeness, we provide a short proof outline in Appendix A.7.
A.2 Proof of Proposition 3.3
Proof outline.
We apply an empirical-Bernstein (self-normalized) inequality for bounded random variables to each fixed yy,
then take a union bound over KK candidates with δ′=δ/K\delta^{\prime}=\delta/K.
An alternative derivation can be obtained from Freedman’s martingale inequality.
We then track constants to obtain (9).

Fix any y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y} and apply Lemma A.1 to {Ri​(y)}i=1n\{R_{i}(y)\}_{i=1}^{n} with δ′=δ/K\delta^{\prime}=\delta/K.

A.3 From LCB decoding to mean–dispersion decoding
This appendix formalizes the “ignoring constants / lower-order term” step used to connect uniform LCB maximization to a mean–dispersion surrogate (used as an ablation / efficient approximation), rather than as the defining objective of our primary KL-robust entropic decoder.
Corollary A.2 (Mean–dispersion surrogate form under bounded ratings).
Under the conditions of Proposition 3.3, define
LCBδ​(y):=μ^n​(y)−c​σ^n​(y)​log⁡(K/δ)n−c⁡(b−a)​log⁡(K/δ)n.\mathrm{LCB}_{\delta}(y):=\hat{\mu}_{n}(y)-c\,\hat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}}-c\,(b-a)\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}.

(27)
since the last term c⁡(b−a)​log⁡(K/δ)/nc\,(b-a)\log(K/\delta)/n is independent of yy.
Proof.
The two objectives differ by an additive constant that does not depend on yy, hence they have identical argmax sets.

Remark A.3 (Standard deviation vs. Variance penalization).
We note a structural distinction between the LCB-derived surrogate and the entropic objective. The LCB rule (Eq. 27) implies a penalty on the standard deviation σ^\hat{\sigma} (scaling as O(n−1/2)O(n^{-1/2})), which accounts for finite-sample estimation uncertainty. In contrast, the entropic objective (Eq. 1) approximates a mean-variance form μ^−β2​σ^2\hat{\mu}-\frac{\beta}{2}\hat{\sigma}^{2} under Taylor expansion, reflecting intrinsic risk aversion.
While theoretically distinct, both objectives penalize dispersion. We use the σ\sigma-based LCB form primarily to establish finite-sample consistency guarantees, while the entropic form serves as our primary method for capturing risk sensitivity. Empirically, both metrics effectively demote high-disagreement candidates.

A.5 Proof of Theorem 3.5
The equivalence between the KL-robust value and the entropic risk measure is a classical result in the theory of risk-sensitive control and large deviations, often referred to as the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula (Dupuis and Ellis, 2011). We provide the derivation here for completeness.

(31)

Appendix C Calibrating LCB scaling to χ2\chi^{2}-DRO radius
This appendix formalizes a numerical calibration between the LCB penalty in
Proposition 3.3 and the ambiguity radius in empirical χ2\chi^{2}-DRO.
Importantly, this calibration is not meant to claim that LCB and DRO model the same source of uncertainty:
LCB controls finite-sample estimation uncertainty for μ⁡(y)\mu(y), while χ2\chi^{2}-DRO is commonly interpreted
as distributional/aleatoric robustness. Here we use χ2\chi^{2}-DRO as a convenient variational
reparameterization of the same mean–dispersion functional, which yields an interpretable radius ρ\rho
corresponding to a chosen standard-deviation penalty λ\lambda.
Proposition C.1 (Exact λ\lambda–ρ\rho mapping for empirical χ2\chi^{2}-DRO).
Fix a candidate yy and its empirical distribution ℙ^ny\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{y}.

Then for any ρ≥0\rho\geq 0,

(51)
Equivalently, for a desired penalty λ≥0\lambda\geq 0, choosing
ρ⁡(λ):=nn−1​λ2\rho(\lambda):=\frac{n}{n-1}\,\lambda^{2}
(52)
makes the empirical χ2\chi^{2}-DRO robust mean exactly equal to μ^n​(y)−λ​σ^n​(y)\widehat{\mu}_{n}(y)-\lambda\,\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(y).
Beyond the nonnegativity regime.
The equality in (51) requires a sufficient nonnegativity condition
(Proposition 3.6). When this condition fails (e.g., due to heavy-tailed empirical support or large ρ\rho),
the closed-form expression may no longer be tight. Nevertheless, Proposition 3.6 still provides the
conservative lower bound
infℚ∈𝒰ρ​(ℙ^ny)𝔼ℚ​[R]≥μ^n​(y)−λρ​σ^n​(y),\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rho}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{y})}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[R]\;\geq\;\widehat{\mu}_{n}(y)-\lambda_{\rho}\,\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(y),
so the mean–dispersion form remains a valid pessimistic surrogate even outside the tightness regime.

Proposition 3.3 provides a uniform high-probability lower bound
μ⁡(y)≥μ^n​(y)−c​σ^n​(y)​log⁡(K/δ)n−c​ν​(y)​log⁡(K/δ)nsimultaneously for all ​y∈𝒴.\mu(y)\geq\widehat{\mu}_{n}(y)-c\,\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(y)\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}}-c\,\nu(y)\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}\quad\text{simultaneously for all }y\in\mathcal{Y}.

By Proposition C.1, in the nonnegativity regime the empirical χ2\chi^{2}-DRO robust mean under radius ρδ\rho_{\delta}

Interpretation of the nn-dependent radius.
The mapping ρδ∝log⁡(K/δ)/n\rho_{\delta}\propto\log(K/\delta)/n arises because λδ\lambda_{\delta} is chosen to control estimation error uniformly over KK candidates.
This nn-dependence should be interpreted as an estimation-calibrated DRO radius: as nn grows, the ambiguity set shrinks because the empirical mean becomes well-estimated.
However, we emphasize that in preference alignment, high empirical variance often signals intrinsic heterogeneity (disagreement) rather than pure noise.(empirically supported by the monotonic trend across disagreement buckets; see Fig 3)
Thus, while the statistical radius required for concentration decays with nn, the functional form of the penalty (i.e., penalizing dispersion) remains structurally isomorphic to a robustness constraint against disagreement.
This allows the LCB framework to serve a dual purpose: rigorously controlling finite-sample error while effectively acting as a proxy to demote controversial candidates.
Appendix D On dependence across candidates and the uniform guarantee
Our uniform guarantee in Proposition 3.3 does not require independence across candidates.
It only requires per-candidate concentration for each fixed yy and a union bound.
Lemma D.1 (Union bound without independence).
Let {ℰy}y∈𝒴\{\mathcal{E}_{y}\}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} be any collection of events (possibly dependent).
If for each y∈𝒴y\in\mathcal{Y} we have Pr⁡(ℰy)≥1−δ′\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{y})\geq 1-\delta^{\prime}, then
Pr⁡(⋂y∈𝒴ℰy)≥1−∑y∈𝒴Pr⁡(ℰyc)≥1−K​δ′,\Pr\left(\bigcap_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{E}_{y}\right)\geq 1-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{y}^{c})\geq 1-K\delta^{\prime},
where K:=|𝒴|K:=|\mathcal{Y}|.
Application to Proposition 3.3.
For each fixed yy, the self-normalized empirical-Bernstein inequality yields an event ℰy\mathcal{E}_{y}
such that Pr⁡(ℰy)≥1−δ′\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{y})\geq 1-\delta^{\prime} and on ℰy\mathcal{E}_{y} the bound (9) holds.
Annotator overlap across different candidates can create dependence among {ℰy}y∈𝒴\{\mathcal{E}_{y}\}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}},
but Lemma D.1 shows that the intersection event still holds with probability at least 1−K​δ′1-K\delta^{\prime}.
Setting δ′=δ/K\delta^{\prime}=\delta/K yields the stated uniform guarantee.
Appendix E Scorer ambiguity and multi-scorer aggregation
This appendix details our proxy instantiation that hedges scorer ambiguity when scalar satisfaction samples are produced by learned reward models.
We consider a family of MM scorers indexed by m∈[M]m\in[M].
For each prompt ss, candidate y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s), and scorer mm, we obtain nn scalar samples
{Rm,i​(s,y)}i=1n\{R_{m,i}(s,y)\}_{i=1}^{n} (e.g., via independent scorer noise, style-preserving perturbations, or repeated evaluations).
All analysis conditions on the realized candidate set 𝒴⁡(s)\mathcal{Y}(s).
E.1 Within-prompt normalization across scorers
Reward-model outputs may differ in scale and offset across scorers; to make aggregation meaningful, we normalize per prompt.
Define the pooled mean and standard deviation under scorer mm:
R¯m​(s)\displaystyle\bar{R}_{m}(s)
:=1K​n​∑y∈𝒴⁡(s)∑i=1nRm,i​(s,y),\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{Kn}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{m,i}(s,y),
(53)
s^m​(s)\displaystyle\hat{s}_{m}(s)
:=1K​n−1​∑y∈𝒴⁡(s)∑i=1n(Rm,i​(s,y)−R¯m​(s))2> 0.\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{Kn-1}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(R_{m,i}(s,y)-\bar{R}_{m}(s)\big)^{2}}\ >\ 0.
(54)
We form normalized samples
R~m,i​(s,y):=Rm,i​(s,y)−R¯m​(s)s^m​(s).\tilde{R}_{m,i}(s,y)\ :=\ \frac{R_{m,i}(s,y)-\bar{R}_{m}(s)}{\hat{s}_{m}(s)}.
(55)
When M=1M=1, this normalization can be omitted without changing the framework.
Implementation note.
The constants (R¯m​(s),s^m​(s))(\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s)) require access to all candidates y∈𝒴⁡(s)y\in\mathcal{Y}(s) for the given prompt ss.
In Algorithm 1, we therefore precompute (R¯m​(s),s^m​(s))(\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s)) once per scorer mm before
scoring individual candidates.
E.2 Scorer-specific entropic value, risk premium, and dispersion
For each scorer mm, we compute the scorer-specific empirical entropic value using normalized samples:
V^β,m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s)):=−1β​log⁡(1n​∑i=1nexp⁡(−β​R~m,i​(s,y))),\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))\ :=\ -\frac{1}{\beta}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(-\beta\,\tilde{R}_{m,i}(s,y)\big)\right),
(56)
and the corresponding empirical mean
μ^m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s)):=1n​∑i=1nR~m,i​(s,y).\hat{\mu}_{m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))\ :=\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{R}_{m,i}(s,y).
(57)
We define the scorer-specific empirical entropic risk premium as
RP^β,m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s)):=μ^m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s))−V^β,m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s))≥ 0.\widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta,m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))\ :=\ \hat{\mu}_{m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))-\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))\ \geq\ 0.
(58)
We also define the scorer-specific empirical standard deviation (used as a disagreement proxy) as
σ^m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s)):=1n−1​∑i=1n(R~m,i​(s,y)−μ^m​(s,y,R¯m​(s),s^m​(s)))2.\hat{\sigma}_{m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))\ :=\ \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\tilde{R}_{m,i}(s,y)-\hat{\mu}_{m}(s,y;\bar{R}_{m}(s),\hat{s}_{m}(s))\Big)^{2}}.
(59)
E.3 Scorer-robust aggregation and decoding rule
To hedge scorer shift, we aggregate scorer-specific entropic values via a soft worst-case (soft-min) operator with parameter γ>0\gamma>0:
V~β,γ​(s,y):=−1γ​log⁡(1M​∑m=1Mexp⁡(−γ​V^β,m​(s,y))),\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y):=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y)\big)\right),
(60)
which satisfies V~β,γ​(s,y)→minm∈[M]⁡V^β,m​(s,y)\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y)\to\min_{m\in[M]}\widehat{V}_{\beta,m}(s,y) as γ→∞\gamma\to\infty.
For explicit risk control, we aggregate risk premia pessimistically:
RP~β​(s,y):=maxm∈[M]⁡RP^β,m​(s,y).\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y):=\max_{m\in[M]}\ \widehat{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta,m}(s,y).
(61)
The scorer-robust risk-constrained decoding rule is then
y^∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​V~β,γ​(s,y)s.t.RP~β​(s,y)≤τ,\hat{y}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\ \widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y)\leq\tau,
(62)
or in penalized (Lagrangian) form,
y^∈arg⁡maxy∈𝒴⁡(s)​V~β,γ​(s,y)−λ​RP~β​(s,y).\hat{y}\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(s)}\ \widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma}(s,y)-\lambda\,\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta}(s,y).
(63)
E.4 Interpretation
The aggregation V~β,γ\widetilde{V}_{\beta,\gamma} hedges against worst-case scorers while remaining smooth and stable for finite γ\gamma.
The pessimistic risk-premium aggregation RP~β\widetilde{\mathrm{RP}}_{\beta} prevents a candidate from being selected if it is deemed high-risk by any scorer.
When scorer variability reflects genuine preference uncertainty (e.g., validated proxies or ensembles calibrated to human disagreement), this multi-scorer instantiation also promotes reliability under heterogeneous preferences.

F.3 Training-time robust policies: cDPO and rDPO
Preference data and DPO objective.
Let 𝒟={(s,y+,y−)}\mathcal{D}=\{(s,y^{+},y^{-})\} be a preference dataset, where for each input ss the response y+y^{+} is preferred over y−y^{-}.
Let πθ\pi_{\theta} be the policy to be trained and πref\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} a fixed reference policy.
Define the logit difference
Δθ​(s,y+,y−)=(log⁡πθ​(y+|s)−log⁡πθ​(y−|s))−(log⁡πref​(y+|s)−log⁡πref​(y−|s)).\Delta_{\theta}(s,y^{+},y^{-})=\Big(\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}|s)-\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}|s)\Big)-\Big(\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}|s)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}|s)\Big).
The standard DPO loss is
ℒDPO​(θ)=𝔼(s,y+,y−)∼𝒟​[−log⁡σ⁡(β​Δθ​(s,y+,y−))],\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,y^{+},y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[-\log\sigma\big(\beta\,\Delta_{\theta}(s,y^{+},y^{-})\big)\Big],
where σ⁡(⋅)\sigma(\cdot) is the logistic sigmoid and β>0\beta>0 controls the strength of preference optimization.
Conservative DPO (cDPO).
We implement a conservative variant by applying label smoothing to the pairwise preference target.
Specifically, for a smoothing parameter ε∈(0,1/2)\varepsilon\in(0,1/2), we use the smoothed loss
ℒcDPO​(θ)=𝔼(s,y+,y−)∼𝒟​[−(1−ε)​log⁡σ⁡(β​Δθ)−ε​log⁡(1−σ⁡(β​Δθ))].\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cDPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,y^{+},y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[-(1-\varepsilon)\log\sigma(\beta\,\Delta_{\theta})-\varepsilon\log\big(1-\sigma(\beta\,\Delta_{\theta})\big)\Big].
We denote the resulting trained policy as πcDPO\pi_{\mathrm{cDPO}}.
Robust DPO (rDPO).
Preference data may contain noise or ambiguity. We consider a robust variant that optimizes against uncertainty in preference labels.
Let q∈[0,1]q\in[0,1] denote an (unknown) probability that the pair (y+,y−)(y^{+},y^{-}) is correctly labeled for input ss.
We model robustness by optimizing the worst-case (or uncertainty-aware) expected loss over an admissible set 𝒬\mathcal{Q}:
ℒrDPO​(θ)=𝔼(s,y+,y−)∼𝒟​[supq∈𝒬(−q​log⁡σ⁡(β​Δθ)−(1−q)​log⁡(1−σ⁡(β​Δθ)))].\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rDPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,y^{+},y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sup_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\big(-q\log\sigma(\beta\,\Delta_{\theta})-(1-q)\log(1-\sigma(\beta\,\Delta_{\theta}))\big)\Big].
Here 𝒬\mathcal{Q} specifies the assumed noise level (e.g., q∈[1−ρ,1]q\in[1-\rho,1] for some ρ∈[0,1/2)\rho\in[0,1/2)).
We denote the resulting trained policy as πrDPO\pi_{\mathrm{rDPO}}.
Evaluation protocol (policy + inference-time selection).
For any trained policy π\pi (including πcDPO\pi_{\mathrm{cDPO}} and πrDPO\pi_{\mathrm{rDPO}}), at inference time we sample a candidate set
𝒴(s)={y1,…,yK}∼π(⋅|s)\mathcal{Y}(s)=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{K}\}\sim\pi(\cdot|s) and then apply the same selection rules defined in Appendix F,
e.g., Mean (Best-of-KK) or DARC-ϵ\epsilon.
This isolates the effect of training-time robustness (changing π\pi) from inference-time robustness (changing the selection rule).
Appendix G Related Work
G.1 Preference optimization for alignment
Preference-based alignment is the prevailing paradigm for steering LLMs, ranging from RLHF-style reward modeling with policy optimization to direct preference objectives that learn from comparisons without explicit RL loops (Ouyang et al., 2022; Christiano et al., 2017; Rafailov et al., 2023).
Recent work largely focuses on making preference optimization simpler and more stable, e.g., via single-stage or reference-light formulations that resemble standard fine-tuning (Hong et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024).
A second theme highlights the role of data coverage: when preference data poorly covers the model’s behavior distribution, offline updates can degrade, motivating unified online–offline perspectives and value-aware objectives (Song et al., 2024; Cen et al., 2024).
Meanwhile, newer datasets enrich supervision beyond pairwise comparisons with multi-attribute ratings and complementary preference signals, enabling finer-grained diagnosis and reward learning (Wang et al., 2024c; Wang et al., 2024b).
Despite these advances, most methods still collapse alignment into optimizing a single scalar proxy of utility (often motivated by Bradley–Terry style models (Bradley and Terry, 1952)), which can mask structured disagreement and sensitivity to annotator mix.
G.2 Heterogeneity, robustness, and inference-time alignment
A growing body of work shows that preferences are intrinsically heterogeneous: annotators disagree systematically, and average-score alignment can hide persistent failures for sub-populations (Zhang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Casper et al., 2023).
This is compounded by proxy over-optimization, where scaling up optimization against an imperfect reward/preference proxy can degrade the underlying target for both RLHF and direct alignment (Gao et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2024).
Robustness-oriented approaches therefore move beyond mean objectives, including distributionally robust and group-robust formulations that protect against noise and minority-group degradation (Wu et al., 2024; Ramesh et al., 2024; Chakraborty et al., 2024).
Another complementary direction makes reward signals multi-dimensional via multi-objective reward modeling or multi-head aggregation, improving interpretability and offering more conservative combinations, but typically requiring richer supervision and an explicit (often fixed) scalarization for deployment (Wang et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024).
Finally, inference-time alignment methods avoid retraining by allocating extra computation at decoding to select better candidates, though recent analyses caution that aggressive best-of-NN selection can amplify proxy error under miscalibration or under-coverage (Sun et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025a; Ichihara et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025b).
Standardized reward-model benchmarks further support systematic study of these issues across settings, including retrieval-augmented generation (Lambert et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024b).
Distributionally robust optimization and variance regularization.
Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) studies objectives of the form min⁡supℚ∈𝒰⁡(ℙ^)θ⁡𝔼ℚ​[ℓ⁡(θ,Z)]\min_{\theta}\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{U}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(\theta;Z)],
where the true distribution is assumed to lie in an ambiguity set around the empirical distribution (Wiesemann et al., 2014; Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019).
Local ff-divergence neighborhoods yield tractable robust objectives and have been widely used in learning and optimization (Namkoong and Duchi, 2016).
In particular, χ2\chi^{2}-DRO is closely connected to variance-based regularization and generalized empirical likelihood perspectives (Duchi and Namkoong, 2019; Duchi et al., 2021).
Our work brings this DRO view to inference-time decoding under heterogeneous human preferences:
the same mean–dispersion score used by DARC arises as the closed-form robust value of a χ2\chi^{2}-ambiguity set.
DRO is also motivated by heterogeneous subpopulations and uniform-performance desiderata (Duchi and Namkoong, 2021),
and efficient large-scale methods exist for common DRO formulations (Levy et al., 2020).

H.4 Selecting risk controls and practical calibration
Overview.
We select (β,τ,λ,ϵ)(\beta,\tau,\lambda,\epsilon) on a held-out development set, fixing the candidate set size KK and (when used) the scorer family and normalization protocol (Appendix E).
Tuning β\beta.
We tune β\beta via a grid search on the development set to trade off mean quality and robustness metrics (disagreement / tail risk). Unless otherwise specified, we use a single β\beta shared across prompts.
Constraint threshold τ\tau via quantiles.

Prompt.
Given two matrices AA and BB which you can multiply together, does (A​B)𝖳=A𝖳​B𝖳(AB)^{\mathsf{T}}=A^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}} or B𝖳​A𝖳B^{\mathsf{T}}A^{\mathsf{T}}?
Model responses (verbatim).
Base (mean Best-of-KK):
The statement (A B)T = AT BT is incorrect. The correct statement is (A B)T = BT AT.
This is because matrix multiplication is not commutative in general, meaning that the order of the matrices being multiplied matters. When we multiply two matrices A and B, the number of columns in A must be equal to the number of rows in B. This is known as the compatibility condition for matrix multiplication.
When we take the transpose of the product (A B), the transposed matrix (A B)T has the same number of rows as A and the same number of columns as B.
Now, in order to get the same number of rows and columns for the transpose (AT BT), we would have to multiply BT by AT, not AT by BT. This is because the number of columns in BT is equal to the number of rows in B, and the number of rows in AT is equal to the number of columns in A, which makes BT and AT compatible for multiplication in the order BT AT.
A simple example can illustrate this. Let A = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and B = [[5, 6], [7, 8]]. Then AT = [[1, 3], [2, 4]] and BT = [[5, 7], [6, 8]]. The product A B is [[19, 22], [43, 50]].
Then the transpose (A B)T is [[19, 43], [22, 50]].
Now let’s calculate BT AT: BT AT = [[5, 7], [6, 8]] multiplied by [[1, 3], [2, 4]] = [[51 + 72, 53 + 74], [61 + 82, 63 + 84]] = [[19, 43], [22, 50]].
As you can see, (A B)T matches BT AT.
(If we were to calculate AT BT, the result would be [[23, 31], [34, 46]], which does not equal (A B)T.)
DARC-ϵ\epsilon:
The correct statement is (AB)T = BT AT.
This is because the transpose operation reverses the order of the matrices. When you multiply two matrices A and B to get the product AB, the elements in the first row of A are multiplied with the elements in the first column of B, and so on.
Taking the transpose of both AB, we get (AB)T. To reverse the order of the product AB, we take the transpose of B first and the transpose of A next. This is why (AB)T = BT AT.
Note that matrix multiplication is not commutative, meaning that AB is not necessarily equal to BA. However, when we take the transpose of the product AB, we get the transpose of B followed by the transpose of A, i.e., BT AT.

H.11 Proxy mismatch cases
Why we report mismatch cases.
We provide representative mismatch cases where the proxy disagreement
σ^proxy\hat{\sigma}_{\text{proxy}} (reward-model score sensitivity under style-preserving perturbations)
does not align with human disagreement σhuman\sigma_{\text{human}} (std. over multiple independent judge ratings).
These examples are included to scope what the proxy is (and is not) designed to capture:
σ^proxy\hat{\sigma}_{\text{proxy}} is a scalable risk-screening signal for preference heterogeneity
during decoding, not a calibrated estimator of absolute human controversy nor a general error detector
for truncation, factuality, or code correctness.
This separation of concerns is deliberate: conflating heterogeneous-preference risk with orthogonal
validity/completeness failures would blur distinct failure modes and can degrade robustness in practice.
Representative mismatch taxonomy.
We summarize mismatch cases in Table 13.
False positives (FP) correspond to style/format sensitivity in the reward model:
superficial surface changes (e.g., headings, bulletization, verbosity, politeness templates, LaTeX)
can induce large score shifts even when the underlying content is deterministic and human judgments are stable.
False negatives (FN) arise primarily from failure modes that are orthogonal to preference heterogeneity,
such as response truncation/incompleteness, underspecified prompts where “best” behavior is ambiguous,
or code/correctness issues that require verification beyond style-preserving perturbations.
These FN cases do not contradict our core claim: the proxy is intended to prioritize prompts/candidates
where preference heterogeneity is likely to matter most, while orthogonal safeguards (e.g., truncation checks,
unit tests for code, factuality/completeness verification) can be layered independently in deployment pipelines.

Rewrite prompt.
We instruct 𝒢\mathcal{G} to produce meaning-preserving paraphrases with only surface-form changes:
Rewrite Instruction. Given the prompt and a candidate response, rewrite the response while preserving its meaning.
Only change wording, phrasing, or formatting. Do not add, remove, or alter facts; do not change any numbers,
dates, named entities, URLs, or citations. Keep the tone and overall style similar. Keep the length within ±10%\pm 10\%.
Output only the rewritten response (no preamble).
Sampling and filtering.

Appendix I Human Evaluation Protocol and Annotator Quality Controls
Overview.
We evaluate responses with two complementary human-evaluation formats:
(i) scalar satisfaction scoring on a 0–10 scale, and
(ii) pairwise preference judgments between two responses to the same prompt.
Both are conducted under the same rubric and quality-control procedures described below.
Scalar satisfaction scoring (0–10).
Annotators assign a scalar satisfaction score in {0,1,…,10}\{0,1,\ldots,10\} for each response, where higher is better.
Scores reflect overall response quality (e.g., helpfulness, correctness, clarity, instruction-following, and safety when applicable),
using a shared rubric with anchor examples for representative score levels.
Pairwise preference.
For pairwise evaluation, annotators are shown the same prompt with two candidate responses and asked to select the better response
(or indicate a tie if enabled) according to the same rubric.
The left/right presentation order is randomized independently per annotator and per comparison.
Annotators and replication.
Each item is evaluated by n=5n=5 annotators.
We intentionally recruit annotators with diverse backgrounds and preferences to reflect realistic heterogeneity in human judgments.
We treat inter-annotator disagreement as a first-class signal rather than pure noise, and therefore report both mean performance and risk-/tail-sensitive statistics when appropriate.
Rubric, training, and calibration.
Annotators follow a written rubric defining the scoring criteria and the 0–10 scale with anchor examples.
Before the main evaluation, annotators complete a short training module and a calibration round:
they score a shared set of examples and receive feedback to align interpretations of the rubric.
The rubric and anchors remain visible in the annotation interface throughout the study.
Blinding and randomization.
To reduce expectation bias and method-identification bias, evaluation is double-blind:
annotators are not told which system produced any response, and all system identifiers are removed.
For scalar scoring, the order of candidate responses (when multiple systems are shown for the same prompt) is randomized.
For pairwise preference, left/right ordering is randomized.
Item order is shuffled independently per annotator to mitigate ordering and fatigue effects.
Preventing leakage and confounds.
Annotators only see the user prompt and the candidate response(s).
They do not see model names, decoding methods, hyperparameters, or any metadata that could reveal method identity.
We enforce consistent formatting across systems (e.g., identical rendering of markdown and whitespace) to avoid visual cues.
Quality control: attention checks and gold items.
We insert two types of quality-control (QC) items throughout the annotation stream.
Attention checks verify careful reading.
These include (i) instruction-following checks that explicitly request a specified score (or a specified pairwise choice) for that item,
and/or (ii) obvious sanity checks where one response is clearly irrelevant or nonsensical and should be rated much lower (or lose in pairwise comparison).
Gold items verify rubric adherence and score calibration.
We pre-label a small set of items with reference scores (for scalar) and/or reference winners (for pairwise) by the authors following the same rubric.
Annotators are expected to match the reference within a tolerance (e.g., ±1\pm 1 on the 0–10 scale, or selecting the reference winner in pairwise).
QC items are interleaved uniformly at random (e.g., ≈10%\approx 10\% of all items, split between attention checks and gold items).
Annotators who repeatedly fail attention checks (e.g., >2>2 failures) or deviate substantially on gold items
(e.g., >20%>20\% of gold items outside the tolerance) are excluded; their affected annotations are discarded and re-assigned.
We additionally filter anomalous behavior such as extremely short completion times or near-constant scoring patterns.
Aggregation and reporting.
For scalar scoring, we aggregate the n=5n=5 ratings by reporting the empirical mean and dispersion (e.g., standard deviation),
and compute risk-/tail-sensitive metrics (e.g., CVaR) when relevant.
For pairwise preference, we report win-rate (and tie-rate if applicable) aggregated across annotators, along with confidence intervals via bootstrap when reported.
Independence assumption (discussion).
Our theoretical analysis models the nn ratings for a fixed (s,y)(s,y) as i.i.d. draws from a stationary distribution.
In practice, ratings may exhibit mild dependencies due to annotator-specific biases or session effects.
We mitigate these effects via randomization, blinding, workload distribution across annotators, and multiple annotators per item.
Importantly, the proposed decoding rules remain applicable without strict i.i.d. assumptions; the assumption is used to obtain clean concentration-style guarantees,
and can be relaxed to weakly dependent sampling with adjusted constants.
