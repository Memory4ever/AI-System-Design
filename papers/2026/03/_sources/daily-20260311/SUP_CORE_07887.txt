Abstract
Inference-time methods that aggregate and prune multiple samples have emerged as a powerful paradigm for steering large language models, yet we lack any principled understanding of their accuracy–cost tradeoffs.
In this paper, we introduce a route to rigorously study such approaches using the lens of particle filtering algorithms such as Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC).
Given a base language model and a process reward model estimating expected terminal rewards, we ask: how accurately can we sample from a target distribution given some number of process reward evaluations?
Theoretically, we identify (1) simple criteria enabling non-asymptotic guarantees for SMC; (2) algorithmic improvements to SMC; and (3) a fundamental limit faced by all particle filtering methods. Empirically, we demonstrate that our theoretical criteria effectively govern the sampling error of SMC, though not necessarily its final accuracy, suggesting that theoretical perspectives beyond sampling may be necessary.
1 Introduction
Recent work on large language models (LLMs) has demonstrated the value of inference-time interventions that steer models towards higher-quality outputs. For instance, existing work has experimented with a combination of using parallel generations from LLMs (Madaan et al., 2025; Brown et al., 2024), techniques to aggregate such generations (Wang et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), and ways to prune or compress them to fit in the context window for subsequent model calls (Yang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Even with no additional training, such interventions can significantly improve performance on challenging tasks such as mathematical reasoning and question answering. However, these methods are largely ad hoc, since we lack a unifying theoretical framework that can explain the benefits of different inference-time interventions and guide algorithm design towards the most effective ones.
Figure 1: Performance of SMC (with NN particles) vs. Best-of-NN on Math500 problems; here we take N=32N=32. Each point is a different problem; thus, SMC with NN particles improves performance over Best-of-NN on most problems. See Section 4.5 for details.
In this paper, we ask:
Can we analyze inference-time interventions such as aggregation and pruning of parallel generations in a principled manner?
To do so, we study the paradigm of guided LLM generation with a process reward model (Wang et al., 2025a; Puri et al., 2025b), where the process reward model numerically scores partial generations. In this paradigm, aggregation and pruning are naturally implemented by particle filtering algorithms, including the popular Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) method, which use the process reward model to adaptively prune and replicate multiple particles (namely, partial generations from the base model), as tokens are generated. These strategies can have robust empirical benefits over simpler interventions such as Best-of-NN; see e.g. Fig. 1 or Puri et al. (2025b).
While particle filtering methods have been extensively applied to tasks ranging from posterior inference (Pitt and Shephard, 1997) and robot localization (Montemerlo et al., 2002) to steering LLMs (Lipkin et al., 2025; Lew et al., 2023; Loula et al., 2025; Grand et al., 2025; Puri et al., 2025a; Zhao et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024) and diffusion models (Skreta et al., 2025; Singhal et al., 2025), few non-asymptotic guarantees have been established for such methods. In this paper, we address this challenge, providing a user-friendly analysis of SMC and identifying benefits and limitations of more sophisticated particle filtering methods.
Towards a theory of inference-time interventions
Increasingly, inference-time interventions utilize models not just for generation and scoring, but also more complex operations such as splicing (Fu et al., 2025) or summarizing (Yu et al., 2025) generations. While these operations are not directly captured by our setting, we believe that our work is a necessary first step towards developing a framework for understanding (and improving) such interventions.
1.1 Setting: Guided Generation with Imperfect Process Rewards
For a fixed prompt, a language model defines a distribution πref∈Δ⁡(𝒜H)\pi_{\texttt{ref}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{H}) over sequences of HH actions in some set 𝒜\mathcal{A}.11
1
For simplicity, we assume that the language model produces generations of fixed length.
One can think of 𝒜\mathcal{A} as the space of tokens, or more generally as blocks of tokens. Given a sequence a1:ha_{1:h}, we can efficiently sample from πref(ah+1=⋅∣a1:h)\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{h+1}=\cdot\mid{}a_{1:h}), which enables autoregressive sampling from πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}}.
We model the task of steering the language model towards some desired reward as a sampling problem, in line with recent work on LLM inference and post-training (Rohatgi et al., 2025; Geuter et al., 2025; Foster et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2024; Zhu and Lu, 2026). Given query access to a reward function r⋆:𝒜H→ℝ≥0r^{\star}:\mathcal{A}^{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}, we aim to sample from the tilted distribution πH⋆(a1:H)∝πref(a1:H)r⋆(a1:H)\pi^{\star}_{H}(a_{1:H})\propto\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{1:H})r^{\star}(a_{1:H}). For instance, in the context of mathematical reasoning, r⋆(a1:H)∈{0,1}r^{\star}(a_{1:H})\in\{0,1\} measures whether a1:Ha_{1:H} correctly solves the prompt.
Imperfect process rewards
Following Rohatgi et al. (2025), we theoretically formalize a process reward model (PRM) as an approximate value function. Given a sequence a1:ha_{1:h} of any length, the PRM provides an estimate V^(a1:h)∈ℝ≥0\widehat{V}(a_{1:h})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} of the
expected reward of a1:ha_{1:h} under πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}}, i.e., V⋆(a1:h):=𝔼a′1:H∼πref[r⋆(a1:H′)∣a1:h′=a1:h]V^{\star}(a_{1:h}):=\mathbb{E}_{a^{\prime}_{1:H}\sim\pi_{\texttt{ref}}}[r^{\star}(a^{\prime}_{1:H})\mid a^{\prime}_{1:h}=a_{1:h}].
In practice, there has been extensive effort to train transformers specifically to be PRMs (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025) for certain problems, such as math reasoning. If such a fine-tuned PRM adapted to a desired reward function r⋆r^{\star} is not available, there are several other popular choices for V^\widehat{V}—e.g. LLM-as-a-judge (Stephan et al., 2024) or LLM log-probabilities (Karan and Du, 2025; Lew et al., 2023); see also Section 4.
The key algorithmic challenge in this setting is that the PRM is imperfect. To address this challenge, numerous parallel and sequential approaches have been proposed, including SMC (Puri et al., 2025b), block rejection sampling (Mudgal et al., 2024), and backtracking (Botta et al., 2025). Thus far, provable guarantees are only known for backtracking (Rohatgi et al., 2025) — an inherently sequential approach. We ask: given access to πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} and V^\widehat{V} as above, what structural properties enable approximate sampling from πH⋆\pi^{\star}_{H} via efficient parallel methods such as particle filtering?
1.2 Theoretical Contributions: A Principled Analysis of Particle Filtering Methods for LLM Inference
Contribution I: Simple criteria for success of SMC
Our first result identifies two  key properties that enable the success of SMC. For h∈[H]h\in[H], let πh⋆,π^h∈Δ⁡(𝒜h)\pi^{\star}_{h},\widehat{\pi}_{h}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{h}) denote the true and approximate intermediate target distributions, defined by πh⋆(a1:h)∝πref(a1:h)V⋆(a1:h)\pi^{\star}_{h}(a_{1:h})\propto\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{1:h})V^{\star}(a_{1:h}) and π^h(a1:h)∝πref(a1:h)V^(a1:h)\widehat{\pi}_{h}(a_{1:h})\propto\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{1:h})\widehat{V}(a_{1:h}). Then we show the following:

[Corollary of Section 3.1]
Suppose that the following hold.
(1) Bounded action-level coverage: for all hh, a1:h+1a_{1:h+1}, π⋆(ah+1∣a1:h)/πref(ah+1∣a1:h)≤C𝖺𝖼𝗍\pi^{\star}(a_{h+1}\mid a_{1:h})/\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{h+1}\mid a_{1:h})\leq C_{\sf act}.

This bound strengthens the guarantees for the backtracking-based algorithm VGB of Rohatgi et al. (2025). Moreover, the parallel runtime of SMC is O⁡(H)O(H) whereas VGB uses time Ω⁡(H2)\Omega(H^{2}) (and is inherently sequential). See Appendix A for additional discussion.
{remark}
[Connection to literature on refinements of SMC]
Interestingly, the two  key properties listed above map quite cleanly onto various efforts in prior work focused on refining SMC algorithms. The latter of the two conditions (that V^\widehat{V} approximates V⋆V^{\star}) is related to the line of work on twisting targets for SMC. In particular, the distributions π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} introduced above are often referred to as (intermediate) target distributions, and prior work has explored how to choose (or learn) V^\widehat{V} so as to bring π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} as close as possible to πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h} for all h∈[H]h\in[H] (Guarniero et al., 2017; Heng et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2024; Lawson et al., 2022), which is a natural goal in light of Section 1.2.
Moreover, the first condition (on action-level coverage) is related to the extensive literature on changing the proposal distribution πref,h\pi_{\texttt{ref},h} to better approximate the locally optimal proposal distribution (Naesseth et al., 2019; Doucet et al., 2000), which is defined as the distribution π^h+1(ah+1=⋅∣a1:h)\widehat{\pi}_{h+1}(a_{h+1}=\cdot\mid a_{1:h}). In the event that π^h+1≈πh+1⋆\widehat{\pi}_{h+1}\approx\pi^{\star}_{h+1}, then the locally optimal proposal distribution has action-level coverage close to 11, the smallest possible value. Thus, as long as V^\widehat{V} is sufficiently accurate, modifying πref,h\pi_{\texttt{ref},h} so as to approximate the locally optimal proposal is a way to decrease action-level coverage.22
2
Indeed, using π^h+1\widehat{\pi}_{h+1} as the proposal and appropriately modifying the weights in SMC yields a variant with improved convergence properties (see Section 3.3 and Section D.3). Summarizing, our result helps to unify the existing literature on variants of SMC by showing that they are effectively minimizing the key quantities Section 1.2.
Contribution II: Beyond Sequential Monte Carlo
Can particle filtering algorithms beyond SMC achieve stronger guarantees? First, we show that if π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} is close to πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h} in a stronger L∞L_{\infty} sense, then it is possible to achieve exponential decay in sampling error (rather than polynomial decay, as in Section 1.2) by wrapping SMC in an outer rejection sampling loop (Section 3.2).
Second, we observe that even when the PRM is perfect, and hence Cχ2=0C_{\chi^{2}}=0, SMC requires Ω⁡(H)\Omega(\sqrt{H}) particles to achieve non-trivial sampling accuracy (Section C.6), even though there is a simple one-particle algorithm for this special case (Rohatgi et al., 2025). We show that a modified algorithm— Sequential Monte Carlo with Rejection Sampling (SMC-RS, Algorithm 2)—avoids this pathology, recovering Section 1.2 as well as the one-particle guarantee (Section 3.3).
Contribution III: Limits of particle filtering
A key limitation of SMC (and our modifications) is that even when πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} has bounded action-level coverage (i.e. C𝖺𝖼𝗍=O⁡(1)C_{\sf act}=O(1)) and the approximate value function V^\widehat{V} is within a constant factor of the truth (and hence Cχ2=O⁡(1)C_{\chi^{2}}=O(1)), our algorithm requires maintaining poly⁡(H)\mathrm{poly}(H) particles and hence requires superlinear work. In Section 3.4 we show that fully avoiding this lower bound would require lookahead: any myopic method needs at least Ω⁡(log⁡H/log⁡log​H)\Omega(\log H/\log\log H) particles (Section 3.4). Closing this gap is open.
Addendum: backtracking as a form of particle filtering
In Appendix E, we show an interesting connection between particle filtering algorithms and the backtracking-based approach VGB (Rohatgi et al., 2025) — previously, the only algorithm for inference-time steering of LLMs that was known to be robust to PRM errors. VGB is a (one-particle) Markov chain method that generates tokens but sometimes also deletes the most recent token. We show that the execution of VGB can be coupled with the execution of a certain particle filtering method, potentially providing a new perspective on the benefits of both methods.
1.3 Empirical Contributions: Does the Theory Predict the Performance of SMC in LLMs?
We investigate empirically whether the quantities identified by Section 1.2 indeed predict the performance of SMC on LLM sampling problems. First, we consider a simple “prompt switching” task in which the base model πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} corresponds to sampling from an LLM given some prompt pref\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}}, and π⋆\pi^{\star} corresponds to sampling from that LLM given another prompt p⋆\texttt{p}^{\star} (see Section 4 for details). By varying pref,p⋆\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}},\texttt{p}^{\star}, and the PRM V^\widehat{V}, we can control for each quantity in Section 1.2. First, in Fig. 2(a), we consider a dataset where V^=V⋆\widehat{V}=V^{\star} for all prompts in the dataset (so Cχ2=0C_{\chi^{2}}=0) but the action level coverage varies, and observe a strong correlation between the action-level coverage (measured via a KL-divergence proxy) and the sampling error of SMC. Second, in Fig. 2(b), we consider a dataset where the action-level coverage is identical across all prompts but the degree of accuracy of V^\widehat{V} with respect to V⋆V^{\star} varies, and again we observe a strong correlation between D𝖪𝖫(πh⋆∥π^h)D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\,\|\,\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right) and the sampling error of SMC. (We report the KL divergence instead of the χ2\chi^{2} divergence since the latter requires prohibitively large sample complexity to estimate.)

Evaluating SMC on math reasoning tasks
Finally, we consider the performance of SMC on challenging math reasoning benchmarks including AIME and Math500. In Fig. 1, we plot, for each problem in Math500, the accuracy of SMC with NN particles against that of Best-of-NN (with N=32N=32); each of these quantities was estimated by running the requisite algorithm 100 times per problem. As most points lie below the line y=xy=x, we see that SMC improves performance over Best-of-NN in a uniform sense over all problems, which extends prior observations in Puri et al. (2025b); Chatziveroglou (2025) which observed that SMC improves upon Best-of-NN in an on-average sense over such benchmarks. Despite this strong evidence for superiority of SMC, as we discuss further in Section 4.5, the quality of the PRM V^\widehat{V} (as measured by an estimate of the chi-square divergence between π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} and πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h}) does not seem to positively correlate with performance, as often larger divergence leads to higher accuracy. We leave as an intriguing open question the development of a more refined framework that captures performance on such benchmarks.

3.1.1 Handling Tail Behavior
While the χ2\chi^{2}-divergences in Section 3.1 provide an easy-to-interpret upper bound, Dχ2(πh⋆∥π^h)D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\;\|\;{}\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right) is sensitive to the tail behavior of π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} with respect to πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h} (equivalently, the tail behavior of V^\widehat{V} relative to V⋆V^{\star}); in the case of heavy-tailed errors, it can blow up and render Section 3.1 vacuous.

3.4 Limits of Particle Filtering Methods
A key downside of SMC (and the discussed variants) is that, when the PRM is imperfect, avoiding error amplification requires the number of particles NN to grow at least linearly in the horizon HH. In this section, we show that some horizon dependence is necessary for all myopic particle filtering methods. Informally, a myopic particle filtering method is any algorithm that maintains a set of particles 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h} at each step hh, and does not use the PRM data from later steps k>hk>h to determine the set 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h}. SMC and SMC-RS are two examples of myopic methods; see Appendix F for a formal definition.
We prove that even with mild (constant-factor) imperfections in the PRM, any myopic particle filtering algorithm requires nearly Ω⁡(log⁡H)\Omega(\log H) particles to even obtain non-trivial coverage of πH⋆\pi^{\star}_{H}:
{theorem}
Let N⁡(H):=log⁡(H)/(4​log⁡log⁡(H))N(H):=\log(H)/(4\log\log(H)). There is no myopic particle filtering algorithm Alg with the following guarantee for all sufficiently large HH: Alg uses at most N⁡(H)N(H) particles, and under Sections 3.1 and 3.1 with C𝖺𝖼𝗍=2C_{\sf act}=2 and 𝖢∞=e3\mathsf{C}_{\infty}=e^{3}, the output distribution ν\nu of Alg satisfies ν(ℰ)≥H−1/5\nu(\mathcal{E})\geq H^{-1/5}
for all events ℰ⊂𝒳H\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{X}_{H} with πH⋆​(ℰ)≥1/2\pi^{\star}_{H}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1/2.
As an immediate corollary, no such algorithm can achieve D𝖳𝖵​(ν,πH⋆)≤1/3D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\nu,\pi^{\star}_{H}\right)\leq 1/3. In contrast, with a perfect PRM (i.e. 𝖢∞=1\mathsf{C}_{\infty}=1), a single particle achieves zero error (Rohatgi et al., 2025). Moreover, Section 3.4 is particularly striking since, under Section 3.1, every intermediate distribution π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} has constant coverage of πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h} (i.e. π^h​(x)/πh⋆​(x)≥𝖢∞−2\widehat{\pi}_{h}(x)/\pi^{\star}_{h}(x)\geq\mathsf{C}_{\infty}^{-2} for all x∈𝒳hx\in\mathcal{X}_{h}). Section 3.4 shows that it is impossible for myopic methods to inductively “maintain” this coverage without a super-constant number of particles — and raises the question of whether some form of lookahead can improve computational efficiency.
4 Experiments in Language Models
Next, we investigate to what extent the quantities from Section 3.1 predict the performance of SMC on sampling problems involving LLMs.
4.1 Controlled Setting: Prompt-Switching Task
A key challenge in evaluating SMC and understanding its performance as a function of the aforementioned quantities is the difficulty of measuring them, as well as measuring the performance of SMC itself. Most notably, we need to measure quantities involving the target distribution πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h}, which is typically not readily available. While prior work (e.g., Zhao et al. (2024)) has studied how to evaluate the performance of SMC using the algorithm’s estimate of the partition function Z:=𝔼πref​[V⋆​(xH)]Z:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\texttt{ref}}}[V^{\star}(x_{H})], such techniques are only guaranteed to provide tight bounds if SMC is accurate. Moreover, these methods do not directly address the challenge of estimating other quantities such as action-level coverage.
Thus, in this section, we consider an alternative approach, in a setting which will conveniently satisfy that πref,π⋆\pi_{\texttt{ref}},\pi^{\star} are both efficiently sampleable.
In particular, we consider the prompt-switching task, where πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} and π⋆\pi^{\star} correspond to the output distribution of a language model under different prompts. Formally, we fix a language model 𝖬\mathsf{M}, and consider the setting of Section 1 where 𝒜\mathcal{A} is the token space of 𝖬\mathsf{M} and 𝒳h=𝒜h\mathcal{X}_{h}=\mathcal{A}^{h}. Each instance of the prompt-switching task is determined by 3 prompts, (pref,p⋆,p)(\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}},\texttt{p}^{\star},\texttt{p}), as follows.
Given choices of these prompts, we let πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} be the output distribution of 𝖬\mathsf{M} for the prompt pref\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}}, and we denote it as πref(⋅)=𝖬(⋅∣pref)\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(\cdot)=\mathsf{M}(\cdot\mid\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}}). We then choose the terminal reward function to be r⋆(a1:H):=𝖬(a1:H∣p⋆)𝖬(a1:H∣pref)r^{\star}(a_{1:H}):=\frac{\mathsf{M}(a_{1:H}\mid\texttt{p}^{\star})}{\mathsf{M}(a_{1:H}\mid\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}})}, which gives that, for any intermediate step hh, V⋆(a1:h)=𝖬(a1:h∣p⋆)/𝖬(a1:h∣pref)V^{\star}(a_{1:h})={\mathsf{M}(a_{1:h}\mid\texttt{p}^{\star})/\mathsf{M}(a_{1:h}\mid\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}})}. It follows that πh⋆(a1:h)=𝖬(a1:h∣p⋆)\pi^{\star}_{h}(a_{1:h})=\mathsf{M}(a_{1:h}\mid\texttt{p}^{\star}). Since it is tractable to sample from 𝖬\mathsf{M} and compute sequence-level probabilities, sampling from πref,π⋆\pi_{\texttt{ref}},\pi^{\star} as well as computing V⋆V^{\star} is tractable. Finally, the prompt p determines V^\widehat{V}, in a way that will be discussed below.
Evaluation
To evaluate SMC on the prompt-switching task, we consider the following metric, inspired by more complex “LLM-as-judge” techniques and also the use of log-probabilities to characterize the output distributions of LLMs (Golowich et al., 2025). Given two distributions ν,ν′\nu,\nu^{\prime} over sequences of length HH, and a set of prompts 𝒫\mathcal{P}, we define the 𝒫\mathcal{P}-logprob discrepancy LPD𝒫​(ν,ν′)\mathrm{LPD}_{\mathcal{P}}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}) between ν\nu and ν′\nu^{\prime} as the difference between the mean token-level log-probabilities assigned to sequences sampled from ν\nu and ν′\nu^{\prime} under the prompts in 𝒫\mathcal{P} (summed over all positions hh). Formally, for ν,ν′∈Δ⁡(𝒜H)\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{H}), we have:
LPD𝒫​(ν,ν′):=\displaystyle\mathrm{LPD}_{\mathcal{P}}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}):=
∑p∈𝒫∑h=1H|𝔼a1:H∼ν[log𝖬(ah∣p,a1:h−1)]−𝔼a1:H′∼ν′[log𝖬(ah′∣p,a1:h−1′)]|.\displaystyle\sum_{\texttt{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left|\mathbb{E}_{a_{1:H}\sim\nu}\left[\log\mathsf{M}(a_{h}\mid\texttt{p},a_{1:h-1})\right]\right.\left.-\mathbb{E}_{a_{1:H}^{\prime}\sim\nu^{\prime}}\left[\log\mathsf{M}(a_{h}^{\prime}\mid\texttt{p},a_{1:h-1}^{\prime})\right]\right|.

It can be thought of as a very simple (albeit more stable) “LLM-as-judge” metric, where instead of generating from 𝖬\mathsf{M} to evaluate ν,ν′\nu,\nu^{\prime}, we simply use the log-probabilities assigned by the model 𝖬\mathsf{M}. In our evaluations, we choose 𝒫={pref,p⋆}\mathcal{P}=\{\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}},\texttt{p}^{\star}\}, which is natural in light of the fact that the two most salient prompts in the prompt switching task are pref\texttt{p}_{\texttt{ref}} and p⋆\texttt{p}^{\star}.

4.5 SMC on Math Problem-solving Tasks
Finally, we study the use of SMC to improve the performance of LLMs on math problem-solving tasks, including AIME and MATH500. In this setting, the base distribution πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} corresponds to the output distribution of a language model prompted to solve a math problem, and the terminal reward function r⋆(a1:H)∈{0,1}r^{\star}(a_{1:H})\in\{0,1\} is the indicator that the answer a1:Ha_{1:H} is correct. Thus, π⋆\pi^{\star} is simply the base model distribution conditioned on producing a correct answer, and V⋆(a1:h)V^{\star}(a_{1:h}) is the probability that πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} produces a correct answer conditioned on the prefix a1:ha_{1:h}.
There are several open-source process reward models fine-tuned for math problems, which purport to yield good estimates of V⋆V^{\star}. In our experiments, we take the PRM V^\widehat{V} to be Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B, and the base model πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} to be Qwen2.5-1B-Instruct. In the application of SMC to math reasoning tasks, since the goal is simply to find some correct answer, it is typical to combine SMC with Best-of-NN sampling in the following manner: at the end of SMC, we select the single particle with the highest value V^(a1:H)\widehat{V}(a_{1:H}). Note that this is distinct from the standard Best-of-NN sampling procedure, in which NN samples are drawn independently from πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} and the one with highest V^\widehat{V}-value is selected.
SMC improves performance on math
Many papers (Puri et al., 2025b; Chatziveroglou, 2025) have reported that SMC typically improves the performance over Best-of-NN (as well as other baselines). However, such results are typically reported on-average over all problems in the dataset, which fails to account for the hetereogeneity between individual problems. For instance, it was not clear if SMC improves performance over Best-of-NN on all problems, or if SMC can actually hurt performance (compared to Best-of-NN) on a non-negligible fraction of the dataset.77
7
Indeed, much prior work has highlighted that SMC can suffer from pathologies such as mode collapse, which might decrease the diversity of responses from which Best-of-NN can choose. Certainly such a distinction is salient for any theoretical explanation for SMC’s performance.
In Fig. 1, we estimate the performance of SMC and Best-of-NN for each problem in Math500 (using 100 trials per problem), and plot the accuracy of SMC against that of Best-of-NN. While there is variability in the relative performance of the two algorithms, we see that for the vast majority of problems, SMC’s accuracy is at least as large as that of Best-of-NN. In Fig. 5, we observe a similar conclusion for AIME24 and AIME25.

SMC and PRM accuracy
Having established that SMC leads to a consistent increase in accuracy across most Math500 and AIME problems, we next turn to the question of what factors influence its performance on a given problem. In light of the fact that significant effort has been spent designing better PRMs V^\widehat{V} (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025), a natural follow-up question from our theory is whether the divergence between πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h} and π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} (as measured by, e.g., the χ2\chi^{2}-divergence Dχ2(πh⋆∥π^h)D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\;\|\;{}\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right)) predicts the performance of SMC.
To address this question, we consider a range of PRMs V^(λ)\widehat{V}^{(\lambda)} by varying the inverse temperature parameter λ\lambda used by the PRM to produce its final probabilities. In Fig. 4, we select 10 Math500 problems, and for each value of λ\lambda, we plot an empirical estimate of Dχ2(πh⋆∥π^h)D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\;\|\;{}\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right) against the accuracy of SMC given V^(λ)\widehat{V}^{(\lambda)}. Interestingly, we observe that larger χ2\chi^{2}-divergence often leads to higher accuracy, which is in contrast to our theoretical findings which predict the opposite. We suspect that larger values of λ\lambda (lower temperature) lead to better performance since they more effectively weed out incorrect partial solutions; however, they will also potentially lead π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h} to miss some modes of πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h}, which causes the χ2\chi^{2}-divergence to blow up. Thus, in order to accurately capture the performance of SMC on such problems, we need a modification to our framework that aims to not approximate πH⋆\pi^{\star}_{H} in a distributional sense but rather to simply cover some portion of its mass.
5 Discussion
In this paper, we have established theoretical guarantees for particle filtering algorithms under varioues assumptions relating the reference distribution πref\pi_{\texttt{ref}} to the target distribution π⋆\pi^{\star} and the PRM V^\widehat{V} (via the induced distributions π^\widehat{\pi}). We highlight below some intriguing directions for future work:
•
There are various polynomial gaps between our upper and lower bounds, including their the dependence on horizon HH, as discussed in Section 1.2, as well as the decay of the total variation distance as a function of NN: whereas our upper bounds of Sections 3.1 and 3.3 decay as 1/N1/\sqrt{N}, the lower bound of Section C.6 decays as only 1/N1/N.
•
Can we develop metrics for the performance of SMC (and other inference-time sampling algorithms) which are weaker than total variation distance but still meaningfully capture “usefulness” of outputs? For instance, for math problems, typically one only desires a correct answer, and the particular phrasing of the solution may not matter too much.
•
Can we develop provable guarantees for particle filtering algorithms or other types of inference-time sampling procedures which rely on weaker notions of discrepancy between πh⋆\pi^{\star}_{h} and π^h\widehat{\pi}_{h}? The values of Dχ2(πh⋆∥π^h)D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\;\|\;{}\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right) seen in, e.g., the experiments in Sections 4.2, 4.3 and 4.4 are extremely large (in particular, much larger than the values of NN seen in our experiments), yet particle filtering algorithms still are able to effectively make use of V^\widehat{V}.

Appendix A Comparison with Prior Work
In this section we discuss how our theoretical guarantees compare with prior work. We specify to the autoregressive generation setting, as the more general setting has not been theoretically studied in the past.
Action-level rejection sampling / sequential importance sampling (Yang and Klein, 2021)
This algorithm samples a1:Ha_{1:H} autoregressively via the conditional distributions
π^(ah+1∣a1:h)∝πref(a1:h+1∣a1:h)V^(a1:h+1).\widehat{\pi}(a_{h+1}\mid{}a_{1:h})\propto\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{1:h+1}\mid{}a_{1:h})\widehat{V}(a_{1:h+1}).
(11)
It can be implemented in expected time O⁡(H​C^𝖺𝖼𝗍)O(H\widehat{C}_{\mathsf{act}}) by rejection sampling / importance sampling Rohatgi et al. (2025), and by Eq. 2, it exactly samples from π⋆\pi^{\star} if V^=V⋆\widehat{V}=V^{\star}. However, even under assumption Section 3.1 with 𝖢∞=1+ϵ\mathsf{C}_{\infty}=1+\epsilon, it can incur Ω⁡(ϵ​H)\Omega(\epsilon\sqrt{H}) sampling error Rohatgi et al. (2025), which is vacuous if e.g. ϵ=Θ⁡(1)\epsilon=\Theta(1).
VGB (Rohatgi et al., 2025)
This algorithm is defined by a random walk on the tree of autoregressive generations, which has state space 𝒜0⊔⋯⊔𝒜H\mathcal{A}^{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{A}^{H} and in which the parent of a1:ha_{1:h} is a1:h−1a_{1:h-1}. The first main result of Rohatgi et al. (2025) is that the random walk samples from π⋆\pi^{\star}, up to total variation error δ\delta, in time roughly O⁡(H2​C𝖺𝖼𝗍​𝖢∞4​log⁡(1/δ))O(H^{2}C_{\sf act}\mathsf{C}_{\infty}^{4}\log(1/\delta)). This guarantee is essentially matched by the guarantee of Section 3.2 for SMC, except the language model evaluations in SMC can be parallelized into HH rounds, whereas the language model evaluations in VGB are inherently sequential.88

The results of Appendix E suggest a potential approach to parallelizing VGB via particle filtering, but it is unclear in what generality such a simulation would be efficient.

C.3 Insufficiency of Existing Analysis
Most non-asymptotic analyses of SMC assume conditions at least as strong as the L∞L_{\infty} closeness in Section 3.1 (Del Moral, 2004; Cérou et al., 2011; Whiteley et al., 2012). Another line of work (Marion and Schmidler, 2018; Lee and Santana-Gijzen, 2024) imposes assumptions on the transition kernel, which are therefore incomparable to those in this paper.
We note that these approaches might fail to capture SMC convergence when we assume only χ2\chi^{2}-closeness (Section 3.1). This is because many explicitly or implicitly control the variance (Var⁡(W^h))h∈[H](\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{h}))_{h\in[H]} (Cérou et al., 2011; Whiteley et al., 2012; Schweizer, 2012, etc.). In contrast, our proof of Section 3.1 proceeds via the absolute deviation 𝔼⁡|MH−Z|\En\lvert M_{H}-Z\rvert. The seemingly more natural attempt to study the variance 𝔼⁡(MH−Z)2\En(M_{H}-Z)^{2} must confront the fact that MH=W^H=∏h=1H(1N​∑i=1Nwhi)M_{H}=\widehat{W}_{H}=\prod_{h=1}^{H}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{h}^{{\scriptscriptstyle i}}\right) is a sequential product. As the following lemma suggests, Var⁡(W^h)\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{h}) need not be controlled when the χ2\chi^{2} divergences (Dχ2(πh⋆∥π^h))h∈[H](D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\;\|\;{}\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right))_{h\in[H]} are bounded.
{lemma}
Suppose that V^​(⟂)=1\widehat{V}(\perp)=1.
For any h∈[H]h\in[H], it holds that
Var⁡(W^h)=𝔼⁡(W^h−Z^h)2≥Varx1∼π1​(𝔼⁡[V^​(xh)∣x1])N.\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{h})=\En(\widehat{W}_{h}-\widehat{Z}_{h})^{2}\geq\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{x_{1}\sim\pi_{1}}(\En[\widehat{V}(x_{h})\mid x_{1}])}{N}.
(43)
In particular, for H≥2H\geq 2, there exists a problem instance (V⋆,V^)(V^{\star},\widehat{V}) such that Dχ2(πh⋆∥π^h)≤2D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}_{h}\;\|\;{}\widehat{\pi}_{h}\right)\leq 2, and both Section 3.1 and Section 3.3 holds with C𝖺𝖼𝗍=2C_{\sf act}=2, but SMC with NN particles incurs
maxh∈[H]⁡Var⁡(W^h)≥2⌊H/2⌋−2N.\displaystyle\max_{h\in[H]}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{h})\geq\frac{2^{\lfloor H/2\rfloor}-2}{N}.
(44)
Therefore, if convergence of SMC is established via upper bounds on Var⁡(W^h)\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{h}), the rate can be vacuous unless we have N≥exp⁡(Ω⁡(H))N\geq\exp(\Omega(H)) particles. For example,
Schweizer (2012) study SMC through the following relation (cf. Lemma 2.1 therein) for f:𝒳H→[0,1]f:\mathcal{X}_{H}\to[0,1]:
𝔼⁡(𝔼ν^H⁡[f]−𝔼πH⋆⁡[f])2≤2​V​a​r​(W^H⋅𝔼ν^H⁡[f])+2​V​a​r​(W^H).\displaystyle\En\left(\En_{\widehat{\nu}_{H}}[f]-\En_{\pi^{\star}_{H}}[f]\right)^{2}\leq 2\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{H}\cdot\En_{\widehat{\nu}_{H}}[f])+2\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{H}).
(45)
Consequently, Schweizer (2012) establish upper bounds of the form
N⋅Var⁡(W^H⋅𝔼ν^H⁡[f])≤C0​(f)+C1​(f)⋅ϵN,\displaystyle N\cdot\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{H}\cdot\En_{\widehat{\nu}_{H}}[f])\leq C_{0}(f)+C_{1}(f)\cdot\epsilon_{N},
(46)
where C0,C1C_{0},C_{1} are two problem-dependent functionals, and ϵN\epsilon_{N} is defined so that
ϵN≥maxh∈[H]⁡Var⁡(W^h).\displaystyle\epsilon_{N}\geq\max_{h\in[H]}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{W}_{h}).
(47)

Appendix E A Unified Perspective on Backtracking and Particle Filtering
Our main theoretical results demonstrated that particle filtering methods like SMC can achieve strong guarantees under the same assumptions on V^\widehat{V} as the backtracking-based method VGB introduced by Rohatgi et al. (2025) — while improving parallel runtime from O~​(H2)\widetilde{O}(H^{2}) to O~​(H)\widetilde{O}(H). In this section, we show that the connection goes deeper: for any problem instance, the execution of VGB can be coupled with the execution of SMC-IND, a variant of SMC in which the children of each particle are sampled independently.
We defer formal definitions until after stating the result (Appendix E), but we make several remarks for context. Henceforth, we specify to the autoregressive setting (Section 2) in which VGB was defined Rohatgi et al. (2025). Recall that VGB implements a single-particle random walk in which the allowed transitions can not only add a token to the current particle (i.e. a1:h↦a1:h+1a_{1:h}\mapsto a_{1:h+1}) but also backtrack (i.e. a1:h↦a1:h−1a_{1:h}\mapsto a_{1:h-1}). For technical reasons, we augment the state space of the random walk with a state 𝔰\mathfrak{s} that is only adjacent to the empty string ⟂\perp. See Sections E.2 and E.1 for the formal definitions of SMC-IND and the VGB random walk.
{theorem}
Let 𝒮𝖣𝖬𝖢\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{DMC}} be the (random) multiset of particles visited by SMC-IND with NN initial particles. Let 𝒮𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{VGB}} be the (random) multiset of particles visited by VGB (initialized at 𝔰\mathfrak{s}) before the N+1N+1-st visit to 𝔰\mathfrak{s}. Then there is a coupling so that 𝒮𝖣𝖬𝖢=𝒮𝖵𝖦𝖡∖{𝔰}\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{DMC}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{VGB}}\setminus\{\mathfrak{s}\} (with multiplicity) almost surely.
Intuitively, each particle a1:ha_{1:h} visited by SMC-IND corresponds to an interval of time in which the VGB random walk started at a1:ha_{1:h}, ended at a1:ha_{1:h}, and never visited the parent node a1:h−1a_{1:h-1} (where we take the parent of ⟂\perp to be 𝔰\mathfrak{s}). This demonstrates that the paradigm of backtracking to tame error amplification Rohatgi et al. (2025) is, in a sense, equivalent to a form of particle filtering.

E.3 Coupling VGB with SMC-IND
We interpret the executions of SMC-IND and VGB as stochastically constructing forests of particles. We then show that these forests are actually the same (up to occurrences of 𝔰\mathfrak{s}) when the randomness is appropriately coupled.
Infinite-particle SMC-IND process
The execution of SMC-IND with N=∞N=\infty naturally defines a rooted forest ℱ𝖣𝖬𝖢\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DMC}} where each node is labelled by a particle in 𝒮0⊔⋯⊔𝒮H\mathcal{S}_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{S}_{H}. The roots are the particles in 𝒮0\mathcal{S}_{0}. For any h>0h>0, the parent of a particle in 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h} is the particle in 𝒮h−1\mathcal{S}_{h-1} that birthed it. Note that there may be multiple (or even infinitely many) occurrences of any particle a1:ha_{1:h} in 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h}, so ℱ𝖣𝖬𝖢\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DMC}} may contain multiple nodes with the same label. The parent of a node labelled a1:ha_{1:h} always has label a1:h−1a_{1:h-1}.
Infinite-time VGB process
We next show how an execution of VGB in the autoregressive tree 𝒯\mathcal{T} can be interpreted as a rooted tree ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}}, similar to above. Fix an infinite trajectory (x0,x1,x2,x3,…)(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots) of VGB with x0=𝔰x_{0}=\mathfrak{s}. Suppose that 𝔰\mathfrak{s} occurs infinitely often in the trajectory. The root node of ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}} is indexed by ℕ\mathbb{N}, and labeled 𝔰\mathfrak{s}. Every other node is indexed by an interval [t,t′]⊂ℕ[t,t^{\prime}]\subset\mathbb{N} with the property that xt−1=xt′+1x_{t-1}=x_{t^{\prime}+1} is the parent of xt=xt′x_{t}=x_{t^{\prime}} in 𝒯\mathcal{T}, and this parent does not occur within {xt,xt+1,…,xt′}\{x_{t},x_{t+1},\dots,x_{t^{\prime}}\}. This node is labeled with xtx_{t}, and its parent in ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}} is the unique node with label xt−1x_{t-1} whose interval contains [t,t′][t,t^{\prime}].
{lemma}
Suppose that VGB almost surely visits 𝔰\mathfrak{s} infinitely often. There is a coupling of the randomness of ℱ𝖣𝖬𝖢\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DMC}} and ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}} such that ℱ𝖣𝖬𝖢\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DMC}} is almost surely equal to the forest ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡∖{𝔰}\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}}\setminus\{\mathfrak{s}\}.
Proof. We construct the coupling layer by layer. By the lemma assumption, it is clear that the first layer of ℱ𝖣𝖬𝖢\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DMC}} is almost surely identical to that of ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}}. Now fix h≥1h\geq 1 and condition on the forest structure and labels xtx_{t} (but not the indices [t,t′][t,t^{\prime}] themselves) of the first hh layers of ℱ𝖵𝖦𝖡\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{VGB}}. Fix any node ι\iota with label a1:h−1a_{1:h-1}.
Consider the law of the VGB random walk between these two visits to a1:h−1a_{1:h-1}. The walk is restricted to the subtree rooted at a1:h−1a_{1:h-1}, but since the walk is Markovian, its law is otherwise unchanged by the conditioning (and it is also independent of the children of other nodes ι′≠ι\iota^{\prime}\neq\iota at layer hh). Let p=ℙ(a1:h−2∣a1:h−1)p=\mathbb{P}(a_{1:h-2}\mid{}a_{1:h-1}) (with the convention a1:h−2=𝔰a_{1:h-2}=\mathfrak{s} if h=1h=1). The number of children D′D^{\prime} of ι\iota is the number of times that the walk goes to a child of a1:h−1a_{1:h-1} in 𝒯\mathcal{T} before it returns to a1:h−2a_{1:h-2}. Thus, ℙ[D′=k]=(1−p)kp\mathbb{P}[D^{\prime}=k]=(1-p)^{k}p for any k∈{0,1,2,…}k\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}. That is, D′D^{\prime} is a geometric random variable with success probability pp. Moreover, the label of each child of ι\iota is independent and distributed according to ℙ𝖽𝗈𝗐𝗇(⋅∣a1:h−1)\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{down}}(\cdot\mid{}a_{1:h-1}). Thus, the coupling can be extended to the children of ι\iota.

F.1 Myopic Particle Filtering Algorithms
{definition}
A myopic particle filtering algorithm with NN particles is defined as follows. The algorithm computes a stochastic sequence of multisets 𝒮0,…,𝒮H\mathcal{S}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{H} where 𝒮h⊂𝒜h\mathcal{S}_{h}\subset\mathcal{A}^{h} for all 0≤h≤H0\leq h\leq H, satisfying the following properties:
1.
It holds almost surely that for all 1≤h≤H1\leq h\leq H, for each y1:h∈𝒮hy_{1:h}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}, we have y1:h−1∈𝒮hy_{1:h-1}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}.
2.
|𝒮h|≤N|\mathcal{S}_{h}|\leq N for all hh almost surely.
3.
The law of (𝒮0,…,𝒮h)(\mathcal{S}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{h}) is determined by (πref(a1:h):a1:h∈𝒜h)(\pi_{\texttt{ref}}(a_{1:h}):a_{1:h}\in\mathcal{A}^{h}) and (V^(a1:k):a1:k∈𝒜k,k≤h)(\widehat{V}(a_{1:k}):a_{1:k}\in\mathcal{A}^{k},k\leq h) for all hh.
The algorithm then (stochastically) outputs any element of 𝒮H\mathcal{S}_{H}.
The first condition is a natural formalization of what it means to be a particle filtering method, i.e. a particle in 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h} cannot appear “out of nowhere”; rather, it must have a parent in 𝒮h−1\mathcal{S}_{h-1}. The second condition asserts that the number of particles is always at most NN. The third condition is the “myopic” condition, which enforces that, at the level of distributions, the algorithm does not use information from later steps k>hk>h to determine the multiset 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h}.
{remark}
[Sequential Monte Carlo and variants]
It is straightforward from Algorithm 1 to see that Sequential Monte Carlo is indeed a myopic particle filtering algorithm, as is Sequential Monte Carlo with Rejection Sampling (Algorithm 2).
{remark}
[Computational efficiency]
We remark that Section F.1 does not enforce any bound on computational efficiency or query complexity; in particular, a myopic particle filtering algorithm is allowed to examine V^(a1:h)\widehat{V}(a_{1:h}) for all a1:h∈𝒜ha_{1:h}\in\mathcal{A}^{h} before deciding on the set of particles 𝒮h\mathcal{S}_{h}; it is not restricted to e.g. only querying the children of particles in 𝒮h−1\mathcal{S}_{h-1}. F.3 Proof of Section F.2
F.3.1 Intuition and Formal Construction
To prove Section F.2, we construct a family of problem instances (each specified by a choice of μ\mu and μ^0,…,μ^H\widehat{\mu}_{0},\dots,\widehat{\mu}_{H}) such that any myopic particle filtering algorithm with at most NN particles must fail on at least one instance in the family. Intuition for construction
First, we provide intuition for the N=1N=1 construction, which essentially follows Example 3.2 in Rohatgi et al. (2025). We will have μ1​(⋅,y⋆)\mu^{{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}(\cdot;y^{\star}) be a product distribution where the marginal μ1​(yh)\mu^{{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}(y_{h}) is biased towards yh⋆y^{\star}_{h}. We will then have μ^1​(⋅,y⋆)\widehat{\mu}^{{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}(\cdot;y^{\star}) be “delayed” compared to μ1​(⋅,y⋆)\mu^{{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}(\cdot;y^{\star}), i.e. the marginal at step hh is biased towards yh−1⋆y^{\star}_{h-1} instead of yh⋆y^{\star}_{h}. Next, we provide intuition for the N>1N>1 construction. The horizon H=ℌ⁡(N)H=\mathfrak{H}(N) is divided into blocks of size K=ℌ⁡(N−1)+2K=\mathfrak{H}(N-1)+2. Again μN​(⋅,y⋆)\mu^{{\scriptscriptstyle N}}(\cdot;y^{\star}) is a product distribution where the marginal at step hh is biased towards yh⋆y^{\star}_{h}. In block [i​K+1,(i+1)​K][iK+1,(i+1)K], we define μ^N​(⋅,y⋆)\widehat{\mu}^{{\scriptscriptstyle N}}(\cdot;y^{\star}) by “delaying” the dependence on yi​K+1⋆y^{\star}_{iK+1} to step (i+1)​K(i+1)K, and embedding a copy of the (N−1)(N-1)-particle construction in the intervening steps. Intuitively, any NN-particle myopic particle filtering algorithm has two choices at step i​K+1iK+1: either irrevocably guess yi​K+1⋆y^{\star}_{iK+1} (i.e. all particles y∈𝒮i​K+1y\in\mathcal{S}_{iK+1} will have the same value of yi​K+1y_{iK+1}), or split up the particles so that some have different values of yi​K+1y_{iK+1}. The basic idea, formalized in Section F.3.3, is that both choices lead to sampling error: the former because no data about yi​K+1⋆y^{\star}_{iK+1} is available at step i​K+1iK+1, and the latter by the inductive hypothesis on the (N−1)(N-1)-particle construction. The multiple blocks are needed to amplify the error induced by the first choice.
Making this argument precise requires a careful probabilistic analysis to ensure that the errors in the two cases cannot “cancel out”. Before proceeding to this analysis, we make the construction formal.

Appendix G Additional Experimental Details
In this section, we provide further details regarding our experimental setup.
G.1 Additional Evaluation Approaches
First, we give additional context and motivation for our choice of the evaluation metric of logprob-discrepancy introduced in Section 4.1.
One approach to evaluate SMC, which has been used in prior work, is to use the fact that the value W^H\widehat{W}_{H} computed in Algorithm 1 satisfies 𝔼⁡[W^H]=Z\mathbb{E}[\widehat{W}_{H}]=Z and moreover the difference log⁡Z−𝔼⁡[log⁡W^H]\log Z-\mathbb{E}[\log\widehat{W}_{H}] is an upper bound on the KL divergence D𝖪𝖫(𝔼[ν^H]∥πH⋆)D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\nu}_{H}]\,\|\,\pi^{\star}_{H}\right) between the output distribution of SMC, 𝔼⁡[ν^H]\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\nu}_{H}], and the target distribution πH⋆\pi^{\star}_{H} (Proposition 5 of Zhao et al. (2024)).99
9
Further, in Section C.1 we show that the difference |W^H−Z||\widehat{W}_{H}-Z| upper bounds the total variation distance D𝖳𝖵​(𝔼⁡[ν^H],πH⋆)D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\nu}_{H}],\pi^{\star}_{H}\right). However, there are several pitfalls with this approach: first, this is only an upper bound and is not guaranteed to be tight; further, it only applies to SMC and so does not allow us to compare the performance of SMC to other baselines. Finally, even for the relatively simple setting in which we operate, the number NN of particles is too small to get actual distributional closeness between 𝔼⁡[ν^H]\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\nu}_{H}] and the target πH⋆\pi^{\star}_{H} (for instance, the action-level coverage is too large).
Thus, we aimed to use a weaker notion of distributional closeness that we can both (a) measure efficiently and (b) hope to have some bearing on the quality of samples produced by SMC. The 𝒫\mathcal{P}-logprob discrepancy certainly satisfies (a). Moreover, since its error is one-sided in nature (i.e., LPD𝒫​(ν,ν)=0\mathrm{LPD}_{\mathcal{P}}(\nu,\nu)=0) and it takes on a positive value in all of our experiments, we believe it is a good candidate in the context of the prompt switching task.
