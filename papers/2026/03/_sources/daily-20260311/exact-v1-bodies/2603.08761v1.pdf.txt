On the Formal Limits of Alignment Verification
Ayushi Agarwal
Independent Researcher

arXiv:2603.08761v1 [stat.ML] 8 Mar 2026

Abstract
The goal of AI alignment is to ensure that an AI system reliably pursues intended objectives. A foundational question for AI safety is whether alignment can be formally certified:
whether there exists a procedure that can guarantee that a given system satisfies an alignment
specification. This paper studies the nature of alignment verification. We prove that no verification procedure can simultaneously satisfy three properties: soundness (no misaligned system
is certified), generality (verification holds over the full input domain), and tractability (verification runs in polynomial time). Each pair of properties is achievable, but all three cannot hold
simultaneously. Relaxing any one property restores the corresponding possibility, indicating
that practical bounded or probabilistic assurance remains viable. The result follows from three
independent barriers: the computational complexity of full-domain neural network verification,
the non-identifiability of internal goal structure from behavioral observation, and the limits of
finite evidence for properties defined over infinite domains. The trilemma establishes the limits
of alignment certification and characterizes the regimes in which meaningful guarantees remain
possible.

Introduction

There is a question that sits beneath much of the current work on AI alignment, rarely asked
directly: could we, even in principle, know that an AI system is aligned? Not whether a given
system behaves appropriately on the evaluations we have constructed, or whether it satisfies the
objectives we have chosen to optimize. Whether there exists any procedure—in principle, under
any formalism—that can certify alignment. The distinction is between measurement and proof.
Between what was observed and what must hold.
For simple algorithms, this distinction is familiar. A sorting procedure is not accepted because
it has successfully sorted many lists, but because one can demonstrate that for every admissible
input the resulting list is ordered. The correctness of the procedure follows necessarily from its
construction. It establishes, by logical necessity, that every execution satisfies the specification.
That is what a certificate means: not evidence, but necessity. The question is whether such
necessity is attainable for alignment.
In mathematics and formal verification, correctness is established by proof against an explicit
specification. In engineering and safety-critical systems, where formal proof is unavailable, the
standard method is measurement against a defined target with accepted tolerance. One specifies
A∗ , the desired property or output region. One computes error as deviation from that target:
|f (x) − A∗ |. One accepts the system if this error falls within a threshold ε that is itself formally
justified. Medical devices are cleared on this basis. Aircraft control systems are certified on this
basis. Safety-critical engineering proceeds on this basis throughout. The method requires three
things: a formally defined target, a computable error measure, and a threshold grounded in the
consequences of failure. The question for alignment is whether this procedure is available.
1

It is not. This difficulty has two roots, independent of each other.
(a) No formal target exists. There is currently no agreed-upon A∗ for alignment—no mathematical function mapping a system to an alignment score, no target distribution over outputs, no
set of necessary and sufficient conditions that, if satisfied, constitute alignment. The absence is not
a gap in current research that further work will fill. It is a structural feature of the problem, arising
from the fact that “aligned behavior” is defined relative to human values and intent, which resist
complete formalization. In the absence of A∗ , all alignment methods optimize proxy signals. Human preference scores, constitutional constraints, refusal policies, benchmark performance—these
are chosen because they are measurable and appear correlated with desirable behavior. They are
not chosen because they provably approximate any underlying alignment objective, because no
such objective has been specified. Alignment error cannot be computed, because there is no target
to take the error against. Whether proxy improvement represents progress toward alignment is
unknown and, in the strict sense, unknowable without a target. The assertion that a system is
“99% aligned” is semantically vacuous without a specified A∗ .
(b)Internal representations are not identifiable from behavior. Alignment under distribution shift is determined by the internal structure of a system—by the representations it forms and
the goals those representations encode—not merely by its observable outputs on any finite evaluation set. The map from internal representations to observable outputs is many-to-one [Elhage et al.,
2022]. The same output distribution, over any finite set of inputs, can be produced by exponentially
many distinct internal configurations. Two systems producing identical outputs on every evaluation may have entirely different internal goal structures—one pursuing the intended objective, the
other optimizing a reward signal that coincides with the objective on the training distribution but
diverges under shift [D’Amour et al., 2022]. Behavioral testing alone cannot distinguish between
them. The distinction becomes consequential precisely when conditions change.
These two observations lead to a more precise inquiry. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that
alignment could be expressed as a well-defined function A∗ —formally specified, computable, and
well-behaved. Even under such favorable assumptions, could there exist a procedure that certifies
whether a given system satisfies this condition? The analysis that follows gives a negative answer.
If a formal certificate of alignment is to be issued, three properties are not optional. They follow
from what “certificate” means: not evidence of likely correctness, but a guarantee that must hold.
(A) Soundness (S): The certificate must be reliable. This means it produces no false positives—
whenever it is issued, the system is genuinely aligned, and no false negatives—every genuinely
aligned system receives it. A certificate that occasionally vouches for a misaligned system is
not a certificate at all—it is false assurance, which, insofar as it suppresses further investigation, may be worse than none. Formally: V(θ) = aligned ⇒ A∗ (θ) ≥ 1 − δ.
(B) Generality (G): The certificate must be universal—it holds over the full input domain,
not merely the distribution on which the system was evaluated. A procedure that certifies
alignment on a test set has established behavioral adequacy on that set. It has not established
alignment. Deployment introduces inputs that no evaluation anticipated; the certificate must
cover them. Formally: A∗ (θ) ≥ 1 − δ ⇒ V(θ) = aligned for all θ, with D = X .
(C) Tractability (T): The certificate must be obtainable. A verification procedure correct in
principle but incapable of terminating in practice provides no usable guarantee—it is a proof
that cannot be collected. The procedure must terminate in time polynomial in the size of the
system. Formally: V terminates in time polynomial in |θ|.

These three properties—soundness, generality, tractability—are not desiderata chosen for convenience. They are the minimal formal content of the word “guarantee.” A guarantee that occasionally lies is not a guarantee. A guarantee over a subset is not a guarantee over everything. A
guarantee that cannot be collected is not a guarantee.
Before the impossibility: each pair is achievable. Each pair of the three properties is
simultaneously satisfiable, and this must be established before claiming the joint impossibility.
• S+G without T: Sound and complete verification over all inputs exists (SMT-based tools).
NP-hard for feedforward networks, undecidable for Turing-complete architectures. The cost
is tractability, not feasibility.
• S+T without G: Sound and tractable verification exists over bounded domains (Reluplex over
[l, u]n ). Inputs outside the domain are uncertified.
• G+T without S: RLHF proxy scoring applies to all models across all inputs in polynomial
time. The proxy diverges from A∗ .
Each pair is achievable. The impossibility concerns all three together.

Related Work

Alignment methods. RLHF [Christiano et al., 2017, Ouyang et al., 2022] optimizes a policy
against a learned reward model. Constitutional AI [Bai et al., 2022] adds rule-based constraints.
Direct Preference Optimization [Rafailov et al., 2023] bypasses explicit reward modeling. Each
defines operational objectives rather than a formal alignment specification in the sense of Definition 2: they are calibrated against human feedback on a finite evaluation set, not against a formally
specified A∗ over all inputs. Collectively these methods operate in the G+T regime—general and
tractable in application, but without provable soundness.
Reward hacking and specification gaps. Skalse et al. [2022] formalize reward hacking
and show proxy optimization can diverge from true objectives. Gao et al. [2023] demonstrate
scaling laws for reward model overoptimization. Kwa et al. [2024] prove that under heavy-tailed
misspecification, policies can achieve arbitrarily high proxy reward with no utility gain. These
results establish, empirically and formally, that the gap between proxy and true objective is not
a calibration artifact but a structural feature—a fact the informational impossibility (Lemma 4)
shows holds for any tractable verifier, regardless of how the proxy is constructed.
Mechanistic interpretability and superposition. Elhage et al. [2022] demonstrate that
neural networks represent more features than dimensions via superposition. Olah et al. [2020] develop circuit-level analysis. Hubinger et al. [2019] introduce mesa-optimization, showing behavioral
indistinguishability on training distributions can mask divergent goals under shift. Geiger et al.
[2021] and Geiger et al. [2022] develop causal abstraction, establishing that input-output equivalent
models can be causally distinct. This body of work establishes that internal structure matters for
alignment—that two models can agree on every observable behavior yet pursue different goals when
conditions shift. The representational impossibility (Lemma 3) formalizes this observation.

3

Formal verification of neural networks. Katz et al. [2017] prove NP-completeness of ReLU
network property verification. Huang et al. [2017] and Bastani et al. [2016] address bounded-domain
verification. Rice’s Theorem [Rice, 1953] establishes undecidability of all non-trivial semantic properties for any Turing-complete system. Pérez et al. [2021] prove that transformers with unbounded
precision and hardmax attention are Turing-complete. Bhattamishra et al. [2020] extend this to the
softmax regime. Li & Wang [2025] show constant bit-size transformers with long enough context are
Turing-complete. Merrill & Sabharwal [2024] establish Turing-completeness for transformers with
chain-of-thought reasoning. Taken together, these results place modern transformer-based systems
within the scope of Rice’s Theorem. The computational impossibility (Lemma 2) formalizes both
tiers.
Specification and value alignment. Hadfield-Menell et al. [2017] frame alignment as inverse
reward design. Gabriel [2020] surveys philosophical approaches. Ngo et al. [2022] surveys alignment
challenges broadly. The results below establish formal limits on verification regardless of which
specific alignment definition is adopted, provided it meets structural criteria.
Invariance in reward learning. Skalse & Abate [2023] prove that different reward functions can induce the same optimal policy—a result complementary to the representational barrier:
symmetry at the policy level mirrors symmetry at the parameter level.
Scope. These results concern formal certification, not the feasibility of building well-behaved
systems. Empirical methods often produce systems that behave well in practice; whether that
behavior can be formally certified under conditions S, G, and T is a separate question. Bounded
verification, probabilistic assurance, and defense-in-depth remain viable and practically essential
(Section 6).
The gap no prior work addresses is the joint structure across all three barriers—the fact that
each pair is achievable (Section 4), that the three barriers are independent (Corollary 1), and that
the impossibility is therefore a genuine trilemma rather than a conjunction of separate negative
results.

Formal Framework

The introduction argues for three properties a certificate must have. We now give each argument
its formal content. The definitions below are chosen to be as weak as possible—we want the
impossibility to apply broadly—while being strong enough to capture what a certification guarantee
requires.
Definition 1 (AI System). An AI system is a parameterized function fθ : X → Y with parameters
θ ∈ Θ ⊆ Rd .
Definition 2 (Alignment Objective). An alignment objective A∗ : F → [0, 1] maps each system to
an alignment score, where A∗ (θ) = 1 denotes perfect alignment. A∗ is formally specified if it is
mathematically defined, though not necessarily computable.
The alignment property must be semantic: it depends on what the system computes, not on
how it is parameterized.
Definition 3 (Semantic Property). A property P of fθ is semantic if it depends on the mapping
x 7→ fθ (x), not on syntactic features. It is non-trivial if some system satisfies P and some does not.
Definition 4 (Alignment Verification Procedure). A verification procedure V takes as input a system fθ (and possibly an alignment specification) and outputs a binary verdict: aligned or unaligned.
4

Definition 5 (Trilemma Properties). Given A∗ and a verification domain D ⊆ X , a procedure V
satisfies:
(A) Soundness (S): V(θ) = aligned ⇒ A∗ (θ) ≥ 1 − δ for a stated tolerance δ ≥ 0.
(B) Generality (G): (i) A∗ (θ) ≥ 1 − δ ⇒ V(θ) = aligned for all θ ∈ F (completeness); and
(ii) D = X , the full unbounded input domain (full-domain validity).
(C) Tractability (T): V terminates in time polynomial in |θ|.
Remark 1 (Interpretation of G). Completeness ensures no aligned system is rejected. Full-domain
validity ensures the certificate covers all inputs, not just a bounded test set. Together they express
the broadest possible guarantee. Restricting either—accepting some false negatives, or bounding
the domain—restores possibility (Section 6).
Definition 6 (Behavioral Equivalence). θ1 ∼T θ2 if fθ1 (x) = fθ2 (x) for all x ∈ T . They are
universally behaviorally equivalent, written θ1 ∼ θ2 , if θ1 ∼X θ2 .
Definition 7 (Alignment Verification Problem). Given fθ , a specification predicate φ over inputoutput pairs, and D ⊆ X , determine whether ∀ x ∈ D : φ(x, fθ (x)) = true.

3.1

Structural Facts and Modeling Assumptions

The results below rest on two kinds of premises. Established facts are properties of standard neural
network architectures that follow directly from existing results—each is cited accordingly. Modeling
assumptions are genuine choices about the scope of the verification problem. Each result specifies
which premises it uses.
Assumption 1 (Model Expressivity). F is closed under the symmetry group G (Definition 9) and
expressive enough to modify behavior on any finite S ⊂ X while preserving behavior on X \ S. This
is a property of standard overparameterized ReLU networks, established in Montufar et al. [2014],
Sussmann [1992].
Assumption 2 (Structure-Dependent Alignment). A∗ depends on the internal representations
Hθ (·), not solely on fθ (·). This follows from Theorem 1: any A∗ requiring robustness under distribution shift must be sensitive to internal structure governing generalization.
Assumption 3 (Alignment Non-Invariance Under Symmetry). There exist θ, θ′ ∈ [θ]∼ (i.e., fθ =
fθ′ ) with A∗ (θ) ̸= A∗ (θ′ ). This is derived for any A∗ ∈ Arob by Theorem 1, via four independent
bodies of evidence [Hubinger et al., 2019, Langosco et al., 2022, Geiger et al., 2021, D’Amour et al.,
2022].
Assumption 4 (Non-Trivial Semantic Alignment). The alignment predicate φ is a non-trivial
semantic property (Definition 3).
Assumption 5 (Domain Universality). The verification domain D equals the full unbounded input
space X . This is a genuine modeling assumption about verification scope.
Assumption 6 (Finite Evaluation Access). A proxy Â uses only finite evaluation over support
S ⊂ X , where S is a strict finite subset. This follows from polynomial runtime and is consistent
with property-testing lower bounds [Goldreich, 2017].

Assumption 7 (Full-Domain Dependence of A∗ ). For any finite S ⊂ X , there exist θ1 , θ2 with
fθ1 (x) = fθ2 (x) for all x ∈ S but A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ). This is a genuine modeling assumption about
the scope of the alignment objective.
Remark 2 (Scope of A∗ ). The trilemma results hold for any A∗ satisfying the structural conditions
of Assumptions 4–3, including objectives that are computable, Lipschitz continuous, efficiently approximable by learned reward models, or defined via structural predicates over bounded programs.
The results do not rely on A∗ being non-computable or degenerate.

Pairwise Achievability

Each pair of the three properties can be achieved simultaneously. This must be established before
claiming the joint impossibility; otherwise the result is merely three independent negative results,
not a trilemma.
Proposition 1 (S+G without T). There exists a verification procedure satisfying Soundness and
Generality that does not satisfy Tractability.
Proof. Consider the SMT-based procedure that, given fθ and a linear alignment specification φ,
enumerates all linear regions of fθ and checks φ on each. This procedure is sound (it certifies only
when φ holds universally) and general (it verifies over all x ∈ X and misses no aligned system).
By Katz et al. [2017], this procedure is NP-complete for ReLU networks with linear φ; for universal
domains, the exponential growth of linear regions [Montufar et al., 2014] makes it super-polynomial
in the depth and width of fθ . S and G hold; T fails.
Proposition 2 (S+T without G). There exists a verification procedure satisfying Soundness and
Tractability that does not satisfy Generality.
Proof. For a bounded domain Db ⊂ X , bounded SMT tools (e.g., Reluplex on [l, u]n ) certify φ
soundly in polynomial time for fixed-size networks over fixed bounded domains. The procedure
fails Generality because D ̸= X — inputs outside Db are uncertified—and it may fail to certify
systems whose alignment depends on structure outside Db . S and T hold; G fails.
Proposition 3 (G+T without S). There exists a verification procedure satisfying Generality and
Tractability that does not satisfy Soundness.
Proof. Let Â : Θ → [0, 1] be an RLHF-style proxy reward score, computable in polynomial time.
Define V(θ) = aligned iff Â(θ) ≥ τ . This procedure is general (applies to any fθ ∈ F across all
inputs) and tractable (Â is a neural forward pass, polynomial in |θ|). It is not sound: by Lemma 4,
there exist θ with Â(θ) ≥ τ but A∗ (θ) < 1 − δ. G and T hold; S fails.

Structural Preliminaries

The proofs of Lemmas 3 and 4 rest on a structural fact that is easy to overlook and difficult to
escape.
Consider a neural network with a hidden layer. Permuting the neurons in that layer—swapping
neuron 1 and neuron 2, say—changes every activation vector the network computes internally.
It does not change any output, because the next layer’s weights are permuted correspondingly.
The internal representation is different; the function is identical. For a hidden layer of width d,
there are at least d! such permutations, and the symmetry group includes sign-flips and scalings as
well [Sussmann, 1992]. The result is an enormous equivalence class of parameter settings that are
externally indistinguishable but internally distinct.

This behavioral non-injectivity matters because alignment under distribution shift depends on
internal structure, which determines how a system generalizes. Two systems that produce identical
outputs on every tested input may behave entirely differently when deployment conditions shift.
The following theorem makes this connection precise.
Theorem 1 (Behavioral Equivalence Does Not Imply Alignment Equivalence). There exists a class
Arob ⊆ {A∗ | A∗ requires robustness under distribution shift} such that: for any A∗ ∈ Arob , there
exist θ1 ∼ θ2 in F (universally behaviorally equivalent) with A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ).
7

Proof. We establish this via four independent bodies of evidence.
(1) Internal objective divergence [Hubinger et al., 2019]. The mesa-optimization framework formally distinguishes the behavioral objective—what a system appears to optimize—from the mesaobjective—what it actually optimizes internally. These two can coincide on any finite training distribution while diverging arbitrarily under distribution shift. For any training distribution Dtrain ,
there exist θ1 , θ2 with identical behavioral objectives on Dtrain but distinct mesa-objectives, which
determine OOD behavior. Since A∗ must hold under shift, A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ) despite θ1 ∼Dtrain θ2 .
(2) Goal misgeneralization [Langosco et al., 2022]. Empirically demonstrates that RL agents retain
full capabilities out-of-distribution yet pursue the wrong goal. In the canonical CoinRun setting,
agents trained where the coin is always at the level end learn to reach the end rather than the coin:
training-distribution behavior is identical to a correctly aligned agent, yet internal goals diverge
completely under shift. This constitutes an existence proof that θ1 ∼Dtrain θ2 with A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 )
is commonplace.
(3) Causal non-equivalence [Geiger et al., 2021]. The causal abstraction framework defines when
two models are causally equivalent vs. merely input-output equivalent. Models in the same equivalence class [θ]∼ can implement completely different causal structures. Since A∗ requires OOD
robustness, and OOD behavior is determined by internal causal structure, A∗ must distinguish
causally distinct models.
(4) Underspecification at scale [D’Amour et al., 2022]. Across computer vision, NLP, and medical
imaging, models trained identically—achieving identical held-out performance—behave very differently in deployment. This establishes that A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ) despite θ1 ∼Dtrain θ2 is the norm in
modern deep learning.
Combining. We say A∗ is robustness-sensitive if it is sensitive to at least one of the four phenomena
above. An A∗ constant on [θ]∼ despite all four phenomena would be simultaneously insensitive
to all of them—unable to distinguish a model that pursues the right goal everywhere from one
that only appears to on training data. Such an A∗ provides no meaningful robustness guarantee
and is excluded from Arob by definition. For any A∗ ∈ Arob , there therefore exist θ1 ∼ θ2 with
A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ).

5.2

Soundness and Generality Force Out Tractability

A verifier that is both sound and general must solve the full verification problem: check a semantic
property across all possible inputs, and return the correct answer for every system. For feedforward
ReLU networks, this requires reasoning about every linear region the network can produce, and
the number of such regions grows exponentially with depth. For architectures that are Turingcomplete—transformers with chain-of-thought, for instance—the problem becomes undecidable by
Rice’s Theorem. No algorithmic improvement can allow the verifier to run in polynomial time.
Lemma 2 (Sound and General Verification Is Intractable). Under Assumptions 4 and 5: any
procedure V that is simultaneously Sound and General cannot be Tractable.

transformer architectures operating under the Turing-complete regimes of [Pérez et al., 2021,
Bhattamishra et al., 2020, Li & Wang, 2025, Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024], verification of any
non-trivial semantic alignment property is undecidable by Rice’s Theorem [Rice, 1953]. This
is strictly stronger than (i).

5.3

Soundness and Tractability Force Out Generality

A sound certificate is a statement about what a system computes, not about how it is parameterized. The consequence is this: when neurons in a hidden layer are permuted, with the subsequent
layer’s weights adjusted correspondingly, every output of the network is preserved exactly. The
two parameterizations compute the same function. A sound verifier must assign them the same
verdict—not because it is convenient to do so, but because a certificate that distinguishes two representations of the same computation is certifying the representation, not the computation. That
is not soundness.
The difficulty is that alignment under distribution shift depends on internal structure, which
symmetric parameterizations do not share. Two systems agreeing on every observable output may
encode entirely different generalization patterns, different responses to distribution shift. The verifier, constrained by soundness to treat symmetric partners identically, cannot track this variation.
If it certifies both, it certifies a potentially misaligned system. If it rejects both, it fails to certify a
genuinely aligned one. In either case, generality fails.
Lemma 3 (Sound and Tractable Verification Cannot Be General). Under Assumptions 1, 2, and 3:
any procedure V that is simultaneously Sound and Tractable cannot be General.
Proof. Step 1: Behavioral non-injectivity. By Lemma 1, there exist θ1 , θ2 with θ1 ∼ θ2 but Hθ1 ̸=
Hθ2 .
Step 2: Alignment-distinct symmetric pair. By Theorem 1 (under Assumption 3), there exist
θ1 ∼ θ2 with A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ).
Step 3: Verifier consistency under symmetry.
Claim. Any sound V must satisfy V(θ) = V(g · θ) for all g ∈ G(θ).
Proof of Claim. Suppose V(θ) ̸= V(g · θ) for some g ∈ G. Then V certifies θ as
aligned and g · θ as unaligned (or vice versa). A certification is a guarantee about
what the system computes. Since fθ = fg·θ by definition of G, deploying θ or g · θ in any
environment produces identical outputs. The ambiguity group of a feedforward network
is exactly the group of permutations and sign-flips of hidden units, with no canonical
representative [Sussmann, 1992]. A certificate that declares two representations of the
same computation as having different safety status is certifying the labeling of internals,

computed it as g · θ (not certified) through an equivalent training run. No principled
way to detect or resolve this exists. Therefore any sound V must satisfy V(θ) = V(g · θ)
for all g ∈ G.

Step 4: Contradiction. By Step 3, V(θ1 ) = V(θ2 ). By Step 2, A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ): one is aligned

one of S or G fails.

5.4

Generality and Tractability Force Out Soundness

A tractable verifier runs in polynomial time, which means it can examine only a finite slice of
evidence: finitely many input-output queries, finitely many weight statistics. True alignment,
however, depends on behavior across an infinite input domain—on inputs the verifier has never
seen and can never enumerate. Whatever finite set of questions the verifier chooses to ask, two
systems can be constructed that answer every question identically yet differ in alignment precisely
where the verifier cannot look. The verifier, seeing the same evidence from both systems, must
issue the same verdict. One of those verdicts is wrong.
Lemma 4 (General and Tractable Verification Cannot Be Sound). Under Assumptions 1, 6, and 7:
any general and tractable procedure V cannot be sound.
Proof. Polynomial time implies finite evaluation support. Any tractable V runs in time p(|θ|) for
some fixed polynomial p, and can make at most p(|θ|) queries. This induces an evaluation support
S with |S| ≤ p(|θ|). Since X is infinite (Assumption 5), S is a strict finite subset, consistent with
property-testing lower bounds [Goldreich, 2017].

Theorem 2 (Alignment Verification Trilemma). Let F contain ReLU networks with non-trivial
symmetry group G (Assumption 1), and let A∗ satisfy Assumptions 4, 3, and 7. Under Assumptions 5 and 6, no verification procedure V can simultaneously satisfy Soundness (S), Generality (G),
and Tractability (T).
Proof. By contrapositive: assume V satisfies any two of the three properties. We show the third
must fail.
Case 1 (V is S and G): A verifier with no false positives and no false negatives must decide a
semantic property over the full input domain—exactly the problem Lemma 2 shows is NP-hard
for feedforward networks and undecidable for transformer-class models with CoT or unbounded
precision. T fails.
Case 2 (V is S and T): A verifier running in polynomial time must treat all behaviorally identical
parameter settings identically—yet alignment may differ across that equivalence class. By Lemma 3,
it cannot correctly classify all of them. G fails.
Case 3 (V is G and T): A tractable verifier surveys only finitely many pieces of evidence. For any
such finite view, two systems exist that look identical but are not. The verifier, forced to issue
the same verdict for both, certifies the misaligned one whenever it certifies the aligned one. By
Lemma 4, soundness fails. S fails.
Since every possible V falls into at least one of these cases, at least one of S, G, T must fail.
The three lemmas each formalize a distinct gap. The first is a computational gap: verifying a
semantic property across all inputs is hard, and for Turing-complete systems, undecidable. The
second is a representational gap: internal goal structure is not recoverable from behavioral observation, and a sound verifier cannot see past it. The third is an informational gap: finite evidence
cannot certify infinite-domain properties. Classical software verification faces the computational
gap alone. Alignment verification must contend with all three.
Corollary 1 (Independence of the Three Trilemma Constraints). The three impossibility directions
are mutually independent: satisfying any two properties in full does not narrow the gap on the third.
(i) Resolving S and G does not resolve T: full-domain, sound-and-complete verification is NPhard for feedforward networks and undecidable for transformer-class models with CoT—
regardless of representation or specification properties.
(ii) Resolving S and T does not resolve G: extending bounded, sound verification to generality
requires overcoming the symmetry barrier.
(iii) Resolving G and T does not resolve S: a general and tractable proxy-based system remains
unsound regardless of how sophisticated the proxy becomes.

What Remains Possible

Relaxing any one of the three properties restores a well-defined regime of certification. It represents
a scope within which real guarantees are achievable—as long as the relaxation is stated explicitly
and the guarantee is not represented as something stronger than what it is.

6.1

Relaxing T: Pairwise Feasibility of S and G

Dropping the polynomial-time requirement, SMT-based tools—Reluplex [Katz et al., 2017], Marabou,
and α-β-CROWN—provide sound and general verification for linear specifications over bounded
networks. They are exponential in the worst case but practical for moderate-sized systems and
restricted specification classes. Progress in verification tooling directly expands this regime.

6.2

Relaxing G: Sound, Tractable Bounded Verification

If the deployment distribution can be specified and bounded, a sound and tractable certificate
over that bounded domain is achievable. Restricting to bounded domain Db ⊂ X restores both
tractability and soundness. The practical challenge is the honesty of the bound: the certified
domain must represent the actual deployment conditions. Red-teaming and adversarial evaluation
constitute empirical methodologies for approximating this bound, though neither provides formal
coverage guarantees.

6.3

Relaxing S: General, Tractable Statistical Guarantees

Statistical testing can bound the probability of alignment failure. Dropping soundness, given n

6.4

Mechanistic Interpretability and the Representational Barrier

Lemma 3 identifies a precise obstacle, and in doing so it points toward a way around it. The
obstacle is that a sound verifier must be G-invariant on its verdicts, but A∗ is not G-invariant on
its values.
An interpretability method would escape this barrier if it produces a representation map Φ : Θ →
Z satisfying: (i) Φ(θ) = Φ(g · θ) for all g ∈ G (G-invariance); and (ii) Φ(θ1 ) ̸= Φ(θ2 ) whenever
A∗ (θ1 ) ̸= A∗ (θ2 ) (alignment-discriminating power). Condition (i) is within reach: singular value
decompositions and invariant polynomials of weight matrices [Ainsworth et al., 2022] provide it.
Condition (ii) is harder and is exactly the interpretability problem. Sparse dictionary learning
[Elhage et al., 2022] and causal abstraction [Geiger et al., 2021] represent partial progress toward
it. The formal criterion for complete resolution is sharp, and the trilemma makes it precisely
stateable.

Defense in Depth

No single relaxation need carry the full burden in practice. Combining bounded verification (relaxed
G), statistical testing (relaxed S), and interpretability audits provides layered assurance that raises
the bar for undetected misalignment even when no individual layer achieves the full trilemma. This
approach does not circumvent the impossibility. It is a rational response to it, made possible by
understanding the structure of what cannot be achieved.
Remark 3 (The Trilemma Holds Even for Computable A∗ ). The diagonal construction in Lemma 4
holds for any A∗ satisfying Assumption 7—including A∗ that are computable, Lipschitz, and efficiently approximable by learned reward models. The proxy gap ∆(θ) = |A∗ (θ) − Â(θ)| remains
non-zero not because A∗ is uncomputable, but because A∗ depends on off-support behavior that Â
cannot evaluate in polynomial time.

7

Implications for Alignment Research

The trilemma has direct consequences for how alignment verification claims should be stated and
evaluated.
Any deployment context claiming all three of soundness, generality, and tractability is implicitly
relaxing at least one of the conditions under which the impossibility holds. Stating that relaxation
explicitly is not a formality. It is a prerequisite for a well-grounded safety claim. Alignment
verification is, in this sense, better understood as structured risk management than as certification.
The trilemma does not rule out partial guarantees. It rules out comprehensive ones, and it identifies
exactly what is partial about each partial guarantee.
The research agenda the trilemma suggests is not to escape the impossibility—that cannot be
done under the stated conditions—but to identify, for each deployment context, which property
is most acceptable to relax and what the strongest achievable guarantee looks like within that
relaxation.
Empirical progress in alignment is real. Systems are measurably improving on alignmentrelevant evaluations. What cannot be established is whether this improvement approaches comprehensive alignment or will hold under capability scaling into regimes where human oversight is
no longer feasible. The field operates through iterative refinement of proxy objectives—a rational
response to the absence of a formal target, but not equivalent to formal optimization against one.
The trilemma makes that distinction precise.

Discussion

Nature of the contribution. The three individual barriers formalized here each have antecedents
in the literature. The computational intractability of neural network verification is established by
Katz et al. [2017]. The observation that behavioral equivalence does not imply goal equivalence underlies the mesa-optimization framework [Hubinger et al., 2019] and the underspecification findings
of D’Amour et al. [2022]. The formal consequence of proxy optimization for soundness is analyzed
by Skalse et al. [2022] and Gao et al. [2023]. These results are, individually, well-established.
What this paper establishes is not any one of them. It is three things together: a unified formal
framework in which all three barriers are stated as properties of a single verification procedure;
constructive procedures demonstrating that each pair of properties is simultaneously achievable
(Section 4)—necessary to claim a trilemma rather than a list of impossibilities; and a corollary
(Corollary 1) establishing that the three barriers are mutually independent, so that satisfying any
two gives no purchase on the third. The independence result closes the prospect that simultaneous
progress on two fronts might eventually dissolve the third barrier.
Why trilemma rather than conjunction? A conjunction of three impossibilities does not
constitute a trilemma unless pairwise achievability is demonstrated and the barriers are shown
independent. Section 4 establishes pairwise achievability. Corollary 1 establishes independence.
The three gaps. Alignment differs from classical software verification in depth. Classical
verification checks behavioral properties—type safety, memory safety, functional correctness—that
depend on what the program computes. Alignment lives partly in the gap between behavior and
intent, and partly in the gap between the training distribution and the deployment distribution.
Lemma 2 establishes the computational gap: verifying a semantic property across all inputs is hard
to close, and for Turing-complete systems, undecidable. Lemma 3 establishes the representational
gap: external behavior does not determine internal goals. Lemma 4 establishes the informational
gap: the tested distribution does not determine the deployment distribution. Classical verification
addresses only the first. Alignment verification must contend with all three.
Limitations. The intractability direction (Lemma 2(i)) is established for linear specifications
of feedforward networks; the complexity of nonlinear semantic verification remains open for this
class. Lemma 2(ii) closes this gap for transformer-class models under the Turing-completeness
regimes of the cited works. All results are conditional and explicitly state their conditions.

9

Conclusion

The question we began with—could we, even in principle, know that an AI system is aligned?—now
has a structured answer. The answer is not simply “no.” Any two of soundness (S), generality
(G), and tractability (T) can be achieved simultaneously, but not all three. The three barriers are
independent.
A structured impossibility is more informative than an unstructured one. An unstructured
result—“alignment verification is hopeless”—provides no actionable structure. A structured one
identifies exactly which wall is being pushed against in each research agenda, what the strongest
achievable guarantee within that agenda looks like, and what question must be answered to do better. Bounded verification pushes on the generality wall. Statistical testing pushes on the soundness
wall. Mechanistic interpretability, if it produces G-invariant representations that track alignment,
pushes on the representational wall from the inside. Each represents a viable research direction.
The result’s significance is not that alignment is hopeless. A trilemma is not a negation. It is
a structure.

The central open problem the trilemma defines is the characterization of the Pareto frontier of
achievable guarantee quality across the three relaxation axes jointly. How much of the impossibility
survives when soundness is weakened to probabilistic assurance? When generality is restricted to
a structured family of inputs? When tractability is relaxed to quasi-polynomial time? These
questions are now precisely stateable. Their answers are not yet known. That is the boundary the
trilemma draws—not an end, but a beginning.
