A Formal Framework for Capability-Based AI Systems

Abstract
Central result. This paper contains the first formal proof that safety is non-compositional
in the presence of conjunctive capability dependencies: two agents each individually incapable of reaching any forbidden capability can, when combined, collectively reach a forbidden
goal through an emergent conjunctive dependency. This result (Theorem 9.2) is tight—it
cannot arise in pairwise graph models, only in systems with AND-semantics—and shows
that component-level safety checks are structurally insufficient for modular agentic systems.
To prove and apply this result, we introduce a formal framework in which AI capability
systems are modelled as directed hypergraphs, where a hyperedge ({u1 , . . . , uk }, {v}) fires
only when all of u1 , . . . , uk are simultaneously present. Traditional capability graphs assume
pairwise dependencies and cannot express this AND-semantics without introducing artificial
conjunction nodes. We prove that capability graphs embed into capability hypergraphs as
a strict special case (Lemma 5.1 and Corollary 5.2), and that planning reduces to a single
fixed-point closure computation grounded in the Horn clause completeness theorem of van
Emden and Kowalski [1976], with an O(n + m · k) worklist algorithm.
Beyond safety, the closure framework supports a formal theory of goal discovery. We
prove the closure gain function is submodular (Theorem 8.5), yielding a (1 − 1/e) greedy acquisition guarantee; establish P-completeness of emergent capability detection (Theorem 8.3)
and coNP-completeness of minimal unsafe set membership (Theorem 9.5); and unify these
results in the Safe Audit Surface Theorem (Theorem 10.1)—a polynomial-time-computable,
formally certifiable account of every capability an agent can safely acquire from any given
deployment configuration. We extend the framework in five further directions: a coalition safety criterion, incremental dynamic maintenance, PAC-learning sample complexity
(O(n2k /ε) trajectories suffice), probabilistic hypergraphs with expected closure computable
in O(n + mk), and an adversarial robustness result (MinUnsafeAdd is NP-hard; single-edge
check is polynomial; greedy defence achieves (1 − 1/e) via submodularity).
Empirically, 42.6% of real multi-tool trajectories (ToolBench G3 and TaskBench DAG)
contain conjunctive dependencies (95% CI: [39.4%, 45.8%]); the hypergraph planner produces zero AND-violations, as proved; the workflow baseline produces AND-violations on
38.2% of conjunctive instances on real asynchronous traces.
Keywords: AI safety; agentic systems; capability composition; directed hypergraphs; formal verification; PAC learning; submodular optimisation.

Introduction

1.1

The Central Result

Theorem 1.1 (Non-Compositionality of Safety, informal). This paper contains the first formal
proof that safety is non-compositional in the presence of conjunctive capability dependencies.
Specifically, two agents each individually incapable of reaching any forbidden capability can, when
their capabilities are combined, collectively reach a forbidden goal. This is not an artefact of
adversarial design; it is a structural consequence of AND-semantics that is invisible to pairwise
graph models. The result is tight: it requires exactly three capabilities, one conjunctive hyperedge,
and cannot arise in any pairwise graph.
This result—proved formally as Theorem 9.2—settles a concern that has been articulated informally in the AI safety literature [Russell and Norvig, 2021] but never formally proved in
the capability-composition setting. It implies that any system deploying component-level safety
checks as its sole safety guarantee is structurally incomplete, independently of how carefully
individual components are designed.

1.2

Context and Motivation

Modern AI systems are increasingly constructed as modular ecosystems of capabilities rather
than monolithic algorithms: agentic architectures, tool-augmented language models, robotic
control systems, and enterprise automation frameworks all achieve higher-level tasks by composing multiple capabilities. Traditional modelling represents these dependencies using directed
graphs, where each edge encodes a single sufficient enabling relationship. This representation
assumes that dependencies are pairwise.
Many real tasks, however, require the simultaneous availability of multiple prerequisites. The
dependency
(intent understanding, user context, database access) −→ answer generation
cannot be represented in a directed graph without introducing artificial conjunction nodes—
vertices representing logical conjunctions rather than genuine system capabilities. This is not a
modelling inconvenience: it is the precise source of the safety failure. Two agents holding {u1 }
and {u2 } individually appear safe. When combined, the conjunctive hyperedge ({u1 , u2 }, {f })
fires, reaching forbidden goal f . No pairwise graph model can represent—or detect—this.
Directed hypergraphs provide the natural remedy. A hyperedge connects a set of source vertices to a set of target vertices, directly expressing AND-semantics without artificial intermediate nodes. Section 4 develops a concrete running example—booking a trip to Paris—that
grounds the abstract framework; the real empirical validation over 900 trajectories from public
benchmarks is presented in Section 15.

1.4

Related Work

Directed hypergraphs. The foundations used here—reachability, closure, and B-graphs—
were laid by Gallo et al. [1993] and surveyed by Ausiello and Laura [2017]. Our closure operator
is a restriction of their reachability notion to the AND-semantics case; the connection to Horn

4

clause inference is classical [van Emden and Kowalski, 1976] and forms the logical foundation
for Theorem 6.3.
Petri nets. Petri nets [Petri, 1962, Murata, 1989] are a closely related formalism. Our framework differs in two key respects: capabilities are treated as persistent (once derived, always
available) rather than consumed by firing, and the focus is on goal discovery and safety certification rather than concurrency analysis.
HTN and AND/OR planning. Hierarchical Task Network (HTN) planning [Erol et al.,
1994] and AND/OR planning graphs [Bonet and Geffner, 2001] both encode conjunctive preconditions. The key distinction is that these formalisms are designed for heuristic search over
task-decomposition trees, not for formal safety certification. In particular, neither provides
(i) a closure-based characterisation that is both sound and complete without search, (ii) the
non-compositionality theorem, nor (iii) a polynomial-time certifiable audit surface.
Submodular optimisation. Submodular optimisation [Nemhauser et al., 1978, Fujishige,
2005] provides the theoretical basis for our greedy acquisition guarantee. The connection between closure systems and submodularity via the polymatroid rank theorem (Theorem 8.5) is
standard; our contribution is applying this to the capability-acquisition setting and connecting
it to the safety boundary.
The adjacent possible. The concept of the adjacent possible [Kauffman, 2000]—the set of
states one step beyond the current state of a complex system—anticipates our near-miss frontier
informally. We provide a formal algebraic characterisation (Proposition 8.2) and prove that it
coincides exactly with the unit-distance boundary under the acquisition distance metric.
AI safety and compositionality. Concerns about emergent dangerous capabilities in composed AI systems have been raised informally in the AI safety literature [Russell and Norvig,
2021, Yao et al., 2023]. To our knowledge, Theorem 9.2 is the first formal proof of noncompositionality in the capability-composition setting.
Compositional verification and assume-guarantee reasoning. The formal methods
community has studied safety in composed systems for decades under the heading of compositional verification [Clarke et al., 1999, Pnueli, 1977]. The dominant paradigm is assumeguarantee reasoning [Jones, 1983]: a component Ci is verified under an assumption Ai about
its environment. Contract-based design [Benveniste et al., 2018, Sangiovanni-Vincentelli et al.,
2012] formalises this idea. Our setting is complementary but structurally distinct in three
respects. First, assume-guarantee reasoning verifies that a fixed composition satisfies a prespecified global property; we instead characterise the set of all properties a dynamically growing
capability set can ever reach. Second, the non-compositionality result addresses a failure mode
that assume-guarantee reasoning is specifically designed to prevent—yet it cannot prevent it
here, because the failure arises not from a component violating its guarantee but from two
individually correct components producing a conjunctive emergent capability when combined.
Third, the complexity of our safety audit (Theorem 9.5: coNP-complete) aligns with known
lower bounds for contract composition and compatibility checking [de Alfaro and Henzinger,
2001, Bauer et al., 2012].
Interface automata [de Alfaro and Henzinger, 2001] and modal interface theories [Raclet et al.,
2011] provide the closest formal analogy. The key difference is that these theories treat components as reactive systems with input/output alphabets, whereas our capability nodes are propositional facts with no internal dynamics. This restriction—capabilities as persistent atomic facts

rather than stateful processes—is precisely what enables the Horn clause completeness result
(Theorem 6.3) and reduces planning from PSPACE to polynomial time.

Capability-Based System Modelling

2.1

Capabilities

A capability represents a functional ability of a system to perform a computational or informational transformation—for example, speech recognition, intent extraction, database query
execution, reasoning over knowledge graphs, or text generation. Capabilities may correspond
to AI models, software services, tools, API calls, or human-assisted processes.

2.2

Capability Graphs and Their Limitation

Definition 2.1 (Capability Graph). A capability graph is a directed graph G = (V, E) where V
is a finite set of capability nodes and E ⊆ V × V is a set of directed edges. The edge (u, v) ∈ E
encodes: possession of u alone is sufficient to make v available.
The capability closure of a set A ⊆ V in a graph is standard graph reachability: clG (A) = {v ∈
V : ∃u ∈ A, u →∗ v} ∪ A.
Fundamental limitation.
tions. It cannot express:

A capability graph can only encode sufficient singleton precondi-

• Conjunction: v requires both u1 and u2 .
• Joint emergence: u1 and u2 together produce v, but neither alone does.
• Multi-target yield: firing u simultaneously produces v1 and v2 as an inseparable unit.
Any attempt to encode conjunction in a plain graph requires an artificial intermediate node—
say, a node called u1 ∧ u2 —which pollutes V with logical artefacts rather than genuine capabilities. This is not merely an aesthetic concern: as Theorem 9.2 shows, it is the source of a
provable safety failure.

3

Directed Hypergraphs

Definition 3.1 (Directed Hypergraph). A directed hypergraph is a pair H = (V, F ) where V is
a finite set of vertices and F is a set of hyperarcs, each of the form e = (S, T ) with S, T ⊆ V and
S ∩ T = ∅. The set S is the tail (preconditions) and T is the head (effects). The hyperarc fires
when all elements of S are simultaneously present, producing all elements of T simultaneously.
Directed graphs are the special case where |S| = |T | = 1 for every hyperarc.
Definition 3.2 (Capability Hypergraph). A capability hypergraph is a directed hypergraph
H = (V, F) where vertices represent capabilities and each hyperarc (S, T ) ∈ F represents a
composition rule: the capabilities in S jointly enable those in T .

Proof. Let V = {u1 , u2 , v} and H = (V, {({u1 , u2 }, {v})}). Then clH ({u1 }) = {u1 }, clH ({u2 }) =

the third requires a path from {u1 , u2 } to v in G. Contradiction.

Capability Closure and Planning

6.1

The Closure Operator

Definition 6.1 (Closure Operator). Let H = (V, F ) be a capability hypergraph and A ⊆ V .
The closure cl(A) is the smallest set C ⊆ V satisfying:
1. A ⊆ C (extensivity)
2. ∀(S, T ) ∈ F : S ⊆ C ⇒ T ⊆ C (closed under firing)
Computed by the fixed-point iteration C0 = A, Ci+1 = Ci ∪{T | (S, T ) ∈ F, S ⊆ Ci }, terminating
in at most |V | steps.
The operator satisfies extensivity (A ⊆ cl(A)), monotonicity (A ⊆ B ⇒ cl(A) ⊆ cl(B)), and
idempotence (cl(cl(A)) = cl(A)), making (V, cl) a closure system (Moore family) [Ganter and
Wille, 1999].

6.2

Planning as Closure

Definition 6.2 (Plan). A plan from A in H = (V, F) is a finite sequence of hyperedges π =
(e1 , . . . , en ) with ei = (Si , Ti ) ∈ F, and sets A = D0 ⊆ D1 ⊆ · · · ⊆ Dn ⊆ V , satisfying:
(1) Si ⊆ Di−1 (applicability); (2) Di = Di−1 ∪ Ti (effect). A plan π achieves goal G ⊆ V if
G ⊆ Dn .
Theorem 6.3 (Planning as Closure). Let H = (V, F) be a capability hypergraph, A ⊆ V an
initial capability set, and G ⊆ V a goal set. A plan from A achieving G exists if and only if
G ⊆ cl(A).
Proof. (⇒) By induction on plan length n, we show Dn ⊆ cl(A). Base case: D0 = A ⊆ cl(A).
Inductive step: if Di−1 ⊆ cl(A) then Si ⊆ cl(A), so Ti ⊆ cl(A), giving Di ⊆ cl(A).

Remark 6.4 (Isomorphism with Horn clause forward chaining). Theorem 6.3 is the capabilityhypergraph instance of the completeness theorem for definite Horn clause forward chaining [van
Emden and Kowalski, 1976]. Each capability v ∈ V corresponds to a propositional atom; each
hyperarc (S, T ) with S = {s1 , . . . , sk } corresponds to the Horn clause s1 ∧ · · · ∧ sk → t for
each t ∈ T . The closure cl(A) equals the minimal Herbrand model of A ∪ F , which by the van
Emden–Kowalski theorem equals the least fixed point of the immediate consequence operator TP .
The worklist algorithm (Algorithm 1) is an optimised implementation of the TP ↑ ω sequence:
counter arrays reduce the O(n · m · k) naive cost to O(n + mk).

Goal Discovery via Hypergraph Closure

Traditional AI systems are given goals externally. The hypergraph closure framework enables
an agent to discover goals it did not know were possible by observing the topology of its
own capability space: goal discovery is an intrinsic consequence of closure computation, not a
separate process.

8.1

Formal Definitions

Fix H = (V, F) and A ⊆ V . Let C = cl(A). Let cl1 (A) denote the closure under the subhypergraph F1 = {(S, T ) ∈ F : |S| = 1} of singleton-tail hyperedges only; by definition
cl1 (A) ⊆ cl(A).

Safety and Containment

Definition 9.1 (Forbidden Set and Safe Region). Let F ⊆ V be a forbidden set. A capability
set A ⊆ V is F -contained if cl(A) ∩ F = ∅. The safe region is R(F ) = {A ⊆ V : cl(A) ∩ F = ∅}.
Theorem 9.2 (Non-Compositionality of Safety). Let F ⊆ V be a forbidden set. The safe region
R(F ) is not closed under union in general: there exist A, B ∈ R(F ) such that A ∪ B ∈
/ R(F ).
Equivalently: two agents each individually incapable of reaching any forbidden capability may,
when combined, collectively reach a forbidden goal.
This is the first formal proof of this result in the capability-composition setting.

Dynamic Capability Hypergraphs

Definition 11.5 (Dynamic Hypergraph). A dynamic capability hypergraph is a finite sequence
of hypergraphs H0 , H1 , . . . where Ht = (V, Ft ) is obtained from Ht−1 by either a hyperedge
insertion (Ft = Ft−1 ∪ {e}) or deletion (Ft = Ft−1 \ {e}).
Theorem 11.6 (Incremental Closure Maintenance). Let H = (V, F), A ⊆ V , and C = clH (A).
1. Insertion. Let e = (S, T ) be a new hyperedge and H ′ = (V, F ∪ {e}). Then clH ′ (A) =
clH (A ∪ T ′ ) where T ′ = T if S ⊆ C, else T ′ = ∅. The update costs O(n + mk) in the worst
case and O(|S|) when S ̸⊆ C.
2. Deletion. Let e = (S, T ) be a removed hyperedge and H ′ = (V, F \ {e}). Then clH ′ (A) ⊆
clH (A), and the recomputation cost is O(n + mk).
3. Acquisition distance stability. For any g ∈ V , insertion of e = (S, T ) can decrease
δ(g, A) by at most |T |. Deletion can increase δ(g, A) by at most |T |.
Theorem 11.7 (Safety Under Dynamic Updates). Let A ∈ R(F ) and let e = (S, T ) be a
candidate new hyperedge. The post-insertion configuration remains safe—A ∈ RH ′ (F )—if and
only if clH ′ (A) ∩ F = ∅, which by Theorem 11.6 reduces to checking whether clH (A ∪ T ′ ) ∩ F = ∅
where T ′ = T · 1[S ⊆ C]. This check costs O(n + mk). A safety monitor for a dynamic agentic
system need only run a single incremental closure check per tool addition.

Implications for Agentic AI

The hypergraph closure framework provides a mathematical model for modern agentic AI architectures with four key consequences.

14

Goals are self-consistent. Every goal in GF (A) is derivable from A via the hypergraph,
eliminating planning failures where agents pursue structurally unreachable goals.
Goals are transparent and auditable. The derivation certificates provided by the Safe
Audit Surface Theorem (Theorem 10.1) make every goal in Emg(A) and NMFF (A) inspectable
and explainable.
Goals are adaptive. When a tool becomes unavailable or a new API is added, F updates
and cl(A) is recomputed. Goals relying on a now-absent capability are automatically removed
from the reachable set; new goals enabled by the addition appear immediately.
Coalition safety is tractable online. Theorem 11.2 reduces the safety question for any
dynamically forming coalition to a single linear-time query against the precomputed antichain
B(F ).
The deepest implication. In classical AI, the goal is the starting point and capability is the
means. The hypergraph closure inverts this relationship: capability is the starting point, and
goals are what the closure reveals. Understanding what a system of agents can become—not just
what it currently does—requires reasoning at the level of hypergraph structure, not individual
components.

This paper introduced capability hypergraphs as a formal framework for modelling compositional AI systems and developed a formal theory of planning, goal discovery, and safety grounded
in the closure operator.
The central result is the Non-Compositionality of Safety (Theorem 9.2): the first formal proof
that two F -contained agents can together reach a forbidden goal via an emergent conjunctive hyperedge that neither possesses alone. This result is tight, minimal, and has direct consequences
for the design and auditing of modular agentic systems.
The primary deliverable is the Safe Audit Surface Theorem (Theorem 10.1): a polynomial-timecomputable, formally certifiable account of every capability an agent can safely acquire from
any deployment configuration, what it is one step from acquiring, and what it can never safely
acquire.
The complexity landscape is fully characterised: planning is polynomial via Theorem 6.3; emergent capability detection is P-complete (Theorem 8.3); and minimal unsafe set membership is
coNP-complete (Theorem 9.5).
Four structural extensions broaden the framework beyond single-agent static systems. The coalition safety criterion (Theorem 11.2) characterises safety for any n-agent system via a linear-time
query against the precomputed minimal unsafe antichain. The incremental closure theorems
(Theorems 11.6 and 11.7) reduce per-tool-addition safety monitoring to a single O(n + mk)
check. The PAC-learning theorem (Theorem 14.2) establishes that the hyperedge structure
itself is learnable from trajectory data. The probabilistic extension (Theorem 14.5) shows that
non-compositionality persists under stochastic firing, and the adversarial robustness theorem
(Theorem 14.7) characterises the complexity of injecting unsafe edges.
Empirically, 42.6% of real multi-tool agent trajectories contain conjunctive dependencies that
pairwise graph models cannot faithfully represent. The hypergraph planner produces zero ANDviolations across all conditions, as proved by Theorem 6.3.
As AI systems become increasingly modular and compositional, the hypergraph closure framework provides a foundational structure for orchestrating complex agentic behaviour: one in
