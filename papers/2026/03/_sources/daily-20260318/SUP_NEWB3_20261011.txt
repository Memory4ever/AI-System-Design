Prior systems have significantly enhanced this approach for DNN workloads by mitigating context-switching overheads through state reuse (Salus [cite139†67 ]), dynamically managing containerized or runtime memory footprints (Antman [cite80†15 ], TGS [cite78†13 ], Zico [cite116†51 ]), and employing predictive scheduling to bound inference tail latency (Clockwork [cite132†64 ], Gandiva [cite79†14 ]).\nL419: However, these solutions inherently operate at the granularity of the entire GPU. Consequently, they enforce strict execution serialization and fail to overlap computation. This limitation prevents them from reclaiming idle capacity during memory-bound phases or bursty workloads, leaving significant hardware resources underutilized.\nL420: ##### Spatial Sharing.\nL421: Spatial sharing partitions hardware to enable concurrent execution. Foundational mechanisms include intra-context CUDA Streams [cite157†77 ] and NVIDIA’s static partitioning tools, MPS and MIG. Systems such as GSlice [cite84†19 ], GPUlets [cite85†20 ], and COLTI [cite158†78 ] build upon these primitives to construct higher-level spatio-temporal abstractions.\nL422: Yet, because their underlying substrates rely on rigid, static partitioning, they cannot elastically adapt to the highly dynamic resource demands characteristic of modern workloads.\nL423: Recent literature pushes for even finer-grained multiplexing. POD-Attention [cite75†10 ] rewrites attention kernels to collocate prefill and decode phases, while LithOS [cite89†24 ] atomizes kernels to pack execution fragments at the Texture Processing Cluster (TPC) level. While effective for utilization, these invasive techniques necessitate user-kernel modifications, risking behavioral inequivalence and complicating offline-to-online correctness guarantees.\nL424: In contrast, DetShare avoids this semantic trap: by decoupling logical contexts from physical hardware, it dynamically remaps quotas at runtime to achieve elastic, fine-grained spatial multiplexing while strictly preserving execution determinism.\nL425: ##### LLM Inference Scheduling.\nL426: The unique resource asymmetries of LLM inference have spurred specialized scheduling techniques. Recent work manages the prefill-decode imbalance via stall-free chunking (Sarathi-Serve [cite112†47 ]) and fairness-aware phase balancing (FairBatching [cite159†79 ]).\nL427: Other efforts explore temporal scheduling through preemptible context migrations (Llumnix [cite160†80 ]) and output-length-guided token-level preemption (TRAIL [cite161†81 ]), or focus on QoS mitigation via adapter-aware caching (Chameleon [cite162†82 ]) and real-time latency attribution (LLMVisor [cite163†83 ], Niyama [cite164†84 ]). While these systems optimize application-level scheduling, DetShare provides a transparent, infrastructure-level foundation.\nL428: Its extensible policy framework allows operators to plug in diverse algorithms while enforcing execution through strict, hardware-level isolation primitives.\nL429: ##### GPU Memory Management and Disaggregation.\nL430: Frameworks like AvA [cite165†85 ], rCUDA [cite81†16 ], and DCUDA [cite166†86 ] leverage API remoting for disaggregated execution and flexible GPU kernel placement. Simultaneously, memory-centric systems like DeepUM [cite167†87 ], Zico [cite116†51 ], and TGS [cite85†20 ] optimize device utilization by reducing allocation overheads and shrinking active working-set footprints.\nL431: These memory optimization and disaggregation techniques are entirely orthogonal and complementary to DetShare, presenting natural avenues for future integration to further maximize efficiency in shared environments.\nL432: ## 8 Conclusion\nL433: In this paper, we identify and resolve the fundamental challenges of execution determinism, performance predictability, and practical extensibility in modern GPU sharing. The assignment task is captured by the graph $\\mathcal{G}=(V=X\\cup Z,E)$, where $X$ is the set of nodes representing the data points and $Z$ is the set of nodes representing the noise samples.\nL416: The set of edges $E=X\\times E$, where the cost of an edge $(x_{i},z_{j})\\in E$ is given by the $c_{ij}=\\|x_{i}-z_{j}\\|_{2}$, defines the complete bipartite graph (see Figure cite70†2 for a visual representation).\nL417: A perfect matching $M$ is a set of $n$ edges in $\\mathcal{G}$ that cover all the vertices. The cost of a matching is the sum of the costs of the edges. An optimal matching is one achieving the minimal cost. The optimality of a matching $M$ is entirely captured by the absence of negative $M$-alternating cycles.\nL418: ###### Definition B.1 (M-alternating cycles).\nL419: \nL420: Given a perfect matching $M$, any cycle in $\\mathcal{G}$ given by a sequence of edges $C=(x_{i_{1}},z_{i_{1}})(z_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})(x_{i_{2}},z_{i_{2}})\\dots(x_{i_{k}},z_{i_{k}})(z_{i_{k}},x_{i_{1}})$ where for any $p\\in[k]$, we have $(x_{i_{p}},z_{i_{p}})\\in M$ is called an alternating $M$-cycle.\nL421: $M$-alternating cycles as their name suggests, pass from $X$ to $Z$ back and forth while passing through edges in $M$ to go from $X$ to $Z$. An $M$-alternating cycle is said to be negative if the sum of the costs of the edges in $M$ is larger than the cost of the edges in absent from $M$:\nL422: \nL423:  | $$\\sum_{e\\in C\\setminus M}c_{e}<\\sum_{e\\in C\\cap M}c_{e}.$$  |  | (34)\nL424: Consequently, for any matching $M$, if a negative $M$-alternating cycle $C$ exists, then matching $M^{\\prime}$ with lower cost can be constructed by taking $M^{\\prime}=(C\\setminus M)\\cup(M\\setminus C)$. In other words, by swapping the going edges in $C$(from $X$ to $Z$) with the return edges (from $Z$ to $X$), we can diminish the cost thanks to Equation cite170†34 . A necessary condition for optimality is thus the absence of negative $M$-alternating cycles.\nL425: The following theorem shows that it is in fact a sufficient condition.\nL426: ###### Proposition B.1 (Thm 2.2 (cite171†Roughgarden, 2016 )).\nL427: \nL428: A matching $M$ is optimal if and only if there are no negative $M$-alternating cycles.\nL429: Given the exposition above, LOOM-CFM can be understood as an iterative negative-cycle elimination scheme. Score-adaptive truncation ($\\alpha{=}0.03$, budget ceiling 4K, top-$K{=}60$) provides a mechanism for deploying a single universal configuration without per-dataset budget tuning.\nL328: The adaptive threshold automatically allocates fewer tokens on LoCoMo (${\\sim}$9K-token conversations) and more on LongMemEval-S (${\\sim}$115K-token conversations), tracking corpus difficulty rather than corpus length (Section cite25†4.3 ). This contrasts with fixed-budget approaches that force a precision–coverage tradeoff between short and long conversations.\nL329: ### 5.2 The Compilation Bottleneck Generalizes Across Scales\nL330: On LoCoMo, oracle analysis shows 98.6% retrieval recall with only 22.5% of gold evidence surviving truncation to the token budget. On LongMemEval-S, the same pattern holds: the indexed and index-free pipelines differ by only 0.023 in budget recall, but gold density differs 2$\\times$. In both cases, the evidence is found; the challenge is surfacing the right passages through a finite token budget.\nL331: This explains why reranker quality (+6.0 pp) and rank fusion (+1.2 pp) are the highest-leverage interventions (Table cite87†4 ), while tool selection and index sophistication yield diminishing returns.\nL332: ### 5.3 Query Expansion Compensates for Index-Free Retrieval at Scale\nL333: On short conversations, the index-free variant loses only 0.9 pp to the indexed pipeline (91.0% vs. 91.9% on LoCoMo), because the original query terms already cover most relevant passages. On long conversations the gap remains small: index-free reaches 88.4% vs. 87.6% indexed on LongMemEval-S (Table cite79†7 ), with both variants using the same expansion mechanisms.\nL334: The ablation study (Table cite86†3 ) confirms that the combined expansion effect (+9.2 pp) is super-additive over the individual mechanisms (+6.0 pp and +1.4 pp), with the largest gains on multi-session questions that require evidence from sessions the original query terms do not reach.\nL335: ### 5.4 Open-Ended Queries Expose a Weakness in Structured Memory\nL336: \nL337: SmartSearch scores 95.4–96.7% on open-ended questions, exceeding all competitors by $>$20 pp (EverMemOS 70.8%, Memora 59.4%). Memory consolidation distills conversation into atomic facts or abstract summaries, discarding conversational texture—tone, hedging, tangential remarks—that open-ended questions probe. SmartSearch retrieves raw passages, preserving this context at no additional engineering cost.\nL338: ### 5.5 Temporal Reasoning Remains the Main Gap\nL339: SmartSearch trails the best competitor on temporal questions by ${\\sim}$10 pp on LoCoMo (80.2% vs. EverMemOS 89.7%) and ${\\sim}$7 pp on LongMemEval-S (82.7% vs. Memora 89.5%). These systems explicitly preserve temporal metadata through LLM-structured memory. Failure analysis (Appendix cite46†D ) attributes the LoCoMo gap predominantly to LLM inference failures—the gold evidence is present, but the answer LLM fails to synthesize correct temporal orderings.\nL340: On LongMemEval-S, expansion partially closes the gap: the baseline scores 67.7% on temporal questions, with expansion lifting it to 80.5% by surfacing passages from different sessions that establish chronology.\nL341: ## 6 Future Work\nL342: \nL343: The compilation bottleneck (Section cite15†3.2 ) suggests that the largest remaining gains lie not in retrieval but in how retrieved passages are filtered and compressed before the answer LLM sees them.\nL344: Passage deduplication. Retrieved passages frequently overlap when multiple search terms match the same conversation region. Deduplicating or merging near-identical passages before ranking would reduce redundancy in the context window, allowing the token budget to cover more distinct evidence. The cross-encoder and ColBERT embeddings already computed during ranking could serve as similarity features for deduplication at negligible additional cost.\nL345: Context compression. Token-level compression—removing low-importance words within passages, stripping image descriptions, abbreviating speaker names and timestamps—can reduce token cost without losing gold passages. Preliminary experiments show 10% reduction at zero gold loss from format-level changes alone. More aggressive query-conditioned pruning (retaining only words relevant to the specific question) remains unexplored.\n"
