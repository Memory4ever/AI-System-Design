L669@P9: • Validation loss: AttnRes achieves consistently lower validation loss throughout training, with the gap widening
L670@P9: during the decay phase and resulting in a notably lower final loss.
L671@P9: • Output magnitude: The Baseline suffers from the PreNorm dilution problem [60, 27]: as hidden-state magnitudes
L672@P9: grow monotonically with depth, deeper layers are compelled to learn increasingly large outputs from fixed-scale
L673@P9: normalized inputs to remain influential. Block AttnRes confines this growth within each block, as selective aggregation
L674@P9: at block boundaries resets the accumulation, yielding a bounded periodic pattern.
L675@P9: • Gradient magnitude: With all residual weights fixed to 1, the Baseline provides no means of regulating gradient
L676@P9: flow across depth, leading to disproportionately large gradients in the earliest layers. The learnable softmax weights
L677@P9: in Block AttnRes (Fig. 8) introduce competition among sources for probability mass, resulting in a substantially more
L678@P9: uniform gradient distribution.

L748@P10: 5.3 Ablation Study
L749@P10: We conduct ablation studies on the 16-head model from Table 2 to validate key design choices in AttnRes (Table 4). All
L750@P10: models share identical hyperparameters and compute budget.
L751@P10: Comparison with prior methods. We compare AttnRes against the PreNorm baseline (loss 1.766) and two representative methods that generalize residual connections. DenseFormer [36] grants each layer access to all previous
L752@P10: outputs but combines them with fixed, input-independent scalar coefficients; it shows no gain over the baseline (1.767),
L753@P10: highlighting the importance of input-dependent weighting. mHC [59] introduces input dependence through m parallel
L754@P10: streams with learned mixing matrices, improving to 1.747. AttnRes takes this further with explicit content-dependent
L755@P10: selection via softmax attention: Full AttnRes achieves 1.737 and Block AttnRes 1.746, outperforming both methods
L756@P10: with only a single query vector per layer.
L757@P10: Cross-layer access. We compare three granularities of cross-layer access. Full AttnRes follows directly from the
L758@P10: time–depth duality (§ 3), applying attention over all previous layers, and achieves the lowest loss (1.737). A simple
L759@P10: way to reduce its memory cost is sliding-window aggregation (SWA), which retains only the most recent W=8 layer
L760@P10: outputs plus the token embedding; it improves over baseline (1.764) but falls well short of both Full and Block AttnRes,
L761@P10: suggesting that selectively accessing distant layers matters more than attending to many nearby ones.
L762@P10: Block AttnRes offers a better trade-off: with block size S=4 it reaches 1.746 while keeping memory overhead constant
L763@P10: per layer. Fig. 6 sweeps S across the full spectrum from S=1 (i.e. Full AttnRes) to increasingly coarse groupings. Loss
L764@P10: degrades gracefully as S grows, with S=2, 4, 8 all landing near 1.746 while larger blocks (S=16, 32) move toward
L765@P10: baseline. In practice, we fix the number of blocks to ≈ 8 for infrastructure efficiency (§ 4). As future hardware alleviates
L766@P10: memory capacity constraints, adopting finer-grained block sizes or Full AttnRes represents a natural pathway to further
L767@P10: improve performance.

L798@P11: Component design. We further ablate individual components of the attention mechanism:
L799@P11: • Input-dependent query. A natural extension is to make the query input-dependent by projecting it from the current
L800@P11: hidden state. This further lowers loss to 1.731, but introduces a d × d projection per layer and requires sequential
L801@P11: memory access during decoding, so we default to the learned query.
L802@P11: • Input-independent mixing. We removed the query and key and replaced them with learnable, input-independent
L803@P11: scalars to weigh previous layers, which hurts performance (1.749 vs. 1.737).
L804@P11: • softmax vs. sigmoid. Replacing softmax with sigmoid degrades performance (1.741). We attribute this to softmax’s
L805@P11: competitive normalization, which forces sharper selection among sources.
L806@P11: • Multihead attention. We test per-head depth aggregation (H=16) on Block AttnRes, allowing different channel
L807@P11: groups to attend to different source layers. This hurts performance (1.752 vs. 1.746), indicating that the optimal
L808@P11: depth-wise mixture is largely uniform across channels: when a layer’s output is relevant, it is relevant as a whole.
L809@P11: • RMSNorm on keys. Removing RMSNorm degrades both Full AttnRes (1.743) and Block AttnRes (1.750). For
L810@P11: Full AttnRes, it prevents individual layers with naturally larger outputs from dominating the softmax. This becomes
L811@P11: even more critical for Block AttnRes, as block-level representations accumulate over more layers and can develop
L812@P11: large magnitude differences; RMSNorm prevents these from biasing the attention weights.

L814@P11: 5.4.1 Optimal Architecture
L815@P11: To understand how AttnRes reshapes optimal architectural scaling, we perform a controlled capacity reallocation
L816@P11: study under a fixed compute and parameter budget. Our central question is whether AttnRes alters the preferred
L817@P11: depth–width–attention trade-off, and in particular, given its potential strength on the depth dimension, whether it favors
L818@P11: deeper models compared to conventional Transformer design heuristics. To isolate structural factors directly coupled
L819@P11: to depth, we fix the per-expert MLP expansion ratio based on internal empirical observations (dff/dmodel ≈ 0.45).
L820@P11: We further fix total training compute (FLOPs ≈ 6.5 × 1019) and active parameters (≈ 2.3 × 108), ensuring that any
L821@P11: performance variation arises purely from architectural reallocation rather than overall capacity differences. Under
L822@P11: this constrained budget, we enumerate 25 configurations on a 5 × 5 grid over dmodel/Lb ∈ {15, 30, 45, 60, 75} and
L823@P11: H/Lb ∈ {0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7}, where Lb = L/2 is the number of Transformer blocks and H the number of attention
L824@P11: heads. The results are shown in Fig. 7.

L412: ### B.1 Experiments on LLaDA
L413: Table cite102†6 presents the comparative results on HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, and MATH500 using the LLaDA-Instruct-8B model with a fixed block size of 32. DOS consistently demonstrates superior generation quality across both Top-1 and parallel sampling settings, proving effective regardless of whether block partitioning is applied. Specifically, in the Top-1 regime, DOS achieves the highest accuracy on all four benchmarks, significantly outperforming standard scoring methods such as Confidence and Entropy.
L414: Furthermore, when integrated with the EB-sampler for parallel decoding, DOS maintains this performance advantage, validating the robustness of our scoring strategy in guiding the model toward high-quality outputs.
L415: In terms of computational efficiency, Fast-dLLM exhibits a substantial reduction in the number of function evaluations (NFE), achieving the lowest inference cost across tasks. This efficiency is largely attributed to its threshold-based mechanism. However, this speed comes with a slight trade-off in accuracy compared to our method. Consequently, a promising avenue for future work is to synergize the precise scoring capability of DOS with dynamic threshold-based strategies similar to Fast-dLLM.
L416: Such an integration could potentially yield a more optimal generation process, maintaining the high accuracy of DOS while significantly accelerating inference.

L435: ### B.2 Experiments on Dream
L436: Table cite103†7 presents the performance of the Dream-v0-Instruct-7B model across four datasets using both Top-1 and parallel sampling strategies with a fixed block size of 32. Consistent with our previous observations, the proposed DOS method demonstrates superior performance on the majority of tasks with and without block partitioning, particularly excelling in code generation and complex mathematical reasoning.
L437: In the Top-1 setting, DOS (w/ block) achieves the highest accuracy on HumanEval and MATH500, significantly outperforming standard baselines like Confidence and Entropy. This advantage extends to the parallel sampling setting, where DOS combined with the EB-sampler attains 45.40% accuracy on MATH500, surpassing robust competitors such as KLASS and Fast-dLLM.
L438: While DOS remains highly competitive overall, we observe a slight performance gap on the GSM8K benchmark compared to KLASS and uncertainty-based methods, suggesting that our scoring mechanism could be further refined for specific arithmetic reasoning patterns.
L439: Furthermore, we note that Fast-dLLM achieves the lowest NFE, demonstrating impressive inference efficiency, while KLASS shows strong adaptability on MBPP; these baselines offer valuable insights into efficiency optimization and task-specific selection, pointing toward promising directions for further enhancing the DOS framework in future work.
