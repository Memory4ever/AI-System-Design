L216: The main term $\left(x\cdot\text{QPS}\right)/|M|\cdot t$ corresponds to the number of requests of class $c$ routed to the instances that cache its prefix, while $\left(\bar{x}\cdot\text{QPS}\right)/|\overline{M}|\cdot t$ is the routing of all other requests to the remaining instances.
L217: Analysis: the benign cases, and are they common?   As long as the batch size of the suspected hotspot instances is not larger than the others, we are sure that it is beneficial to route requests to these instances, because such a routing decision can improve KV$ hit rates without causing load imbalance. Since our multiplication faithfully does so, these are benign cases for our method. The remaining question is whether such cases are common in practice.

L227: Analysis: the failure cases, and our two-phase detector.   Based on our previous analysis, we can see that if Equation cite98†2 does not hold, i.e.,
L228: 
L229:  | $$\frac{x}{\bar{x}}>\frac{|M|}{|\overline{M}|}$$  |
L230: , then there is a potential for KV$ hotspots to cause load imbalance that may break our multiplication method. This gives a clear indication of the failure cases detector based purely on the workload pattern and KV$ states, so we can develop a detector before we make a scheduling decision to catch potential failure cases.
L231: Specifically, for each request class, we profile the two terms $\frac{x}{\bar{x}}$ and $\frac{|M|}{|\overline{M}|}$ in real time, and if we observe that Equation cite98†2 does not hold, we raise an alarm and filter out the suspected instances ($M$) from the routing targets. To avoid the overhead of tracking too many request classes, we only track the requests with the highest KV$ hit rates.
L232: 
L233:  | $$\text{P-token}_{t}\times BS_{t}\leq\text{P-token}_{\bar{t}}\times BS_{\bar{t}}$$  |  | (4)
L234: One problem of the one-phase detector described above is that even if Equation cite98†2 does not hold, it may still be beneficial to route requests to the suspected instances with high KV$ hit rates, as long as we do not route too many requests to the hotspots.
L235: Therefore, we further augment the detector with a second phase by delaying the filtering until a consecutive number of requests are routed to hotspots, as consecutive routing decisions may imply that the increase in the batch size of the suspected instances cannot offset the high KV$ hit rates.
L236: Specifically, after the first phase raises an alarm, we further track the new prefill tokens of the requests in this class, and if we observe that more than $2\times M$ consecutive requests in the class have a smaller multiplicative score (Equation cite100†4 ) on a suspected hotspot instance ($t$) than on the rest of the instances ($\bar{t}$), then we raise a second alarm that filters out the suspected instances from the routing targets.

L336: ### A.2 Baseline Methods
L337: 
L338: This subsection provides implementation details of the key baseline methods used in our experiments, including uncertainty-based methods and accelerated samplers. Denote the discrete distribution induced by the MDLM $f_{\theta}$ at time $t$ with input $x_{t}$ for position $i$ as $p_{t}^{i}=f^{i}_{\theta}(x_{t})$.
L339: #### Confidence
L340: 
L341: (cite64†Chang et al., 2022 ; cite59†Nie et al., 2025 ) For MDLM $f_{\theta}$ at time $t$ and position $i$, Confidence selects tokens based on the maximum value of the discrete distribution $p_{t}^{i}$ over the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, defined as
L342: 
L343:  | $$\textit{conf}_{t}^{i}=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}^{i}(v).$$  |
L344: #### Entropy
L345: 
L346: (cite65†Koh et al., 2024 ) For MDLM $f_{\theta}$ at time $t$ and position $i$, Entropy measures the uncertainty of the discrete distribution $p_{t}^{i}$ over the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, defined as
L347: 
L348:  | $$\textit{ent}_{t}^{i}=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}^{i}(v)\log p_{t}^{i}(v).$$  |
L349: #### Margin confidence
L350: 
L351: (cite66†Kim et al., 2025a ) For MDLM $f_{\theta}$ at time $t$ and position $i$, Margin confidence considers the difference between the highest and the second-highest probabilities in the discrete distribution $p_{t}^{i}$,, defined as
L352: 
L353:  | $$\textit{margin}_{t}^{i}=p_{t}^{i}(v^{*})-\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v^{*}\}}p_{t}^{i}(v),$$  |
L354: 
L355: where $v^{*}=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}^{i}(v)$.
L356: #### Fast-dLLM
L357: 
L358: Fast-dLLM performs confidence-based parallel decoding by unmasking all positions whose confidence exceeds a confidence threshold $\epsilon$. Concretely, at each step $t$, let $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ denote the set of masked positions. It computes the confidence score $\text{conf}_{t}^{i}$ and masks the subset of positions
L359: 
L360:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}$  | $\displaystyle=\left\{i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}\,\middle|\,\text{conf}_{t}^{i}=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}^{i}(v)>\epsilon\right\}.$  |
L361: Compared to fixed-budget top-$K$ unmasking, this approach yields a dynamic number of updates per iteration and can be integrated with semi-autoregressive block decoding for enhanced efficiency. Furthermore, while Fast-dLLM supports block-wise KV cache to eliminate redundant computations of key and value vectors across previous blocks, our evaluation focuses on decoding strategy rather than hardware-specific wall-clock speed; consequently, we do not utilize block-wise KV cache in our experiments.
L362: #### KLASS.
L363: 
L364: KLASS performs stable-token selection by identifying positions where the predictive distribution is stable across steps and reaches high confidence. At each step $t$, let $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ denote the set of masked positions. KLASS selects a subset of stable tokens $\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{t}$ to be unmasked based on a history length $n$, a KL-divergence threshold $\epsilon_{\text{KL}}$, and a confidence threshold $\tau$:
L365:  | $\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}=$  | $\displaystyle\Big\{i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}\;\Big|\;\forall k\in\{1,\dots,n\},$  |
L366:  |  | $\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(p_{t-k}^{i}\,\|\,p_{t-k+1}^{i})<\epsilon_{\text{KL}}\wedge\;\text{conf}_{t}^{i}>\tau\Big\},$  |
L367: where $D_{\text{KL}}$ denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the predictive distributions of consecutive steps at position $i$, and $\text{conf}_{t}^{i}=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}^{i}(v)$ is the confidence score. By requiring predictions to remain consistent over $n$ steps, KLASS ensures that only stable tokens are updated in parallel, thereby improving generation reliability.
L368: #### EB-Sampler.
L369: 
L370: The entropy-bounded sampler accelerates generation by unmasking a number of tokens per step that satisfy the entropy constraint. Concretely, at each step $t$, it first ranks all masked positions in $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ based on a specific score like confidence conf or our proposed dependency dep. Then, starting from the highest-ranked token, it selects the largest subset $\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{t}$ that satisfies the cumulative entropy constraint:
L371:  | $$\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\text{ent}_{t}^{i}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\text{ent}_{t}^{j}\leq\gamma,$$  |
L372: 
L373: where $\text{ent}_{t}^{i}=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{t}^{i}(v)\log p_{t}^{i}(v)$ denotes the entropy of the predictive distribution at position $i$.
