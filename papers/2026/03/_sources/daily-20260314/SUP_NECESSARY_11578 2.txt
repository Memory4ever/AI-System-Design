Abstract
Simultaneous machine translation (SiMT) has traditionally relied on offline machine translation models coupled with human-engineered heuristics or learned policies. We propose Hikari, a policy-free, fully end-to-end model that performs simultaneous speech-to-text translation and streaming transcription by encoding READ/WRITE decisions into a probabilistic WAIT token mechanism. We also introduce Decoder Time Dilation, a mechanism that reduces autoregressive overhead and ensures a balanced training distribution. Additionally, we present a supervised fine-tuning strategy that trains the model to recover from delays, significantly improving the quality-latency trade-off. Evaluated on English-to-Japanese, German, and Russian, Hikari achieves new state-of-the-art BLEU scores in both low- and high-latency regimes, outperforming recent baselines.

streaming, machine translation ICML

Introduction
Simultaneous translation has emerged to meet the need for uninterrupted, almost real-time translation. A good simultaneous translation must not only be accurate and complete, but also not lag unacceptably far behind the original message. This requires simultaneous translators (or more commonly called interpreters) to strategically decide whether to translate what has been said so far – often making assumptions about the speaker’s intended message – or delay translation until more information is revealed by later context
(
Ilyukhin, 2001
;
Chernov, 2004
;
Setton, 2005
;
Amos et al., 2022
)
.

This decision-making is especially challenging for syntactically divergent language pairs such as English and Japanese. English provides the principal subject and verb early (SVO), whereas Japanese, a head-final (SOV) language, requires these elements – along with complex relative clauses – to be restructured. As illustrated in Fig.
1
(top), when translating the sentence “I met the man who was wearing a red hat and carrying a blue suitcase,” the translator (human or machine) must remember/buffer the core components (the subject and the main verb “met”) for several seconds while it processes the long English descriptive clause. The Japanese output cannot be safely committed until the entire qualifying phrase (“red hat wearing, blue suitcase carrying”) has been heard, resulting in an inherent temporal lag.
The machine translation research community has attempted to model this decision-making process either with learned READ/WRITE
1
1
1
where “READ” and “WRITE” refer to ingesting more input audio and committing partial translation, respectively.
policies or human-crafted heuristics, designed to break input audio (or text in case of a cascaded system) along some sensible semantic or syntactic boundaries, and feed each chunk into another, usually pre-trained, machine translation (MT) model.
In a departure from those approaches, our proposed model, which we call
Hikari
, follows a policy-free approach, where READ and WRITE actions are encoded in the probability distribution of the next token. If the model is uncertain, the probability of a text token drops, and the probability of a special
WAIT
token rises. This unifies the “what” (translation) and the “when” (policy) into a single probabilistic framework. Thus, Hikari jointly learns an audio-conditioned language model and an implicit READ/WRITE policy.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We propose a unified speech-to-text framework that jointly learns translation and transcription without external READ/WRITE policies.
•
We introduce Decoder Time Dilation, a mechanism that addresses the dominance of
WAIT
tokens in training data by increasing the decoder’s effective step size relative to the audio.
•
We develop a fine-tuning method that trains the model to actively minimize lag and recover from latency drift during inference.
•
Our model establishes new SOTA in English-to-Japanese, German, and Russian simultaneous translation of long-form speech, outperforming current baselines in both low- and high-latency scenarios.
•
We provide open access to our model weights and codebase to foster reproducibility and research in streaming sequence modeling.

2.4
Policy-Free Paradigm
In a notable departure from these modular designs, later works
(
Koshkin et al., 2024

Hikari follows this design philosophy by building on the “causal alignment” technique and extending it to the speech-to-text domain to build a policy-free, fully end-to-end SiMT architecture. As illustrated in Figure

3.1
Architecture
Our architecture (Fig.
2
) is based on the Whisper encoder-decoder Transformer
Radford et al. (2023)
. While the original Whisper model is designed for
offline
processing – requiring the entire 30 s audio window to be encoded
before

WAIT

WAIT
represents a READ action, while the emission of any text token represents a WRITE action. The decoder therefore generates a sequence of READ/WRITE actions that are
temporally synchronized
with the encoder. Second, to support incremental audio ingestion, we apply a standard lower-triangular causal mask to all encoder self-attention blocks. Third, to enforce strict temporal monotonicity and prevent the decoder from attending to the encoder’s future keys during training, we apply a dilated causal mask to the cross-attention.
Decoder time dilation.
As in the original Whisper model, the input waveform (mono, sampled at 16000 Hz, with a maximum duration of 30 s) is transformed into a log-mel spectrogram with 80-dimensional feature vectors, each capturing 10 ms of input audio. The spectrogram is temporally downsampled by a factor of 2 by the convolutional layers to 1500 audio embeddings which are fed into the encoder.

3.4
Inference
At inference we use a fixed-size sliding window. For the audio encoder, we process input in 80 ms (
20
​
ms
×
D
20\text{ ms}\times D
) increments. At each step, the new audio chunk is appended to the buffer; if the total duration is less than 30 s, the input is padded to 30 s. Once the buffer exceeds 30 s, we maintain a rolling window by discarding the oldest audio chunk before appending the new one, ensuring the encoder always receives exactly 30 s of context.
A similar sliding window is applied to the decoder. We use greedy decoding. Generation begins with 4 prompt tokens. As new tokens are generated, they are appended to the context until the sequence reaches a maximum length of 375 tokens. Beyond this limit, for every new token generated, the earliest non-prompt token (i.e., the fifth token in the sequence) is removed. This preserves the initial prompt while keeping the decoder’s context window bounded.

4.2
Quality-latency tradeoff
Figure 6:
Quality-latency tradeoff. Note how SFT shifts the Pareto frontier towards smaller latencies. Expected latencies are obtained by forcing the model to output the reference (“oracle”) tokens.
In simultaneous translation, there is an intrinsic tradeoff between quality and latency
(
Bangalore et al., 2012
)
: delaying output allows the model to capture more context and improve accuracy, while emitting tokens early reduces latency at the cost of potential errors.
Although Hikari does not rely on explicit policies (e.g., wait-k) to control this behavior, we can probe its latency response through inference-time logit biasing. By applying varying penalties to the
WAIT
token, we can modulate the model’s threshold for emitting text, forcing it to generate translation earlier or allowing it to buffer more audio. As evidenced by the shift in the quality-latency curves (Fig.
6
), the SFT model achieves an overall better trade-off profile, maintaining comparable BLEU scores with latencies generally lower than the base model.

The stark contrast between performance on clean and challenging out-of-distribution data suggests that the architecture itself is sound, and the remaining gap is a data scaling issue rather than a fundamental limitation of the policy-free approach.

Ablations and other experiments
5.1
Effect of SFT
We observe that the base model exhibits systematic latency drift, consistently exceeding the theoretical latency floor (indicated by dashed vertical lines in Figure
6
). This behavior typically arises when the model generates extended sequences of
WAIT
tokens during periods of silence or ambiguity. These unnaturally long pauses push the decoder into an out-of-distribution state, frequently causing it to become “stuck” in a generation loop or fail to resynchronize with the source audio once the input resumes.
While penalizing the
WAIT
token logits at inference can force lower latency, this naive approach often degrades translation quality by truncating necessary context. In contrast, our SFT strategy (Section
3.3.2
) explicitly trains the model to recover from induced delays. As illustrated in Figure
6
, SFT effectively shifts the quality-latency Pareto frontier to the left (compare thick vs. thin curves), enabling the model to “catch up” to the source with little or no loss of quality. Table
5
quantifies this impact, showing that SFT yields an approximate 1-second reduction in StreamLAAL across most tasks while maintaining BLEU parity with the base model.
Figure
1
provides a real example of how Hikari efficiently recovers from delays. Note how the final burst (from 9.2s onwards) completes a sequence of 10+ Japanese tokens in under a second. This demonstrates that the SFT “catch-up” training enables the model to reduce accumulated lag without sacrificing the syntactic integrity of the target language.
5.2
Effect of model size
To evaluate the impact of parameter scale, we trained Hikari-small, initialized from Whisper-small. As summarized in Table
6
(see Appendix
C
), the smaller variant proved significantly less robust than the medium model, suffering from both lower peak BLEU scores and poor latency control. This suggests that the ”policy-free” paradigm benefits from larger model capacity to simultaneously manage translation fidelity and causal alignment.
Additionally, we experimented with a variant using a frozen Mimi
(
Défossez et al., 2024
)
speech encoder (
∼
\sim
39M parameters). Preliminary results indicate a notable performance gap compared to our medium-sized model, likely due to the reduced capacity and frozen state of the encoder. Detailed metrics and a comparative analysis of these architectures are provided in Appendix
C
.
5.3
Multitask learning and encoder grounding
We investigated the impact of the auxiliary ASR task by training a variant of Hikari-medium exclusively on translation objectives. As shown in Table
8
(Appendix
C
), removing ASR supervision led to a significant degradation in both BLEU scores and latency across all language pairs. These results suggest that ASR provides essential acoustic grounding for the causal encoder, preventing representation drift and facilitating the precise, low-latency alignment needed for simultaneous translation. Further details and the full ablation table are provided in Appendix
C
.
6
Conclusions and Future Work
There are a few ways in which Hikari could be improved or extended. First, our ablations indicate a significant performance gap between the small and medium models. Future research could explore scaling Hikari to larger architectures (to the scale of Whisper-large and beyond) to push the limits of translation quality. Thankfully, given its current RTF, this size increase will still allow Hikari to work faster than real time even without any performance optimization tricks. Second, Hikari currently utilizes a fixed dilation factor (
D
=
4
D=4
). While it already provides a good balance between alignment precision and token density, a dynamic dilation mechanism that adjusts the generation rate based on real-time speech density could further improve the quality-latency tradeoff. Third, besides logit manipulation of the
WAIT
token at inference, there are other, potentially better, ways to control latency – e.g. through speculative decoding, or controlling the latency regime via a special token during generation. Fourth, the current Hikari model could be used as the backbone in a streaming speech-to-speech translation model (e.g. via continued training with an RQ-transformer
(
Lee et al., 2022
)
, similarly to
Zeghidour et al. (2025)
. Finally, our training set was dominated by podcasts, and the inclusion of more challenging/noisy data, covering a wider range of domains, could further boost Hikari’s streaming ASR and translation performance.
Impact Statement
This paper advances the field of Machine Learning by introducing a policy-free, end-to-end framework for simultaneous speech-to-text translation and transcription. By establishing an implicit READ/WRITE policy directly from causally aligned audio-text pairs, our approach eliminates the need for complex human-engineered heuristics or external decision-making modules. We anticipate this technology will significantly facilitate real-time cross-lingual communication at international conferences and in education. In support of open science, we release our inference code and model weights to the research community. At the same time, we encourage practitioners to remain mindful of risks regarding data privacy and potential algorithmic biases that may be learned from the large-scale datasets used for training.

C.1
Scaling Performance with Hikari-small
While Hikari-medium establishes a strong baseline, we investigated if similar performance could be achieved with fewer parameters using Hikari-small. Despite following the same training and SFT recipe, the smaller model frequently failed to stabilize within standard latency envelopes (e.g., under 5s for en-ja). As shown in Table
6
, achieving meaningful measurements required relaxing the evaluation threshold to 6s StreamLAAL. This performance degradation highlights that sufficient decoder depth is critical for the model to internalize the implicit READ/WRITE policy without external guidance.

C.2
Frozen Mimi Encoder
To explore the utility of using a high-quality pre-trained streaming speech encoder, we replaced the Whisper-based causal encoder with a frozen Mimi encoder
(
Défossez et al., 2024
)
, specifically designed for high-efficiency streaming audio compression and providing a much smaller footprint (its encoder part is only
∼
\sim
39M parameters). In this variant of Hikari, we use a single trainable linear layer to project the unquantized output embeddings (of size 512) into the latent space of Hikari’s decoder (of size 1024). Note that since Mimi’s frame rate is 12.5 Hz, there’s a one to one correspondence between the number audio embeddings and Hikari’s decoder context length (375 for
D
=
4
D=4
).
In our tests, the Mimi-based variant yielded much lower translation quality and higher latency drift (far beyond the HL upper limit) than our primary model. We hypothesize that because the encoder was relatively small and frozen, the decoder had to compensate for acoustic representations that were not specifically optimized for the causal alignment task, leading to a capacity bottleneck.

C.3
The Role of ASR for Translation Performance
To determine whether the ASR objective competes for model capacity or provides beneficial regularization, we conducted a task-ablation study. We trained a version of Hikari-medium using the same dataset and hyperparameters but removed the transcription task entirely, leaving only the English-to-Japanese, German, and Russian translation objectives.
The results in Table
8
demonstrate that translation-only models perform consistently worse than their multitask counterpart. In the high-quality podcast domain, we observed drops of up to 5.85 BLEU points and a marked increase in latency. The performance gap was even more pronounced in the IWSLT25Instruct set, where BLEU scores plummeted by as much as 15.98 points, accompanied by a doubling of StreamLAAL values in some instances.
We hypothesize that the ASR task serves as an anchor for the causal encoder. By forcing the model to reconstruct the source text, it preserves acoustic features that might otherwise be lost if the model is optimized solely for semantic abstractions. This grounding seems to be important for the
WAIT
token mechanism, as accurate READ/WRITE decisions rely on the model’s ability to precisely detect word boundaries and silences in the incoming audio stream.
