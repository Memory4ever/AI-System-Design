Abstract.
RTL generation demands more than software code synthesis: designs must be syntactically valid, synthesizable, functionally correct, and hardware-efficient. Existing evaluations stop at functional correctness, leaving synthesizability and implementation quality unmeasured.
We evaluate 32 language models on 202 Verilog tasks from VerilogEval and RTLLM, with five attempts each, scoring via the Hardware Quality Index (HQI), a 0–100 metric integrating post-synthesis area, delay, and warning count relative to expert references under a Nangate45 45 nm flow. Three performance tiers emerge: 13 frontier models achieve Global HQI above 71, led by Gemini-3-Pro (87.5% coverage, 85.1 HQI); 11 mid-tier models cluster at 53–68; 8 fall below 53. The capability-to-deployment gap (best-of-five vs. single-attempt) spans 3.8–22.1 HQI points, motivating multi-sample strategies. A tool-adjudicated taxonomy of 195 genuine synthesis failures reveals systematic divergence: proprietary models fail late through elaboration errors and synthesis timeout; open-weight models fail early through missing module wrappers and non-synthesizable constructs, consistent with training on simulation-grade rather than synthesis-grade RTL. Rankings hold across three technology libraries at Spearman

Introduction
Large language models have rapidly advanced code generation
(
Chen et al., 2021
;
Austin et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2022
)
, motivating their application to hardware description languages
(
Liu et al., 2023a
;
Liu et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
. RTL generation is a more demanding setting than software synthesis: a design must be syntactically valid, synthesizable into gates, functionally correct under simulation,
and
efficient in area and timing
(
Lu et al., 2024
;
Pinckney et al., 2025
)
, four requirements that software-style evaluation metrics cannot simultaneously capture.
Register-transfer-level design represents the primary productivity bottleneck in the chip development cycle. Authoring synthesizable hardware descriptions for a competitive system-on-chip demands that engineering teams simultaneously satisfy functional correctness, timing closure, and area budgets across every module in a potentially million-gate design. This highly constrained process is measured in months to years per tape-out, and RTL authoring consumes a dominant share of pre-silicon engineering effort. Automated RTL generation could compress this cycle substantially by shifting human effort from authoring to reviewing and verifying, but only if generated designs satisfy the full synthesis stack. Passing a simulation testbench is necessary but not sufficient for a design to be ready for physical implementation; synthesis-aware quality evaluation, rather than simulation pass rate alone, is the appropriate criterion for assessing whether LLM-generated RTL can realistically substitute for expert-authored designs in production flows.
Existing HDL benchmarks evaluate primarily syntax validity or functional pass rates
(
Liu et al., 2023a
;
Thakur et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024
;
Pinckney et al., 2025
)
, which do not distinguish designs that merely pass a testbench from those that also compile into efficient hardware. Benchmark conclusions can be further distorted by evaluator artifacts
(
Liu et al., 2023b
;
Siddiq et al., 2024
;
Aleithan, 2025
)
, a concern compounded in RTL by the absence of synthesis-stage quality measurement in most prior work. The gap between simulation correctness and synthesis readiness is not merely quantitative: a design may pass all testbench assertions yet contain constructs that no synthesis tool can map to gates (behavioral
initial
blocks, while loops with non-constant bounds, or system tasks such as
$display
embedded in synthesized modules). More subtly, a functionally correct and synthesizable design may still incur area or timing penalties of 2–5
×
\times
relative to an expert reference, rendering it unsuitable for competitive process nodes. Neither failure mode is detectable by simulation alone, making a synthesis-in-the-loop evaluation pipeline necessary for measuring hardware-grade model capability.
We evaluate 32 language models on 202 Verilog tasks from VerilogEval and RTLLM, with five independent attempts each, under a staged pipeline: syntax validity (Icarus Verilog), synthesizability (Yosys + Nangate45 45 nm), and functional correctness (testbench simulation). HQI, a 0–100 score, is awarded only to designs clearing all three gates and is then anchored to how closely post-synthesis area, delay, and warning count match an expert golden reference under the same flow. Complexity-weighted Coverage and Global HQI ensure that harder multi-module designs contribute proportionally more than trivial ones.
Three principal findings emerge. The 32 models stratify into three distinct capability tiers, with a
4.7
×
4.7\times
spread in hardware implementation quality between the strongest and weakest, far wider than typical software-benchmark gaps. Simulation-based pass rates systematically overstate hardware readiness by up to 15 HQI points relative to synthesis-aware quality scores. Best-of-five capability consistently exceeds single-attempt deployment quality, motivating multi-sample generation strategies. A tool-adjudicated failure taxonomy reveals that proprietary and open-weight models fail through qualitatively different mechanisms (late elaboration errors versus early structural violations), implicating training data composition as a key lever for closing the capability gap.
This paper makes three contributions:
(1)
A synthesis-in-the-loop evaluation pipeline and HQI, a 0–100 metric anchoring post-synthesis area, delay, and warning count to expert golden references under a Nangate45 flow, enabling principled comparison of RTL implementation quality beyond pass/fail correctness.
(2)
An ecosystem-wide empirical characterization of 32 language models on 202 RTL tasks with five independent attempts each, revealing a structured three-tier capability landscape and quantifying the deployment-reliability gap across all tiers.
(3)
A tool-adjudicated nine-subtype synthesis failure taxonomy exposing systematic differences in
how

2.
Background
Large language models (LLMs), transformer-based neural networks pretrained on large text and code corpora and capable of generating code from natural-language prompts, have demonstrated strong performance on software synthesis benchmarks such as HumanEval
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
and MBPP
(
Austin et al., 2021
)
. These results have motivated applying LLMs to hardware description languages, where early work showed that general-purpose models can generate syntactically valid Verilog with reasonable functional accuracy
(
Thakur et al., 2024
)
.
Dedicated HDL benchmarks grade correctness primarily via simulation. VerilogEval
(
Liu et al., 2023a
)
grades 156 single-module tasks with Icarus Verilog, establishing pass@
k
k
(the probability that at least one of
k
k
independent generation attempts produces a passing solution) as the field’s primary metric. RTLLM
(
Lu et al., 2024
)
and its expansion OpenLLM-RTL
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
extend coverage to 47 real-world RTL problems, but retain simulation-pass as the sole quality criterion. Specialized models have emerged to optimize for precisely these benchmarks: VeriGen
(
Thakur et al., 2024
)
fine-tunes on open-source Verilog corpora, and RTLCoder
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
shows a lightweight model can exceed GPT-3.5 on VerilogEval pass rates. A systematic re-evaluation
(
Pinckney et al., 2025
)
confirms year-over-year progress on simulation metrics but argues that quality-of-results (QoR) measures such as area and timing are necessary for a complete picture. The blind spot is consistent: all prior benchmarks stop at simulation correctness and do not assess whether generated designs are synthesizable or hardware-efficient.
Logic synthesis transforms RTL into a gate-level netlist and is the critical step between design intent and physical implementation. Post-synthesis metrics (area, critical-path delay, and synthesis warnings) are standard QoR indicators in digital design flows. Open-source tools such as Yosys
(
Wolf, 2013
)
paired with standard-cell libraries (e.g., Nangate45
(
Nangate Inc., 2008
)
) enable reproducible, technology-realistic synthesis evaluation. None of the prior LLM RTL benchmarks integrate synthesis-stage QoR into their scoring, leaving a gap that this work addresses.

3.2.
Evaluation Pipeline
Each model–task pair receives
K
=

independent generation attempts. A generated design must clear three sequential gates before its hardware quality is assessed:
syntax validity
(Icarus Verilog parse succeeds),
synthesizability

functional correctness

Two design decisions ensure evaluator soundness. First, simulation verdicts use fail-first matching: a pass requires an explicit pass string with no preceding failure signal, preventing false positives from partially-correct outputs. Second, top-module resolution for RTLLM tasks (which frequently embed the target module after helper submodule definitions) strips comments and matches the task-derived module name before falling back to the first syntactic
module
keyword; naive first-keyword selection would produce spurious synthesis failures that are evaluator errors rather than model errors.
Designs that pass Icarus but fail Yosys constitute genuine synthesis failures. We classify them into a nine-subtype taxonomy by deterministic pattern matching on Yosys diagnostic output (e.g., undefined module reference, non-synthesizable construct, elaboration-time syntax error, synthesis timeout). Per-task inference metadata (API cost, reasoning token count, output throughput, and time-to-first-token) is recorded via OpenRouter for all 32 models. To validate that HQI rankings generalize beyond Nangate45, all passing designs are re-synthesized under two additional real technology libraries: IHP SG13G2 (130 nm) and OSU 0.35

Pass rate versus implementation quality.
Simulation-based pass rate systematically overstates hardware readiness. Across all 32 models, best-of-five pass rate exceeds Global HQI by 7.5 points on average, reflecting designs that are functionally correct but area- or timing-inefficient relative to expert references. The gap is most pronounced for models that solve many tasks at mediocre quality: GPT-4.1 passes 76.7% of tasks yet achieves only 62.8 Global HQI (gap 13.9), and Gemini-2.0-Flash passes 54.5% but scores only 39.6 HQI (gap 14.9). GPT-5.4-Pro nearly closes the gap (81.2% pass, 81.3 HQI), indicating that its passing designs closely match expert quality. A benchmark that reports only pass rates would rank GPT-4.1 above GPT-5.4 despite GPT-5.4 producing substantially better hardware.

4.3.
Deployment Reliability: Best-of-Five versus Per-Attempt Quality
The gap between Global HQI and Expected HQI does not diminish at the frontier. In Tier 1, the gap ranges from 3.8 to 12.9 points (median 8.2); even the top model drops 6.6 HQI points from its best-of-five ceiling to its single-attempt expectation. In Tier 2 and Tier 3, several models show gaps exceeding 17 points, meaning a single call delivers less than half the quality that five independent attempts would achieve. Synthesis Failure Analysis
Among the 32,320 total generation attempts, 195 exhibit genuine synthesis failures (designs that pass the Icarus Verilog parser but fail during Yosys elaboration or synthesis). Tool-adjudicated pattern matching on Yosys diagnostic output yields a nine-subtype taxonomy, in which three dominant modes account for 76.6% of all failures.
Late syntax errors
(59 tags, 30.0%) arise when code passes the Verilog parser but is rejected at Yosys elaboration due to malformed always-blocks, misplaced begin/end delimiters, or other structural issues not caught by the front-end parser; 55 of these 59 cases originate from RTLLM tasks, reflecting the structural complexity of multi-module designs.
Undefined module references
(50 tags, 25.4%) occur exclusively on VerilogEval tasks where models omit the required
top_module
wrapper.
Non-synthesizable constructs
(41 tags, 20.8%) include while loops with non-constant bounds, non-synthesizable initial blocks, and other behavioral constructs valid in simulation but unsupported by synthesis, predominantly on RTLLM tasks (31 of 41). The remaining subtypes are less frequent but diagnostically informative:
simulation-only system tasks
(14 tags, 7.1%), such as
$finish
and
$display
embedded in synthesized module bodies, indicate confusion between testbench and design code;
synthesis timeout
(14 tags, 7.1%) occurs exclusively on VerilogEval tasks and exclusively for proprietary models, where pathologically large or deeply nested generated netlists exhaust the 30-second synthesis budget; and
invalid nesting
(11 tags, 5.6%) covers illegal structural constructs such as nested always-blocks. The benchmark-level split confirms that the taxonomy captures genuine task-format differences: RTLLM failures concentrate in late syntax errors and non-synthesizable constructs (84%), while VerilogEval failures cluster on undefined module references and synthesis timeout (69%). 5.
Conclusion
We introduced a synthesis-in-the-loop evaluation framework covering 32 models, 202 tasks, and five independent attempts each, and showed that functional correctness alone captures neither the capability ceiling nor the deployment reliability of current models. HQI reveals a structured three-tier landscape in which simulation-based pass rates systematically overstate hardware readiness: models can pass 77% of tasks while achieving only 63 Global HQI, confirming that synthesis-aware quality metrics are necessary for credible RTL evaluation.
Three findings carry practical implications. The best-of-five-to-single-attempt gap (3.8–22.1 HQI points) motivates multi-sample deployment: practitioners should generate multiple candidates and select via automated synthesis verification rather than relying on a single API call. Within-generation model capacity matters disproportionately for RTL: the largest GPT-5 variant outperforms the smallest by over 45 HQI points, far exceeding typical software-benchmark separations, and generational transitions can regress substantially before point releases recover. Per-model failure signatures (synthesis timeout for Claude-4.5-Sonnet, missing module wrappers for open-weight models, invalid nesting for Claude-3.7-Sonnet) suggest that targeted fine-tuning on specific synthesis failure modes could yield outsized improvements relative to general-purpose scaling. The central qualitative finding is a systematic divergence in failure mode between proprietary and open-weight models: proprietary models fail late (elaboration-time errors and synthesis timeout) while open-weight models fail early through structural RTL discipline violations consistent with training on simulation-grade corpora, identifying curation of synthesis-grade RTL training data as a concrete, actionable lever for improving open-weight model performance.
Limitations and future work.
Our evaluation uses single-turn zero-shot prompting; iterative refinement with synthesis feedback
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
may narrow capability gaps. The task set spans eight hardware categories but does not cover system-level integration, analog/mixed-signal, or timing-constrained floorplan-aware design. HQI captures post-synthesis area and delay but not post-place-and-route metrics, which may further differentiate models on routing congestion and hold-time violations. The benchmark reflects a single snapshot in time; the rapid pace of model updates means rankings will evolve. Extending the framework to multi-turn agentic workflows with synthesis-in-the-loop feedback, incorporating place-and-route quality metrics, and constructing curated synthesis-grade RTL fine-tuning corpora to test the training-data hypothesis are the most promising next steps.
