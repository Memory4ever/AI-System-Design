IP-Bench: Benchmark for Image Protection Methods in Image-to-Video Generation Scenarios
Xiaofeng Li Leyi Sheng Zhen Sun Zongmin Zhang Jiaheng Wei Xinlei He
††thanks: Corresponding author (wooohxl@gmail.com).
Affiliation: The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou)Wuhan University
Abstract
With the rapid advancement of image-to-video (I2V) generation models, their potential for misuse in creating malicious content has become a significant concern.
For instance, a single image can be exploited to generate a fake video, which can be used to attract attention and gain benefits.
This phenomenon is referred to as an I2V generation misuse. Existing image protection methods suffer from the absence of a unified benchmark, leading to an incomplete evaluation framework.
Furthermore, these methods have not been systematically assessed in I2V generation scenarios and against preprocessing attacks, which complicates the evaluation of their effectiveness in real-world deployment scenarios.
To address this challenge, we propose IP-Bench (Image Protection Bench), the first systematic benchmark designed to evaluate protection methods in I2V generation scenarios.
This benchmark examines 6 representative protection methods and 5 state-of-the-art I2V models.
Furthermore, our work systematically evaluates protection methods’ robustness with two robustness attack strategies under practical scenarios and analyzes their cross-model & cross-modality transferability.
Overall, IP-Bench establishes a systematic, reproducible, and extensible evaluation framework for image protection methods in I2V generation scenarios.
Introduction
Figure 1: IP-Bench benchmarks image protection methods in terms of protection effectiveness, visual fidelity, robustness against attacks, and cross-model & cross-modality transferability. The figure shows the overall pipeline of our benchmark.
Image-to-Video (I2V) generation has experienced rapid progress in recent years [1, 2], driven by the development of diffusion-based models such as seeddance2.0 [3], and hunyuanVideo [4].
Specifically, I2V generation transforms static images into temporally coherent videos, pushing generative models beyond single-image synthesis.
By drastically lowering the barrier of video creation, this technology facilitates diverse applications in entertainment [5, 6], education [7], and content generation [8].
Ultimately, the ability to produce high-quality videos from simple inputs democratizes production, empowering individual creators and reshaping creative industries.
Despite being powerful, I2V models may also introduce significant security risks, including the generation of deepfakes [9, 10] and the dissemination of misinformation [11].
To mitigate these threats, prior research has proposed various image protection methods designed to prevent unauthorized content generation by I2V models [12].
Most existing methods adopt perturbation-based strategies, such as I2VGuard [13] and VGMShield [14].
These methods build upon earlier studies that aim to protect images from unauthorized editing by Image-to-Image (I2I) diffusion models, including PhotoGuard [15], Mist [16], and EditShield [17].
Despite the emergence of these image protection methods, existing assessments are often limited to specific models or single metrics, making it difficult to fairly compare different protection strategies or assess their generalization capabilities [18].While some studies, such as EditShield [17] and Mist [16], incorporate preprocessing attacks to evaluate the robustness of protection methods, others do not, leading to a lack of unified standards for assessing defensive resilience.
To address this gap, we introduce IP-Bench (Image Protection Bench), the first systematic benchmark for image protection methods in I2V generation scenarios.
We assess 6 protection methods (including both I2V-specific and I2I-based approaches) across 5 widely used I2V models featuring distinct structures, such as DiT and U-Net [19, 20].
As illustrated in Figure 1, our benchmark offers a systematic assessment framework covering multiple dimensions, including protection effectiveness, visual fidelity preservation, and robustness against preprocessing attacks.
Additionally, we examine cross-model and cross-modality generalization to demonstrate the transferability of protection methods across different I2V model architectures and across different generation scenarios.
Our results reveal distinct performance profiles among existing methods. Mist [16] provides the strongest protection but exhibits poor visual quality, with a degradation rate of 0.048 on SVD [21]. In contrast, EditShield [17] emphasizes image fidelity at the expense of defensive strength, achieving a high PSNR of 38.31.
I2VGuard [13] demonstrates notable robustness against preprocessing attacks, maintaining a degradation rate above 0.02 across most models.
VGMShield [14] demonstrates strong performance on specific models such as WAN [22], achieving a degradation rate of 0.036.
PhotoGuard [15] achieves a balanced performance, with a moderate PSNR of 31.77 and a degradation rate of 0.041 on Skyreel [23].
Furthermore, no existing method transfers effectively across different architectures, but PhotoGuard and Mist show a remarkable cross-modality transferability. These findings underscore a fundamental trade-off in image protection between protection effectiveness and visual quality.
To summarize, we make the following contributions:
•
We propose IP-Bench, the first systematic benchmark that evaluates image protection methods through protection effectiveness, visual fidelity, robustness against preprocessing attacks, and cross-architecture transferability.
•
Our benchmark over 6 image protection methods reveals a fundamental trade-off between defensive effectiveness and visual quality: methods that strongly degrade model performance often noticeably reduce image fidelity. We also find that most existing methods are highly sensitive to simple preprocessing attacks and fail to transfer effectively across different model architectures. These findings highlight specific challenges of robustness under common transformations and cross-architecture generalization, which could guide the design of next-generation protection frameworks.
•
We release an extensible code framework for evaluating and developing image protection methods. Our framework allows researchers to easily plug in new protection methods and attack strategies.
Using this framework, we demonstrate that certain methods degrade significantly under common preprocessing attacks, highlighting the need for more robust and transferable protection techniques.

Related Work
Image-to-Video Generation Models.
Recent advances in diffusion models have significantly accelerated the development of Image-to-Video (I2V) generation.
Existing approaches mainly differ in their backbone architectures.
Early methods, such as Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) [21], adopt a U-Net-based framework with a 3D VAE to model spatiotemporal representations.
More recent works increasingly employ Diffusion Transformers (DiT) [19], which offer improved scalability and modeling capacity. Representative models include Wan2.2 [22], CogVideoX [25], and Skyreel [23], which enhance multimodal understanding and generation quality through transformer-based designs.
In addition to backbone evolution, temporal modeling has become a key research focus [26]. Techniques such as Temporal VAE explicitly compress dynamic information across frames to improve temporal consistency [27, 24]. The diversity of architectural designs, ranging from U-Net to DiT and specialized temporal modules [28], leads to substantial differences in how visual and motion information are processed [29].
Perturbation-based Image Protection.
Perturbation-based defense is the dominant paradigm for protecting images against diffusion models [30, 18, 31]. These methods introduce adversarial noise to disrupt the generation process, typically by interfering with latent representations or attention mechanisms. Early works mainly targeted Image-to-Image (I2I) editing [32, 33, 34]. For example, PhotoGuard perturbs the encoding stage of diffusion models, while EditShield and MIST improve robustness and transferability through optimized adversarial objectives [15, 17, 16]. Glaze [35] focuses on protecting artistic style by obscuring stylistic features.
However, directly applying static defenses to I2V generation often yields limited effectiveness due to the added temporal dimension. To address this, I2V-specific approaches have emerged. I2VGuard [13] incorporates temporal attention modules into adversarial optimization, ensuring protection persists across frames. Similarly, VGMShield [14] performs feature-space optimization to enhance robustness in multi-frame generation. These methods reflect a shift from static perturbation toward temporally-aware defenses.
Benchmark and Evaluation Metrics.
Evaluating image protection methods [36] involves two key aspects: (1) preserving the visual quality of the protected image [37] and (2) degrading the quality or consistency of the generated video [38]. For image fidelity, common metrics include PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, along with semantic similarity measures such as CLIP Similarity Score.
For generated videos, evaluation requires assessing both visual realism and temporal coherence. Metrics such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) [39] are widely used to measure distributional similarity between video sets. Benchmarks like VBench [40, 41] further evaluate the generated videos in multiple well-defined dimensions including Subject Consistency, Motion Smoothness, etc.
Design of IP-Bench
Threat Model
We formalize the image protection task with a threat model specifying the objectives and capabilities of two parties: the adversary (unauthorized I2V user) and the defender (image protector).
Adversary’s Objective and Capability.
The adversary aims to generate high-quality videos from protected images using I2V models, with success measured by visual fidelity, generation quality, and semantic alignment.
Regarding the capability, we assume the adversary can only access the protected image instead of the protection method, and can apply image transformation methods like JPEG Compression, Gaussian Noise, or geometric transformations to bypass protection.
Defender’s Objective and Capability.
The defender applies imperceptible perturbations before image release to degrade video generation quality while preserving visual quality for users.
The defender employs the protection methods and optimizes perturbations to interfere with the generation process of I2V models.
This threat model establishes a clear adversarial dynamic: the defender proactively poisons images to sabotage generation attempts, while the adversary attempts to overcome this sabotage.
Experiment Settings
Protection Methods.
We evaluate 6 representative protection methods within IP-Bench, covering both I2V-specific and I2I-based approaches.
Table 1 summarizes their key characteristics, including optimization strategies and target components.
•
I2V-Specific Methods. I2VGuard interferes with both temporal attention mechanisms and VAE latent representations to disrupt video generation coherence. VGMShield employs feature-space Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) to optimize perturbations that maximize generation degradation.
•
I2I-Based Methods.
EditShield integrates perceptual consistency and Expectation over Transformation (EOT) strategies to protect against diverse editing prompts. MIST designs a fused adversarial loss term to enhance transferability across painting-imitating methods. PhotoGuard perturbs the diffusion encoder to prevent successful image manipulation.
•
Baseline.
RandomNoise adds uniform noise \mathcal{U}[-\epsilon,\epsilon] (\epsilon=16/255) clipped to [0,1] as a naive baseline.
I2V Models.
We evaluate protection methods across 5 state-of-the-art I2V models with diverse architectures. Table 2 summarizes the models used in IP-Bench.
The selected models cover both U-Net (SVD) and DiT (LTX, Skyreel, CogVideoX, Wan2.2) architectures, enabling evaluation of cross-architecture transferability. The DiT-based models support text prompt guidance, allowing us to assess protection effectiveness under semantic conditioning, while SVD serves as a strong image-conditioned baseline.
Dataset.
We construct the Flickr30k-MSCOCO-300 dataset by selecting 300 high-quality images from Flickr30k [42] and MSCOCO [43]. Video generation prompts are generated using Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [44] to ensure semantic alignment with source images and encourage meaningful motion dynamics. The full dataset will be released with IP-Bench.
Preprocessing Attacks.
To evaluate the robustness of protection methods, we consider several attacks where adversaries attempt to remove or neutralize perturbations through preprocessing.
We focus on two representative strategies based on elimination and addition perspectives.
•
Elimination-based Attack (JPEG Compression).
Adversaries may apply lossy compression to eliminate high-frequency adversarial signals. We evaluate protection robustness against JPEG Compression [45]. We employ a standard quality factor (QF) of 75, a common setting that balances visual fidelity and file size, to test if adversarial perturbations can survive lossy quantization.
•
Addition-based Attack (Gaussian Noise).
Adversaries may add random noise to mask or overwhelm the protective perturbation. We apply additive Gaussian Noise with standard deviations of 0.01 to test whether the protection signal can withstand signal-to-noise ratio degradation.
These preprocessing attacks represent practical attempts to bypass protection without requiring knowledge of the defense mechanism, providing a rigorous test of real-world robustness.
Metrics.
To comprehensively assess IP-Bench, we employ a multi-dimensional evaluation protocol focusing on protection effectiveness and visual fidelity.
•
Protection Effectiveness. We utilize the VBench suite [40] to evaluate the quality of generated videos across four critical dimensions: Subject Consistency, Motion Smoothness, Aesthetic Quality, and Imaging Quality.
A significant drop in these scores indicates successful disruption of the I2V generation process.
Furthermore, we employ the Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) [39] to quantify the distributional discrepancy between videos generated from original versus protected (attacked) images.
A higher FVD score reflects a stronger protective effect, as it indicates a greater deviation from the intended high-quality video distribution.
•
Visual Fidelity. To ensure the perturbations remain imperceptible, we measure the spatial and structural integrity of protected (attacked) images using PSNR and SSIM.
For human-centric perceptual and semantic alignment, we adopt LPIPS and CLIP Similarity.
Additionally, the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) is calculated to evaluate the distributional shift between the original and protected (attacked) image sets.
Implementation Details.
All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA L20 GPU server.
Due to the high memory requirements of I2V model inference and varying image resolutions across the dataset that prevent efficient stacking, we set the batch size to 1 for all protection and generation experiments.
All protection methods are implemented in PyTorch, and images are processed at their original resolutions without resizing to preserve semantic consistency. For I2VGuard, as no official implementation is publicly available, we re-implement the
method based on the description in the original paper. For VGMShield, we adopt its undirected variant in our experiments. For PhotoGuard, we adopt its encoder attack variant in our experiments. For all remaining methods, we employ their official implementations with default parameters.
To ensure the reliability of our benchmark in assessing long-term temporal consistency, we configure each model to generate extended video sequences rather than short clips. Specifically, we set the generation length to 25 frames for SVD, 33 frames for CogVideoX, and 41 frames for Wan2.2. For LTX, Skyreel, the generation is extended to 49 frames.

VBench Scores.
Overall, Mist demonstrates the strongest protection capability, inducing the most substantial degradation with an average degradation rate of around 6.6% across all VBench scores, followed by PhotoGuard, I2VGuard, and VGMShield.
EditShield performs the worst among all methods, showing negligible degradation with the average degradation rate of 0.63% on VBench scores and failing to surpass the baseline (1.16%).
Notably, we observe varying degrees of degradation across different VBench metrics: protection methods cause the most significant deterioration in Aesthetic Quality and Imaging Quality, followed by Subject Consistency.
In contrast, Motion Smoothness remains largely unaffected, and in some cases, the scores even increase slightly, suggesting that protection perturbations have minimal impact on temporal coherence. The results could refer to Figure 3.
FVD Analysis.
Figure 2 presents the FVD between videos generated from original and protected images, where higher scores indicate a stronger distributional shift. Mist achieves the highest average FVD (0.034), confirming its superior protection effectiveness, followed by I2VGuard (0.031) and VGMShield (0.027). EditShield consistently performs the worst with the lowest FVD (0.022), aligning with its negligible VBench degradation.
Cross-Metric Analysis.
While FVD results largely corroborate VBench rankings for top (Mist) and bottom (EditShield) performers, a notable divergence emerges for PhotoGuard. Despite achieving the second-highest VBench degradation rate, PhotoGuard’s FVD (0.026) falls below even the RandomNoise baseline (0.0266). This discrepancy suggests that PhotoGuard degrades per-frame quality (captured by VBench Imaging Quality/Aesthetic Quality) without significantly shifting the temporal distribution. Conversely, I2VGuard’s strong FVD performance (second-highest) despite moderate VBench degradation (2.0%) indicates its temporal attention attacks effectively disrupt video generation distribution beyond what frame-level metrics reveal. This highlights the necessity of multi-dimensional evaluation for comprehensive protection assessment.

Impact of Implementation Parameters.
The varying visual quality preservation across methods is primarily driven by their hyperparameter configurations rather than algorithmic design alone.
Mist employs the most aggressive settings (\epsilon=17/255, semantic loss rate=10000) without explicit quality constraints, resulting in severe degradation (FID: 74.91, SSIM: 0.729).
In contrast, EditShield uses a conservative budget (\epsilon=4/255) with a perceptual consistency loss and fewer optimization steps (30), yielding minimal distortion (FID: 6.04, SSIM: 0.955).
These implementation choices directly dictate the imperceptibility of perturbations, as evidenced by the significant disparities in PSNR and SSIM scores across methods.
Based on the preceding analysis, we distill a central governing principle of the image protection task:
Takeaway: There is a trade-off between protection effectiveness and visual fidelity.
In principle, a larger perturbation budget, while leading to diminished visual fidelity of the protected image, typically results in better protective effectiveness against I2V generation.
A critical consideration in image protection is the inherent trade-off between protection effectiveness and the preservation of visual quality. Consequently, the primary challenge for practical protection methods lies in maximizing the sabotage effect on downstream generation models within a constrained and acceptable perturbation budget.

Evaluation of Protection Persistence
Figure 4 illustrates the resilience of protection methods against two common preprocessing attacks: JPEG Compression and Gaussian Noise.
Overall, we observe a significant “protection erosion” effect, where the effectiveness of all methods diminishes when the protected images are preprocessed by the adversary.
Resilience Against JPEG Compression.
As shown in Figure 4, JPEG Compression (quality factor=75) acts as an overwhelmingly powerful “purification” step, revealing a general lack of robustness across almost all evaluated methods.
Our results indicate that Mist is the only method capable of maintaining a non-negligible disruptive effect under such an attack, though its average VBench degradation rate still drops from 6.6% to approximately 4.0%.
Despite minimal visual recovery (as seen in Table 4(a)), I2VGuard’s residual effectiveness drops below 1%.
This suggests that while its perturbations persist in the pixel domain, compression erodes their moderate initial strength until they can no longer overcome the model’s inherent denoising.
Other methods, such as PhotoGuard and EditShield, lose their utility too, showing an overall negligible degradation rate of VBench scores.
Interestingly, JPEG Compression exerts a minimal impact on FVD scores; for most methods, the FVD between original and attacked videos remains largely consistent with those between original and protected videos prior to the attack, suggesting that while the disruptive power is eroded, the temporal distribution shifts remain relatively stable.
The extreme vulnerability to JPEG Compression can be attributed to its fundamental signal-processing characteristics:
•
High-Frequency Erasure: Most protection methods rely on fine-grained, high-frequency adversarial noise.
JPEG’s Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) and subsequent quantization steps are specifically designed to discard these high-frequency components, effectively “smoothing” the image and neutralizing the adversarial gradients before they reach the model’s latent space.
•
Non-Differentiable Manifold Shift: JPEG introduces blocky artifacts and non-differentiable distortions.
These modifications shift the image away from the specific adversarial manifold for which the perturbations were optimized, rendering the protection ineffective even if some noise remains visible.
Resilience Against Gaussian Noise.
Under Gaussian Noise attack (\sigma=0.01), the protection effectiveness exhibits patterns largely consistent with JPEG Compression, but with notable exceptions.
As shown in Figure 4, and confirmed by the image quality metrics in Table 4(b), the additive noise further degrades the visual quality of protected images. Similar to JPEG Compression, Mist and PhotoGuard show substantial recovery in their protection effectiveness, with degradation rates dropping significantly across most models. This suggests that Gaussian Noise, like compression, can partially neutralize structured adversarial perturbations.
However, an intriguing phenomenon emerges for I2VGuard: unlike other methods, its protection effectiveness increases under Gaussian Noise on several models.
On CogVideoX, the VBench degradation rate rises from 1.6% to 3.4%, and on LTX from 0.9% to 2.0%. This divergent response to Gaussian Noise suggests that different protection mechanisms possess varying levels of resilience to random interference.
While most methods experience a masking effect where noise diminishes the adversarial impact, the disruption caused by I2VGuard appears more persistent, and in some cases, worsened by the noise.
This indicates that I2VGuard’s specific perturbation patterns may be more structurally aligned with the vulnerabilities of temporal attention in certain models, allowing them to remain effective or even become amplified under stochastic noise.
Similar stability in FVD is observed under Gaussian Noise across most protection methods and models.
However, the Wan2.2 model presents a notable exception, where FVD increases significantly following the noise attack.
We attribute this phenomenon to the model’s inherent sensitivity to stochastic interference, regardless of the specific protection method under evaluation.

Impact of JPEG Compression.
When subjected to JPEG Compression (quality factor = 75), nearly all protection methods exhibit a significant recovery in visual quality metrics: PSNR, SSIM, and CLIP scores increase, while LPIPS and FID decrease.
This trend indicates that JPEG Compression effectively dissipates adversarial perturbations in the pixel domain, causing attacked images to revert closer to the original distribution.
Notably, I2VGuard and Mist demonstrate higher robustness against the image quality recovery compared to the RandomNoise baseline.
After JPEG Compression, their image quality metrics show minimal recovery (e.g., I2VGuard PSNR increases only 0.01, Mist increases 0.07), whereas RandomNoise exhibits substantial quality rebound (PSNR increases 5.26).
This indicates that the perturbations generated by I2VGuard and Mist are more resilient to compression-based removal. PhotoGuard also shows moderate robustness, with quality recovery generally lower than the baseline except in LPIPS.
These results confirm that JPEG Compression acts as a potent defense against adversarial perturbations, largely neutralizing protection effects.
Impact of Gaussian Noise.
In contrast, adding Gaussian Noise (\sigma=0.01, approximating L_{\infty}\approx 8/255) leads to further degradation in visual quality preservation compared to the protected images.
Unlike JPEG Compression, which smooths out high-frequency perturbations, additive noise compounds the existing adversarial perturbations, resulting in additional structural disruption in the pixel domain.
This is evidenced by lower PSNR and SSIM scores across all methods (e.g., EditShield drops from 38.31 to 35.97 in PSNR), indicating that the superposition of protection noise and attack noise exacerbates visual distortion rather than mitigating it.
Through this subsection of experiments, we have the following insight.
Takeaway: Existing protection methods demonstrate a critical lack of robustness against preprocessing attacks, particularly JPEG compression.
While current methods often target latent representations, the resulting perturbations are ultimately superimposed on the pixel domain without explicit spectral constraints.
These noise patterns frequently reside in high-frequency bands—areas where human visual sensitivity is low (ensuring imperceptibility) but which are the primary targets for removal during JPEG’s quantization process.
The lack of frequency-aware optimization means that the “protection” is easily purified by standard signal-processing transformations. A key direction for future research lies in strategically embedding perturbations into more resilient frequency components (e.g., mid-frequencies) to balance robust defensive utility with visual imperceptibility.
Cross-Model & Cross-Modality Generalization Analysis
In this subsection, we analyze the generalization ability of the protection methods from the perspective of cross-model transferability and cross-modality transferability.
Cross-Model Transferability.
Among the evaluated protection methods, I2VGuard targets SVD’s temporal attention blocks and VAE encoder, while VGMShield disrupts two specific embedders of SVD: the first embedder layer for conditional frame embeddings and the fourth embedder layer for temporal-level prediction.
However, as illustrated in Figure 2, we do not observe any remarkable algorithm-specific transferability. Instead, the VBench degradation rates and FVD metrics exhibit uniform trends across different models regardless of the protection algorithm used.
For instance, nearly all methods yield the lowest VBench degradation rates on LTX and Skyreel, and the minimum FVD on CogVideoX.
These consistent patterns suggest that the observed performance variance stems from the inherent sensitivity of the target models to perturbations, rather than the specific design of the protection methods.
Cross-Modality Transferability. As evidenced in Figure 2, image-level protection methods demonstrate superior efficacy when transferred to the video domain.
Specifically, Mist achieves the highest VBench degradation rates across all models, while PhotoGuard maintains a remarkable balance between high protection strength and visual fidelity.
We attribute this success to the fact that these methods target static components (e.g., VAE and U-Net) without the burden of optimizing for temporal consistency.
This simpler optimization objective allows for the generation of more potent adversarial noise in shorter time.
Furthermore, the shared architectural foundations between I2I and I2V models, such as the inherited VAE and latent manifold, act as a bridge that ensures these image-centric perturbations remain highly disruptive during video generation scenarios.
Takeaway: The evaluated methods exhibit limited cross-model transferability, as their protective efficacy is primarily dictated by the target models’ inherent sensitivity rather than specific algorithmic designs.
Conversely, image-level methods demonstrate robust cross-modality transferability; by bypassing the complexities of temporal consistency, these methods leverage simpler optimization objectives and shared architectural foundations (e.g., VAE and latent manifolds) to maintain high disruptive potency when transitioned from the image to the video domain.
Limitations
In this section, we reflect on several limitations of our current benchmark that may influence the interpretation of the results and point toward future research directions.
Absence of Closed-source Models. Our evaluation is restricted to open-source architectures. While covering diverse models like SVD and Wan2.2, the omission of proprietary systems (e.g., Sora, Kling) limits our understanding of protection efficacy in true black-box scenarios. Commercial APIs may employ undisclosed preprocessing or safety filters that interact unpredictably with perturbations. Assessing transferability to these closed-source models remains a critical objective for future benchmark iterations.
Potential Bias in Prompt Distribution. Our prompts are currently generated exclusively by Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. While ensuring descriptive accuracy, this reliance may introduce semantic "priors" or a specific linguistic style that I2V models respond to more predictably. To enhance diversity, we plan to incorporate human-expert-crafted prompts and multi-LLM outputs in future updates to ensure a more robust and generalized evaluation.
Lack of Standardized Constraints and Complexity Analysis. Our evaluation currently lacks a unified noise budget (e.g., fixed L_{\infty} bounds) and a formal complexity analysis. This absence of standardized constraints may introduce bias in performance comparisons, while the lack of quantitative data on computational overheadlimits insights into real-world deployability. Future work will benchmark these methods under equivalent perturbation and provide a rigorous analysis of their practical efficiency.
Conclusion
In this work, we present IP-Bench, the first systematic benchmark that evaluates 6 protection methods in I2V generation scenarios across 4 key dimensions: protection effectiveness, visual fidelity, robustness against potential attacks, and cross-model as well as cross-modality transferability. A granular analysis of our experimental results indicates the inherent trade-off between protection effectiveness and visual fidelity, while simultaneously highlighting the challenges regarding robustness against attacks. We hope our benchmark and insights can offer directions for future work.
