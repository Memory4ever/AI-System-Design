On the Expressive Power of Contextual Relations in Transformers
Demián Fraiman
Affiliation: Instituto de Cálculo, Universidad de Buenos Aires, Buenos Aires, Argentina
Correspondence to: fraimandemian@gmail.com
Abstract
Transformer architectures have achieved remarkable empirical success in modeling contextual
relationships in natural language, yet a precise mathematical characterization of their
expressive power remains incomplete.
In this work, we introduce a measure-theoretic framework for contextual representations in
which texts are modeled as probability measures over a semantic embedding space, and
contextual relations between words, are represented as coupling measures between them. Within this setting, we introduce Sinkhorn Transformers, a transformer-like architecture.
Our main result is a universal approximation theorem: any continuous coupling function
between probability measures, that encodes the semantic relation coupling measure, can be uniformly approximated by a Sinkhorn Transformer with
appropriate parameters.
Keywords: Transformers,
Attention,
Universal approximation,
Optimal transport,
Coupling measures,
Entropic optimal transport,
Functional analysis
1 Introduction
Transformer architectures have achieved remarkable empirical success in modeling contextual relationships in natural language and other structured data, and its precisely its biggest breakthrough with respect to older recurrent neural networks. Despite this success, a precise mathematical characterization of the class of contextual relations representable by attention mechanisms remains incomplete. Existing analyses typically describe attention as a heuristic weighting scheme over representations, leaving open the question of what functional objects attention layers are fundamentally capable of approximating.
In this work, we study whether Transformers can learn every possible system of semantic contextual relations. Where each contextual system is an assignment that maps text sequences to contextual semantic graphs, representing semantic relations between words. The case of a single text is similar to self-attention (note that the coefficient could be asymmetric), an example of a semantic graph would be Figure 1. whereas the case of two texts is linked with cross-attention mechanisms, such as those arising in translation, where the model outputs a word-to-word semantic similarity structure across texts as it can be seen in Figure 2. For clarity, coefficients with small magnitude are omitted.
Figure 1: One text contextual semantic graph.
Figure 2: Two texts contextual semantic graph.
For dealing with arbitrary length sequences, we propose a measure-theoretic framework in which contextual representations are modeled as probability measures over a semantic space, and contextual relations between inputs are represented as coupling measures between these distributions. Within this setting, attention mechanisms are naturally interpreted as operators that map pairs of input measures to joint distributions with prescribed marginals. This perspective shifts the focus from pointwise similarity scores to the approximation of structured probabilistic relations.
Our main result establishes a universal approximation theorem for contextual relations. Under mild regularity assumptions on the semantic space, we prove that a class of Transformer-style attention mechanisms is dense in the space of continuous coupling-valued maps between probability measures. In other words, attention-based architectures can approximate arbitrary continuous assignments of joint distributions with fixed marginals, providing a rigorous characterization of their expressive power at the level of semantic relations.
1.1 Contributions
•
We introduce a measure-theoretic framework for contextual representations in which attention mechanisms are modeled as coupling-valued maps between probability measures.
•
We present a structured Transformer-like architecture Sinkhorn Transformers to address this problem.
•
We prove a universal approximation theorem by showing the density of Sinkhorn Transformers architecture in the space of continuous coupling-valued maps.
2 Related Work
Expressivity and universality of attention mechanisms.
The expressive power of attention-based architectures has been studied from multiple perspectives, including universality results for sequence-to-sequence models and analyses of architectural limitations
(Vaswani et al., 2017; Yun et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2021; Pérez et al., 2021; Furuya et al., 2024).
These works typically model attention as a weighted aggregation operator acting on finite-dimensional representations, and establish universal approximation results for functions with codomain \mathbb{R}^{d}.
For instance, (Yun et al., 2020; Furuya et al., 2024) prove universality for classes of functions of the form
P(X)\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d} or sequence-to-sequence maps with vector-valued outputs.
In contrast, our work studies attention at the level of the contextual relations it induces, modeling attention mechanisms as operators that assign joint probability measures to pairs of input distributions.
This shift from vector-valued outputs to coupling-valued outputs enables a characterization of expressivity in terms of approximation of structured relations, rather than pointwise representations in \mathbb{R}^{d}.
Optimal transport in attention and representation learning.
Optimal transport and entropic regularization have been widely adopted in machine learning as tools for comparing distributions and inducing geometry-aware similarities
(Cuturi, 2013; Genevay et al., 2016; Peyré and Cuturi, 2019).
Several works incorporate Sinkhorn iterations into attention mechanisms to improve stability or to encode prior structure
(Frogner et al., 2015; Mena et al., 2018).
In these approaches, optimal transport is typically used as a computational primitive or loss function acting on learned representations.
By contrast, we take coupling measures themselves as the objects of approximation.
This allows us to treat Sinkhorn-based attention as a parametrized family of coupling-valued maps and to study their expressive power within a functional-analytic and measure-theoretic framework
(Léonard, 2012; Nutz and Wiesel, 2022b; Nutz and Wiesel, 2022a). (Tay et al., 2020a) propose replacing standard softmax attention with Sinkhorn normalization at every layer, enforcing (approximate) double stochasticity throughout the network.
Similarly, (Tay et al., 2020b) introduce Sparse Sinkhorn Attention, combining Sinkhorn iterations with sparsity constraints to obtain more efficient attention patterns.
Our approach differs in both scope and objective.
Rather than modifying all attention layers, we introduce the Sinkhorn operator only at the final interaction stage, keeping standard attention mechanisms in intermediate layers.
This allows us to retain the expressive structure of classical Transformers while introducing a principled probabilistic interpretation of the final attention map as a coupling between measures.
Moreover, while prior works are primarily motivated by architectural design and computational efficiency, our focus is on expressivity and approximation theory.
In particular, we study the ability of Transformer-like architectures to approximate structured relational operators (i.e., couplings between measures), providing a theoretical characterization of contextual relation learning.
Semantic interpretation of attention mechanisms.
Attention mechanisms are often heuristically interpreted as capturing semantic similarity or contextual relevance in language models.
Such interpretations, however, typically lack a precise mathematical formulation of what it means for a model to represent semantic relations.
Our work differs by providing a formal, measure-theoretic interpretation of contextual understanding, in which semantic relations are modeled as coupling measures between semantic distributions.
Within this framework, our universal approximation result yields a precise characterization of the class of semantic relations that attention mechanisms can represent, going beyond vector-valued similarity scores and embedding-based interpretations.
3 A Measure-Theoretic Framework for Semantic Relations
In this section we introduce the conceptual and mathematical framework underlying our
approach.
We explain why texts are naturally modeled as probability measures and how semantic
relations between texts can be formalized as coupling-valued mappings.
3.1 Texts as Probability Measures
Texts are inherently variable-length objects: different sentences or documents may contain
different numbers of tokens, words, or subword units.
Any mathematical framework aiming to model semantic relations between texts should therefore
be able to manage texts with variable and unbounded length.
To this end, we represent a text as a probability measure over a semantic embedding space.
Let X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a compact space representing token embeddings.
Given a text consisting of tokens (w_{1},\dots,w_{n}) with embeddings (x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X, we associate the
probability measure
\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\delta_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(X),
Where \mathcal{P}(X) is the space of probability measures over X. Note that the position of the tokens could be encoded as a positional embedding in the space of X.
This representation allows texts of arbitrary length to be embedded into a common compact space. Also
\mathcal{P}(X) enables the use of tools from measure theory and optimal transport to study
relations between texts.
3.2 From Attention to Joint Distributions
The attention mechanism assigns weights to pairs of tokens, measuring how strongly one token
attends to another in a row normalized manner.
Let (w_{1},\dots,w_{n}) be a sequence of tokens with embeddings (x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X. In a standard Transformer, attention coefficients are defined as
A(w_{i},w_{j})=\frac{\exp(W_{Q}x_{i}\cdot W_{K}x_{j})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}\exp(W_{Q}x_{i}\cdot W_{K}x_{j})}.
So A(w_{i},\cdot) could be interpreted as a conditional probability over tokens.
From a probabilistic perspective, conditional distributions naturally induce joint
distributions.
Given a source measure \mu_{text}\in\mathcal{P}(X) and attention weights A(x,y), one can define a joint measure \pi by
\pi(dx,dy)=A(x,y)\,\mu(dx).
The resulting measure \pi encodes joint relevance between elements of the two
representations.
This observation motivates modeling attention mechanisms directly as joint probability
measures, since joint measures are more tractable than conditional probabilities.
Since we are interested in the case of having also two texts, that could be input and output of transformer for instance, we would then have two texts \mu_{input} and \nu_{output}, Then, the contextual relation between both is a
probability measure \pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y) whose marginals are \mu_{input} and \nu_{output}. We will call the joint probability measures encoding contextual relations as coupling, and the set of all such couplings between two measures \mu,\nu is denoted by \Pi(\mu,\nu).
For now on, we will work only with the case of having two texts, since in the case we have two equal texts we recover the one text only case.
We interpret couplings as representations of contextual relations.
Intuitively, a coupling specifies how semantic meaning of one text words relates to the other text words.
This joint structure captures relational information that cannot be reduced to independent
or pointwise similarities. We could then define a whole system that computes for each pair of texts \mu_{input},\nu_{output} their contextual relations \pi\in\Pi(\mu_{input},\nu_{output}).
We formalize this idea by introducing the notion of a coupling system.
Definition 3.1 (Coupling System).
A coupling system is a continuous mapping in the weak topology
F:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
such that for all (\mu,\nu), the measure F(\mu,\nu) belongs to \Pi(\mu,\nu).
3.3 Transformers as maps of probabilities
We follow the construction of the transformers as in (Furuya et al., 2024). This formulation will serve as the building block for our proposed architecture in section 6 the Sinkhorn Transformers.
Definition 3.2 (Measure-valued multi-head attention).
Given a probability measure \mu\in\mathcal{P}(X) and a point x\in X, we define the attention map
\Gamma_{\theta}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\Gamma_{\theta}(\mu,x)=x+\sum_{h=1}^{H}W^{h}\int_{X}\operatorname{softmax}^{\theta^{h}}_{\mu}(x)(y)\,V^{h}y\,d\mu(y),
where \theta=(\theta^{1},\dots,\theta^{H}), \theta^{h}=(Q^{h},K^{h},V^{h},W^{h}), and
\operatorname{softmax}^{\theta^{h}}_{\mu}(x)(y)=\frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{h}}}\langle Q^{h}x,K^{h}y\rangle\big)}{\int_{X}\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{h}}}\langle Q^{h}x,K^{h}z\rangle\big)\,d\mu(z)}.
This definition extends classical multi-head attention, recovering the standard formulation when \mu is an empirical measure supported on finitely many points, since the integral with respect to a a discrete measure turns into a sum, we have
\displaystyle\int_{X}\operatorname{softmax}_{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}}^{\theta^{h}}(x)(y)\,V^{h}y\,d(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}})(y)
\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{k}}}\langle Q^{h}x,\,K^{h}x_{j}\rangle\big)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{h}}}\langle Q^{h}x,\,K^{h}x_{i}\rangle\big)}\,V^{h}x_{j}.
Since the \frac{1}{n} factors cancels each other out, we recover the classical case.
Definition 3.3 (Context maps and context operators).
Let X and Y be locally compact metric spaces. A map
\Gamma:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to Y
is called a context map if \Gamma(\mu,\cdot) is continuous on X for every \mu\in\mathcal{P}(X).
The associated context operator \mathcal{C}(\Gamma):\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(Y) is defined by
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma)(\mu)=\Gamma(\mu,\cdot)_{\#}\mu.
Definition 3.4 (Composition of context maps).
Let \Gamma_{1}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to Y and \Gamma_{2}:\mathcal{P}(Y)\times Y\to Z be context maps. Their composition
\Gamma_{2}\diamond\Gamma_{1}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times X\to Z
is defined by
(\Gamma_{2}\diamond\Gamma_{1})(\mu,x)=\Gamma_{2}(\mu_{1},\Gamma_{1}(\mu,x)),\qquad\mu_{1}=\mathcal{C}(\Gamma_{1})(\mu).
Definition 3.5 (Deep Transformer).
A Transformer with L layers is defined as the composition
F_{\xi_{L}}\diamond\Gamma_{\theta_{L}}\diamond\cdots\diamond F_{\xi_{1}}\diamond\Gamma_{\theta_{1}},
where each \Gamma_{\theta_{\ell}} is a measure-valued attention map of the form (3.2) and each F_{\xi_{\ell}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell+1}} is a multilayer perceptron acting pointwise.
3.4 Problem Statement
The central question addressed in this work is the following:
Are the Transformer-architecture capable of learning any coupling system?
Our goal is to characterize the expressive capacity of such architectures. This perspective shifts the focus from general function approximation to relational structure, which is at the core of Transformer architectures.
4 The Space of Coupling systems
We now study the functional-analytic structure of semantic coupling functions.
Our goal is to characterize the space in which these objects live and to identify
natural regularity assumptions under which approximation results can be established.
We consider the space of continuous functions:
\mathcal{C}=C(\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y),\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)),
where \mathcal{M}(X\times Y) denotes the space of signed Radon measures on X\times Y equipped with the weak* topology (i.e., the topology induced by the dual pairing with C(X\times Y)). We endow \mathcal{C} with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets, which in this case is equivalent to uniform convergence on the entire space since \mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y) is compact. It is a known fact from functional analysis that \mathcal{C} is a locally convex topological vector space.
Define the reference product map F_{0}(\mu,\nu)=\mu\otimes\nu and the linear subspace \mathcal{F} defined as:
\{G\in\mathcal{C}:\operatorname{proj}_{X}G(\mu,\nu)=0,\ \operatorname{proj}_{Y}G(\mu,\nu)=0\ \forall(\mu,\nu)\}.
We have the affine representation \mathcal{A}=F_{0}+\mathcal{F}.
Using the weak continuity of the projection operator we have that:
Proposition 4.1.
The affine space \mathcal{A} is closed.
Now consider the subset of functions taking values in probability measures:
\mathcal{C}_{P}=\{F\in\mathcal{C}:F(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)\ \forall(\mu,\nu)\}.
we observe that \mathcal{C}_{P} is convex since \mathcal{P}(X\times Y) it is.
Proposition 4.2.
The convex set \mathcal{C}_{P} is closed.
Observe that the space of coupling systems \mathcal{A_{P}}=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{C}_{P} is the intersection between those previous spaces. Thus,
Corollary 4.3.
The space of coupling systems \mathcal{A_{P}} is convex and closed.

5 Sinkhorn Operator
We now show that entropy-regularized optimal transport provides a rich and stable class
of coupling systems. Since this problem is very closely related to the attention mechanism, we will use it as final layer in our proposed architecture.
It is worth highliting that in the discrete case this problem is efficiently resolved by the Sinkhorn algorithm (Cuturi, 2013).
Let X,Y be two polish spaces, the entropic regularized optimal transport problem is:
\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\,\text{KL}(\pi\parallel\mu\otimes\nu),
where c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R} is a continuous cost function and KL is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The unique minimizer \pi^{\varepsilon} is called the Sinkhorn plan and admits the factorization (Léonard, 2012):
\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}=u(x)K(x,y)v(y),\quad K(x,y)=e^{-c(x,y)/\varepsilon},
with continuous potentials u,v>0. The mapping S_{c,\varepsilon}:(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\pi^{\varepsilon} defines the Sinkhorn operator. In the following we will usually use \varepsilon=1, and we will note the correspondent Sinkhorn operator as S_{c}.
The Sinkhorn Operator checks the next stability properties:
Proposition 5.1.
(Nutz and Wiesel, 2022a)
The Sinkhorn Operator is Lipschitz with respect to the cost function:
W_{1}\Big(S_{c}(\mu_{,}\nu),\,S_{c^{\prime}}(\mu,\nu)\Big)\;\leq\;L_{\varepsilon}\|c-c^{\prime}\|_{\infty},
where L_{\varepsilon} is independent of \mu,\nu .
Proposition 5.2.
(Nutz and Wiesel, 2022b)
Let \mu_{n}\to\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X) and \nu_{n}\to\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y) then the density of the solution of the optimal transport problem converges uniformly \frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu_{n}\otimes\nu_{n})}\rightrightarrows\frac{d\pi^{\varepsilon}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}.
and we prove that also checks:
Proposition 5.3.
Fix \mu,\nu and let c_{n}\xrightarrow{L_{1}(\mu\otimes\nu)}c\in C(X\times Y) then the solutions of the entropic optimal transport converges weakly \pi_{n}\rightharpoonup\pi.
Proof.
See appendix.
∎
In order to prove the universality of a Transformer architecture with the Sinkhorn operator, we must ensure first that the Sinkhorn operator it is itself universal. As a first step we prove that we can approximate pointwise any transport plan by a entropic optimal transport solution, as we can see in the next lemma:
Lemma 5.4.
Every transport plan \pi in \Pi(\mu,\nu) can be approximated by the solution of of an entropic optimal transport problem, i.e. a transport plan with continuous density with respect to the product measure \mu\otimes\nu.
Sketch of the proof.
The first step is to prove that we can approximate each transport plan \pi for a transport plan \tilde{\pi}\ll\mu\otimes\nu,. Then to ensure continuity we approximate the density for a continuous one in L_{1}(\mu\otimes\nu). Finally to recover \mu,\nu marginals, we approximate it for the solution of the optimal transport problem with cost -\log(f), being f the density. Using the stability of the minimizers with respect to the cost, we conclude the desired lemma.
∎
Using this lemma, we arrive the next important theorem.
Theorem 5.5.
The family of Sinkhorn Operators with continuous costs \{S_{c_{(\mu,\nu)}(x,y)}\} is dense in the space of coupling systems.
Sketch of the proof.
Let F be the semantic coupling, using the Lemma 5.4 we can construct a multivalued function \Gamma that for each \mu,\nu we considered the set of densities of \mu\otimes\nu such that their correspondent measure lives in a small neighborhood of F(\mu,\nu), then by the approximate selection theorem (Deutsch and Kenderov, 1983), we can extract an approximate selection s:P(X)\times P(Y)\to C(X\times Y) of that densities. Finally, using -\log(s) as the cost, and the stability of the Sinkhorn Operator between the costs, we conclude the theorem.
∎
6 The Sinkhorn Transformer
We introduce the Sinkhorn Transformer \mathcal{T}:P(X)\times P(Y)\to P(X\times Y), which consists of three main components:
Figure 3: Sinkhorn Transformer.
Two Transformer-based encoders Q and K that process input measures into query and key embeddings:
\displaystyle Q(\mu,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
\displaystyle K(\nu,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
These encoders are implemented as deep Transformer like in Definition 3.5.
Then we define a cost function based on the dissimilarity between embeddings:
c_{(\mu,\nu)}(x,y)=-\langle Q(\mu,x),K(\nu,y)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.
This generalizes the QK^{\top} affinity in standard attention.
Instead of softmax normalization (which produces conditional probabilities), we apply the Sinkhorn operator to obtain a joint measure:
\mathcal{T}(\mu,\nu)=S_{c(\mu,\nu)}(\mu,\nu).
6.1 Practical Implications and Relation to Standard Transformers
In practical applications, the proposed architecture admits a direct implementation. In the discrete setting, texts correspond to uniform empirical measures (as discussed in Section 3.3), and Transformer encoders are implemented in the standard way. The Sinkhorn operator can be efficiently computed using the Sinkhorn algorithm, resulting in an architecture that closely matches a standard Transformer with a final Sinkhorn normalization applied to the score matrix \langle Q_{i},K_{j}\rangle.
Regarding the processing of inputs, we distinguish two cases. In the single-text setting (i.e., self-attention), both inputs coincide, and the architecture processes the same text through the encoders. This corresponds to a standard Transformer without cross connection between attention heads and MLP of each branch. As illustrated in Figure 4, the architecture follows a classical part of the Transformer architecture, with the Sinkhorn operator applied as a final layer.
Figure 4: Sinkhorn Transformer architecture in the single-text setting.
In the two-text setting, the architecture follows the standard encoder-decoder paradigm: each text is first processed independently, and the interaction between them is introduced at the final stage through the Sinkhorn layer. This plays a role analogous to cross-attention. Figure 5 illustrates the corresponding structure and highlights the part of the standard Transformer architecture that is preserved.
Figure 5: Sinkhorn Transformer architecture in the two-text setting.
We now discuss the role of the Sinkhorn operator compared to the classical softmax attention. The softmax operation produces row-stochastic matrices, corresponding to conditional probability distributions. In contrast, the Sinkhorn operator produces approximately doubly stochastic matrices, enforcing both row and column normalization. This additional structure can be interpreted as promoting balanced interactions between tokens.
Importantly, empirical evidence (e.g., (Tay et al., 2020a)) suggests that attention matrices in trained models are often close to doubly stochastic. The Sinkhorn operator can be efficiently implemented as an iterative normalization procedure, typically converging in a small number of iterations, and is fully differentiable.
In this sense, our architecture remains very close to standard Transformers: it preserves classical attention mechanisms in all intermediate layers and modifies only the final normalization step. This is in contrast with approaches such as (Tay et al., 2020a), which replace all softmax layers with Sinkhorn normalization. This shows that the proposed framework does not depart from standard Transformer architectures, but rather provides a structured reinterpretation of their final interaction mechanism.
7 Main Theorem
Theorem 7.1 (Universal Approximation of Semantic Couplings).
Let X and Y be compact metric spaces, for any F semantic coupling, and any \varepsilon>0, there exists a Sinkhorn Transformer
\mathcal{T}^{*} such that
\sup_{(\mu,\nu)\in P(X)\times P(Y)}W_{1}\bigl(\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mu,\nu),F(\mu,\nu)\bigr)<\varepsilon.
sketch of the proof.
Let F be a coupling system, the first step is to approximate it using Theorem 5.5 with a Sinkhorn operator, then by Stone-Weirstrass theorem, we can approximate the cost c_{(\mu,\nu)}(x,y) by a dot-product between two functions \langle Q(\mu,x),K(\nu,y)\rangle. Finally using Transformers universality for in context functions (Furuya et al., 2024) we can approximate each function by transformers.
∎
Our result shows that a single Sinkhorn Transformer can approximate arbitrary joint relations between texts, i.e., it can represent all contextual relations simultaneously as it is approximating the whole coupling system.
8 Conclusion and Future Work
We introduced a measure-theoretic framework for contextual representations in which attention mechanisms are modeled as operators that assign coupling measures between semantic distributions. Within this framework, we established a universal approximation result characterizing the expressive power of Transformer-style attention mechanisms at the level of contextual relations.
Beyond the formal result, our analysis provides a principled interpretation of semantic representation in attention-based models. Rather than treating attention weights as heuristic similarity scores, our framework views semantic understanding as the ability to represent structured probabilistic relations between meanings, formalized as coupling measures with prescribed marginals.
Our work focuses on representational expressivity and does not address questions of learning dynamics, statistical efficiency, or generalization. Understanding how such coupling-valued representations can be learned from finite data, and under what conditions they arise in practice, remains an open problem.
Several directions for future work naturally emerge from our results. First, it would be of interest to derive quantitative approximation rates and sample complexity bounds for coupling-valued attention mechanisms. Second, extending the present framework to sequential or dynamical settings could provide insight into the role of attention in autoregressive and generative models. Finally, exploring alternative regularization schemes beyond entropic optimal transport may lead to richer classes of contextual relations while preserving analytical tractability.
9 Impact Statement
This paper presents theoretical work whose primary goal is to advance the mathematical understanding of Transformer architectures and attention mechanisms. In particular, it develops a rigorous framework connecting contextual attention with optimal transport and establishes a universal approximation result for semantic coupling functions.
The results are foundational in nature and do not introduce new models, training procedures, datasets, or deployment strategies. As such, the work does not pose direct ethical risks nor immediate societal consequences beyond those already well established in the broader field of machine learning.
More broadly, improved theoretical understanding of widely used architectures may contribute in the long term to the development of more interpretable, reliable, and principled learning systems. Any potential societal impact of this work is therefore indirect and mediated by future applications and deployments, which fall outside the scope of the present paper.
