Abstract
The attention mechanism has been the core component in modern transformer architectures. However, the computation of standard full attention scales quadratically with the sequence length, serving as a major bottleneck in long-context language modeling. Sliding window attention restricts the context length for better efficiency at the cost of narrower receptive fields. While existing efforts attempt to take the benefits from both sides by building hybrid models, they often resort to static, heuristically designed alternating patterns that limit efficient allocation of computation in various scenarios.
In this paper, we propose Switch Attention (SwiAttn), a novel hybrid transformer that enables dynamic and fine-grained routing between full attention and sliding window attention. For each token at each transformer layer, SwiAttn dynamically routes the computation to either a full-attention branch for global information aggregation or a sliding-window branch for efficient local pattern matching. An adaptive regularization objective is designed to encourage the model towards efficiency. Moreover, we adopt continual pretraining to optimize the model, transferring the full attention architecture to the hybrid one.
Extensive experiments are conducted on twenty-three benchmark datasets across both regular (4K) and long (32K) context lengths, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed method.

1 Introduction
The transformer architectures Vaswani et al. (2017); Dai et al. (2019); Kitaev et al. (2020) have been widely adopted in recent large language models (LLMs) Anil et al. (2023); OpenAI (2023); Singh et al. (2025). Despite their success in language modeling, standard transformer architectures suffer from quadratic computational complexity due to the attention mechanism Vaswani et al. (2017). To address this limitation, efforts have been made in reducing the compuation of standard full attention Shazeer (2019); Dao et al. (2022); Ainslie et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024a), sparsifying full attention Child et al. (2019); Kitaev et al. (2020); Liu et al. (2022a); Zhang et al. (2023); Xiao et al. (2023); Song et al. (2024); Xu et al. (2025); Yuan et al. (2025), developing sub-quadratic attention mechanisms Katharopoulos et al. (2020); Ma et al. (2021); Han et al. (2023); Peng et al. (2023a); Yang et al. (2024); Gu and Dao (2024); Peng et al. (2025); Guo et al. (2025a), and designing hybrid transformer models Beltagy et al. (2020); Zaheer et al. (2020); Jiang et al. (2023); Fu et al. (2024); Du et al. (2025).
Figure 1: Conventional hybrid transformers (a) often adopt manually designed, static hybrid schemes. In contrast, the proposed SwiAttn (b) dynamically selects the type of attention for each input token at each transformer layer, enabling fine-grained allocation of computation.
Among these solutions, hybrid transformer models leverage both standard full attention and its efficient variants, e.g., sliding window attention (SWA, Beltagy et al. 2020), and have been widely adopted in larger industrial models Gemma (2024); Yang et al. (2025); OpenAI (2025); Gao et al. (2026). While they balance efficiency and performance of language modeling, most hybrid transformers adopt a static hybrid scheme (e.g., alternating between full attention and sliding window attention with a fixed ratio) designed heuristically prior to the pretraining stage Brown et al. (2020); Jiang et al. (2023); Singh et al. (2025); Du et al. (2025). This limits the model’s potential to allocate computation more efficiently. For example, when the context beyond the range of a sliding window is less relevant to the response that the model is about to generate, one would expect a lower ratio of expensive standard full attention, and vice versa.
Towards this end, this paper proposes a novel hybrid attention mechanism named Switch Attention (SwiAttn). As is shown in Figure 1, the proposed SwiAttn is a dynamic and fine-grained hybrid solution compared to prior efforts. In particular, SwiAttn inserts a router before each attention module and dynamically decides whether the input feature should be processed by standard full attention or sliding window attention for each token at each transformer layer. The full attention branch gathers long-range information accurately from the entire sequence, while the sliding window branch models short-term memory efficiently from a narrower context. The router dynamically decides the specific branch for each token at each layer based on the input feature. In order to encourage the router towards the more efficient branch (i.e., the SWA branch), we adopt a regularization objective that is optimized with the log-likelihood loss. Additionally, to efficiently transfer the knowledge from pretrained full attention models, we utilize continual pretraining (CPT, Sun et al. 2020; Mendieta et al. 2023; Yıldız et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025b) to convert the standard full attention architecture to the proposed SwiAttn. We perform extensive experiments on twenty-three benchmark datasets covering different context lengths against several competing baselines, and the results demonstrate the effectiveness of SwiAttn. The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
❶
New Perspective: We present a dynamic and fine-grained perspective of hybrid transformer models for effective allocation of computation between full attention and its efficient variants.
❷
Novel Methodology: We propose Switch Attention (SwiAttn), consisting of both the full attention branch and the SWA branch, to adaptively allocate computation for each token’s representation at each transformer layer. Additionally, we design an adaptive regularization objective and optimize the model from standard transformers through continual pretraining.
❸
Empirical Validation: We conduct extensive experiments on twenty-three benchmark datasets covering commonsense reasoning, in-context retrieval, and long-context understanding. Further experiments reveal the dynamics of the routing, enabling fine-grained allocation of computation.
2 Related Works
2.1 Efficient Attention Mechanism
The quadratic computational complexity has become a major bottleneck of efficient transformer-based LLMs as input sequences grow longer. This leads to a plethora of efforts in designing efficient attention mechanisms Tay et al. (2022); Sun et al. (2025). One line of research attempts to reduce the computation of the standard full attention itself by sharing Shazeer (2019); Ainslie et al. (2023) and compressing Ge et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024a); Liu et al. (2024b); Liu et al. (2024c) the key and value vectors, or optimizing hardware compatibility Dao et al. (2022); Chang et al. (2025); Sanovar et al. (2025). Another line of work designs mechanisms to sparsify the standard full attention through heuristic Zaheer et al. (2020); Xiao et al. (2023) or data-dependent patterns Xu et al. (2025); Nawrot et al. (2025); Lai et al. (2025); Yuan et al. (2025). To circumvent the quadratic complexity, efforts are made in employing sub-quadratic attention Yang et al. (2024); Han et al. (2024); Dao and Gu (2024); Peng et al. (2025); Guo et al. (2025a); Kimi et al. (2025). However, their efficiency comes at the expense of degraded long-context performance, as their memory is limited by the size of the state matrices Guo et al. (2025a); Nazari and Rusch (2026); Sun et al. (2026). Conceivably, efficiency often comes at a price, and different mechanisms might be suitable for distinct scenarios, motivating the design of hybrid transformer models.

The selection result is then processed by a feed-forward network (FFN Layer). During the decoding stage (b), the router is used to decide the branch for attention computation. The full attention branch and the SWA branch share a unified KV cache for better efficiency.
2.2 Hybrid Transformers
Hybrid transformer models have been widely adopted to enhance the efficiency of LLMs Jiang et al. (2023); Gemma (2024); Yang et al. (2025). Most hybrid transformers consists of both standard full attention and its efficient variants, such as sliding window Child et al. (2019), sparse Zaheer et al. (2020), or linear attention Yang et al. (2024); Kimi et al. (2025). A common practice among existing designs is to interleave full and efficient attention layers (e.g., Figure 1(a)) according to a static ratio empirically defined before training Wang et al. (2025); OpenAI (2025); Xiao et al. (2026). Given the heuristic nature of these methods, efforts are made in automated search for optimal hybrid configurations under certain data distributions Gu et al. (2025); Acun et al. (2025); Xia et al. (2026). Nevertheless, most existing approaches adhere to a static hybrid scheme, either heuristically defined Kitaev et al. (2020); Kimi et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2025) or searched with data Gu et al. (2025); Liu and Wang (2025), while recent works suggest distinct difficulty in generating tokens Leviathan et al. (2023); Cai et al. (2024); Gloeckle et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2024a); Fu et al. (2025). Therefore, this work proposes a dynamic and fine-grained hybrid transformer to efficiently allocate computation across tokens and transformer layers.
3 Methodology
3.1 Preliminaries and Framework Overview
Preliminaries and Notations. We consider the standard language modeling task. During the pretraining stage, the input sentence is first tokenized and then transformed to the hidden representations \bm{H}^{(1)}=[\bm{h}_{1}^{(1)},\bm{h}_{2}^{(1)},...,\bm{h}_{T}^{(1)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}, where T is the sequence length and d is the hidden dimension size. At the l-th transformer layer, the input hidden representations \bm{H}^{(l)} is mapped into queries, keys and values through a linear mapping \phi, i.e., [\bm{Q}^{(l)},\bm{K}^{(l)},\bm{V}^{(l)}]=\phi(\bm{H}^{(l)}). Attention (standard full attention or a more efficient one) is computed using \bm{Q}^{(l)},\bm{K}^{(l)},\bm{V}^{(l)} to obtain the attended output \bm{O}^{(l)}. Finally, a feed-forward network is applied to obtain the input hidden representations (i.e., \bm{H}^{(l+1)}) of the next transformer layer. The final hidden representation \bm{H}^{(L+1)} (where L is the number of transformer layers) is used to predict the next token. During the decoding stage, the model generates one token each time with the current query \bm{q}_{t}^{(l)}, key \bm{k}_{t}^{(l)} and value \bm{v}_{t}^{(l)}, and uses KV cache \{\bm{k}_{i}^{(l)},\bm{v}_{i}^{(l)}\}_{i=1}^{t-1} to store previously computed key and value vectors. 11
1
For simplicity, we omit some details here and in the following, such as layer normalization, residual connection, position embeddings, attention heads, etc.
Framework Overview. The proposed SwiAttn is a dynamic and fine-grained hybrid of standard full attention and SWA, as illustrated in Figure 2. At each transformer layer during the continual pretraining stage, both full attention and SWA are computed using the shared queries, keys and values. A router is applied to the input hidden representations to decide which branch to use for each token. The attended outputs from the two branches are then gathered and processed by a feed-forward layer. An adaptive regularization objective is applied to the output of each router to encourage the model towards the SWA branch. During the decoding stage, the router is first applied to the hidden representation to decide the branch for computing, and the model only computes the selected branch using a shared KV cache.
3.2 Dual-branch Routing
The proposed SwiAttn consists of two branches: the full attention branch and the SWA branch. Standard full attention excels in capturing long-range dependencies, while SWA efficiently computes attended output using local contexts. Not all tokens require global information at all transformer layers: there is a natural sparsity in both layer Yang et al. (2025); Kimi et al. (2025); Gao et al. (2026) and token Leviathan et al. (2023); Cai et al. (2024); Fu et al. (2024); Fu et al. (2025) dimensions. Therefore, we use a router function to decide the specific branch to use.
Concretely, given the input hidden representation \bm{H}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T} at the l-th layer, we first compute the soft gate using the router function \mathcal{R} as:
\tilde{\sigma}^{(l)}=\operatorname{sigmoid}\left(\mathcal{R}(\bm{H}^{(l)})\right)\in[0,1]^{T},
(1)
where \operatorname{sigmoid}(\cdot) is the sigmoid function. The (hard) gate indicating the path of computation for each token at the l-th layer, \sigma^{(l)}\in\{0,1\}^{T}, is then obtained by applying a threshold \tau=0.5, i.e.,
\sigma^{(l)}=\mathds{1}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{(l)}>\tau\right)+\tilde{\sigma}^{(l)}-\operatorname{sg}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{(l)}\right),
(2)
where \mathds{1}(\cdot) is an element-wise indicator function and \operatorname{sg}(\cdot) is the stop gradient function. Note that we adopt straight-through estimation (STE, Fedus et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022b; Huh et al. 2023) to enable differentiation of the router’s parameters during training. The gate is used to control whether a corresponding token should be computed with full (when the value is 1) or sliding window attention (when the value is 0). Subsequently, we compute both the full attention branch and the SWA branch using a shared set of queries, keys and values, i.e.,
\displaystyle[\bm{Q}^{(l)},\bm{K}^{(l)},\bm{V}^{(l)}]=\phi(\bm{H}^{(l)}),
(3)
\displaystyle\bm{O}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)}=\mathcal{A}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)}(\bm{Q}^{(l)},\bm{K}^{(l)},\bm{V}^{(l)}),
(4)
\displaystyle\bm{O}_{\text{SWA}}^{(l)}=\mathcal{A}_{\text{SWA}}^{(l)}(\bm{Q}^{(l)},\bm{K}^{(l)},\bm{V}^{(l)}),
(5)
where \mathcal{A}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)} and \mathcal{A}_{\text{SWA}}^{(l)} denote full attention and SWA, respectively. The gate is then used to select the output of attention \bm{O}^{(l)}, i.e.,
\bm{O}^{(l)}=\sigma^{(l)}\odot\bm{O}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)}+(1-\sigma^{(l)})\odot\bm{O}_{\text{SWA}}^{(l)},
(6)
where \odot denotes element-wise product (in the sequence length dimension). Finally, the feed-forward network (FFN) is applied to obtain the hidden representation at the (l+1)-th layer.
During inference, the attention output is computed similarly. At the prefill stage, the attention output can be computed via Eq.1-6, and especially, the computation of the two branches could be further merged with a hardware-efficient implementation. At the decode stage, we compute the (hard) gate for the t-th token at the l-th layer as a scaler \sigma^{(l)}_{t}\in\{0,1\}, and then compute the full attention or SWA accordingly. Notably, in the decoding stage, both branches share a single set of KV-cache to increase inference efficiency. We summarize the inference procedure of SwiAttn in Algorithm 1.

3.3 Adaptive Regularization
In the dual-branch routing framework, the full attention branch provides richer information than the SWA branch. Therefore, to encourage the model towards the SWA branch for efficiency, we also design an adaptive regularization objective that is optimized during continual pretraining. Conceivably, when the ground-truth token exhibits lower log-likelihood under the predicted distribution, the model may struggle to predict this token, and therefore, we expect higher ratios for the more powerful full attention branch. Additionally, when the two branches disagree, we also tend toward the more reliable full attention branch. These require the regularization objective to be more adaptive.
Specifically, given the soft gate of the t-th token at the l-th transformer layer \tilde{\sigma}_{t}^{(l)}, the regularization objective is designed as follows:
\mathcal{L}_{t}^{(l)}=\gamma_{t}^{(l)}\operatorname{sp}\left(\mathcal{R}(\bm{h}_{t}^{(l)})\right),
(7)
where \gamma_{t}^{(l)} is the adaptive weight of regularization discussed in the following, and \operatorname{sp}(x)=\ln(1+e^{x}) is the softplus function, a smooth version of the ReLU function Nair and Hinton (2010), providing a small penalty to values less than 0 and increasing penalty to values greater than 0. The adaptive weight \gamma_{t}^{(l)} of the t-th token at the l-th layer is determined by several factors: (1) the base weight \gamma_{\text{base}}, (2) the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the ground-truth token under the currently predicted distribution of language modeling, i.e., \operatorname{NLL}(t), and (3) the disagreement of the two branches as measured by the mean squared error (MSE), i.e., \lVert\bm{o}_{\text{FULL}}^{(t,l)}-\bm{o}_{\text{SWA}}^{(t,l)}\rVert^{2}_{2}, where \bm{o}_{\text{FULL}}^{(t,l)} and \bm{o}_{\text{SWA}}^{(t,l)} denotes the output of the full attention branch and the SWA branch of the t-th token at the l-th layer. Considering these factors, the adaptive weight \gamma_{t}^{(l)} can be written as follows:
\gamma_{t}^{(l)}=\frac{\gamma_{\text{base}}}{\varepsilon+\operatorname{NLL}(t)+\alpha\cdot\lVert\bm{o}_{\text{FULL}}^{(t,l)}-\bm{o}_{\text{SWA}}^{(t,l)}\rVert^{2}_{2}},
(8)
where \varepsilon and \alpha are hyper-parameters.
Remark. Eq.8 adaptively reweight the regularization objective of the router. The hyperparameter \varepsilon avoids division by zero and provides an upper bound of the weight, i.e., \gamma_{t}^{(l)}\leq\gamma_{\text{base}}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}. The NLL and MSE terms reduce the weight of regularization when the token prediction loss increases (high NLL) and/or the two branches diverge (high MSE). Under such conditions, the reduced regularization weights encourage the router to use fewer SWA branches and more full attention branches, since the SWA branch may be less reliable.

3.4 Continual Pretraining
Different from many hybrid transformers that require training from scratch, the proposed SwiAttn adopts a continual pretraining approach, utilizing the learned knowledge from an existing off-the-shelf full attention transformer.
In particular, given a standard transformer and its full attention module at the l-th layer \tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)}, we initialize parameters of both the full attention branch {\mathcal{A}}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)} and the SWA branch {\mathcal{A}}_{\text{SWA}}^{(l)} of SwiAttn using the parameters of \tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{FULL}}^{(l)}. Other parameters like the FFN layer are also inherited from the standard pretrained transformer. During the continual pretraining stage, we train the entire hybrid transformer using both the language modeling objective \mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}} and the aforementioned adaptive regularization objective, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}+\frac{1}{LT}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}^{(l)}.
(9)
By continual pretraining from an existing standard transformer, we save computation resources and stabilize the optimization process. The CPT process of SwiAttn is presented in Algorithm 2.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setups
Datasets. Three types of datasets are used. \blacktriangleright Commonsense Reasoning, including Lambada (LMB., Paperno et al. 2016), Wikitext (Wiki., Merity et al. 2016), BoolQ Clark et al. (2019), PIQA Bisk et al. (2020), SIQA Sap et al. (2019), HellaSwag (Hella., Zellers et al. 2019), WinoGrande (Wino., Sakaguchi et al. 2021), ARC-easy and ARC-challenge (ARC-e and ARC-c, Clark et al. 2018), and OpenBookQA (OBQA, Mihaylov et al. 2018); \blacktriangleright In-Context Retrieval from Arora et al. 2024, including SWDE Lockard et al. (2019), FDA Arora et al. (2023), TriviaQA (TQA, Joshi et al. 2017), NQ Kwiatkowski et al. (2019), DROP Dua et al. (2019), and SQuAD (SQD, Rajpurkar et al. 2018); \blacktriangleright Long-Context Understanding from LongBench-E Bai et al. (2024), including MultiFieldQA (MFQA, Bai et al. 2024), TRec Li and Roth (2002), MultiNews (MNs, Fabbri et al. 2019), GovReport (GvR, Huang et al. 2021), Qasper (Qas, Dasigi et al. 2021), TriviaQA (TQA, Joshi et al. 2017), and SAMSum (SSM, Gliwa et al. 2019). Since we are experimenting with a 1.5B model, we only include datasets that yield reasonable scores. More details can be found in Appendix A.
Baselines. We compare the proposed SwiAttn against several baselines, including sliding window attention (both zero-shot and continual pretraining versions, marked as SWA-ZS and SWA-CPT), the static hybrid of full attention and SWA commonly used in larger industrial LLMs Brown et al. (2020); OpenAI (2025); Xiao et al. (2026) (with ratio of 1:3, marked as StaticHybrid), and full attention (marked as FullAttn). More details about baseline implementation can be found in Appendix B.
Implementation Details. For the transformer architecture, we use a 1.5B language model with 22 transformer layers (i.e., L=22). Each layer contains a dual-branch routing module and an FFN layer. The router function \mathcal{R}(\cdot) is implemented with a linear mapping, and the threshold \tau is set to 0.5. For the SWA branch, the window size is set to 1024. In adaptive regularization, we set the base weight \gamma_{\text{base}} to 1\times 10^{-3}, the \varepsilon to 0.1 and the weight \alpha to 100. For model optimization, we first pretrain the 1.5B full attention model on 4K-length sequences using 240B tokens and then continually pretrain the SwiAttn model for 20B tokens. To evaluate tasks of longer sequences, we optimize the 32K full attention model by pretraining on 4K context lengths for 192B tokens and then extending the context length using YaRN Peng et al. (2023b) to 32K with 24B tokens. The corresponding SwiAttn is obtained by continual pretraining on the full attention model for another 8B tokens. More details can be found in Appendix C.

4.2 Main Results
We compare the performance of SwiAttn against baselines on 28 benchmark datasets, and the results are shown in Table 1, Table 2 and Table 3.
Commonsense Reasoning. We present the results on commonsense reasoning datasets in Table 1. As the contexts of most instances are within the sliding window size (i.e., 1024), the performance of SwiAttn is similar to the baselines, including both full attention and SWA models.
In-Context Retrieval. The results on in-context retrieval datasets are presented in Table 2. For these datasets, most contexts are within the 4096 token limit of 4K-length models, and some instances require attention spans longer than the sliding window size. The results show that full attention and hybrid models (including SwiAttn) outperform SWA baselines by a large margin on average. Additionally, the proposed SwiAttn achieves a relative improvement of \bm{27.5\%} compared to SWA-CPT and \bm{4.5\%} compared to StaticHybrid, reaching the same average score as FullAttn.
Long-Context Understanding. The results on long-context understanding datasets are shown in Table 3. For these datasets, the contexts of most instances grows beyond the 1024 window size and can be as long as the maximum context of 32K, which requires strong long-context capability of the models. As can be seen from the results, SwiAttn achieves the best average performance among the baseline methods, surpassing all baselines on average. Particularly, SwiAttn achieves a relative improvement of \bm{6.3\%} compared to the StaticHybrid baseline, despite using full attention less frequently (as we will show in Section 4.4).

4.3 Ablation Studies
To investigate the effect of different mechanisms in the proposed method, we design several variants of the original SwiAttn. Specifically, V1 disables adaptive regularization and uses \gamma_{\text{base}} instead. V2 removes the NLL term in Eq.8. V3, V4, and V5 changes \alpha from 100 to 10, 1, and 0, respectively. V6 and V7 perturb \varepsilon from 0.1 to 0.2 and 0.01. We present results on three summarization datasets from LongBench-E in Table 4.
Effect of Adaptive Regularization. From V1, V2, and V5, we can see that adaptive regularization, along with several factors in adaptive weights, is effective. The removal of adaptive regularization reduces average performance by 1.3. Besides, when the NLL (V2) or MSE (V5) terms are removed, the performance also declines.
Effects of Hyperparameters. V3 and V4 show the effect of the hyperparameter of \alpha. When \alpha is changed, the accuracy drops, as \alpha balances the relative importance of the MSE term in adaptive regularization. Variants V6 and V7 show the effect of \varepsilon. The model is less sensitive to this hyperparameter, and when \varepsilon is perturbed, the accuracy decreases slightly. \varepsilon controls the upper bound of the adaptive weights, which affects the optimization process.

Inference Efficiency. We compare the inference efficiency of SwiAttn and the FullAttn baseline. For the prefill stage, we plot the amount of computation required (in terms of GFLOPs) at every token position, following Liu et al. 2025, and the results are shown in Figure 4(a). For the decode stage, we measure efficiency with the amount of memory access of the KV cache (in terms of the number of tokens at each layer on average), as it is the primary bottleneck of the decoding speed of attention. As the efficiency of SwiAttn is related to the data distribution, we provide results on both standard (under the same distribution of training corpus) and simple (under the distribution of needle-in-a-haystack task where the needle is within the sliding window) setups in Figure 5(a). As can be seen from the results, the proposed SwiAttn achieves over \bm{4\times} speedup at 32K context lengths under standard data distribution and as much as \bm{12\times} speedup for decoding under simple data distributions.
Ratios of Full Attention across Layers. We then investigate how different transformer layers behave in dual-branch routing. Specifically, we collect the routing decisions of each transformer layer and calculate the ratio of the model choosing full attention under the 32K continual pretraining data distribution and count the decisions of 400M tokens. The results are presented in Figure 5. As can be seen from the results, different transformer layers exhibit distinct patterns of routing: some layers (e.g., Layer 1, 2, 3, 22) tend to use more SWA branches while others (e.g., Layer 8, 11, 14) use full attention branches more frequently. This suggests different layers specialize in different functionalities. Additionally, we also compute the averaged full attention ratio of all layers, and the result is around 0.13, which is lower than the 0.25 ratio of conventional static hybrid models with one full attention layer after three SWA layers.
Dynamics in Dual-Branch Routing. We also investigate the dynamics of dual-branch routing. When the next token can be easily predicted based on information within the sliding window, we expect the model to use the SWA branch more frequently, and when the next token is hard to forecast with local information, the full attention branch should be more frequently used as a default choice. To verify whether SwiAttn behaves in a computation efficient way, we resort to the NIAH retrieval task, where needles to be retrieved are inserted in different locations of the context (i.e., different depth percent, higher percents indicate closer distance to the end of the prompt), and measure the full attention ratio of SwiAttn when generating the answer (i.e., the inserted needle). The results are presented in Figure 6, where we illustrate the ratio under different context lengths (i.e., 1.9K, 2.8K, 3.7K, 4.6K, 30.2K). The results show that when the needle is inserted in lower depth percentages (outside the sliding window), the model chooses around 30% of full attention, while when the model is closer to the end of the prompt (within the sliding window), a lower ratio of full attention around 5% is observed. This demonstrates that SwiAttn can dynamically and efficiently decide the specific branch to use based on the context.

5 Conclusion
This paper presents Switch Attention (SwiAttn), a novel hybrid transformer that can dynamically route the hidden representation to either full attention or sliding window attention at each transformer layer, enabling efficient and fine-grained allocation of computation based on the contexts. SwiAttn can be optimized via continual pretraining from off-the-shelf standard full attention transformers. An adaptive regularization objective is designed to enhance efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed SwiAttn, and further empirical results reveal its dynamics and efficiency.
Limitations
One limitation of this work is the lack of a hardware-efficient implementation for computing the two branches during the prefill stage. Another limitation is that we have not verified the effectiveness of the method on larger models beyond 1.5B parameters due to limited computational resources. In the future, we plan to develop hardware-efficient kernels and verify the method on larger models.
