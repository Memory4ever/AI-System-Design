
I Introduction


Diffusion Models (DMs) [1, 2, 3, 4]
evidenced advancements in generating high-quality images compared to other generative models such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Variational Auto-encoders (VAEs).
Due to the flexibility and power of DMs,
DMs have been used in many areas including computer vision, natural language generation, temporal data modeling, and multi-model learning [5]. The Federated Learning (FL) paradigm originated in [6] has gained substantial attention in distributed learning by connecting data islands with collaborative training over distributed clients, e.g., edge devices, roadside units, edge micro-servers, while preserving privacy.
For instance, FL-based Google’s Gboard learns new words and phrases without exposing clients’ privacy.


With the development of DMs and FL, their integration provided a two-fold benefit [7]. FL mitigates data scarcity during DMs training by engaging distributed clients at the network edge or organizations [7].
On the other hand, DMs are powerful for high-resolution image generation, which can be deployed as data augmentation techniques to cope with data heterogeneity and high communication costs for downstream tasks in FL [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].


Fig. 1: FID score comparison over CIFAR-10 data using DDIM [2] via FedAvg FL infrastructure and centralized training with IID and Non-IID data (number of clients is K=20K=20, selection ratio is κ=0.2\kappa=0.2; more details are provided in Section V-A).


Recent efforts focus on leveraging DMs to solve challenges in FL, however, disregarding the reverse side, i.e., the issues when training DM in FL environments. Although training DMs is an unsupervised learning task, it faces challenges in FL, such as data heterogeneity, i.e., non-IID data [14, 15, 16], due to e.g., the bias and divergence of gradients in traditional supervised learning task [17, 18, 19].
The performance degradation caused by such divergence
is shown in Fig. 1.
Specifically, consider the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) score, which assesses the quality of images created by a generative model; lower scores indicate that the generated images are more similar to real images w.r.t. quality.
Fig. 1 shows the FID score over the CIFAR-10 dataset using the Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model (DDIM [2]) in a FL environment adopting FedAvg [6].
One can observe the FID scores across different training epochs for FedAvg with IID data (independent and identically distributed)
and non-IID data compared against centralized training.
FedAvg using IID and non-IID data shows higher FID scores than the centralized approach, with non-IID data exhibiting significantly higher scores due to statistical heterogeneity.


Limited research copes with the challenges of training DMs in FL environments. The Phoenix mechanism in [14] aims to solve the non-IID data challenge by data sharing, which is not applicable in distributed learning environments.
The authors in [15] focus on training subsets of ML models while ‘freezing’ the rest thus achieving communication efficiency in distributed learning. The approach in [16] investigates the potential of training DMs for privacy-sensitive vision tasks.
In this context, a research question arises: How can we obtain DMs that can generate relatively high-quality images efficiently trained in distributed learning environments?


The results in Fig. 1 and [20] suggest that more frequent model aggregation mitigates the weight divergence problem over non-IID data.
Therefore, we introduce FedPhD, a novel framework that addresses data heterogeneity, excessive communication, and computation costs through Hierarchical Federated Learning (HFL) and distributed structured pruning. The FedPhD frameworkemploys HFL by introducing the layer, the edge server layer, to the traditional Cloud server-based FL framework.
This additional layer engages edge servers between edge devices (clients) and the central server. Due to the proximity of edge servers to clients, frequent aggregation at the edge servers becomes feasible. To further reduce computation and communication costs,
FedPhD leverages structured pruning for the U-Net (a convolutional neural network introduced for image segmentation)
used in the diffusion process. Depending on devices’ requirements and purposes, the structured pruning can be initially randomized or based on the L2L_{2} group-norm after the initial sparse training stage. Moreover, FedPhD introduces an edge-server selection mechanism in HFL to ensure data diversity and discrepancy-aware aggregation. The major contributions of this paper are:



• 

We demonstrate that frequent model aggregation addresses data heterogeneity in FL of DMs. This led to the introduction of our hierarchical FL paradigm, FedPhD.

• 

We introduce a new edge-server selection and model aggregation strategy to ensure data homogeneity alleviating the non-IID data problem in HFL.

• 

We integrate a structured pruning method for DMs into the HFL framework, significantly reducing communication and computation costs.

• 

FedPhD achieves the best Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Inception Scores (IS) with 44%44\% less communication, computation, and storage requirements compared to well-known baselines [6], [21], [15], [22], [23].





II Related Work


II-A Training Diffusion Models in FL


A few studies consider training DMs within FL environments.
Those that achieve distributed DM training often provide a basic framework without addressing the key challenges of non-IID data and efficiency. [24] compared the advantages and disadvantages between federated Foundation Models (FM) and centralized FM optimization, generalizing the FM of collaborative learning. [15] customize the FedDiffuse method into USPLIT, ULATDEC, and UDEC within the concept of split learning, which trains DMs with FedAvg [6] to split or limit the updates of parameters. This approach involves a central server collecting only parts of the DMs, thus reducing communication costs.
Phoenix in [14] is a DM learning framework designed to address non-IID data using a data-sharing policy and layer-wise personalization.



II-B Diffusion Models Compression


DMs, though with powerful generative capabilities for high-quality images, are computationally intensive and memory-demanding, raising notable obstacles for their deployments on edge devices and clients.
To address these concerns, various strategies have been proposed.
Diff-Pruning [25] is a pioneering method designed specifically for pruning DMs.
This approach relies on Taylor expansion across pruned time steps acknowledging that not all time steps are equally important in DMs.
[26] introduced VQ-Diffusion, a vector-quantized diffusion method based on a
Vector-Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) for text-to-image generation. [27] proposed a 8-bit post-training quantization technique to accelerate the DM generation process while accounting for normally distributed time step calibration.
[28] observed that smaller student models introduce more noise during the DM forward process, and thus proposed DiffKD. DiffKD is a knowledge distillation method where a trained teacher model denoises the student model’s features.
To the best of our knowledge, FedPhD is the first method for applying pruning within the FL framework to train DMs in a hierarchical fashion.




III Problem Fundamentals


III-A Background on Diffusion Models


Diffusion Models (DMs) are latent variable models inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics.
[1] proposed the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM), which leverages the diffusion process and reverse process to mimic the image generation process.
The model progressively adds Gaussian noise to an image in the forward process and then iteratively denoises the image in the reverse process to recover the image from white noise.

III-B Hierarchical Federated Learning with DMs


Without loss of generality, we consider a distributed learning system consisting of one server, a set of 𝒩e\mathcal{N}_{e} edge servers, and a set of 𝒩\mathcal{N} distributed clients (edge devices),
where |𝒩e|=Ne≪N=|𝒩|\lvert\mathcal{N}_{e}\rvert=N_{e}\ll N=\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert.
In each communication round, C=κ​NC=\kappa N clients are selected for aggregation in a typical FL system, like FedAvg, where C≪NC\ll N and κ∈(0,1)\kappa\in(0,1) is the participation ratio.
Each client, indexed by nn, holds local data 𝒟n={(𝐱i(n),yi(n))}\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(n)},y_{i}^{(n)})\}, where 𝐱i(n)∈ℝd\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and yi(n)y_{i}^{(n)} is the label. The total number of samples in client nn is Dn=|𝒟n|D_{n}=\lvert\mathcal{D}_{n}\rvert.

IV The FedPhD Framework

Homogeneity-aware aggregation is achieved on both: Cloud server and Edge servers to improve model consistency across diverse environments.
Structured pruning occurs only on the Cloud server after RsR_{s} sparse training rounds (or at initialization). Clients perform local training and select Edge servers establishing client-edge associations n∈ℳen\in\mathcal{M}_{e}. Every rgr_{g} round, active Edge servers connect to Cloud for central aggregation, uploading accumulated distributions qe​(y)q_{e}(y) and models θer\mathbf{\theta}_{e}^{r}; while every rer_{e} round, selected clients upload updates θnr\mathbf{\theta}_{n}^{r} and client distribution qn​(y)q_{n}(y) to their assigned Edge servers.


FedPhD aims to efficiently train high-performance DMs in a HFL framework. As stated in ShapeFL [20], imbalanced data cause instability and divergence in HFL-based training,
which advocates maximizing the data diversity in aggregation.
Hence, considering the computational and communication efficiency in the edge nodes, FedPhD leverages structured pruning rather than unstructured pruning to achieve efficient compression. Subsequently, we introduce a homogeneity-aware aggregation and edge server selection mechanism to ensure data homogeneity in HFL. In the remainder of this section, we elaborate on the two main mechanisms of FedPhD.


IV-A Structured Pruning for DMs


Structured pruning removes entire groups, such as channels or layers, ensuring better CPU/GPU compatibility and more efficient model deployment than unstructured pruning.
DepGraph [29] identifies layer dependencies to guide structured pruning, making it adaptable to any model architecture. Diff-Pruning [25] builds on DepGraph but assumes pre-trained DMs, limiting its applicability in FL. FedPhD overcomes this by integrating dependency-aware pruning during decentralized training. DepGraph models dependencies between adjacent layers in a symmetric matrix 𝐒\mathbf{S}, capturing inter-layer and intra-layer relationships. This informs the parameter group 𝒢={θ1,θ2,⋯,θ|G|}\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{\theta}^{1},\mathbf{\theta}^{2},\cdots,\mathbf{\theta}^{\lvert G\rvert}\} for structured pruning.


FedPhD employs two pruning strategies based on hardware constraints:(1)Pruning from scratch (FedPhD (OS)): One-Shot (OS) pruning before training using an L2L_{2}-norm-based criterion for random pruning. (2)Pruning after sparse training: Group norm-based pruning applied after initial training rounds.


For resource-limited clients, FedPhD-OS prunes before training, while clients with more capacity benefit from structured pruning after initial training. The local sparse training loss at client nn is:




Fn​(𝜽)=1Dn​∑(𝐱i(n))∈𝒟nℒ⁡(𝜽,𝐱i(n))+𝛀⁡(𝒢,k),F_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{D_{n}}\sum_{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(n)})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(n)})+\mathbf{\Omega}(\mathcal{G},k),

(16)

where 𝛀⁡(𝒢,k)\mathbf{\Omega}(\mathcal{G},k) denotes a regularization term defined as:




𝛀⁡(𝒢,k)=∑k=1K∑θg∈𝒢λg​‖θg​[k]‖22.\mathbf{\Omega}(\mathcal{G},k)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\mathbf{\theta}^{g}\in\mathcal{G}}\lambda_{g}\|\mathbf{\theta}^{g}[k]\|^{2}_{2}.

(17)

The λg\lambda_{g} factor refers to the group regularization scale parameter, while

at the kthk^{\text{th}} prunable dimension.
Considering the specific U-Net architecture, the redundancy in the middle layers is greater than that in input and output layers. Hence, we introduce a mechanism to determine λg\lambda_{g}.
First, we calculate the mean layer index of θ\mathbf{\theta} as lmidl_{\text{mid}}. For each layer ll in group θg\mathbf{\theta}^{g}, the distance between the mean layer and ll is given by |l−lmed|\lvert l-l_{\text{med}}\rvert. The score for the entire group θg\mathbf{\theta}^{g} is calculated by:

where LL denotes the number of layers in group θg\mathbf{\theta}^{g}.
Therefore, the parameter λg\lambda_{g} for group θg\mathbf{\theta}^{g} is defined as inversely proportional to score Q⁡(θg)Q(\mathbf{\theta}^{g}), i.e., λg=λ0Q⁡(θg)\lambda_{g}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{Q(\mathbf{\theta}^{g})}
where λ0\lambda_{0} is a regularization factor.



IV-B Homogeneity-Aware Aggregation and Server Selection


We propose a novel aggregation method for HFL that addresses data imbalance by leveraging a Statistical Homogeneity (SH) score and an edge selection policy. Specifically, we introduce the concept of homogeneity-aware aggregation, detail the computation of the SH score, and illustrate how it guides both client selection and model aggregation.
ShapeFL [20] measures data diversity using cosine distance between model updates but is computationally expensive and requires manual layer selection, limiting its applicability. Additionally, its edge server selection relies on offline pre-training, which is unsuitable for FL. Instead, we propose a lightweight approach that focuses on data distribution rather than model-specific layers.
Following [30, 31], the uniform distribution serves as a reference point for evaluating statistical data homogeneity. Given local data 𝒟n=(𝐱i,yi)\mathcal{D}_{n}={(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})} on client nn with label distribution qnq_{n}, we define quq_{u} as the uniform distribution over target labels 𝒴\mathcal{Y}. If the global distribution is non-uniform, quq_{u} is set as target label distribution accordingly. The SH score μn\mu_{n} for client nn is:




μn=2−∑y∈𝒴|qn​(y)−qu​(y)|2,\mu_{n}=2-\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left|q_{n}(y)-q_{u}(y)\right|^{2}},

(18)

where qn​(y)q_{n}(y) is the frequency of label yy in client’s dataset and qu​(y)=1|𝒴|q_{u}(y)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|} is the uniform probability of yy.

According to [30] and [17], SH is closely related to data diversity, as it reflects the extent to which the global model approximates the performance of a model trained on IID data. In this context, we aim for a DM that generalizes well on non-IID data by using techniques that maximize SH. However, in HFL, clients are assigned directly to edge servers for aggregation, which makes the SH score at client level in (18) insufficient.
Therefore, we compute the SH score at edge server level for the aggregation on the central server.
In HFL, the accumulated distribution for an edge server is the weighted sum of the label distributions from all clients assigned to that edge server.
This accumulated distribution must be refreshed (re-initialized) dynamically at a specific frequency to maintain an accurate representation of the data of the participating clients. In HFL, aggregation occurs more frequently than in FL, so this refreshment is synchronized with the frequency of FL aggregation, which is denoted by rgr_{g} in this work.
Specifically, given a current accumulated distribution qe​(y)q_{e}(y) and attached client samples nen_{e},
if each client nn contributes nnn_{n} samples to the edge server then the updated accumulated distribution qe′q_{e}^{\prime} for edge server ee is updated as:




qe′​(y)=qe​(y)+∑n∈ℳeqn​(y)⋅nnne+∑n∈ℳenn,q_{e}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{q_{e}(y)+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}_{e}}q_{n}(y)\cdot n_{n}}{n_{e}+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}_{e}}n_{n}},

(19)

where ℳe\mathcal{M}_{e} is the set of clients assigned to edge server ee and qn​(y)q_{n}(y) is the proportion of label yy in client nn’s dataset. By continually updating the accumulated distribution, the edge server maintains an accurate representation of its clients’ data diversity. This ensures that the subsequent model aggregation steps consider the underlying data distribution of participated clients. Consequently, the SH score for edge server ee in HFL is similarly calculated as in (18) by using the accumulated distribution qe​(y)q_{e}(y) in (19), which is computed as:




μe=2−∑y∈𝒴|qe​(ye=y)−qu​(yu=y)|2,\mu_{e}=2-\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left|q_{e}(y_{e}=y)-q_{u}(y_{u}=y)\right|^{2}},

(20)

where qu​(y)q_{u}(y) represents the target (uniform) distribution at the central server.
Note that neither the central server nor the edge servers possess any raw data.


Based on SH, we develop a strategy for homogeneity-aware aggregation and edge server selection in HFL. The aggregation process takes place at both the central server and edge server levels. Starting at the central server level, each edge server’s contribution to the global model is weighted by a factor that considers both the number of samples it has aggregated and its SH.
Let θe\mathbf{\theta}_{e} denote the model parameters from edge server ee, and let nen_{e} represent the corresponding number of samples. The central server aggregates the global model parameters θ\mathbf{\theta} using
the weighted sum:




θ=∑e=1Neρe⋅θe,\mathbf{\theta}=\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}\rho_{e}\cdot\mathbf{\theta}_{e},

(21)

where ρe\rho_{e} is the weight for edge server ee, calculated as:




ρe=ReLu​(ne+a⋅μe+b)∑e=1NeReLu​(ne+a⋅μe+b).\rho_{e}=\frac{\textit{ReLu}(n_{e}+a\cdot\mu_{e}+b)}{\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}\textit{ReLu}(n_{e}+a\cdot\mu_{e}+b)}.

(22)

μe\mu_{e} represents the SH of the edge server, aa and bb are tunable parameters that balance the contribution of the sample size and SH; ReLu(x)=max⁡(0,x)(x)=\max(0,x).
This approach ensures that edge servers with higher SH, i.e., indicating a distribution closer to
target distribution, contribute more significantly to the global model update, thereby improving the overall performance of the global model on non-IID data [31]. Similarly, the edge server aggregates the edge server model θe\theta_{e} according to:




θe=∑n∈ℳeρe​n​θn,\theta_{e}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}_{e}}\rho_{en}\theta_{n},

(23)

where ρe​n\rho_{en} is the weight for selected clients for aggregation on edge server ee, computed as:




ρe​n=ReLu​(nn+a⋅μn+b)∑n∈ℳeReLu​(nn+a⋅μn+b)\rho_{en}=\frac{\textit{ReLu}(n_{n}+a\cdot\mu_{n}+b)}{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}_{e}}\textit{ReLu}(n_{n}+a\cdot\mu_{n}+b)}

(24)



Note that unlike traditional FL frameworks such as FedAvg, FedPhD allows clients to select their edge servers for aggregation. This selection process is guided by the SH, which evaluates how closely an edge server’s accumulated distribution aligns with the target distribution. For each client nn, the selection of the edge server ee is determined based on a probability distribution, expressed as:




Pn​(e)=ReLu(μen′⋅a−nen′+b)∑e=1NeReLu(μen′⋅a−nen′+b),P_{n}(e)=\frac{\textit{ReLu}\left(\mu_{e}^{n\prime}\cdot a-n_{e}^{n\prime}+b\right)}{\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}\textit{ReLu}\left(\mu_{e}^{n\prime}\cdot a-n_{e}^{n\prime}+b\right)},

(25)

where nen′n_{e}^{n\prime} and μen′\mu_{e}^{n\prime} represent the updated sample size and SH score for edge server ee after including client nn, respectively. The client is then assigned to the edge server ee based on the probability distribution Pn​(e)P_{n}(e), ensuring a higher likelihood of selection for an edge server that better matches the target distribution while also considering the load balance by favoring servers with fewer clients communicated before.



IV-C The FedPhD Algorithm


The workflow of FedPhD is illustrated in Algorithm 1 and Fig. 2. We outline the processes of clients, edge servers, and the central server in the FedPhD algorithm as follows.


IV-C1 Central Server Process


The homogeneity-aware aggregation and structured pruning occur in the central server. If constraints such as memory limit exist for devices, the central server performs random pruning initially (i.e., at r=0r=0); otherwise, the central server performs group-norm based pruning at r=Rsr=R_{s} after RsR_{s} sparse training rounds (lines 24-26). The central server performs aggregation every rgr_{g} rounds iteratively, maximizing the SH score to counter the data heterogeneity issue in HFL (lines 22-23). The model parameters are received from the edge servers, which are the aggregations of respective clients.



IV-C2 Edge Server Process


Edge servers aggregate models more frequently than the central server (re<rgr_{e}<r_{g}, line 19). After aggregation, the aggregated results are returned to their respective clients (line 20). In addition, they maintain and update the accumulated distribution based on the model updates received from clients (line 18).



IV-C3 Client Process


The client is responsible for selecting the edge servers and conducting local training of DMs.
At each round, the client selects the edge server for aggregation based on the probability Pn​(e)P_{n}(e) as described in (25), ensuring a higher SH for aggregation (lines 4-5). If sparse training is required and in initial rounds, i.e., r<Rsr<R_{s}, the group regularization term is added to the local loss function, and the client performs Algorithm 2 on the loss function as in (16); otherwise, it uses the loss function as in (12). The procedure is shown in Algorithm 1 (lines 8-12).


Note, both the label distribution of clients and aggregated distribution of edge servers are necessary to compute SH.
Edge servers track the client distributions, where each edge server ee holds the values qn​(y)q_{n}(y) and nnn_{n} for all clients nn.
As a result, the label distribution does not need to be communicated during the FedPhD training process. For the accumulated (updated) distribution, computed at the edge servers, qe​(y)q_{e}(y) is transmitted to clients during each global round for edge server selection and to the central server every rgr_{g} rounds for homogeneity-aware aggregation. Since only distribution information is shared, FedPhD ensures stronger privacy preservation compared to Phoenix [14], with negligible additional communication overhead.




IV-D Balancing Efficiency and Privacy in FedPhD


FedPhD uses information on the category distribution of client data to perform homogeneity-aware aggregation, which improves the model convergence and eases the data heterogeneity issue. However, sharing these label distributions could potentially expose sensitive information, raising privacy concerns. To mitigate these concerns, we introduce several privacy-preserving strategies that could be integrated into the FedPhD framework: (i) Noise Injection: Using differential privacy methods, such as adding controlled noise to category counts, results in individual data contributions that are less identifiable. (ii) Secure Aggregation: Employing techniques like secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) to combine distributions that prevent the central server from identifying individual client information. Such strategies are proposed to strike a balance between improving the model’s efficiency and protecting the privacy of client data. While these methods are not currently implemented in our FedPhD, they offer a pathway to enhance privacy in our future agenda.

VI Conclusions


Training DM using FL enables access to diverse and distributed data, while the challenges of high communication costs and data heterogeneity persist. FedPhD is a first attempt to address these challenges.
By leveraging Hierarchical FL (HFL), homogeneity-aware aggregation and server selection,
FedPhD mitigates data heterogeneity while incorporating structured pruning of DMs, thus reducing model size and enhancing computational and communication efficiency.
Experimental results demonstrate that FedPhD improves the image quality of DMs by at least 34%34\% while achieving approximately 40%40\% reduction in model size, computation, and communication costs.
Our future agenda includes automatic synchronization of EMA updates in HFL to enhance model convergence balancing computational and communication overhead.
