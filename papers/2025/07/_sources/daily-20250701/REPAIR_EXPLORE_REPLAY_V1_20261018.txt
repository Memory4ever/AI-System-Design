Abstract
Recent advances in reinforcement learning (RL) have significantly enhanced the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an efficient variant of PPO that lowers RL’s computational cost, still faces limited exploration, low sample efficiency and instability, constraining its performance on complex reasoning tasks. To address these limitations, we introduce EFRame, an Exploration-Filtering-Replay framework that systematically augments GRPO along three critical dimensions. EFRame performs additional rollouts to explore high-quality trajectories, applies online filtering to eliminate low-quality samples that introduce noise and variance, and leverages experience replay to repeatedly exploit rare but informative samples. EFRame establishes a complete and stable learning cycle, guiding the model through a structured transition from exploration to convergence. Our experiments across a variety of reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that EFRame not only improves the robustness and efficiency of training, but also enables access to deeper reasoning capabilities that remain unattainable under vanilla GRPO. Furthermore, EFRame enables a more fine-grained categorization of training samples, allowing for a deeper analysis of how different types of samples contribute to the learning process in RL. 1 Introduction
Reinforcement learning (RL) has played a pivotal role in the post-training of large language models (LLMs) GLM et al. (2024); Touvron et al. (2023). In the early stages, approaches such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) were widely adopted under the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), effectively aligning model outputs with human preferences through preference-based rewards Schulman et al. (2017); Rafailov et al. (2023); Zhong et al. (2024); Wang et al. (2024b). More recently, the focus has shifted towards Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), which seeks to provide more consistent and scalable supervision by leveraging structured, verifiable reward signals Mroueh (2025). Within this paradigm, DeepSeek-R1 introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a lightweight and efficient variant of PPO that compute relative rewards within rollout groups to eliminate the need for the critic model Shao et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2024); Guo et al. (2025). GRPO has demonstrated remarkable improvements in training efficiency and has shown promising potential in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs.
RLVR relies on extracting reward signals from the model’s own pass@kk outputs to improve its pass@1 performance. However, this design inherently couples final performance to the choice of kk, as the reward signal vanishes when all sampled responses are either correct or incorrect, making it impossible for the model to extract useful knowledge from such data. This constrains RLVR’s learning capacity to the base model’s pass@kk, limiting further exploration and improvement Yue et al. (2025). Although increasing the sampling number kk per prompt may seem like a straightforward solution, it typically yields only marginal improvements while significantly reducing sample efficiency Lin et al. (2025). This results in GRPO exhibiting limited exploration and low sample efficiency, and indirectly leads to training instability in the later stages due to the disappearance of reward signals Yu et al. (2025); Li et al. (2025).
To address the aforementioned issues, we propose EFRame: an Exploration-Filtering-Replay RL Framework. For challenging prompts that cannot be effectively handled by regular rollouts, EFRame introduces an additional rollout phase to enable targeted exploration. During this process, the model generates a small number of high-quality positive samples along with a large number of low-quality negative samples. To reduce training variance and improve efficiency, we apply an online filtering mechanism to discard low-quality samples. Meanwhile, high-advantage samples are stored in a replay buffer and reused during training to amplify their impact and enhance model stability.
Previous studies have shown that both positive and negative samples contribute to RLVR training Zhu et al. (2025). With the support of the EFRame, we categorize training samples to better understand their roles in learning:
•
Regular samples refer to both correct and incorrect responses from prompts of moderate difficulty. They form the foundation of the reward signal, helping to improve model performance while ensuring training stability.
•
High-quality samples consist of correct responses obtained through exploration on difficult prompts. They are crucial for activating deeper model knowledge and enhancing the performance ceiling, but their effective use requires experience replay to avoid instability caused by their low sampling probability.
•
Low-quality samples refer to the numerous incorrect responses generated from challenging prompts. These samples offer minimal learning value and may even harm the training process by accelerating convergence to suboptimal solutions.
EFRame provides a systematic solution to the inherent trade-offs among training stability, efficiency, and exploration capacity in reinforcement learning. By enabling the model to effectively identify and leverage deeper latent knowledge, EFRame enhances the expressiveness and learning potential of large language models. Through the coordinated integration of targeted exploration, online sample filtering, and experience replay, the framework not only addresses the limitations of GRPO in handling complex reasoning tasks but also establishes a more principled and controllable training paradigm. Moreover, by introducing a categorization of training samples based on their difficulty and informativeness, EFRame offers a new analysis on the roles of different sample within the RLVR framework. Empirical evaluations confirm the efficacy of EFRame: on the challenging Geometry3K benchmark, it outperforms GRPO by 36.5%, and it achieves consistent gains across a wide range of multimodal and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating strong generalizability and robustness.
2 Related Work
The early application of RL in LLMs and MLLMs OpenAI (2023); Team et al. (2024); Zhu et al. (2023); Wei et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2023) was primarily reflected in RLHF, where algorithms such as PPO were employed to align language model outputs with human preferences and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Rafailov et al. (2024) bypass the need for reward modeling and instead optimize the model policy directly from preference data. Recently, RL has advanced toward integrating rule-based reward mechanisms with sophisticated optimization techniques, as exemplified by models such as DeepSeek-R1 Guo et al. (2025) and Kimi-1.5 Team et al. (2025). As a lightweight variant of PPO, GRPO Shao et al. (2024) significantly improves training efficiency, but at the cost of increased instability.
A series of subsequent studies have introduced optimizations from GRPO. These include gradient loss refinements, as demonstrated in DAPO Yu et al. (2025) and Dr.GRPO Liu et al. (2025); online filtering methods like Meng et al. (2025); advantage enhancement techniques, exemplified by VL-Rethinker Wang et al. (2025a) and Luffy Yan et al. (2025); clip acceleration methods such as CPPO Lin et al. (2025); curriculum learning strategies explored in Chen et al. (2025b); Chen et al. (2025c); and unsupervised RL such as Wei et al. (2025a); Zhao et al. (2025); Agarwal et al. (2025).
While existing methods typically focus on individual components, we systematically review the fundamental limitations of GRPO and propose a comprehensive framework with an integrated solution that addresses three critical aspects: exploration capacity, training stability, and sample efficiency.
This helps create a more robust and effective GRPO-based training paradigm.

3.2 Limitations of GRPO
Although GRPO has demonstrated strong performance in various settings, it encounters several practical challenges when applied to complex reasoning tasks. In the following discussion, we highlight three main issues: limited exploration capacity, training collapse, and low sample efficiency. These limitations reveal the need for more robust and adaptive RL strategies.
Limited Exploration Capacity. GRPO lacks effective mechanisms to promote exploration, leading to persistently low entropy levels in the early stages of training and an increased risk of convergence to local optima. Moreover, it does not differentiate between prompts of varying difficulty, which limits its ability to stimulate deeper reasoning capabilities. As a consequence, only superficial knowledge is activated, while deeper knowledge remains underutilized.

Low Sample Efficiency. GRPO computes sample advantage based on relative advantage. When the number of rollouts is small, it suffers from a severe issue of advantage vanishing, leading to a lack of effective training signals. However, as the number of samples increases, the relative advantage becomes diluted, making it harder for high-quality samples to exert sufficient influence. This results in low sample efficiency: although the computational overhead grows nearly linearly with the number of samples, the corresponding performance improvement is significantly diminished, as shown in Fig. 1.
Instability. During the later stages of training, the GRPO algorithm may exhibit signs of instability or collapse, characterized by a sharp decline in reward and sudden spikes in both gradient and policy entropy, as illustrated in Fig. 2. In extreme cases, this can escalate into gradient explosion. Such instability presents a serious challenge to reliable long-horizon optimization, particularly in complex reasoning tasks where consistent gradient signals are critical for convergence. One contributing factor is the increased variance caused by the disappearance of relative advantages in the later stages of training. Supplementing with additional samples with non-zero advantages can help alleviate this issue.

4 Methodology
To address the issues in the previous section, we propose the Exploration-Filtering-Replay (EFR) framework, which consists of three main components: additional rollout, online filtering and replay buffer, as illustrated in Figure 3.
Figure 3: The overall workflow of EFR builds upon the vanilla GRPO framework by introducing three key components: additional rollout, online filtering, and replay buffer.
4.1 Regular Rollout
For each batch of prompts, we begin with a regular-rollout phase, following the original GRPO training process. This initial rollout serves two key purposes:
•
Obtain reward signals from regular (i.e., solvable) problems.
•
Identify more challenging prompts that fail to yield positive samples, which will be handled in subsequent stages.
Valuable samples are temporarily stored in the buffer for gradient updates.
4.2 Additional Rollout
GRPO computes loss over G1G_{1} sampled outputs, making G1G_{1} pivotal. Smaller G1G_{1} limits learning capacity; while larger G1G_{1} adds linear cost with limited benefit.
For most questions, at least one positive sample can typically be found within a small number of rollouts. However, for more challenging problems, a larger number of samples and a higher temperature are often necessary to promote exploration and obtain meaningful reward signals.
To address this, we introduce an additional rollout mechanism: we perform an extra sampling round (e.g. G2G_{2}=20, temperature = 1.2) for challanging prompts.

The additional rollout is designed to activate deeper reasoning capabilities and increase the frequency of emergent “aha moments.”
4.3 Online filter
While additional rollout enhances the model’s ability to explore complex prompts, it also introduces computational overhead and potential training noise. In addition to filtering overlong samples, we design two additional filtering strategies to further enhance the stability and efficiency of the training process.
Regular Filter: In the first-round rollout, when the prompt is of regular difficulty—we adopt a conservative filtering strategy that retains all regular samples ORO_{R} to balance efficiency and performance.
Definition 4.2.
Regular samples ORO_{R} refer to any samples with a non-zero advantage on the prompt set 𝒟\mathcal{D}:
OR={{oi}∼πθo​l​d(⋅|q):(q,a)∼𝒟,A^i,t=Ri−mean⁡({Ri}i=1G1)std⁡({Ri}i=1G1)≠0};O_{R}=\left\{\{o_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|q):(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\ \hat{A}_{i,t}=\frac{R_{i}-\mean(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G_{1}})}{\std(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G_{1}})}\neq 0\right\};
At this stage, due to the moderate difficulty of prompts, all regular samples—both positive and negative—provide informative learning signals that contribute meaningfully to training.

When the prompt becomes challenging, we encourage the model to explore extensively in order to discover correct answers.

According to Definition 4.3 and Theorem 4.4, low-quality samples are significantly more numerous than high-quality ones, whereas the advantage values of high-quality samples are substantially higher.
In such an exploration-driven setting, penalizing incorrect trajectories is not only inefficient but may also introduce noisy and unstable gradients. If gradients are computed from all samples, most of which are low quality, the informative signal from high-quality samples may be drowned out by chaotic updates from low-quality ones. To address this issue, we apply a more aggressive filtering strategy during the additional rollout—discarding all negative samples and retaining only positive samples for training. In practice, since this exploration phase targets only challenging prompts, the vast majority of generated samples (over 95%) are filtered out under this strategy.

4.4 Replay buffer
While GRPO is fundamentally an online RL algorithm, we find that experience replay with high-quality offline samples remains highly effective. In particular, trajectories discovered during the second-round rollout—often of higher quality due to deeper exploration and carry rich reward signals.
By selectively storing high-quality samples in the replay buffer, we amplify their influence during training, particularly when their gradients are frequently clipped due to low sampling probabilities. Furthermore, by enabling the model to repeatedly leverage these rare yet informative samples, experience replay plays a pivotal role in enhancing stability of entropy.

The high-quality samples identified through additional rollouts are typically responses to difficult prompts and thus are with relatively low probability. Using them only once yields limited and short-lived gradient effects, leading to an increase in model entropy. By incorporating these samples into the replay buffer, their probability of reoccurrence is effectively increased during training, enabling the model to transition from entropy increase to eventual convergence. In fact, the replay buffer here serves to reduce policy entropy; detailed discussions can be found in the following discussion section.
4.5 Discussion
The above subsections yield several practical insights regarding the interplay between Exploration-Filtering-Replay:
Additional rollout: By prompting LLM to generate multiple responses to challenging queries (x∈𝒟¯x\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}) under a high temperature setting, the resulting positive samples are obtained with extremely low probability log⁡πθ​(y∣x)\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) and high advantage A^​(x,y)\hat{A}(x,y). According Theorem 4.5, this high-advantage, low-probability scenario rapidly increases the policy entropy HH, thereby preventing collapse of the entropy and maintaining effective exploration. At the same time, an excessive presence of low-quality samples can cause a nosedive of entropy, which is difficult to control. To address this, we filter out such samples and instead adopt a more controllable strategy to regulate entropy stability.
Replay Buffer: By storing high-quality positive samples within the replay buffer, the model can repeatedly utilize their gradient information for subsequent updates. As the model has already been updated on these (problem, answer) pairs (x,y)(x,y), the recalculated log⁡πθ​(y∣x)\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) becomes larger upon rehearing these pairs. According to the theorem, this shift leads to a reduction in policy entropy, preventing excessive growth in entropy.
Applying any techniques in isolation may result in unhealthy dynamics of policy entropy. Subsequent ablation studies further corroborate these findings.

5.1 Experimental setup
Training Datasets. Our experiments are conducted on two datasets to evaluate the effectiveness of the algorithm in both in-domain and out-of-domain settings: (1) Geometry3K focused on geometric problems Lu et al. (2021a), (2) a 6k out-of-domain dataset randomly sampled from the dataset proposed in  Wei et al. (2025b); Wei et al. (2025a), which randomly sampled from established open-source resources, including Geometry3K Lu et al. (2021a), GeoQA Chen et al. (2021), GeoQA-Plus Cao & Xiao (2022), Geos Seo et al. (2015), AI2D Kembhavi et al. (2016), TQA Kim et al. (2018), FigureQA Kahou et al. (2017), TabMWP Lu et al. (2022b), ChartQA Masry et al. (2022), IconQA Lu et al. (2021b), Clevr-Math Lindström & Abraham (2022), M3CoT Chen et al. (2024), and ScienceQA Lu et al. (2022a).
Evaluation Benchmarks. Our experiments consist of two parts. First, we evaluate the overall performance of the algorithms on four open multimodal math benchmarks: MathVision Wang et al. (2024a), MathVista Lu et al. (2023), MathVerse Zhang et al. (2024), and We-Math Qiao et al. (2024). These benchmarks cover diverse problem types such as geometry, charts, and tables, spanning multiple subjects and levels of visual reasoning difficulty. Second, we conduct a detailed analysis for algorithms on the Geometry3K dataset to examine the algorithmic behaviors.
Baselines.
We compare our models with a range of advanced multimodal large language models. These include GPT-4o Hurst et al. (2024) and Claude-3.5-Sonnet Anthropic (2024), which are state-of-the-art closed-source MLLMs. QvQ-Preview-72B[2]
and Skywork R1V-32B  Peng et al. (2025) are multimodal large reasoning models specifically designed for R1-like chain-of-thought capabilities. At the 7B scale, we compare with MM-Eureka-7B Meng et al. (2025), OpenVLThinker-7B Deng et al. (2025), ThinkLite-VL-7B Wang et al. (2025b), and VLAA-Thinker-Qwen2.5VL-7B Chen et al. (2025a). All of these 7B models are based on Qwen2.5-VL and are enhanced for mathematical reasoning via fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or iterative self-improvement techniques. In addition, we compare the performance of GRPO and DAPO on the Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct model to assess the impact of different strategies under consistent model and training settings.
Implementation details.
Our work is based on EasyR1 and VeRL framework Yaowei et al. (2025); Sheng et al. (2025). We initialize all policy models with Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and conduct experiments on 8 A800 GPUs (40GB). Unless otherwise specified, we follow the default EasyR1 settings: maximum response length of 2048, global batch size 128, rollout batch size 512, rollout G1G_{1}=5, temperature 1.0, and learning rate 10−610^{-6}. GRPO and our method adopt ϵl​o​w=ϵh​i​g​h=0.2\epsilon_{low}=\epsilon_{high}=0.2 and a binary reward to avoid relative advantage bias (see detail in Appendix A), while DAPO includes ϵh​i​g​h=0.3\epsilon_{high}=0.3 and an additional length-aware linear penalty (up to 0.5) for responses exceeding 1536 tokens. For our method, we perform additional rollouts with G2=20G_{2}=20, temperature = 1.2; responses with absolute advantage larger than 1 are stored in a replay buffer of size 5120, with experience replay triggered every 5 steps.

6 Ablation Studies
To assess the contribution of each component in our framework, we conduct an ablation study to examine whether all three modules—additional rollout, online filtering, and replay buffer—are all essential for achieving the observed improvements in stability and overall performance.
6.1 Additional Rollout
Disabling the additional rollout module exposes the model to a fundamental trade-off inherent in GRPO-based training. When the number of samples G1G_{1} is small, the reward signal is constrained by the base model’s pass@G1G_{1}, limiting the model’s ability to solve challenging problems and suppressing the emergence of “aha moments.”
Claim 1.
High-quality samples have the potential to activate deeper reasoning abilities within the model.
As shown in Fig. 5, the model’s reward on the training set plateaus at a relatively low level, restricting further gains in test accuracy. In the early stages of training, the entropy remains low, indicating limited exploration. However, once the model hits this performance bottleneck, entropy begins to rise—yet this delayed exploration yields minimal returns, suggesting that the model struggles to escape local optima. The additional rollout module is specifically designed to break this situation, enabling targeted deep exploration.

6.2 Online Filtering
Removing the online filtering module results in significant instability and inefficiency during training. Without filtering, the model treats all samples—regardless of their advantage—as equally important, allowing zero-advantage samples to increase gradient variance and low-quality negative trajectories to distorb the learning signal. This problem is further exacerbated during the additional rollout phase, where abundant incorrect samples significantly amplifies gradient variance, making it difficult for the model to retain meaningful patterns from rare high-quality responses. As a result, training becomes slower, more chaotic, and more prone to divergence.
Claim 2.
Low-quality samples from challenging prompts often mislead optimization, causing the model to converge prematurely to suboptimal solutions and impeding further learning.
As shown in Fig. 5, during the early stages of training, the inclusion of a large number of negative samples significantly boosts the model’s performance on the training set, but yields little improvement on the test set. This indicates that most low-quality samples lead the model to converge more quickly to a local optimum, offering little benefit to its generalization ability.
Moreover, the forward and backward computation of these negative samples incurs substantial overhead, reducing the sample efficiency of the overall training process.
Online filtering plays a crucial role in maintaining gradient clarity and training stability by selectively preserving informative samples and discarding neutral or low-impact ones.
6.3 Replay Buffer
When the replay buffer is disabled, we observe a drastic drop in performance, even falling below that of the vanilla GRPO baseline, as shown in Fig. 5. This counterintuitive result reveals that the abundant high-quality samples generated during the additional rollout phase can actually become detrimental without a proper replay mechanism.
Claim 3.
While high-quality samples may induce high-entropy exploration, incorporating experience replay enables the model to retain and consolidate useful gradients, thus restoring training stability.
As high-quality samples typically have low generation probabilities, rewarding them shifts the model’s response distribution toward low-probability but correct outputs, disrupting the convergence trajectory and consequently increasing entropy. Due to their high advantage values and low sampling probabilities, high-quality samples are more likely to have their gradients clipped during training. In the absence of a replay buffer, such valuable samples contribute only short-lived and unstable gradients that are quickly overwritten. Instead of reinforcing beneficial behavior, this transient gradient signal increases policy uncertainty, pushing the model into a state of chaotic, high-entropy exploration, ultimately harming both learning stability and performance. To address this issue, we leverage experience replay to ensure that high-quality samples continuously provide effective gradients, enabling the model to converge along the new trajectory.
7 Conclusion and Discussion
In this work, we investigate the impact of sample quality on RL for large language models, with a particular focus on addressing challenging prompts. Our findings show that low-quality incorrect samples—those with low absolute advantage—tend to trap the model in local optima and offer little or even negative contribution to training. In contrast, high-quality samples with large advantages play a critical role in guiding the model through a meaningful transition from entropy-driven exploration to entropy-reducing convergence. This process is essential for eliciting deeper reasoning capabilities within the model.
To this end, we propose an Exploration-Filtering-Replay framework that emphasizes the discovery, selection, and sustained utilization of high-quality samples. Our method introduces additional rollouts to enhance reward density, employs online filtering to suppress noisy samples, and integrates experience replay to amplify the impact of high-quality samples across training iterations. This strategy not only helps transform isolated “aha moments” into a sustained “aha state,” but also shifts reinforcement learning from a “reinforcement lottery” toward a more structured and reliable process of reinforcement learning.

Appendix A Relative Advantage Bias of GRPO
In GRPO, computing relative advantages within a group can introduce bias, especially when the reward becomes multi-dimensional. For example, as shown in Fig. 6, if the reward comprises both accuracy and auxiliary signals such as length, format, or entropy, a significant issue arises when the accuracy signal vanishes within a group—i.e., when all responses are either correct or incorrect. In such cases, auxiliary rewards (e.g., length) dominate the advantage computation. This may result in incorrect responses being rewarded simply for being shorter, or correct responses being penalized for being longer. Such misleading gradients introduce irreversible noise into training and lead to reward hacking. Therefore, in the training involved in this paper, we use binary rewards whenever possible.
Appendix B Key Hyperparameters
The quantity of high-quality samples varies significantly with dataset difficulty, which makes our algorithm sensitive to three key hyperparameters: clip higher, replay buffer size, and replay frequency. This is because the gradients from high-quality samples must be just perfect—between underfitting, proper fitting, and overfitting—to be effective. Notably, on relatively simple datasets such as Math, where high-quality samples are inherently scarce, the improvements of our framework over the original GRPO are less pronounced.
