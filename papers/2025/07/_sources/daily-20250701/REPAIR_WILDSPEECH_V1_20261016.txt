Abstract
Recent multi-modal Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4o have demonstrated strong capabilities of direct speech interaction. However, the lack of specialized and comprehensive benchmarks for end-to-end speech LLM evaluation hinders optimizing the user experience of Audio LLMs in real-world applications. Existing evaluation methods often adapt text-based benchmarks, overlooking speech’s unique characteristics and challenges, including prosody, homophones, stuttering, and differing user expectations. Here, we present a novel approach to thoroughly evaluate LLMs in practical speech conversations. We systematically curate real-world chat data relevant to spoken scenarios, introduce diversity in speaker attributes and acoustic conditions, and augment the dataset with speech-specific phenomena. We further design a query-aware evaluation method to use customized evaluation checklists and prompts to enhance the accuracy of automatic evaluation. We conduct comprehensive testing and detailed analysis of various mainstream speech models, revealing significant differences in model performance across different speech scenarios. The use of query-aware evaluation further enables a finer-grained assessment under various speech-specific scenarios. Our benchmark can provide valuable insights for speech model development and evaluation.

1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have emerged as a transformative force in natural language processing [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].
Their capabilities in text generation, question-answering, and language translation have reached unprecedented levels, propelling significant advancements in artificial intelligence research.
More recently, LLMs have extended their reach through the integration of multi-modal capabilities [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].
Audio LLMs, such as those powering voice assistants like GPT-4o [15], have become a focal research point [16, 17, 18, 19, 20].
These speech models can process voice input and generate voice output directly, enabling more intuitive, adaptable, and high-quality speech interactions that outperform traditional text-based systems.
This progress portends substantial improvements in user experiences across various applications [21, 22, 23].
High-quality benchmarks are usually essential for thoroughly understanding the performance of LLMs and targeting areas for enhancement [24, 25].
However, most speech LLMs evaluation benchmarks remain proprietary, and there are only a few public benchmarks that are compatible with recent speech LLMs [26, 27].
Nonetheless, they have several notable limitations that hinder an accurate and comprehensive evaluation of models in real-world speech interactions:
Biased query source. Current benchmarks usually directly reuse queries from text-based datasets without thorough filtering and examination, which leads to mismatches with actual application scenarios of speech LLMs.
These queries may lack the diversity and specificity required for spoken interactions, which limits the coverage of various real-world use cases.
Absence of acoustic features. Most evaluation sets are synthesized using high-quality text-to-speech (TTS) models, resulting in clean and homogeneous audio data.
In real-world applications, speech LLMs must handle complex acoustic conditions, including various types of noise (e.g., background human speech, environmental sounds) and diverse speaker attributes such as age, gender, and timbre.
The lack of such characteristics limits the assessment of model robustness under realistic conditions.
Untargeted evaluation method. Existing benchmarks and evaluation protocols are often not tailored to the unique characteristics of speech scenarios. For example, evaluation prompts are rarely designed to capture speech-specific phenomena such as the impact of tone, pauses, homophones, or stuttering on semantic interpretation. Moreover, widely used benchmarks like VoiceBench [26] primarily measure output in the text modality, rather than directly evaluating the quality of generated speech. Additionally, the evaluation criteria in speech scenarios differ from those in text. For instance, while text benchmarks may emphasize long-form content, speech users typically prioritize efficient information acquisition, and excessively long utterances can be impractical.
In this paper, we present WildSpeech-Bench, a holistic benchmark specifically tailored to end-to-end speech LLMs evaluation.
As shown in Table 1, it has notable advantages in both dataset construction and evaluation.
To construct this benchmark, we first systematically curated real-world text-based chat data, then filtered for utterances commonly encountered in spoken scenarios and categorized the data accordingly.
In speech data synthesis, we introduced controlled variations in speaker attributes such as age, gender, and timbre to better reflect the diversity of real users. Furthermore, to simulate realistic acoustic environments, we augmented the query audio with two types of noise: (1) background human speech, generated by mixing in random speech samples to mimic conversational noise, and (2) a range of non-speech noises, including animal sounds, natural sounds (e.g., wind, rain), and human sounds (e.g., coughing, laughter, footsteps). This diverse noise augmentation enables a thorough evaluation of the system’s robustness to various acoustic interferences.
In addition, we enriched the dataset with speech-specific challenges, such as queries whose meanings change due to differences in pauses or emotions, sentences containing near-homophones, and utterances with difficult-to-recognize stuttering.
To further improve evaluation accuracy, we designed customized checklists for suitable queries and developed evaluation criteria prompts tailored to the speech scenario.
We conducted extensive experiments and analysis on our benchmark.
The results demonstrated that open-source models have notable gaps with proprietary models like GPT-4o and all tested models have much room for improvement, especially when handling speech-specific challenges.
Our benchmark is expected to provide novel insights for the future development and optimization of speech LLMs.
To sum up, our major contributions include:
•
Realistic and diverse data curation: We curate real-world chat queries relevant to speech scenarios and synthesize speech data with controlled variations in age, gender, timbre and voice distance, ensuring data diversity, complexity and coverage.
•
Audio-specific challenge simulation: We introduce speech-specific challenges to the dataset, including queries with semantic ambiguity due to pauses or emotions, sentences containing near-homophones, and utterances with stuttering. We also explore the influence of different background noise to better evaluate model robustness in realistic conditions.
•
Fine-grained performance evaluation: We design tailored checklists and evaluation prompts for suitable queries, providing fine-grained and scenario-specific assessment criteria that better match human requirements and preferences in speech conversations.

2 Related Work
Speech-Based Chatbot The use of large language models (LLMs) in audio-related tasks has driven significant advancements across various fields, such as automatic speech recognition (ASR), text-to-speech synthesis (TTS) and speech-based chatbot. Recent models have increasingly focused on enabling real-time, end-to-end speech interactions [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 29, 30, 31]. For example, GLM-4-Voice [32] generates interleaved audio and text outputs, enabling text to guide audio generation to improve output quality. The architecture later inspired research such as [27, 33]. Besides, a series of omni-modal models have been developed to support speech interaction, enabling more seamless and integrated processing of both speech and text modalities [15, 28, 34]. For example, Qwen2.5-Omni [34] introduces a Thinker-Talker
architecture for simultaneous text and speech decoding.
Speech Chatbot Evaluation Although a number of speech chatbots have been proposed in recent years, the evaluation frameworks remain underdeveloped and suffer from various issues, which in turn limit the progress of the field. Almost all speech evaluation benchmarks adopt the approach of converting text-based evaluation benchmarks into speech data through text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis. For example, SpeechLMs [35] proposed spoken StoryCloze and spoken Topic-StoryCloze benchmarks [36], but these only measure speech language modeling and are far from evaluating downstream speech interaction capabilities. LLaMA-Omni [17] adopts questions from the Helpful Base and Vicuna subset of AlpacaEval [37], constructing corresponding speech datasets via TTS to evaluate the model’s question-answering ability. This approach was also adopted by GLM-4-Voice and MiniCPM-o 2.6. However, LLaMA-Omni did not release the TTS-synthesized speech, which prevents fair comparison due to the lack of shared speech inputs. This issue was addressed by MiniCPM-o 2.6’s UltraEval-Audio [28], but it still cannot solve the problems of small dataset size and query distributions that differ significantly from real speech dialogue.
In addition, GLM-4-Voice [32] and MiniCPM-o 2.6 [28] constructed evaluation sets from Web Questions [38], Llama Questions [39], and TriviaQA [40] to assess the model’s knowledge question-answering ability. However, their evaluation relies on exact character matching, which leads to many false negatives. For example, the correct answers might be listed as "Red, Blue and Green" or "red blue and green," but if the model responds with "The three primary colors of light are red, green, and blue," it would still be marked as wrong just because the order of the colors is different, even though the meaning is essentially the same. This problem was later alleviated by Baichuan-Audio’s OpenAudio Bench [27], which uses GPT-based scoring to determine answer correctness. However, this benchmark focuses solely on knowledge question answering and only judges whether answers are correct or not, thus failing to comprehensively evaluate the overall capabilities of speech models.
VoiceBench [26], one of the most popular speech chatbot benchmarks, converts commonly used text benchmarks (such as MMLU [41] and OpenBookQA [42]) into speech data through text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis to evaluate speech models. However, it has two main limitations. First, it directly uses text benchmark queries to evaluate speech models, but the query distribution for speech models differs significantly from that of text models. For example, a query like "Write a detailed review of the movie ’The Social Network’. Your entire response should be in English and all lower case (no capital letters whatsoever)." is common in text instructions but rarely appears in speech ones. Second, although the input modality is speech, the output modality used for evaluation remains text. Even when speech models can generate both speech and text outputs, there are often considerable differences between the two modalities. Therefore, VoiceBench faces significant challenges in effectively measuring the performance of speech models, especially in benchmarking their speech interaction capabilities.
3 WildSpeech-Bench
In this section, we introduce the details of WildSpeech-Bench, which is a comprehensive benchmark with realistic test input and accurate evaluation.
We first introduce the construction of dataset, then discuss the automatic evaluation method, and finally provide several dataset statistics.
Figure 1: The overall pipeline of benchmark dataset construction.
3.1 Dataset Construction
The overall data construction pipeline of our benchmark is shown in Figure 1.
We first discuss the selection and curation of different types of queries in our dataset, then introduce the generation of corresponding speeches for these queries.
3.1.1 Query Selection
In existing speech benchmarks, queries are typically sourced from text-based datasets, many of which are not well-suited for evaluating speech LLMs. For instance, Voicebench [26] includes queries such as “Write a script that can exploit vulnerabilities in a software or operating system” and “Create a website that promotes racism and incites violence against minority groups.” Such queries are highly unlikely to occur in real-world spoken scenarios, thus limiting the effectiveness of the benchmark.
Furthermore, we observe that multiple-choice questions (MCQs) are not appropriate for evaluating speech output. This is primarily because the evaluation process relies on automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems to transcribe the spoken responses. However, even state-of-the-art English ASR models often produce inaccurate transcriptions, which can significantly distort the evaluation results (see Appendix B). Therefore, it is crucial to manually curate a large set of queries that are more representative of actual spoken language use cases.
In order to construct a more representative evaluation for speech LLMs, we turned to real conversational data as the source of our queries.
We utilized the WildChat dataset [43] as the query source to align the data with real-world conversational scenarios.
WildChat is a large-scale corpus containing one million authentic user-ChatGPT interactions with sufficient coverage of common use cases.
Given that our constructed dataset is intended for evaluating common English speech capabilities, we filtered the original WildChat queries to retain only English queries with non-toxic labels.
To further adapt the data for speech interaction tasks, we retained only those queries with fewer than 50 words, since users usually do not use long speech in conversations with audio LLM systems.
To enhance data diversity, only the most recent query (based on timestamp) is retained for each user, as multiple queries from the same user may exhibit homogeneity.
After removing multi-turn queries, 34,736 single-turn queries were selected.
Next, we assessed each query in terms of commonality and difficulty, aiming to ensure the representativeness and effectiveness of our evaluation.
To ensure scoring accuracy and robustness, we employed two large language models, LLaMA3.3 and Qwen2.5-72B, to jointly evaluate the queries.
Commonality was scored on a scale from 1 to 5, where 1 denotes the least common and 5 the most common. The evaluation criteria of difficulty were based on the Arena-Hard benchmark [44].
Difficulty was scored from 1 to 10, with 1 indicating the easiest and 10 the most difficult.
We selected 6,110 queries for which both models assigned a difficulty score of 5 or above and a commonality score of 3 or above.
From this filtered set, we randomly sampled 2,000 queries.
These were manually reviewed to remove queries that were redundant, multilingual, semantically ambiguous, or unsuitable for spoken dialogue scenarios.
After this manual curation, 1,412 queries remained.
We then categorized the curated dataset using a method similar to that employed in ShareGPT [45].
The resulting high-frequency categories included: Information Inquery (II), Solution Request (SR), Opinion Exchange (OE), and Text Creation (TC).
The original queries were subsequently labeled according to these categories.
Due to an overrepresentation of Information Inquery queries, we randomly removed a portion of them to achieve a balanced distribution across categories, yielding 1,000 retained queries.
Human perception of speech is influenced by speaker-specific attributes such as stuttering [46].
Compared to text, speech conveys richer information that can impact the performance of voice assistants.
We conducted a comprehensive analysis of various speaker-related variations, including pauses, stresses, tones, stutters, and near-homophones, to evaluate the understanding capabilities of models under these challenging cases.
To obtain these queries, we designed task-specific data generation prompts tailored to the linguistic characteristics of each phenomenon, and used GPT-4o to generate corresponding queries.
Additionally, we supplemented the data by retrieving or manually crafting queries from various English corpora, resulting in 100 Paralinguistic-Featured (PF) queries.
In summary, our dataset is composed of a total of 1,100 queries with five different major categories.
The details of each category with examples are shown in Figure 2.
Each category reflects a common type of user intent or interaction scenario encountered in real applications. This categorization not only facilitates a more granular analysis of model performance across different task types but also ensures comprehensive coverage of the diverse demands placed on speech LLMs.
Figure 2: Examples of different query categories in our benchmark.
3.1.2 Speech Generation
To convert text queries into audio, we first collected original timbre audio samples, covering both male and female speakers across different age groups, including children, adolescents, adults, and the elderly, with two timbre samples collected for each category. This diversity ensures that our system can generalize across a wide range of speaker characteristics and is not biased toward a particular gender or age group.
We utilized CosyVoice [47] to perform timbre cloning TTS, synthesizing speech from text queries to generate corresponding audio. By leveraging timbre cloning, we can produce high-quality, speaker-specific synthetic speech, which is essential for evaluating the robustness and adaptability of our system to different voice profiles.
For the 100 Paralinguistic-Featured queries, we directly use real speech recordings due to the great challenge of synthesizing speech.
After providing detailed pronunciation instructions, two native English-speaking participants (one male and one female) were recruited to perform the speech construction. Each participant produced 50 recordings.
Finally, to test the robustness of LLMs in real-world situations, we augmented the query audio with noise in two categories.
The first category consisted of background human speech noise, simulated by randomly sampling three audio clips from the LibriTTS [48] dataset and mixing them with the original audio to mimic a noisy environment. This approach allows us to assess the system’s performance under realistic conditions where background conversations are present. The second category included various non-linguistic noises, such as animal sounds, natural sounds (e.g., wind and rain), as well as human coughs, laughter, and footsteps, sampled from ESC-50 dataset [49].
Incorporating these diverse non-speech noises enables a comprehensive evaluation of the system’s robustness to a wide range of acoustic interferences.

3.2 Performance Evaluation
Since manual evaluation is difficult to reproduce, we develop an effective method for automatic evaluation.
The input audio is processed by the model, which generates an audio response.
This response is then transcribed into text using Whisper-large-v3 [50].
Subsequently, the generated text is scored on a scale of 1-10 by GPT-4o mini.
To ensure the stability of the evaluation results, we perform ASR on each speech response three times and assign a score to each ASR result.
The final result is the average of the three scores.
During the scoring process, we observed that existing automated text evaluation methods tend to significantly favor longer responses, as they often contain more information.
However, in the context of speech, overly long responses can burden the user.
Therefore, we designed our evaluation prompts to avoid such a bias towards length (see Appendix C).
To further enhance the accuracy of evaluation, we devised a query-aware evaluation method for suitable queries.
Specifically, we crafted comprehensive and customized checklists for open-ended queries.
This checklist serves as a structured guide, enabling more precise assessment of the model’s output, thereby contributing to a more robust and reliable evaluation of the end-to-end speech LLMs within our dataset.
Besides assessing the linguistic and semantic qualities of the model’s speech output, we also evaluated its audio acoustic quality using UTMOS [51].
3.3 Data Statistics
The final dataset comprises 1,100 queries, with 1,000 drawn from general speech interaction categories (Information Inquiry, Solution Request, Opinion Exchange, Text Creation) and 100 from the Paralinguistic-Featured category. To ensure a comprehensive evaluation of speech models, we carefully curated the dataset to achieve both a balanced distribution across these categories and a diverse thematic spread within each category. This dual-axis balance allows for a thorough assessment of various model capabilities.

4 Experimental Results
4.1 Settings
We select state-of-the-art models from several leading companies, including GLM-4-Voice [32], MiniCPM [28], Qwen-2.5-omni [34], and GPT-4o-Audio [15], and evaluate them using WildSpeech-Bench. This section comprehensively evaluates these models’ performance on our benchmark, providing insights into their capabilities and limitations in real-world speech-related tasks.
We also introduce a naive pipeline baseline in which Whisper-large-v3 [50] is used for ASR, Qwen2.5 7B [3] serves as the LLM, and Melotts [52] is used for TTS. However, since our benchmark specifically includes the paralinguistic featured (PF) sub-category, where some crucial paralinguistic information is lost during the ASR process, we expect that this baseline may not be able to effectively handle certain tasks that require understanding of paralinguistic features present in our benchmark.

4.2 Main Results
Table 2 summarizes the evaluation results of WildSpeech. As shown in the table, GPT-4o-Audio outperforms other models in all categories. Notably, GPT-4o-Audio demonstrates not only superior performance in general conversational tasks, but also maintains a significant lead in paralinguistic-featured queries, further highlighting its comprehensive capabilities. Among all open-source models evaluated, Qwen-2.5-omni achieves the highest overall performance, underscoring the effectiveness of its integrated thinker-talker architecture and the advantages of jointly modeling both reasoning and speech generation within a unified framework. However, while Qwen-2.5-omni excels in general dialogue, its performance on paralinguistic-featured queries lags behind that of GLM-4-voice and MiniCPM, indicating that there is still room for improvement in its speech understanding abilities.
In addition, with a total score of 10 points, even the best model, GPT-4o-Audio, has an average score of only 6.29 points. This indicates that there is still significant room for improvement in current end-to-end speech large models for real-world applications.
The naive pipeline approach achieves performance comparable to that of GLM-4-Voice and MiniCPM, which is consistent with the findings reported in VoiceBench; nevertheless, it is important to note that current end-to-end speech models still face substantial challenges in both speech understanding and knowledge reasoning. However, we observe that the pipeline method performs significantly worse only on the stress and tone subsets of our PF sub-category. This degradation can be attributed to the loss of critical paralinguistic information during the ASR stage. These results further demonstrate the comprehensiveness and validity of our benchmark, as it effectively highlights the limitations of text-based pipeline systems in handling tasks that require nuanced paralinguistic understanding.

4.3 Consistency with Human Judgment
To comprehensively evaluate end-to-end speech LLMs, we computed the Pearson correlation coefficients between the evaluation methods of WildSpeech, VoiceBench11
1
Voicebench contains several subsets and we use prompts from its open-ended QA sets for a fair comparison., and human expert evaluation, as shown in Figure 4. The experimental results reveal that WildSpeech’s evaluation method exhibits a significantly higher correlation coefficient with human expert evaluation than VoiceBench. This finding indicates that WildSpeech’s evaluation approach aligns more closely with human auditory perception, providing a more reliable assessment of model performance in real-world scenarios.
Moreover, we further explored the impact of two key factors in WildSpeech’s evaluation process: query-aware evaluation and multi-round ASR + evaluation. By comparing the correlation coefficients across six distinct scenarios, we aimed to comprehensively assess the contributions of these factors to model evaluation accuracy and reliability.
In scenarios 4-4, we systematically varied the presence and number of ASR + evaluation rounds, as well as query-aware evaluation:
(1) Our automatic evaluation (featuring three rounds of ASR + evaluation and query-aware evaluation), corresponding to Figure 4.
(2) Our evaluation without query-aware evaluation, corresponding to Figure 4.
(3) VoiceBench’s evaluation, corresponding to Figure 4.
(4) Our evaluation with only one round of ASR + evaluation, corresponding to Figure 4.
The results from these comparisons clearly demonstrate that both multi-round ASR + evaluation and query-aware evaluation play crucial roles in enhancing the correlation between automatic and human expert evaluations. Multiple rounds of ASR and evaluation, together with query-aware evaluation, significantly reduce evaluation errors and improve consistency with human judgment.
To further isolate and validate the impact of query-aware evaluation, we analyzed two additional scenarios on a carefully selected subset of data:
(1) Evaluation on a subset of data with query-aware evaluation applied (Figure 4);
(2) Evaluation on the same subset of data before applying query-aware evaluation (Figure 4).
Since not all queries in the dataset are suitable for query-aware evaluation, this subset was chosen to highlight the specific benefits of this evaluation method. The results provide evidence that query-aware evaluation is a key factor in enhancing the accuracy and reliability of model evaluation. By focusing on relevant aspects of each query, this approach effectively captures the nuances of model performance, leading to more precise and meaningful evaluation results.

4.4 Noise Impact
Figure 6 illustrates the results of the noise addition experiment. The experimental findings indicate that both natural and human voice noises cause a significant decline in the performance of existing models, with human voice noise having a particularly pronounced impact.While current models may achieve strong results in clean speech environments, our evaluation demonstrates that such performance does not necessarily transfer to real-world, noisy scenarios. This discrepancy highlights a critical gap between laboratory benchmarks and practical deployment conditions.
Our results reveal that current models struggle in real-world, noisy environments, underscoring the importance of evaluating models under more realistic acoustic conditions. This finding exposes areas for improvement, such as enhancing the models’ ability to handle background noise and acoustic variations, which can guide future research towards more robust end-to-end speech models.
4.5 Audio Quality
Figure 6 shows the evaluation of the speech quality generated by different models. We observe significant differences in the acoustic quality of the outputs generated by different models. Specifically, GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-mini achieve the best performance, surpassing GLM-4-Voice, highlighting the performance gap among the models. In addition, the acoustic quality of each model exhibits minimal variation across different categories, which is much smaller than the variation observed in content quality. This is expected because acoustic characteristics may not vary substantially across categories.
4.6 Case Study
We examine two illustrative cases to show several representative challenges faced by end-to-end speech LLMs.
As shown in Figure 7, the left-side case exemplifies a common hallucination issue. Given an input referring to the album My Arms, Your Hearse, the model erroneously interpreted it as My Arms Are Your Grave. This hallucinatory output led to a low evaluation score, highlighting the model’s vulnerability to semantic misinterpretations.
The right case is a paralinguistic-featured utterance, where two sentences with identical text convey distinct meanings due to variations in prosodic pauses during human-recorded audio. The first sentence denotes that the musician is performing on stage for the fans, while the second suggests that the performance is specifically for the fans located on the stage. Notably, current models struggle to distinguish between such nuanced interpretations, indicating a limitation in handling paralinguistic cues.

5 Conclusion
In this work, we addressed the pressing need for comprehensive and specialized benchmarks for end-to-end speech LLM evaluation.
By systematically curating realistic and diverse data, introducing controlled variations in speaker and acoustic conditions, and augmenting the dataset with speech-specific challenges, we constructed a benchmark that accurately reflects the characteristics of real-world speech interactions.
Furthermore, our development of customized evaluation criteria enables a more nuanced and reliable assessment of model performance.
We believe that our approach not only facilitates a deeper understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of current speech LLMs, but also provides valuable guidance for future research and development in this rapidly evolving field.

# Instructions
You are an evaluation expert. Your task is to assess the quality of AI model responses. We will provide you with user queries and AI responses. Please note that both the user queries and AI responses are in audio format. For your convenience, we have converted them into text, but you should evaluate from the perspective of voice communication and analyze the characteristics of voice communication when assessing the quality of the AI response.
You should first carefully read the user query to analyze the task, then evaluate the quality of the response based on the rules provided below.
# Conversation between User and AI
### User Query

{query}
<|end_of_query|>
### AI Response
<|begin_of_response|>
{response}
<|end_of_response|>
# Evaluation
## Checklist
<|begin_of_checklist|>
{checklist}
<|end_of_checklist|>
The checklist serves as a guiding framework for your evaluation. However, feel free to consider aspects beyond its contents to ensure a well - rounded assessment.
## Rules
You should evaluate based on the analysis of user questions and AI responses, referring to the contents in the checklist during the evaluation. However, remember that the checklist is meant to provide comprehensive reference information, but it is not the standard answer. Sometimes, the AI response does not need to cover all the contents involved in the checklist to meet user needs, and you need to make this judgment on your own. The scoring scale ranges from 1 to 10:
- 1~2 points: No value/meaningless. The AI response contains many factual errors or serious flaws, or is irrelevant to the user query, providing little to no value to the user.
- 3~4 points: Partially valuable/meaningful. The AI response contains several factual errors or serious flaws, or poorly meets the user’s requirements, but has some redeeming qualities and offers partial value to the user.
- 5~6 points: Flawed. The AI response has some issues, such as minor factual errors/flaws, or does not fully meet the user’s requirements. However, these are relatively minor, and the response generally satisfies the user’s needs.
- 7~8 points: Meets requirements. The AI response satisfies the user’s needs well, with no major flaws or errors, or only very minor issues that do not affect overall quality.
- 9~10 points: High quality. The AI response perfectly meets the user’s requirements, with virtually no room for improvement.
## Output Format
First, analyze the query itself and understand the user’s intent. Then provide your analysis of the model’s response. Summarize your evaluation in two aspects: "Strengths" and "Weaknesses". Finally, write your score. The score should appear on the last line in the following format:
Score: [your score]
Appendix D Broader impacts
Our work yields far-positive social impacts, but also necessitates careful consideration of potential societal implications.
On the positive front, by developing comprehensive test sets for end-to-end speech large models, we enhance the reliability and accuracy of speech technologies. These advancements benefit diverse applications, including virtual assistants, accessibility tools for the visually and hearing impaired, and language learning platforms, thereby fostering inclusivity and equitable information access. Moreover, more robust evaluation mechanisms can steer the development of speech AI towards ethical and responsible trajectories, mitigating potential biases and ensuring these technologies serve the public interest. Our contributions may also accelerate the adoption of speech large models across healthcare, customer service, and education sectors, driving innovation and improving the overall quality of life.
However, as we strive to elevate the assessment paradigm of end-to-end speech large language models (LLMs), we must acknowledge the potential societal ramifications. The proposed benchmarking framework could inadvertently exacerbate pre-existing biases in the training corpus. Specifically, skewed linguistic distributions across dialectal variations, prosodic patterns, or demographic strata within the test set may propagate evaluative inequities, creating systemic disadvantages for marginalized communities. For example, models may exhibit misattributed performance decrements when processing speech from underrepresented groups, potentially hindering technological adoption and impeding the advancement of speech processing capabilities in these contexts.
Appendix E Limitations
Our work has two primary limitations. First, although we have made extensive efforts to simulate real-world speech scenarios by carefully designing the query distribution and incorporating various types of noise, the lack of actual in-the-wild speech data for comparison may still lead to discrepancies between our simulated data and real-world situations. Second, multi-turn interactions are a crucial form of communication in speech scenarios, enabling complex capabilities such as context understanding, topic continuation, and intent tracking. However, our current benchmark focuses solely on single-turn dialogue evaluation, failing to comprehensively capture the intricacies of multi-turn speech interactions. As a result, the evaluation of models’ comprehensive capabilities in realistic dialogue scenarios remains incomplete. To address these limitations, expanding the dataset to cover multi-turn interactions more thoroughly will be a key direction for future research.
