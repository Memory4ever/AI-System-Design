Abstract
Inference-time scaling has emerged as a powerful technique for enhancing the reasoning performance of Large Language Models (LLMs). However, existing approaches often rely on heuristic strategies for parallel sampling, lacking a principled foundation.
To address this gap, we propose a probabilistic framework that formalizes the optimality of inference-time scaling under the assumption that parallel samples are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.), and where the Best-of-N selection strategy follows a probability distribution that can be estimated. Within this framework, we derive a theoretical lower bound on the required number of samples to achieve a target performance level, providing the first principled guidance for compute-efficient scaling.
Leveraging this insight, we develop OptScale, a practical algorithm that dynamically determines the optimal number of sampled responses. OptScale employs a language model-based predictor to estimate probabilistic prior parameters, enabling the decision of the minimal number of samples needed that satisfy predefined performance thresholds and confidence levels.
Extensive experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (including MATH-500, GSM8K, AIME, and AMC) demonstrate that OptScale significantly reduces sampling overhead while remaining better or on par with state-of-the-art reasoning performance. Our work offers both a theoretical foundation and a practical solution for principled inference-time scaling, addressing a critical gap in the efficient deployment of LLMs for complex reasoning.

1 Introduction
The reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have become a pivotal research area, given their ability to tackle complex cognitive tasks spanning mathematical reasoning, decision-making, and problem-solving [28, 25].
Recent advances demonstrate that inference-time scaling can substantially enhance reasoning performance by enabling the model to generate diverse candidate solutions, creating a richer space for comprehensive inference through answer aggregation [5, 7, 20].
This approach is particularly valuable for proprietary LLMs, as it can operate purely at inference time without requiring model modifications.
Although effective, these techniques face a fundamental efficiency challenge: the linear relationship between candidate solutions and computational costs leads to prohibitive token consumption.
This has created an urgent need for optimized scaling strategies that navigate the Pareto frontier between reasoning performance and computational efficiency, maximizing accuracy while minimizing redundant computation [19].
Parallel inference-time scaling, a dominant paradigm in this space, operates by generating NN candidate responses in parallel and selecting the optimal output via learned rules (e.g., verifiers) [29] or consensus (e.g., majority voting) [21].
Current approaches fall into three categories:
verifier-based approaches that train auxiliary models to rerank candidates [25]; data-driven methods that fine-tune LLMs on high-quality reasoning traces to improve solution quality [15]; and inference-time techniques that dynamically adjust the number of candidates per input [5].
While these methods demonstrate practical effectiveness, they suffer from significant theoretical limitations.
Current strategies rely heavily on empirical heuristics and implicit priors (such as learned verifier preferences or empirical token budgets) without proper mathematical formulation.
Crucially, the field lacks systematic derivation of these priors from first principles or rigorous quantification of the fundamental efficiency-accuracy trade-offs inherent in parallel scaling.
This paper presents a foundational study that bridges this critical gap through three key contributions:
First, we formulate a probabilistic framework that formally establishes the optimality conditions for parallel inference-time scaling under i.i.d. assumptions, where the Best-of-N selection process follows an estimable probability distribution.
This theoretical formulation provides a principled understanding of scaling behavior in probabilistic terms.
Second, we derive a theoretical lower bound on the number of samples required to achieve any target performance level.
This result offers mathematically grounded guidance for compute-efficient scaling, representing a significant advance beyond current heuristic approaches.
Third, we develop OptScale, an efficient algorithm that implements these theoretical insights in practice. OptScale dynamically determines the optimal sample size by employing an LM-based predictor to estimate prior parameters, ensuring minimal computational cost while satisfying predefined performance thresholds and confidence levels.
Our experiments demonstrate OptScale’s computational efficiency: it automatically terminates sampling for simple questions while preventing excessive computation on intractable ones.
To our knowledge, this work presents the first comprehensive treatment of parallel inference-time scaling that: 1) establishes a rigorous probabilistic model of the scaling process, 2) derives fundamental limits on scaling efficiency, and 3) provides a practical algorithmic implementation that approaches these theoretical optima.
2 Related Work
2.1 LLM Reasoning
The ability of LLMs to perform complex reasoning tasks has garnered significant attention and seen rapid advancements [27, 24]. Early successes with prompting strategies like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) [22] demonstrated that LLMs could produce step-by-step reasoning traces, significantly improving performance on tasks requiring logical deduction, mathematical problem-solving, and multi-step planning [1, 17, 11]. This foundational work has spurred the development of more sophisticated prompting techniques, including Self-Consistency [20], which generates multiple reasoning paths and selects the most consistent answer, and Tree-of-Thought (ToT) reasoning, which allows models to explore diverse reasoning trajectories in parallel [26, 12].
2.2 Parallel Inference-time Scaling
Despite these advances, efficiently scaling LLMs at inference time remains a challenge. Parallel inference-time scaling has emerged as a key strategy to enhance LLM performance by enlarging computational resources during inference. This approach involves generating NN candidate solutions in parallel and selecting the best one using various methods [17]. Existing techniques can be categorized into the following three groups:
Verifier-based Scaling Methods.
To improve inference-time performance, many studies use auxiliary models, known as verifiers or Process Reward Models (PRMs), to assess the quality of candidate solutions generated by a primary LLM [16, 15]. These verifiers predict the correctness of a solution or assign it a quality score, guiding the selection of the final answer by re-ranking the NN outputs. For example, some methods train verifiers using human feedback or generated labels [2]. Recent work examines how the interaction between generators, PRMs, and problem difficulty affects optimal test-time scaling [29]. However, relying on additional models increases computational overhead, and the strategies for determining NN often lack a theoretical foundation.
Model-tuning Based Methods with High-quality Data.
Some approaches aim to improve the output quality by enhancing the generator LLM itself. This is often done by fine-tuning the base LLM on datasets rich in high-quality reasoning traces or problem-solution pairs [15, 4]. Research efforts focus on creating specialized datasets for mathematical or coding reasoning to embed these capabilities directly into the model [23]. Techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or process supervision, where models are rewarded for correct reasoning steps instead of just final answers, also fall into this category [10]. While these methods enhance the generator’s initial quality, they do not address computational efficiency during inference, which is a critical aspect of scaling.
Dynamic Inference-based Scaling Methods.
Recognizing the limitations of fixed NN, several methods explore dynamic approaches to adjust computation at inference time. These techniques adapt the number of generated candidates or computational resources based on the input query or the ongoing generation process [7, 20]. For example, some methods allocate more computation to complex problems or where initial candidate solutions show high uncertainty or disagreement [5]. Approaches like dynamic decomposition break down solution paths into manageable steps, dedicating more resources to challenging sub-problems. The goal is to avoid unnecessary computation on simpler instances while providing sufficient resources for harder ones. While these dynamic methods improve efficiency, they often rely on heuristic rules or learned strategies without a clear theoretical framework for the trade-off between efficiency and accuracy.
These dynamic methods represent a step towards more efficient scaling. However, the rules governing the dynamic allocation are often heuristic or learned without an explicit, foundational theory of the efficiency-accuracy trade-off. Our work distinguishes itself by providing a first-principles probabilistic formulation of the parallel scaling process. This allows us to derive a provably optimal strategy for dynamically allocating compute to maximize accuracy per token and to quantify the theoretical upper bound for efficiency, addressing the lack of systematic, theoretically grounded priors in current dynamic scaling approaches.
3 Inference-time Scaling with Probabilistic Optimality
In this section, we formulate a theoretical framework for inference-time scaling from a probabilistic optimality perspective (Section 3.1) and present OptScale, an implementation that effectively achieves the goal of compute-efficient inference-time scaling (Section 3.2).
3.1 Theoretical Framework: Scaling with Probabilistic Optimality
Preliminaries.
Given an input question qq processed by a large language model ℳ\mathcal{M}, which generates NN candidate answers {ai}i=1N\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}.
Each answer aia_{i} consists of TiT_{i} reasoning steps {ai,t}t=1Ti\{a_{i,t}\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}}.
A verification mechanism ℛ\mathcal{R} (e.g., a Process Reward Model, PRM) assigns normalized scores {si,t∈[0,1]}t=1Ti\{s_{i,t}\in[0,1]\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}} to each step.
Defining 𝒜⁡(⋅)\mathcal{A}(\cdot) as an aggregation operator (typically averaging multi-step scores or using the final step score), we compute the overall verification score for answer aia_{i} as:
si=𝒜⁡[ℛ⁡(ai,t|q,ai,<t,ℳ)]s_{i}=\mathcal{A}\big[\,\mathcal{R}(a_{i,t}\,|\,q,a_{i,{<t}},\mathcal{M})\,\big]
(1)
The optimal answer a∗a^{*} is selected through:
a∗=arg​maxi∈{1,…,N}⁡sia^{*}=\argmax_{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}s_{i}
(2)
Previous research on inference-time reasoning has primarily differed in their choices of the language model ℳ\mathcal{M}, verifier ℛ\mathcal{R}, and aggregator 𝒜\mathcal{A}.
In contrast, our work makes a fundamental shift in perspective by investigating the probabilistic distributions that govern these components.
It is a crucial distinction that sets our approach apart from prior studies.
Verifier Score Distribution.
Let us assume that the verifier scores {si}\{s_{i}\} for a fixed question qq are samples for a random variable SS that is conform to a continuous probability distribution with probability density function (PDF) fS​(⋅)f_{S}(\cdot) and cumulative distribution function (CDF) FS​(⋅)F_{S}(\cdot) as:

(3)
where θ\theta represents the parameters in ℳ\mathcal{M}, ℛ\mathcal{R}, and hyperparameters for generation (e.g., the temperature tt and Top-pp values).
Distribution of the Maximum Verifier Score.
Let Y=max⁡{s1,s2,…,sN}Y=\max\{s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{N}\} denote the maximum verification score among NN candidates, which serves as the core selection criterion in Eq. (2).
For any observed score ss, the optimal decision on whether to sample more candidates can be determined when we know the exceedance probability

This represents the likelihood that ss is the maximal score in the complete population of possible verifications.

Note that both the PDF and the CDF of YY are functions with respect to NN. Figure 3 shows a few examples of such PDF functions in different values of NN.
Probabilistic Optimality.
After NN sampling rounds, conventional methods (e.g., Best-of-N) confront a critical trade-off: whether to continue sampling (potentially finding higher-scoring answers) or terminate (to conserve computational resources).
Our solution derives the probabilistically optimal sample size
N∗N^{*} as the minimal value satisfying:
ℙ⁡(Y≥smin)≥α\mathbb{P}(Y\geq s_{\min})\geq\alpha
(6)
where sm​i​ns_{min} denotes the quality threshold for valid solutions, α\alpha represents the required confidence level.

Solving for NN yields the closed-form solution:
N∗≥⌈log⁡(1−α)log⁡FS​(smin)⌉,N^{*}\geq\left\lceil\frac{\log(1-\alpha)}{\log F_{S}(s_{\min})}\right\rceil,
(8)
where ⌈⋅⌉\lceil\cdot\rceil ensures integer sample sizes.
3.2 Implementation of the Optimal Scaling
Translating the theoretical framework (Section 3.1) into practice raises a critical question: how can we reliably estimate the verifier score distribution fS​(s|θ,q)f_{S}(s|\theta,q)?
Let (μ,σ)(\mu,\sigma) parameterize this distribution, where these parameters need not imply Gaussianity.
Direct estimation of (μ,σ)(\mu,\sigma) fails, because at inference time, there are only a limited number of samples.
We propose a two-stage estimation method to address this challenge.
During training, we leverage abundant data to learn the underlying parameter trends (μ,σ)(\mu,\sigma).
At inference time, we refine these estimates using the available scored samples for each query qq, achieving accurate distribution modeling despite limited observations.
General Trend Modeling and Learning.
To enable practical implementation, we incorporate error tolerance into our parameter estimation framework.
We model the parameters (μ,σ)(\mu,\sigma) as random variables drawn from their respective mean values (μ¯,σ¯)(\bar{\mu},\bar{\sigma}) with Gaussian-distributed errors:
μ∼𝒩⁡(μ¯,σμ):fμ​(μ|μ¯,σμ),σ∼𝒩⁡(σ¯,σσ):fσ​(σ|σ¯,σσ),\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mu},\sigma_{\mu}):f_{\mu}(\mu|\bar{\mu},\sigma_{\mu}),\,\sigma\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\sigma},\sigma_{\sigma}):f_{\sigma}(\sigma|\bar{\sigma},\sigma_{\sigma}),
(9)
where σμ\sigma_{\mu} and σσ\sigma_{\sigma} govern the permissible estimation error bounds.
The Gaussian assumption is justified by the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), as these parameters represent aggregate statistics that converge to normality.
This formulation offers both theoretical stability and practical learning efficiency.
As the global trends (μ¯,σ¯)(\bar{\mu},\bar{\sigma}) are determined by the language model, we implement this through fine-tuning with additional MLP layers:
μ¯,σ¯=MLP​(LLM​(q|θ∗))\bar{\mu},\bar{\sigma}=\text{MLP}(\text{LLM}(q|\theta^{*}))
(10)
where θ∗\theta^{*} denotes the fine-tuned model parameters.
Modeling the Verifier Score Distribution.
Before refining the estimations, we need a hypothesis for the actual distribution of fS​(s|θ,q)f_{S}(s|\theta,q).
We propose a truncated normal distribution constrained to the interval [0,1][0,1].
This choice reflects both the bounded nature of verification scores and prevents model overfitting.
The probability density function is given by:
fS​(s|θ,q)∝fS​(s|μ,σ)=ϕ⁡(s−μσ)σ⁡[Φ⁡(1−μσ)−Φ⁡(0−μσ)]f_{S}(s|\theta,q)\propto f_{S}(s|\mu,\sigma)=\frac{\phi(\frac{s-\mu}{\sigma})}{\sigma[\Phi(\frac{1-\mu}{\sigma})-\Phi(\frac{0-\mu}{\sigma})]}
(11)
where ϕ⁡(⋅)\phi(\cdot) and Φ⁡(⋅)\Phi(\cdot) denote standard normal PDF and CDF respectively.
MAP Estimation for Parameter Refinement.
Given a set of observed scores D={sk}k=1‖D‖D=\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\|D\|} for question qq, we refine the initial estimates (μ¯,σ¯)(\bar{\mu},\bar{\sigma}) through Maximum-a-Posteriori (MAP) estimation.
With the likelihood (Eq. (9)) and Gaussina priors of paraterms (Eq. (11)), the joint posterior when given a set of scores for sampled answers of qq is
fS​(μ,σ|D)∝fS​(D|μ,σ)⋅fμ​(μ|μ¯,σμ)⋅fσ​(σ|σ¯,σσ).f_{S}(\mu,\sigma|D)\propto f_{S}(D|\mu,\sigma)\cdot f_{\mu}(\mu|\bar{\mu},\sigma_{\mu})\cdot f_{\sigma}(\sigma|\bar{\sigma},\sigma_{\sigma}).
(12)
The MAP estimates are obtained by solving:
μ∗,σ∗=arg​maxμ,σ⁡(∑k=1‖D‖log⁡fS​(sk|μ,σ)+log⁡fμ​(μ|μ¯,σμ)+log⁡fσ​(σ|σ¯,σσ)).\mu^{*},\sigma^{*}=\argmax_{\mu,\sigma}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\|D\|}\log f_{S}(s_{k}|{\mu},{\sigma})+\log f_{\mu}(\mu|\bar{\mu},\sigma_{\mu})+\log f_{\sigma}(\sigma|\bar{\sigma},\sigma_{\sigma})\Big).
(13)
Adaptive Scaling.
Using the estimated μ∗\mu^{*} and σ∗\sigma^{*} in Eq. (8), we derive a question-specific and closed-form optimal N∗N^{*} to determine when to stop.
This adaptive strategy optimizes computational efficiency: it terminates early for simple questions to avoid unnecessary computation and limits prolonged processing for intractable ones.
We name this approach OptScale.
4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We evaluate OptScale on the following representative math reasoning benchmarks:
MATH-500 [9]: A challenging subset of the MATH dataset with 500 high school competition problems across algebra, geometry, number theory, and calculus, following the OpenAI evaluation split.
GSM8K [6]: A dataset of 8.5K grade school word problems assessing basic arithmetic and textual reasoning.
AIME 2024 [13] and AIME 202511
1
https://huggingface.co/datasets/math-ai/aime25.: Each contains 30 pre-Olympiad level problems from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination, designed to test advanced mathematical reasoning. AMC22
2
https://huggingface.co/datasets/math-ai/amc23.: A collection of intermediate-level problems from the 2023 American Mathematics Competitions, bridging standard high school and AIME difficulty.
Backbone Models.
To assess generalizability and efficiency, we use two open-source backbone LLMs of different scales and architectures: 1) Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct [14]: A 1B-parameter instructional model from Meta’s Llama 3.2 series, ideal for evaluating inference-time scaling techniques with a relatively small model size, even increasing compute. 2) DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B [8]: A 7B-parameter reasoning model distilled from larger DeepSeek R1 models into a Qwen model, representing a state-of-the-art distilled model optimized for complex reasoning.
Baseline Methods.
We benchmark OptScale against a comprehensive set of inference-time scaling methods, including:
1) Best-of-N (BoN): It samples NN reasoning paths, with a Process Reward Model (PRM) scoring on them and then selects the best answer.
2) Self-Consistency (SC) [21]: It samples multiple diverse responses and aggregates the final answer based on majority voting.
3) Multi-round Thinking MR-Thinking [18]: It iteratively refines the reasoning process through multiple rounds.
4) Beam Search: It explores the most probable sequence of tokens during decoding by maintaining a fixed number of candidate sequences.
5) Diverse Verifier Tree Search (DVTS) [3]: It is an extension of beam search that splits the initial beams into independent subtrees, which are then expanded greedily using a PRM.
Implementation Details.
In OptScale, we employ separate predictors for the mean and standard deviation of the data point distribution, conditioned on the question context and initial samples. These predictors are trained for 30 epochs with a learning rate of 1e-5, weight decay of 0.01, and gradient clipping at 0.1. The linear layer includes a dropout rate of 0.2.
For scoring, we use the Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B as the process reward model (PRM). During maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), we vary the number of sampled data points within 3 to 10, which is dynamically determined according to the maximum number of generations.
To ensure fair comparison, we re-ran all baseline methods under the same experimental conditions. For all datasets, we use a sampling temperature of 0.7 and a top-p of 0.95. All experiments are conducted in one NVIDIA A6000 server with 8 GPUs. We report accuracy and completion token usage as the evaluation results.
4.2 Can OptScale Ensure Optimal Scaling?
To evaluate scaling efficiency, we conducted comprehensive experiments across the four benchmark datasets, varying the maximum number of answer candidates from 11 to 6060.
This experimental design required 240 total runs per method (44 datasets ×\times 6060 candidate limits), representing a significant computational investment.
Due to these resource constraints, we focused our comparison on the two most representative baseline methods: Best-of-N (BoN) and Self-Consistency (SC).
Figure 1 shows that our OptScale robustly achieves optimal scaling. We conduct a grid search over different threshold scores, ranging from 0.95 to 0.98, and confidence probabilities, from 0.01 to 0.1.
The results demonstrate that OptScale’s performance closely follows the Pareto frontier (red dashed line), achieving near-optimal efficiency.
This confirms its ability to maintain scaling optimality across configurations.
Notably, all OptScale implementations along the frontier consistently outperform baselines, delivering higher accuracy with lower computational cost (token consumption).
More generally, Figure 1 (II) demonstrates that OptScale is optimal across various datasets in terms of performance-to-computation tradeoffs. For each method, we plot accuracy against the average token count consumed. The results reveal several key insights:
1) OptScale consistently outperforms both baseline methods across all datasets, achieving either higher accuracy with comparable token consumption or similar accuracy with substantially fewer tokens. On GSM8K with LLaMA1B, OptScale reaches 83% accuracy with approximately 8,000 tokens, while Best-of-N requires nearly 12,000 tokens to achieve the same performance – a 33% reduction in computational cost.
2) The performance gap is particularly apparent on more challenging datasets. On AIME24 with Qwen7B, OptScale achieves 50% accuracy while Best-of-N plateaus at 47%, demonstrating that our method’s adaptive allocation strategy is especially beneficial for complex reasoning tasks where problem difficulty varies significantly.

Besides consistently consuming significantly fewer tokens than BoN at the same accuracy levels, more remarkably, through proper dynamic allocation of inference-time compute, OptScale sometimes achieves accuracy levels that BoN fails to reach even when scaled indefinitely to a substantial amount of tokens, as Figure 2 (b) demonstrates. These advantages stem primarily from OptScale’s ability to evaluate question difficulty in advance and allocate resources optimally through our probabilistic framework. Notably, our case study reveals that excessive scaling can actually degrade performance by generating too many incorrect answers, which OptScale avoids. 4.3 Can OptScale Estimate the Verifier Score Distribution Accurately?
To evaluate our approach, we compare the estimated distributions against the ground truth.
Figure 3 shows various alignment cases between our modeled distributions and empirical observations.
In most cases (e.g., Questions 1, 2, 3, and 7), the ground truth verifier scores follow a unimodal distribution, which is accurately captured by a truncated normal distribution over [0,1].
For bimodal distributions (e.g., Questions 4, 6, 8, 9), OptScale maintains reasonably accurate predictions.
Only in rare instances do we encounter more complex distributions: cases with multiple peaks (Question 10) or extremely narrow, high peaks (Question 5) challenge the truncated normal approximation.
However, these exceptions are statistically insignificant and do not materially impact overall prediction quality.
4.4 How OptScale is Sensitive to Hyperparameters?
Figure 4: Sensitivity analysis of quality threshold smins_{\min}: Model performance across target levels.
Figure 5: Sensitivity analysis of confidence level α\alpha: Model performance across target levels.
OptScale employs two hyperparameters, namely the quality threshold smins_{\min} and the desired confidence level α\alpha.
We repeat 66 combinations of these two parameters by varying smins_{\min} in the range of [0.80, 0.99] and α\alpha in [0.90, 0.99] with a step size of 0.02 and 0.01 respectively to evaluate how sensitive OptScale’s performance on accuracy and completion tokens is with regard to hyperparameters. We employ Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct backbone with GSM8K benchmark for this focused study.
Impact of the Quality Threshold. Figure 4 shows the changes in accuracy, token consumption, and efficiency (accuracy per token consumption) corresponding to the quality threshold smins_{\min} changes. As smins_{\min} increases, the accuracy growth gradually slows down and converges. However, the required token consumption continues to grow exponentially, with the token consumption multiplying by 2.5 times when the quality threshold increases from 0.8 to 0.99. This trend results in a persistent decline in efficiency from 0.00016 to 0.0006, with the rate of decrease becoming increasingly steep.
Impact of the Confidence Level α\alpha. Similarly, Figure 5 shows the metrics changes along different confidence level α\alpha. Both accuracy and the number of completion tokens grow with α\alpha. However, different from the exponentially growing pattern elaborated above smins_{\min}, the number of completion tokens grows almost linearly, increasing 100 tokens per 0.01 increase in confidence. The overall efficiency only drops slightly as the confidence grows.
Overall, we find that the quality threshold smins_{\min} is a much more sensitive hyperparameter in terms of determining both accuracy and token consumption compared to the confidence level α\alpha, making the selection of smins_{\min} much more important than α\alpha. During empirical experiments, we noticed that choosing a very high quality threshold (e.g., 0.99) could harm efficiency and result in suboptimal utilization of completion tokens, while a low quality threshold of below 0.9 could lead to accuracy degradation. A quality threshold smins_{\min} of around 0.95 to 0.96 is usually recommended, while there’s much more space in selecting confidence level α\alpha: the default could be set to 0.9.
4.5 Can OptScale Achieve a New SOTA Performance across Benchmarks?
We compare the overall performance of our method with all baseline methods in both accuracy and token consumption.
As shown in Table 1, OptScale achieves a new state-of-the-art (SOTA) accuracy superiority in 9 out of 10 experimental
configurations, with an average improvement of 50.40% over competing methods. Moreover, OptScale maintains this advantage while consuming substantially fewer tokens than comparable methods. For instance, on MATH-500 with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, OptScale attains 93.2% accuracy while reducing token usage by 67.52% versus BoN. On GSM8K with LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct, OptScale reduces token consumption by 38.67%, demonstrating its strong ability to balance optimal accuracy performance and optimal efficiency.
These results highlight OptScale’s advantage in delivering robust and efficient reasoning performance across diverse reasoning benchmarks.
Compared to sequential scaling methods such as MR-Thinking, which often achieve lower token consumption when using reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the token consumption gap here is largely due to the inherent limitations of sampling-based strategies in parallel scaling. In contrast, OptScale balances parallel efficiency with robust accuracy gains.
As detailed in Section 3.2, a key advantage of OptScale is its compatibility with a wide range of existing methods. Since it operates in a distinct scaling dimension, orthogonal to the reasoning or decoding strategies, OptScale can be seamlessly integrated with other techniques such as search-based inference or dynamic verification.
Rather than competing with these methods, OptScale serves as a general-purpose optimization layer that enhances both performance and efficiency.

5 Conclusion
We introduce a principled probabilistic framework for parallel inference-time scaling in LLMs, addressing the limitations of existing heuristic-based approaches. By modeling the Best-of-N selection under i.i.d. assumptions, we derive a theoretical lower bound on the sample size required to achieve target performance, offering the first formal guidance for compute-efficient scaling.
Building on top of this, we propose OptScale, a practical algorithm that dynamically adjusts the number of samples using an LLM-based predictor.
Extensive experiments on various reasoning benchmarks show that OptScale achieves superior performance with significantly reduced computational cost.
Our work provides both theoretical foundations and practical implementations for more efficient and adaptive inference-time scaling.
