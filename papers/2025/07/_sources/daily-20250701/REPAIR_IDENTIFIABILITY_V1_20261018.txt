Abstract
Text watermarks in large language models (LLMs) are an increasingly important tool for detecting synthetic text and distinguishing human-written content from LLM-generated text. While most existing studies focus on determining whether entire texts are watermarked, many real-world scenarios involve mixed-source texts, which blend human-written and watermarked content. In this paper, we address the problem of optimally estimating the watermark proportion in mixed-source texts. We cast this problem as estimating the proportion parameter in a mixture model based on pivotal statistics. First, we show that this parameter is not even identifiable in certain watermarking schemes, let alone consistently estimable. In stark contrast, for watermarking methods that employ continuous pivotal statistics for detection, we demonstrate that the proportion parameter is identifiable under mild conditions. We propose efficient estimators for this class of methods, which include several popular unbiased watermarks as examples, and derive minimax lower bounds for any measurable estimator based on pivotal statistics, showing that our estimators achieve these lower bounds. Through evaluations on both synthetic data and mixed-source text generated by open-source models, we demonstrate that our proposed estimators consistently achieve high estimation accuracy.
1 Introduction
Over the past two years, the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has profoundly transformed content generation, with diverse applications spanning creative writing, code generation, text summarization, and educational assistance [23]. Their rapid adoption, however, has raised pressing concerns regarding content authenticity in critical domains such as scientific peer review [21], education [22], journalism [29], and data authenticity [26, 7].
To mitigate potential misuse of these powerful generative tools, researchers have developed text watermarking methods that embed imperceptible yet statistically verifiable signals into LLM-generated output [15, 1, 17]. These watermarking techniques facilitate reliable detection of synthetic text, enabling one to distinguish fully watermarked content from human-written text [24].
However, real-world scenarios frequently involve mixed-source texts, where human-written and LLM-generated content are commonly interwoven [40, 12, 19]. This is particularly evident in academic peer reviews, as demonstrated by Liang et al. [21], who reported a growing prevalence of LLM-modified reviews in machine learning conferences. Beyond academia, evidence suggests that human-AI collaboration is increasingly reshaping content creation practices across various domains [3]. In this context, quantifying the extent of human versus LLM contribution becomes crucial [39]. Indeed, accurate estimates of human-written proportions have significant implications for determining content ownership and authenticity in academic writing, education, and legal documents [37]. For instance, a homework assignment essay with only 10% student contribution might be deemed unacceptable, while one with 80% or more student input might be considered permissible.
While prior studies have primarily focused on binary detection of fully watermarked versus fully human-written texts [20], recent work has begun to address the challenges posed by mixed-source content. From a statistical standpoint, however, the problem of estimating the proportion of LLM-generated content in mixed-source texts remains insufficiently explored, despite its critical role in use cases such as authorship attribution. Notably, Li et al. [19] formulated watermark detection in mixed-source texts as a mixture detection problem and identified optimal detection methods through the lens of statistical robustness. Other studies have examined the more granular problem of identifying watermarked segments within mixed-source texts. Examples include Aaronson [1], Kirchenbauer et al. [16]’s continuous span search, Zhao et al. [44]’s online learning approach, and Li et al. [18]’s application of change point detection to identify watermarked segments.
While identifying watermarked segments can, in principle, suffice for our purpose, it often yields an unnecessarily fine level of granularity and, more critically, may be statistically infeasible for pinpointing precisely which segments are human-authored. In many scenarios, a single numerical estimate of the proportion of LLM-generated content is more convenient and interpretable. For instance, it is practical to require AI-generated content to not exceed a 10% threshold in critical documents such as legal briefs and academic papers, while a higher limit (e.g., 50%) may be tolerable in less critical contexts such as homework assignments. Moreover, precisely localizing human-written text is arguably more challenging than simply estimating the overall proportion. This parallels findings from the broader mixture detection literature, wherein identifying individual signal instances is inherently more difficult than detecting their aggregate presence. Indeed, signals can be statistically detectable even when it is information-theoretically impossible to locate each instance accurately [10, 5].
1.1 Our Contributions
In this paper, we address the problem of estimating the proportion of tokens—smallest units in LLM generation that, without loss of generality, can be thought of as words—generated by watermarked LLMs in mixed-source texts under general watermarking schemes. Our main contributions are:
Problem formulation and identifiability: We formulate the estimation problem as a mixture model parameter estimation task using pivotal statistics [20].
Roughly speaking, a pivotal statistic quantifies the evidence that a given word (or token) is watermarked. Through rigorous analysis, we show that the proportion parameter in green-red list watermarking schemes [15] is not identifiable, which leads to the inherent bias in traditional approaches like maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). However, for watermarks with continuous pivotal statistics, we establish conditions under which identifiability can be achieved.
New estimators with theoretical guarantees: We develop computationally efficient estimators for identifiable cases and provide theoretical guarantees for their estimation accuracy. Our comprehensive experimental validation, conducted on both synthetic data and mixed-source texts from open-source LLMs, shows the consistent and superior performance of our proposed estimators.
Matched minimax lower bounds: We establish minimax lower bounds for broad classes of measurable estimators and prove that our proposed methods generally achieve these bounds, thereby establishing their theoretical optimality.
1.2 Related Work
Most watermarking studies focus on the binary detection problem, which aims to determine whether a text is fully watermarked or entirely human-written. However, this narrow scope fails to address real-world scenarios where texts often originate from mixed sources. While recent work has begun exploring the detection of watermarked segments in mixed-source texts, their approaches differ significantly from ours.
Li et al. [19] investigated the optimal detection of watermarked segments under human editing, assuming that the text is initially fully generated by a watermarked LLM and later modified by a human editor. Using the Gumbel-max watermark as an example, they demonstrate that a family of truncated goodness-of-fit tests achieves optimal detection, where optimality is defined by the phase transition boundary and feast-favorable efficiency notion introduced in [20]. However, their work focuses exclusively on detection and does not address estimation.
Other researchers attempt to locate watermarked segments by assigning scores to individual tokens or intervals of tokens and identifying watermarked (or LLM-generated) content based on extreme values. For example, Kirchenbauer et al. [16] searched for the largest intervals or continuous token spans with the highest zz-scores by testing all possible window sizes and traversing the entire text for each size, resulting in a computational complexity of O~​(n2)\widetilde{O}(n^{2}). Aaronson [1] improved on this by using dynamic programming to identify the largest continuous token span, achieving a reduced complexity of O⁡(n1.5)O(n^{1.5}). Zhao et al. [44] defined token scores based on the expectations of pivotal statistics and employs an online learning algorithm for estimation, achieving a complexity of O⁡(n​log⁡n)O(n\log n) for texts of length nn. Inspired by change point detection, Li et al. [18] used bootstrap-based pp-values as token scores, but the method incurs a higher computational cost of approximately O~​(n2)\widetilde{O}(n^{2}) due to its analysis of dependencies across all sliding token windows and pseudorandomness.
Despite these advancements, existing methods neither address the problem of watermark proportion estimation nor provide a systematic analysis of their performance or hyperparameter tuning (e.g., threshold selection). In contrast, our work presents the first comprehensive framework for watermark proportion estimation. We address key questions such as what to estimate (estimand and estimability), how to estimate (estimation methods), how accurately it can be estimated (estimation accuracy), and the theoretical limits of estimation (minimax lower bounds). Furthermore, our proposed estimator achieves O⁡(n){O}(n) complexity by requiring only a logarithmic number of passes through the samples.
2 Preliminaries
LLMs, such as the GPT series [25, 4], generate text by autoregressively sampling discrete units called tokens. Let 𝒲{{\mathcal{W}}} denote the vocabulary (token universe), and let w∈𝒲{w}\in{{\mathcal{W}}} be a single token. At each step tt, given the prefix w1:(t−1):=w1w2⋯wt−1{w}_{1:(t-1)}:={w}_{1}{w}_{2}\cdots{w}_{t-1}, the model computes a multinomial distribution 𝑷t:=(Pt,w)w∈𝒲\bm{P}_{t}:=(P_{t,w})_{w\in{{\mathcal{W}}}} over the vocabulary, and samples the next token wt∼𝑷t{w}_{t}\sim\bm{P}_{t}. This distribution 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t}, called the next-token prediction (NTP) distribution, is unknown to the verifier, as it depends on the user-supplied prompt, and potentially hidden system prompts [32].
A general framework for watermarking.
We adopt a general three-party framework that reflects real-world applications of watermark-based verification [17, 38].
The language model provider (e.g., OpenAI) embeds statistical watermark signals into the generated text using a designated watermarking scheme. Under this setup, every token generated by the model is watermarked by design.
The user (e.g., a student) interacts with the model, receives the watermarked output, and may optionally modify the text before sharing or submitting it.
The verifier (e.g., a teacher) is tasked with estimating how many tokens in a given document originated from the watermarked LLM.
Watermarking schemes operate by modifying the standard sampling process to embed statistical signals that can later be recovered for verification. This is typically achieved by introducing a pseudorandom variable ζt\zeta_{t} at each step tt, which serves as a random seed that guides token selection. Instead of sampling wt{w}_{t} directly from 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t}, the model applies a decoding rule wt:=𝒮⁡(𝑷t,ζt){w}_{t}:={\mathcal{S}}(\bm{P}_{t},\zeta_{t}), where 𝒮{\mathcal{S}} is a (possibly stochastic) decoding function. The pseudorandom variable ζt\zeta_{t} is generated using cryptographic techniques and can be deterministically reconstructed by the verifier if the construction is known.11
1
For example, ζt=𝒜(w(t−m):(t−1),𝙺𝚎𝚢)\zeta_{t}={\mathcal{A}}({w}_{(t-m):(t-1)},{\mathtt{Key}}) for some hash function 𝒜{\mathcal{A}}, context window size mm, and secret key 𝙺𝚎𝚢{\mathtt{Key}}. Given the full text w1:n{w}_{1:n}, the hash function, and the key, one can reconstruct ζ1:n\zeta_{1:n}. In this work, we assume ζt\zeta_{t} are i.i.d. samples from a known distribution, consistent with the perfect pseudorandomness assumption used in prior work [36, 43]. A watermark is called unbiased if the resulting token still marginally follows the original NTP distribution 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t}; otherwise, it is biased [20].
In contrast, human-written tokens are also drawn from 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} but do not depend on ζt\zeta_{t}, leading to a different joint distribution over (wt,ζt)({w}_{t},\zeta_{t}).
Detection thus reduces to testing whether wt{w}_{t} and ζt\zeta_{t} are statistically dependent. For human-written text, they are independent, since human users know nothing about the pseudorandomness; for watermarked text, dependence is introduced through the decoding rule 𝒮{\mathcal{S}}.
To detect this dependence, Li et al. [20] proposed computing a pivotal statistic Yt=Y⁡(wt,ζt)Y_{t}=Y({w}_{t},\zeta_{t}), designed to control Type I error and boost power.
These statistics are scalars that summarize the degree of dependence between wt{w}_{t} and ζt\zeta_{t}, which follow a known distribution under the null (i.e., no watermark). Specifically, Yt∼μ0Y_{t}\sim\mu_{0} i.i.d. when no watermark is present, and Yt|𝑷t∼μ1,𝑷tY_{t}\mid\bm{P}_{t}\sim\mu_{1,\bm{P}_{t}} under the alternative due to watermark signals.
Many watermarking schemes can be cast in this form, and detection is based solely on the sequence Y1:nY_{1:n}. This approach enables the verifier to statistically infer the presence and extent of watermarked content, even without access to the original prompt or NTP distributions.

Three representative watermarks.
We study three representative watermarking schemes under the general decoding framework described above. These schemes differ primarily in the choice of decoder 𝒮{\mathcal{S}} and the construction of the pseudorandom number ζt\zeta_{t}. Once these components are specified, the watermarking scheme is fully determined. For detection purposes, once the pivotal statistics are defined, the null distribution μ0\mu_{0} and the alternative distributions μ1,𝑷\mu_{1,\bm{P}} are also fixed. Therefore, it suffices to highlight these structural differences. Table 1 summarizes the key components of each scheme.
We briefly introduce each scheme below.
The Gumbel-max watermark [1], recognized as the first unbiased watermark, has been implemented internally at OpenAI. It uses the Gumbel-max trick [13] to sample tokens, which by construction ensures unbiasedness.
The inverse transform watermark [17] applies inverse transform sampling to the CDF Fπ​(x)=∑wPw​𝟏π⁡(w)≤xF_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{{w}}P_{{w}}{\bf 1}_{\pi({w})\leq x}, which defines a distribution over the vocabulary 𝒲{{\mathcal{W}}} based on a permutation π\pi.
The green-red list watermark, though biased, is widely used due to its simplicity. It partitions the vocabulary into green and red token sets, secretly boosts the probability of green tokens, and signals watermarking if the observed frequency of green tokens exceeds an expected threshold. Follow-up work has improved this scheme through parameter tuning [35] and by developing unbiased [14, 36, 38] or robust variants [42, 45].
3 Proportion Estimation Methods
In this section, we study how to estimate the proportion of watermarked content in mixed-source text. Section 3.1 defines the problem, Section 3.2 analyzes its estimability, and Section 3.3 presents our estimators.
3.1 Target Estimand
The first step is to identify the quantity we aim to estimate and the specific model where it arises.
Most existing watermark detection methods rely on pivotal statistics [20], which provide quantitative evidence of watermark signals at each token–pseudorandom pair (wt,ζt)({w}_{t},\zeta_{t}).
Those statistics exhibit a key dichotomy based on watermark integrity.
When the watermark signal in (wt,ζt)({w}_{t},\zeta_{t}) is disrupted—such as by modifying wt{w}_{t} or altering its preceding tokens so that ζt\zeta_{t} no longer generates wt{w}_{t}—wt{w}_{t} becomes statistically independent of ζt\zeta_{t} due to the statistical sensitivity of hash functions.
In such cases, the pivotal function YY ensures that Yt=Y⁡(wt,ζt)Y_{t}=Y({w}_{t},\zeta_{t}) always follows a known distribution μ0\mu_{0}, regardless of the distribution of wt{w}_{t}. Conversely, if the watermark signal in (wt,ζt)({w}_{t},\zeta_{t}) remains intact, wt{w}_{t} continues to depend on ζt\zeta_{t} via the decoder 𝒮{\mathcal{S}} so that Yt∼μ1,𝑷tY_{t}\sim\mu_{1,\bm{P}_{t}} with 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} the NTP distribution for wt{w}_{t}.
So, given 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t}, YtY_{t} follows either the null μ0\mu_{0} or the alternative μ1,𝑷t\mu_{1,\bm{P}_{t}}. The expected proportion following the alternative distributions naturally becomes our target estimand, as in Model 1.

Remark 3.1.
The σ\sigma-field ℱt{\mathcal{F}}_{t} represents the full information available up to token tt, including the generation history w1:t{w}_{1:t} and the NTP distributions 𝐏1:(t+1)\bm{P}_{1:(t+1)}. The formulation in (1) implies that, conditioned on 𝐏t\bm{P}_{t}, the pivotal statistic YtY_{t} follows a mixture of the null distribution μ0\mu_{0} and the alternative μ1,𝐏t\mu_{1,\bm{P}_{t}}. While ℱt{\mathcal{F}}_{t} is a useful concept, it includes unobservable components like 𝐏t\bm{P}_{t}, which the verifier does not have access to in practice. This model thus reflects the verifier’s uncertainty over user edits without assuming any specific editing strategy.
While Model 1 accurately captures the autoregressive behavior of LLMs, its application in practice and theory is constrained by two factors. First, from a practical standpoint, the verifier does not have access to critical components of the filtration ℱt{\mathcal{F}}_{t}, particularly each NTP distribution 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t}, which depend on the original prompt and the internal state of the LLM. This makes it infeasible to use the exact filtration in any detection or estimation procedure. Second, from a theoretical standpoint, the fact that each 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} evolves with the token history introduces complex temporal dependencies. Analyzing this dependence typically requires advanced martingale-based techniques, as seen in prior work [20, 19]. These challenges motivate the use of a simplified surrogate model, which we describe next.
Model 2 (Surrogate model).
We assume that each 𝐏t\bm{P}_{t} is fixed at the start of a generation, and the pivotal statistic YtY_{t} is still generated according to (1). In this setup, Y1,…,YnY_{1},\ldots,Y_{n} are independent but not identically distributed.
The key difference between Model 2 and Model 1 is the removal of the statistical dependence of the NTP distributions on token history.
We argue that Model 2 is a realistic and reasonable simplification—primarily because, from the verifier’s perspective, Models 1 and 2 are operationally indistinguishable.
This indistinguishability arises from the fact that the verifier lacks access to critical internal information used during generation, such as the original prompt, the internal state of the language model, and the NTP distributions themselves.
This can be further illustrated through the following thought experiment.
Consider a sequence w1:nw_{1:n} generated under Model 1, where each 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} depends on the full token history: wt=𝒮⁡(𝑷t,ζt)w_{t}={\mathcal{S}}(\bm{P}_{t},\zeta_{t}) with 𝑷t=LLM(w1:(t−1))\bm{P}_{t}=\mathrm{LLM}(w_{1:(t-1)}).
Now imagine freezing this exact sequence 𝑷1:n\bm{P}_{1:n} and defining a new model, LLMnew\mathrm{LLM}_{\mathrm{new}}, that always uses the pre-computed 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} at step tt to sample tokens regardless of the generation history.
If w1:nw_{1:n} is produced by LLM\mathrm{LLM}, we are in Model 1; if by LLMnew\mathrm{LLM}_{\mathrm{new}}, we are in Model 2.
Although the internal mechanisms differ, both models yield the same tokens and pivotal statistics—making them indistinguishable to the verifier, who observes only the final tokens and pseudorandom numbers, but not the model internals.
For this reason, we adopt Model 2 for method development and theoretical analysis, while evaluating our methods under the more realistic Model 1 in experiments.
As we will show, the estimators derived under Model 2 remain robust and effective when applied to data generated under Model 1.
Remark 3.2.
It is worth noting that the watermark proportion ε{\varepsilon} is defined in terms of pivotal statistics rather than individual tokens. As introduced in Section 2, each pivotal statistic is a deterministic function of consecutive tokens. As a result, modifying a single token may affect multiple pivotal statistics, leading to a discrepancy between the proportion of watermarked tokens and that of watermarked pivotal statistics. We adopt this definition for its simplicity and because nearly all existing detection rules rely on pivotal statistics.
3.2 Estimability
The next question is whether the proportion ε{\varepsilon} is well-defined—or, more precisely, identifiable—when the NTP distributions 𝑷1:n\bm{P}_{1:n} are unknown and potentially vary, as is the case for real-world verifiers. Identifiability, in this context, means that ε{\varepsilon} can be uniquely determined from the observed pivotal statistics alone, even when 𝑷1:n\bm{P}_{1:n} are unknown and arbitrary.
If ε{\varepsilon} is not identifiable, multiple values of ε{\varepsilon} could yield the same data distribution, making it impossible to obtain a unique and accurate estimate. This ambiguity challenges the interpretability of any downstream use of the estimate, as its real-world meaning is unclear.
Impossibility for the green-red list watermark.
Unfortunately, for the green-red list watermark, the proportion ε{\varepsilon} is not identifiable. To illustrate this, consider a straightforward example of proportion estimation in an i.i.d. binary mixture model.

4.4 Inherent Bias Caused by Non-identifiability
The green-red list watermark does not satisfy Assumption 3.1, so we cannot expect the aforementioned estimators to obtain high accuracy for this watermark. One piece of evidence is that, although Theorem 4.1 still holds for the green-red list watermark, the bias term ε​F¯𝑷​(δ)F0​(δ)=ε⁡(1−ε+ε​1−μ¯t1−γ){\varepsilon}\frac{\bar{F}_{\bm{P}}(\delta)}{F_{0}(\delta)}={\varepsilon}\left(1-{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}\frac{1-\bar{\mu}_{t}}{1-\gamma}\right) does not depend on δ\delta and will never vanish as δ→0\delta\to 0.
This is consistent with the non-identifiability issue: μ¯t\bar{\mu}_{t} is unobservable since each 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} is unobservable (see Table 1 for their relation), and the binary YtY_{t} signals provide negligible information about μ¯t\bar{\mu}_{t}. Even attempting to estimate μ¯t\bar{\mu}_{t} fails to resolve this issue. For instance, applying MLE to the i.i.d. binary mixture model in Lemma 3.1 to estimate unknown parameters μ\mu and ε{\varepsilon} introduces inherent bias, as shown in Theorem 4.5.

5 Experiments
We first evaluate estimation accuracy and inherent bias on simulation datasets and then check performance on open-source LLMs. Additional details and results are in the appendix. The code is available at: https://github.com/lx10077/WatermarkProportion.
5.1 Simulation Study
Following [19], we use a vocabulary of size |𝒲|=103|{{\mathcal{W}}}|=10^{3} and generate 2,500 sequences of 400 tokens each, yielding n=106n=10^{6} pivotal statistics as watermarked samples. The generation process mimics LLM behavior with a control parameter Δ∈(0,1)\Delta\in(0,1). For each token wt{w}_{t}, we construct its distribution 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} by uniformly sampling the largest probability from U⁡(0,1−Δ){U}(0,1-\Delta) and interpolating between Zipf’s law [46] and uniform distributions to ensure valid probabilities over 𝒲{{\mathcal{W}}} (Algorithm 1). We then sample wt∼𝑷t{w}_{t}\sim\bm{P}_{t}.
To simulate mixed-source data, we generate n=106n=10^{6} null samples from F0F_{0}. Across 200 uniformly spaced values ε∈[0,1]{\varepsilon}\in[0,1], we create mixture datasets combining n⁡(1−ε)n(1-{\varepsilon}) null samples with n​εn{\varepsilon} watermarked samples to estimate each ε{\varepsilon}. We aggregate all watermarked statistics into a single dataset for estimating g^\widehat{g} and F^𝑷\widehat{F}_{\bm{P}}, though some pivotal statistics in this aggregated dataset may be entirely irrelevant to the current task of estimating a specific ε{\varepsilon}.
An equivalence for implementation.
Since F0F_{0} is known, the probability integral transform [9] ensures that F0​(Y1),…,F0​(Yn)F_{0}(Y_{1}),\ldots,F_{0}(Y_{n}) are i.i.d. U⁡(0,1){U}(0,1) samples. We can therefore assume without loss of generality that μ0=U⁡(0,1)\mu_{0}={U}(0,1), with μ1,𝑷=F𝑷∘F0−1\mu_{1,\bm{P}}=F_{\bm{P}}\circ F_{0}^{-1} where F0−1F_{0}^{-1} is the generalized inverse. Assumption 3.1 remains valid under this transformation.
For all experiments, we apply this transform to ensure μ0=U⁡(0,1)\mu_{0}={U}(0,1), so our pivotal statistics are basically F0​(Y1),…,F0​(Yn)F_{0}(Y_{1}),\ldots,F_{0}(Y_{n}). For simplicity, we continue denoting them as Y1,…,YnY_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}.
How we obtain g^\widehat{g}.
The approach to estimating the density ratio gg is highly flexible, with many methods available in the statistical literature.
We estimate the density ratio gg using a simple histogram-based approach. The data in [0,1][0,1] is divided into N=500N=500 bins, with bin heights normalized to form probability densities that integrate to 1, yielding a piecewise constant estimator. While more sophisticated methods like kernel density estimation could provide smoother estimates, they are computationally prohibitive for the large datasets (n=106n=10^{6}).
The histogram approach offers better computational efficiency and scalability.

Non-identifiability.
Our estimators are expected to fail on the green-red list watermark because ε{\varepsilon} is not identifiable in this case, given that NTP distributions 𝑷1:n\bm{P}_{1:n} are inaccessible.
A further issue is that binary Ywm1:n≡1Y^{\mathrm{wm}}_{1:n}\equiv 1, which results in F^𝑷≡1\widehat{F}_{\bm{P}}\equiv 1 and provides no information about F¯𝑷\bar{F}_{\bm{P}}, leading to abnormal estimation behavior. Figure 1(d) confirms this theoretical prediction: INI often gives zero estimates while IND and OPT give estimates near one.
Inherent bias.
We then empirically validate the inherent bias predicted by Theorem 4.5 by generating nn i.i.d. samples from (1−ε)​Ber​(γ)+ε​Ber​(μ)(1-{\varepsilon})\text{Ber}(\gamma)+{\varepsilon}\text{Ber}(\mu), computing the L2L_{2}-regularized MLE via (12), and plotting ε^λ\widehat{{\varepsilon}}_{\lambda} against ε{\varepsilon}. The red dashed line representing the theoretical limit from (13) closely matches empirical estimates. Similar patterns across different parameter settings (Figure 7 in the appendix) show that non-identifiability renders standard estimation methods ineffective.
5.2 Open-source Model Experiments
We evaluate watermark proportion estimation using open-source LLMs under a controlled setup. We begin by sampling 500 documents from two corpora: the news-like C4 dataset [27] and the scientific arXiv dataset [6]. For each document, we extract the last 50 tokens as a prompt and generate 500 continuation tokens using one of three models: OPT-1.3B, OPT-13B [41], or LLaMA3.1-8B [11].
To introduce mixture data, at each step tt, the LLM generates wt{w}_{t} using a specified watermarking scheme with probability ε{\varepsilon}; with the remaining probability, it samples wt{w}_{t} directly from 𝑷t\bm{P}_{t} without watermarking.
We further apply 1-sequence repeated context masking [8], which watermarks wt{w}_{t} only if the current context window w(t−m):(t−1){w}_{(t-m):(t-1)} is unique in the generation history. This technique improves text quality but lowers the realized watermark proportion below the nominal ε{\varepsilon}, yielding a more realistic mix of watermarked and unwatermarked tokens.
All experiments are conducted under two temperature settings T∈{0.7,1}\mathrm{T}\in\{0.7,1\}.
We adopt WPL [44] as our baseline since no existing method directly estimates watermark proportions.22

We exclude another token-level localization method, SeedBS [18], due to its poor accuracy and prohibitive runtime.
WPL is a token-level localization method that assigns a binary label to each token indicating whether it is watermarked, and the overall proportion can be estimated by averaging these labels.

Performance after modification.
We then evaluate the performance of proportion estimators under post-processing modifications. Starting from a fully watermarked dataset, we modify a given proportion of tokens using random substitution, deletion, or insertion, and estimate the resulting watermark proportion. The true proportion is defined as the fraction of pivotal statistics following the alternative distribution. Specifically, if a token wtw_{t} or any of its mm preceding tokens is modified, we treat YtY_{t} as null. Thus, modifying an ε{\varepsilon}-fraction of tokens may affect up to an m​εm{\varepsilon}-fraction of pivotal statistics, resulting in a watermark proportion of at least 1−m​ε1-m{\varepsilon}. Results on the C4 dataset are shown in Table 4, with similar findings on the arXiv dataset in Table 5 in the appendix.
We have two observations.
First, our estimators remain robust under post-processing modifications, with OPT achieving the lowest MAE and variance in most cases, though INI or IND may occasionally perform better.
Second, all estimators perform slightly better than in the mixture setting (Table 3). This improvement arises because, in the post-processing setup, only a subset of tokens is modified, while the rest remain fully watermarked. This results in longer uninterrupted spans of watermarked text, making the underlying signal easier to detect than in the mixture setting, where watermarked and non-watermarked tokens are interleaved throughout.
This improvement also underscores the broad applicability of our formulation in Section 3.1.

6 Discussion
In this paper, we address the problem of estimating the proportion of watermarked pivotal statistics in mixed-source texts. We formulate this task as a coefficient estimation problem in a time-varying mixture model and establish conditions under which the proportion is identifiable. For the green-red list watermark, the proportion is not identifiable due to the binary nature of its pivotal statistic, which provides limited information. As a result, common estimation methods, such as maximum likelihood estimation, suffer from inherent and unavoidable bias. In contrast, for watermarks with continuous pivotal statistics, such as the Gumbel-max and inverse transform watermarks, we demonstrate that the proportion is identifiable. We propose several efficient estimators and show that they achieve the minimax optimal lower bounds within their respective classes. Our methods exhibit strong performance on both synthetic datasets and LLM-generated data.
Several open directions remain for future research. This work measures the extent of AI-generated content through pivotal statistics but does not account for its importance within mixed-source texts. For instance, 10% AI-generated content might carry critical significance in some contexts but be less relevant in others. Incorporating additional information, such as token semantics or entropies, could enable more nuanced proportion estimation. Exploring how to integrate these features is a promising avenue for future study.
Another direction is to investigate the theoretical limits of identifying AI-generated segments, including possible phase transitions, similar to classic work [10, 5]. Developing efficient and adaptive methods for segment identification could further advance the field.
Additionally, while our paper focuses on scalar pivotal statistics, some watermarking schemes, such as [8], use high-dimensional pivotal statistics. It even requires training neural networks for detection due to their complexity. Estimating the watermark proportion in such high-dimensional settings remains an open challenge. Addressing these issues could significantly enhance our understanding and capabilities in watermark proportion estimation.

Appendix C Open-source LLM Experiments: Details and Results
C.1 Details on Mixture Data Setting
Watermark generation.
We use a context window of size m=4m=4, where the random variable ζt\zeta_{t} is derived as ζt=𝒜(s(t−m):(t−1),𝙺𝚎𝚢)\zeta_{t}={\mathcal{A}}(s_{(t-m):(t-1)},{\mathtt{Key}}), based on the preceding mm tokens. The randomness is generated using the hash function 𝒜{\mathcal{A}}, as introduced in [43].
To avoid repetitive outputs, a watermark is applied only when the current text window is unique within the generation history, following the strategies outlined in [14, 36, 8]. This technique is known as 1-sequence repeated context masking in [8]. If no watermark is applied, tokens are sampled directly from the NTP distribution using multinomial sampling.
When constructing a dataset with the true proportion ε{\varepsilon}, we aim for approximately ε{\varepsilon}-proportion of the samples (i.e., observed pivotal statistics) to be watermarked and (1−ε)(1-{\varepsilon})-proportion to be null samples.
So we independently watermark each token with probability ε{\varepsilon}. However, applying 1-sequence repeated context masking can reduce the effective watermark proportion, as it may prevent some tokens that should be watermarked from being marked as such.
As a result, in our experiments, we calculate the average number of watermarked tokens to determine the ground truth ε{\varepsilon}.
Prompt generation.
Our prompt generation approach largely follows the methodology described in Appendix C.1 of [19]. Prompts are created by sampling documents from the news-focused section of the C4 dataset [27]. Each document is required to have at least 50 tokens, and shorter documents are excluded. Since retokenization may not perfectly align with the original token counts, we ensure that the verifier always processes at least nn tokens by adding special padding tokens, which are model-dependent. Additionally, to reduce the need for padding, we generate extra tokens—referred to as buffer tokens—beyond nn. In all experiments, 20 buffer tokens are generated, which generally eliminates the necessity for padding.
Details for IND and OPT.
The computation for the refined estimators (namely IND and OPT) follows the procedure outlined in Appendix B. To evaluate MAEs across various proportions ε{\varepsilon}, we generate a mixed-source dataset for each trial, ensuring that approximately ε{\varepsilon}-proportion of the samples are watermarked. Importantly, these datasets consist solely of pivotal statistics, not tokens.
After generating datasets for different values of ε{\varepsilon}, we aggregate all the watermarked pivotal statistics into a fully watermarked dataset. This combined dataset is then used to estimate g^\widehat{g} and F^𝑷\widehat{F}_{\bm{P}}. As a result, some pivotal statistics are entirely relevant to the current estimation task.
Details for the baseline WPL.
WPL, proposed by Zhao et al. [44], uses an existing online learning algorithm to compute a watermark score for each token, classifying it as watermarked if the score exceeds a specified threshold. For the Gumbel-max watermark, the score is defined as −log⁡(1−Y)-\log(1-Y), while for the inverse transform watermark, it is simply YY. The corresponding pivotal statistics YY are summarized in Table 1. To ensure a fair and competitive comparison, we tune the threshold over a predefined set and report the best result that achieves the lowest MAE across both figures and tables. Specifically, the threshold set is {1.3,1.5,1.7}\{1.3,1.5,1.7\} for Gumbel-max and {0.73,0.75,0.77}\{0.73,0.75,0.77\} for the inverse transform watermark.

C.3 Details on Post-processing Modification
The post-processing modification setup closely follows the earlier mixture data setting. We use a context window of size m=4m=4 and fix the temperature to 1. The same three LLMs are evaluated on both the C4 and arXiv datasets. Since the high temperature introduces sufficient randomness, we didn’t apply 1-sequence repeated context masking [8]. For each run, we sample prompts using the last 50 tokens of randomly selected documents and generate 500 continuation tokens. A specified fraction of tokens is then modified via random substitution, deletion, or insertion, and we apply various methods to estimate the watermark proportion.
The ground-truth proportion is defined as the fraction of pivotal statistics following the alternative distribution. Specifically, for any segment w(t−m):tw_{(t-m):t} that appears in the original modified text, the corresponding statistic Yt=Y(wt,ζt)=Y(wt,𝒜(w(t−m):(t−1),𝙺𝚎𝚢))Y_{t}=Y(w_{t},\zeta_{t})=Y(w_{t},{\mathcal{A}}(w_{(t-m):(t-1)},{\mathtt{Key}})) is treated as watermarked. This is equivalent to assuming Yt|𝑷t∼μ1,𝑷tY_{t}|\bm{P}_{t}\sim\mu_{1,\bm{P}_{t}}. We compute the proportion of such pivotal statistics as the true ε{\varepsilon}.
