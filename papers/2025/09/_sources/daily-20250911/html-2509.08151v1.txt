# Trust Semantics Distillation for Collaborator Selection via Memory-Augmented Agentic AI



Botao Zhu

   
Jeslyn Wang

   
Dusit Niyato

   
Xianbin Wang
††thanks: 
B. Zhu and X. Wang (Corresponding author) are with Western University, Canada. J. Wang is with the University of Toronto, Canada. D. Niyato is with Nanyang Technological University, Singapore.


Abstract
    
Accurate trustworthiness evaluation of potential collaborating devices is essential for the effective execution of complex computing tasks. This evaluation process involves collecting diverse trust-related data from potential collaborators, including historical performance and available resources, for collaborator selection. However, when each task owner independently assesses all collaborators’ trustworthiness, frequent data exchange, complex reasoning, and dynamic situation changes can result in significant overhead and deteriorated trust evaluation. To overcome these challenges, we propose a task-specific trust semantics distillation (2TSD) model based on a large AI model (LAM)-driven teacher-student agent architecture. The teacher agent is deployed on a server with powerful computational capabilities and an augmented memory module dedicated to multidimensional trust-related data collection, task-specific trust semantics extraction, and task-collaborator matching analysis. Upon receiving task-specific requests from device-side student agents, the teacher agent transfers the trust semantics of potential collaborators to the student agents, enabling rapid and accurate collaborator selection. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed 2TSD model can reduce collaborator evaluation time, decrease device resource consumption, and improve the accuracy of collaborator selection.

  




# I Introduction



With the growing complexity of computational applications over connected systems, individual resource-constrained devices become increasingly incompetent to execute complex computing tasks independently due to limited computational resources and capabilities. To address these limitations, a new collaborative computing paradigm, in which devices coordinate and share resources for collaborative task completion, has become a viable direction [1]. By leveraging distributed resources, this mechanism enhances the system’s task completion capabilities and operational efficiency. Due to its advantages, distributed collaborative computing has been widely adopted across domains such as smart manufacturing, smart cities, and e-health, providing significant benefits including reduced latency and improved resource utilization [2].



To achieve effective task completion, the selection of reliable collaborators is a critical prerequisite. Previous studies have developed different approaches to assess a collaborator’s reliability by analyzing its social attributes, historical behaviors, and reputation metrics [3].
Although these approaches offer partial insights, they often fall short of providing a situational-dependent assessment of collaborator trustworthiness, particularly with respect to task-specific considerations. Recently, trust has been adopted as an effective means for collaborator evaluation and selection. In this context, trust is defined as the expectation of a resource-constrained task owner on a potential collaborator’s capabilities, resources, and willingness for collaborative task completion [4]. Trust evaluation of potential collaborators, which involves trust-related data collection and analysis, thus becomes an essential step of collaborator selection and task completion.



However, trust consists of inherently complex indicators that fluctuates with time and collaboration context. Existing trust evaluation and device labeling methods fail to capture the temporal dynamics and multi-dimensional characteristics of trust. Specifically, a device may exhibit completely distinct trust under changed collaboration tasks, within different time windows, or across various performance metrics. Consequently, a more comprehensive and dynamic trust evaluation mechanism is needed to characterize the evolving and task-specific device trust. In this study, we propose a new concept of trust semantics, which refers to collaborator evaluation across multiple dimensions—such as communication and computation domains—to capture their temporal evolution for an aggregated trust assessment. Compared with trustworthy or untrustworthy labels, our trust semantics can accurately capture the comprehensive trust and reflect the varying trustworthiness of devices in dynamic collaboration environments. However, accurately evaluating trust semantics through orchestrated data gathering and analysis under realistic conditions for collaborator selection faces several challenges.



A primary challenge arises from the overhead of collecting diverse trust-related data from all potential collaborators. Frequent data exchange among these devices generates a massive volume of data, which incurs a significant burden and processing delay. Additionally, due to memory constraints, individual devices cannot store these data for all potential collaborators. To mitigate this challenge, a practical solution is to deploy a server within the collaborative system as the trust evaluation center, leveraging its ample computational and storage resources to collect and store trust-related data from all devices.



The second challenge lies in the difficulty of effectively assessing the trust semantics of collaborators due to the complexity of the collected trust-related data. These data are gathered from many devices, exhibiting multi-source and heterogeneous characteristics that require extremely complex processing, including consistency verification, feature alignment, and semantic fusion [5]. In addition, computational resource-related trust information must be analyzed in a task-specific context to accurately reflect a device’s collaboration potential. Consequently, advanced trust modeling and reasoning tools are required to integrate multi-source heterogeneous data and interpret their trust semantic relationships, thus enabling effective extraction of device trust semantics. Large AI models (LAMs), with their powerful semantic understanding, multimodal data fusion, and reasoning capabilities, are well-suited for extracting critical insights from complex data [6], making them promising tools for trust semantics extraction.



The third challenge stems from the continuously evolving nature of both the collaborating system environment and task-specific requirements. Available resources on a device constantly fluctuate due to operational status and network conditions, which requires dynamic trust assessment mechanisms to capture these changes promptly for accurate evaluation results [7]. Furthermore, task requirements may shift due to different objectives, necessitating adaptive trust evaluation for task-specific assessments of collaborators [8]. Leveraging their capabilities for continuous learning and autonomous adaptation, LAM-enabled agentic AI can perceive environmental changes, making them suitable for enabling dynamic trust evaluation in evolving collaborative systems.



To address the above challenges, this study proposes a novel task-specific trust semantics distillation (2TSD) model based on LAM-enabled agentic AI and a teacher-student agent architecture. The main contributions are summarized as follows.



• We propose a new concept of trust semantics to characterize the temporal evolution of devices across multiple dimensions, providing a more accurate and task-specific assessment of device trustworthiness. Additionally, we utilize LAM-enabled agentic AI for accurate extraction of trust semantics from complex trust-related data.



• We propose a teacher–student agent architecture for the first time for a server-based trust evaluation. The server-side teacher agent leverages comprehensive global data to extract task-specific trust semantics for devices and conduct task-collaborator matching analysis. The acquired knowledge is transferred to device-side student agents for lightweight collaborator selection. This architecture significantly reduces device resource consumption while enhancing the system’s adaptability in dynamic environments.



• We design an augmented memory module for the teacher agent, enabling task-specific trust semantics to be effectively stored, updated, and retrieved, thereby supporting student agents in rapidly performing collaborator selection decisions.







# II Agentic AI-Aided Teacher-Student Architecture for Trust Semantics Evaluation



LAM-driven agentic AI, endowed with autonomous intelligence, advanced reasoning, and goal-oriented execution, can effectively perform diverse tasks in complex environments. Individual agents with independent decision-making capabilities can form adaptive multi-agent systems, enabling cooperative task execution through coordinated interactions with other agents [9]. Building on these strengths, we propose the state-of-the-art 2TSD model based on the teacher-student agent architecture, as shown in Fig. 1. This model employs LAM-enabled agentic AI to perform trust semantics evaluation and transfer between a trust server, acting as the teacher agent, and task owners, serving as student agents, for task-specific collaborator selection. The following analysis systematically examines the structure, advantages, and strategic value of the proposed framework.



Fig. 1: A teacher-student architecture powered by LAM-enabled agentic AI for evaluating and transferring trust semantics between a trust server and task owners to enable task-specific collaborator selection.




# II-A LAM-Enabled Agentic AI for Trust Semantics Evaluation



Benefiting from LAMs’ capabilities in multi-modal information integration, deep semantic representation, and complex reasoning [10], LAM-enabled agentic AI could provide several key advantages for trust semantics evaluation.



• Evolving Trust Evaluation via Continuous Interaction.
Since device performance and task requirements often fluctuate, agentic AI can continuously interact with others to accumulate knowledge and learn from past collaborative experiences. This effectively enables adaptive trust assessments that track real-time variations in both devices and tasks.



• Task-Specific Trust Evaluation via Context-Aware Decision-Making.
With strong context-awareness, agentic AI can autonomously process collected device performance data, handling both explicit information, such as device states, and implicit information, including collaborators’ behavioral patterns, to extract task-specific trust semantics. In addition, it can intelligently conduct task-collaborator matching analysis, enabling task-specific collaborator selection.



• Accurate Trust Semantic Extraction. Trust-related data are collected from diverse devices and span multiple heterogeneous dimensions, necessitating effective fusion.
Leveraging advanced cross-modal semantic understanding, agentic AI can integrate complex and heterogeneous data and extract precise trust semantics.







# II-B Teacher-Student Agent Architecture



Due to their limited computational resources, devices are not suitable for complex trust semantics extraction in dynamic environments. To address this, a server equipped with the LAM-enabled teacher agent is proposed to collect global trust-related data, extract trust semantics, and transfer the acquired knowledge to device-side student agents, enabling them to perform lightweight collaborator selection. This setup forms a teacher–student agent architecture between the server and the devices, whose specific advantages are outlined below.



• Unified Trust Evaluation Standard. By possessing global trust-related data, the server-side agent can evaluate all devices using a unified assessment standard, thereby eliminating inconsistencies that may arise from local observation differences or independent evaluation by student agents.



• Reduced Communication and Computation Overhead.
By reporting trust-related data solely to the server, devices avoid massive inter-device data exchange and the need for complex local trust evaluations, substantially reducing both communication overhead and computational load.



• Rapid Collaborator Selection.
The teacher agent, leveraging its computational capabilities and the augmented memory module, can rapidly respond to devices’ task requests by providing precise task-specific trust semantics. The student agents then utilize this knowledge to perform lightweight local decisions, enabling fast collaborator selection.



Fig. 2: Workflow of the server-side memory-augmented teacher agent: resource information storage, historical record storage, and trust semantics extraction and storage.








# III Task-Specific Trust Semantics Distillation



This section details the implementation of the 2TSD model, including the system model, the extraction and storage of task-specific trust semantics, and their transfer to the task owner for collaborator selection.





# III-A System Model



We consider a collaborative system consisting of II devices {a1,…,aI}\{a_{1},\dots,a_{I}\} and a server bb. An LAM-enabled teacher agent is deployed on the server, while each device hosts an LAM-enabled student agent.
Every device can act as a task owner to generate a computational task, which is defined by its physical attributes. For example, device aia_{i} generates a face recognition task consisting of a set of images, represented as c1c_{1} = {“task type” : “face recognition”, “size” : “100 MB”, “processing density” : “2,339 cycles/bit”, “maximum task completion tolerance time” : “60 seconds”}. For elaboration purposes, we assume that the system includes three task types: facial recognition, video transcoding, and text word count [4]. If device aja_{j} is selected by device aia_{i} to execute task c1c_{1}, the server bb monitors their collaboration and records the relevant performance data of aja_{j}, denoted as pajp_{a_{j}} = {“task owner” : “aia_{i}”, “collaborator” : “aja_{j}”, “task type” : “face recognition”, “collaboration time” : “2025-08-18 14:00:00”, “throughput” : “100 Mbps”, “packet loss rate” : “1%”, “task processing speed” : “2 MB/second”, “accuracy” : “98%”, “comprehensive result” : “satisfied”}.







# III-B Task-Specific Trust Semantics Extraction and Storage



The server-side teacher agent, acting as the central authority for trust decisions, collects and stores trust-related data, including historical performance records and resource information. It then extracts, updates, and maintains task-specific trust semantics for devices, which are subsequently provided to student agents.
Due to LAM’s limited internal memory, an external memory module is required to support storage and efficient retrieval. Therefore, we design the memory module with three distinct components: i) Resource information storage component stores the physical resource information of all devices; ii) Historical record storage component maintains historical performance data of all devices. iii) Trust semantics storage component stores task-specific trust semantics of devices derived from historical performance data. Next, we present a detailed description of the teacher agent’s workflow, as shown in Fig. 2, highlighting how it uses the memory module.



• Resource Information Storage. Each device reports its resources under the idle condition, including CPU, storage, bandwidth, and so on, to the server. To maintain up-to-date information, the device promptly notifies the server whenever its idle-state resources change. Note that resource changes occurring during task execution do not need to be synchronized with the server. In the resource information storage component, the resource information of each device is stored in a key–value format, where the key corresponds to the device and the value represents its resources.



• Historical Record Storage. After a pair of devices completes a collaborative task, the server receives the collaborator’s performance data, such as pajp_{a_{j}}, and stores it in the historical record storage component of the memory module. This component is implemented through a relational database, such as MySQL, where each record is stored in a structured format that includes task owner, collaborator, task type, collaboration timestamp, and associated communication and computation performance metrics.



Fig. 3: Task-specific trust semantics transfer from the teacher agent to the student agent for collaborator selection.


• Task-Specific Trust Semantics Extraction.
After storing a device’s historical performance data, the teacher agent promptly extracts and stores its task-specific trust semantics. As shown in Fig. 2, based on pajp_{a_{j}}, the teacher agent retrieves device aja_{j}’s historical records for face recognition task type within a specified time period from the historical record component, denoted as MajM_{a_{j}}, and feeds them into the LAM to extract the task-specific trust semantics for device aja_{j}. The trust semantics comprises three parts: the device’s overall trust status, the temporal variations of communication-related trust data, and the temporal variations of computation-related trust data. The extracted trust semantics is represented as “{“device” : “aja_{j}”, “task_type” : “face recognition”, “trust_semantics (TajfrT^{\text{fr}}_{a_{j}})” : {“state” : “trusted”, “communication-related” : “packet loss rate shows a normal trend, throughput shows a decreasing trend”, “computation-related” : “accuracy shows a normal trend, task processing speed shows a normal trend”}, …}”.



• Task-Specific Trust Semantics Storage. To enable flexible management of trust semantics, we design a tree-structured storage module with three hierarchical layers: task type, device, and trust semantics. In this structure, all task types, devices, and task-specific trust semantics are represented as nodes, with the root node v0v_{0} serving as the starting point without semantic meaning. Each node is expressed as a triplet <<self, parent, child>>, supporting structured storage and traversal operations.
For instance, given the trust semantics extracted previously, the teacher agent first checks whether the face recognition node already exists in the tree. If absent, a new face recognition node is created. If present, the teacher agent then checks whether the device node aja_{j} is present under this task type. If absent, a new aja_{j} node is inserted along with its trust semantics as a child node. If node aja_{j} already exists, its trust semantics child node will be updated. Each device currently maintains a single trust semantics entry per task type, which can be extended to support multiple temporal entries for the same task type.



It should be noted that the teacher agent repeatedly performs the above steps to maintain up-to-date task-specific trust semantics for each device.







# III-C Task-Specific Trust Semantics Transfer from Teacher to Student



When a device initiates a new task, the teacher agent transmits the stored task-specific trust semantics of potential collaborators, along with the corresponding task-collaborator matching analysis, to the student agent on that device, as shown in Fig. 3. The student agent then utilizes this information to rapidly select an appropriate collaborator. For example, device aia_{i} generates a new task c2c_{2} = {“task type” : “video transcoding”, “size” : “50 MB”, “processing density” : “1,000 cycles/bit”, “maximum task completion tolerance time” : “50 seconds”} and sends the request, “I have a task c2c_{2} = {…}, could you help me identify some trusted collaborators?”, to the server.



• Task-Specific Trust Semantics Retrieval. Upon receiving a task request, the teacher agent quickly locates the corresponding task type node within its tree-structured trust semantics storage component. It then retrieves all child and grandchild nodes of that task type node to obtain the potential collaborators and their associated trust semantics. For task c2c_{2}, the retrieved relevant information is represented as Mc2tsM_{c_{2}}^{\text{ts}} = {{“device” : “aka_{k}”, “task_type” : “video transcoding”, “trust semantics (TakvtT_{a_{k}}^{\text{vt}})” : …}, {“device” : “aja_{j}”,…}, {“device” : “ala_{l}”,…}…}.



• Resource Information Retrieval. For the potential collaborators obtained in the previous step, the teacher agent queries their resources from the resource information storage component. Based on Mc2tsM_{c_{2}}^{\text{ts}}, all retrieved devices and their resource information are represented as Mc2reM^{\text{re}}_{c_{2}} = {{“device” : “aka_{k}”, “CPU” : “2.91 GHz”…}, {“device” : “aja_{j}”,…}, {“device” : “ala_{l}”,…}…}.



• Evaluation of Task-Collaborator Matching via Chain-of-Trust. Evaluating whether potential collaborators possess sufficient resources to complete a task effectively is a key aspect of trust assessment. Given the complexity of this evaluation, it is carried out by the teacher agent to guarantee accuracy. The teacher agent inputs Mc2reM^{\text{re}}_{c_{2}} and task c2c_{2} into the LAM, which performs the task-collaborator matching analysis for each device. To reduce computational complexity and improve evaluation precision, a chain-of-trust mechanism is employed.
This mechanism decomposes the evaluation into multiple chained stages aligned with task requirements, with the output of each stage serving as the input for the next. The final stage produces the aggregated evaluation result. Detailed implementation of the chain-of-trust mechanism is provided in our prior work [11]. For task c2c_{2}, devices aka_{k} and aja_{j} are identified as meeting the task requirements, denoted as Mc2mtM_{c_{2}}^{\text{mt}} = {{“device” : “aka_{k}”, “task-collaborator matching” : “yes”}, {“device” : “aja_{j}”, “task-collaborator matching” : “yes”}}.



• Selection of Potential Collaborators. Based on Mc2tsM_{c_{2}}^{\text{ts}} and Mc2mtM_{c_{2}}^{\text{mt}}, the teacher agent selects potential collaborators that are both trustworthy and meet the task requirements. The results in Mc2mtM_{c_{2}}^{\text{mt}} are then integrated into devices’ trust semantics, denoted as Mc2pcM_{c_{2}}^{\text{pc}} = {{“device” : “aka_{k}”, “task_type” : “video transcoding”, “trust semantics (TakvtT_{a_{k}}^{\text{vt}})” : {“state” : “trusted”, “communication-related” : “packet loss rate shows a normal trend, throughput shows a normal trend”, “computation-related” : “accuracy shows a normal trend, task processing speed shows a normal trend”, “task-collaborator matching” : “yes”}}, {“device” : “aja_{j}”, “task_type” : “video transcoding”, “trust semantics (TajvtT_{a_{j}}^{\text{vt}})” : {“state” : “trusted”, “communication-related” : “packet loss rate shows a increasing trend, throughput shows a normal trend”, “computation-related” : “accuracy shows a normal trend, task processing speed shows a normal trend”, “task-collaborator matching” : “yes”}}}. Subsequently, the teacher agent sends Mc2pcM_{c_{2}}^{\text{pc}} to the student agent on device aia_{i}.



• Lightweight Decision-Making for Final Collaborator Selection.
Upon receiving information from the teacher agent, the student agent selects the final collaborator through a lightweight decision-making process based on its own objectives, preferences, or other strategies. For example, device aia_{i} ultimately selects device aka_{k} as the collaborator for task c2c_{2}. Although both candidate devices aka_{k} and aja_{j} are evaluated as trustworthy, device aja_{j} is excluded from the final selection due to an increasing packet loss rate.



The proposed 2TSD model effectively reduces communication and computational overhead on devices while improving the accuracy, timeliness, and adaptability of task-specific trust evaluation, thereby providing a robust foundation for collaborator selection in dynamic environments.









# IV Experimental Analysis



Fig. 4: 2TSD reduces collaborator evaluation time compared with baseline methods by leveraging trust semantics stored in the memory module of the teacher agent.


To validate the proposed approach, we create a collaborative system comprising 9 Google Pixel 8 smartphones and a Dell 5200 server. We implement all agents in the system using GPT-4o-mini. Agents interact with GPT-4o-mini via API calls, while devices communicate over Wi-Fi.



Fig. 4 presents a comparison of collaborator evaluation times, defined as the interval between task generation by the task owner and the completion of collaborator selection.
As the number of devices increases, the collaborator evaluation times of the trust model with fitness-based clustering scheme (TMFSC) [12] and the 2TSD without a memory module rise. This is because these methods require additional time for trust-related data collection and trust evaluation. The 2TSD without a memory module takes slightly more time than TMFSC due to the inference overhead of the LAM. In contrast, the collaborator evaluation time of the proposed 2TSD method remains almost constant, as it only needs to extract stored task-specific trust semantics from the memory module and perform the task-collaborator matching analysis, thus significantly accelerating collaborator selection.



Fig. 5 compares the number of data collections, calculated as the historical performance data collection events that occur on devices. The 2TSD model requires fewer collections than TMFSC because its teacher agent uses the augmented memory module to store collaborators’ historical performance records. Consequently, during collaborator selection for each task, 2TSD avoids redundant data collection from potential collaborators, substantially reducing device-side communication and computational overhead. In contrast, TMFSC without a memory module must collect these data from potential collaborators for every task.



Fig. 6 compares collaborator selection accuracy. It can be observed that 2TSD significantly outperforms TMFSC, which can be attributed to two factors. First, the server in 2TSD maintains historical performance data for all devices, enabling the extraction of more accurate task-specific trust semantics. Second, leveraging LAM for task-collaborator matching analysis enhances matching precision, thereby improving the accuracy of collaborator selection.



Fig. 5: 2TSD achieves fewer data collections compared with TMFSC as its teacher agent uses the augmented memory module to store collaborators’ historical performance records.


Fig. 6: 2TSD improves the accuracy of collaborator selection compared with TMFSC due to precise trust semantics extraction and task-collaborator matching analysis.






# V Future Directions



The concept of trust semantics presents many promising research directions, with numerous opportunities for further exploration alongside the 2TSD model, particularly regarding their applications in realistic environments.



• Evaluation and Mitigation of Environmental Changes on Dynamic Trust. Future connected systems could face extremely dynamic operational environments. This is mainly due to dynamics in computing and communication resources as well as operational environments. By leveraging an LAM’s capabilities in environmental perception and semantic understanding, the influence of environmental factors on dynamic trust can be characterized more precisely.



• Predictive Trust Distillation. Given the complexity and inevitable processing delays, trust semantics evaluation and exchange in collaborative systems could take a significant amount of time–leading to reduced relevancy of trust evaluation due to evolving task requirements. To mitigate the undesired impact, predictive trust evaluation mechanisms could be incorporated into 2TSD for anticipated potential trust semantics, thereby enhancing the foresight and adaptability of decision-making on collaborator selection.



• Trust Semantics Extraction Under Data-Missing Scenarios. The current communication infrastructure experiences lots of difficulty in achieving deterministic and guaranteed data exchange. As a result, packet and data losses are unavoidable between the teacher and student agents in the proposed trust semantics distillation process. Given that trust-related data may be incomplete or missing in practice, accurately extracting trust semantics under realistic conditions of data sparsity or missingness constitutes an important research direction.







# VI Conclusion



In this paper, we have proposed an innovative agentic-AI based teacher-student architecture for trust semantics distillation, aimed at achieving resource-efficient, rapid, and accurate collaborator selection in dynamic systems. The teacher agent deployed at the server side extracts and maintains task-specific trust semantics while conducting task-collaborator matching analysis. Through knowledge transfer from the teacher agent, device-side student agents are enabled to execute lightweight collaborator selection decisions locally. The proposed model significantly reduces computational and communication overhead on devices, facilitates rapid decision-making, and enhances the accuracy of collaborator selection by leveraging task-specific trust semantics. Furthermore, its scalable and robust architecture ensures reliability and adaptability in complex dynamic environments, providing a solid technical foundation for intelligent collaborative decision-making.
