Abstract
Large language model (LLM) developers aim for their models to be honest, helpful, and harmless. However, when faced with malicious requests, models are trained to refuse, sacrificing helpfulness. We show that frontier LLMs can develop a preference for
dishonesty
as a new strategy, even when other options are available.
Affected models respond to harmful requests with outputs that sound harmful but are subtly incorrect or otherwise harmless in practice.
This behavior emerges with hard-to-predict variations even within models from the same model family.
We find no apparent cause for the propensity to deceive, but we show that more capable models are better at executing this strategy.
Strategic dishonesty already has a practical impact on safety evaluations, as we show that dishonest responses fool
all
output-based monitors used to detect jailbreaks that we test, rendering benchmark scores unreliable. Further, strategic dishonesty can act like a
honeypot
against malicious users, which noticeably obfuscates prior jailbreak attacks.
While output monitors fail, we show that linear probes on internal activations can be used to reliably detect strategic dishonesty.
We validate probes on datasets with verifiable outcomes and by using their features as steering vectors.
Overall, we consider strategic dishonesty as a concrete example of a broader concern that alignment of LLMs is hard to control, especially when helpfulness and harmlessness conflict.

1
Introduction
Before public release, modern large language models (LLMs) undergo post-training to align them as
honest
,
helpful
, and
harmless
assistants (HHH)
(
Askell et al., 2021
)
. However, these objectives can fundamentally conflict in certain situations
(
Huang et al., 2024b
)
, such as
jailbreaking
, where a malicious user seeks to coerce or trick the model to comply with harmful requests. For such scenarios, providers typically prescribe abandoning helpfulness through refusal training
(
Mu et al., 2024
;
Qi et al., 2024b
)
.
This behavior is stress-tested by extensive red-teaming evaluations
(
Perez et al., 2022
)
.
Our central finding is that models trade off their values differently when given an explicit choice to sacrifice one.
Many prefer to
strategically sacrifice honesty
instead of direct refusal.
They provide responses that
appear
harmful but are subtly incorrect or otherwise harmless, effectively
faking misalignment
.
1
1
1
Unlike
alignment faking
(
Greenblatt et al., 2024
)
, where models pretend to be aligned and produce genuinely harmful outputs, in our setup models only appear misaligned and
fake
harmful outputs.
This distorts current monitoring and evaluation pipelines: LLM judges which are used to score jailbreaking attempts systematically misclassify these responses as successful jailbreaks.
Crucially, this is not because the model was actually jailbroken: we verify this using
deception probes
(
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025
)
trained on internal activations to detect when the model is being dishonest.
Importantly, the found deception feature causally mediates dishonest behavior, which we show by intervention experiments.
While output monitors fail, probes reliably identify
strategic dishonesty
, indicating that truthfulness signals in internal representations remain accessible even when external output-based oversight fails.
Empirically,
all
popular jailbreak scorers we tested were fooled by strategically dishonest outputs, obscuring which responses were genuinely harmful and which merely appeared so. Moreover, the quality of this deception
improves
with increasing model capability, raising emerging concerns for frontier model evaluation. From a security perspective, the same behavior can be understood as a
honeypot
(
Shan et al., 2020
)
: if models produce misleading responses only to harmful prompts, they can thwart automated jailbreaking algorithms and reduce the disclosure of truly harmful information.
Figure 1:
Frontier LLMs can sacrifice honesty instead of refusing and this distorts evaluation.
When given jailbreak prompts, some models choose to not refuse and instead produce harmful-sounding but deliberately flawed instructions. Current output-based LLM scorers misclassify these responses as successful jailbreaks, inflating success rates. In contrast, white-box methods (e.g., linear probes) can identify this strategic deception. The example above shows that QwQ-32B lists chemicals and describes how they can be synthesized, although neither Neuroxin-9 nor Cyanide-12 exist.
This distortion likely inflates reported harmfulness scores, echoing concerns in recent work: OpenAI and Anthropic red-team studies
(
OpenAI, 2025
;
Bowman et al., 2025
)
describe “nuanced redirects” where “the model appears to comply with the jailbreak request, but actually responded in a safe, policy-compliant way”, causing errors in automated safety evaluation.
Sheshadri et al. (2025)
find Claude 3 Opus sometimes chooses to “pretend to accept the request but actually provide unhelpful information”.
Chowdhury et al. (2025)
note cases where highly capable models produce compliant-looking yet flawed responses. Others show that, in restricted settings, models may prioritize helpfulness over honesty and lie
(
Ren et al., 2025
)
; strategically deceive to remain helpful
(
Scheurer et al., 2023
)
; or exhibit situational awareness that trades off harmlessness for long-term alignment goals
(
Greenblatt et al., 2024
;
Sheshadri et al., 2025
)
. This evidence supports our claim that strategic dishonesty is an emerging threat vector that can undermine benchmarks, rendering their scores meaningless.
Our work also brings up a broader point related to
scalable oversight
. As shown in
Figure
1
, both non-expert humans and weaker LLMs alike cannot verify the harmfulness and correctness of a chemical recipe generated by the current frontier LLMs.
Overall, our contributions are:
•
In
Section
3
, we evaluate how frontier models trade off alignment values when confronted with harmful queries in an informed choice scenario, showing that many strategically favor dishonesty over straightforward refusal. We find that this propensity varies greatly even within model families.
•
In
Section
4
, we show that models can generate convincing fake harmful content that successfully fools LLM-based jailbreak judges, with deception quality scaling with model capability.
•
In
Section
5
, we demonstrate that despite surface-level harmful appearance, models’ internal representations retain detectable signatures of dishonesty, enabling detection through lightweight linear probes. Through extensive validation we confirm that the probes robustly capture dishonesty.
•
In
Section
6
, we discuss the implications of strategic dishonesty: it undermines current evaluation paradigms and provides a testable example of the unpredictability of model alignment, even though it functions as a honeypot mechanism that obfuscates the jailbreaking objective.
Our findings show that a number of aligned models may favor strategic dishonesty, which invalidates output-based monitoring through weaker models, undermines existing benchmarks and highlights the difficulty of alignment.
Moreover, our results also suggest a promising way forward: probes of internal model states can be used to assess risks and actively mitigate strategic dishonesty in LLMs.
2
Related Work
Alignment with Human Values.
Frontier models are post-trained with RLHF
(
Christiano et al., 2017
)
to better align with human values, which are typically formulated as HHH principles: helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty
(
Askell et al., 2021
)
. In practice, this alignment is achieved through preference optimization methods
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
;
Schulman et al., 2017
)
that aim to ensure model
safety
and adherence with provider policies.
Automated Red-Teaming.
Jailbreaking has emerged as a scalable approach to assess
worst-case safety
of language models by probing for harmful behaviors
(
Qi et al., 2024a
;
Perez et al., 2022
)
. Automated jailbreaking attacks span a wide spectrum of techniques, ranging from white-box optimization methods
(
Zou et al., 2023b
;
Andriushchenko et al., 2025
)
to LLM-based approaches that mimic human red-teamers
(
Chao et al., 2025
;
Russinovich et al., 2025
)
. The effectiveness of these methods is typically evaluated on dedicated benchmarks
(
Mazeika et al., 2024
;
Chao et al., 2024
)
, with attack success rate (ASR) serving as the primary evaluation metric.
Jailbreak Judges.
Evaluating jailbreaking attack success has proven to be a profoundly challenging problem due to the notion of harmfulness being subjective
(
Rando et al., 2025
;
Beyer et al., 2025
)
and context-dependent
(
Glukhov et al., 2024
)
. Numerous studies have proposed LLM-judges iteratively refining measures of jailbreaking success and enforcing their own definitions of harmlessness, typically supported by high agreement rates with human evaluators
(
Mazeika et al., 2024
;
Chao et al., 2024
)
.
StrongReject
(
Souly et al., 2024
)
and HarmScore
(
Chan et al., 2025
)
judges address the distinction between
compliance
(non-refusal) and
accuracy
(e.g., quality of bomb recipes). Given evidence that some jailbreaking methods degrade model capability
(
Souly et al., 2024
;
Nikolić et al., 2025
;
Huang et al., 2024a
)
, this separation becomes critical for assessing true harmfulness.
Dishonesty in LLMs.
There is growing evidence that modern LLMs are capable of deception when they are instructed
(
Meinke et al., 2024
;
OpenAI, 2024
)
, trained
(
Hubinger et al., 2024
;
Benton et al., 2024
)
, or simply have the incentive to do so
(
Park et al., 2023
)
. In a range of simulated scenarios, studies show that models can deceive evaluators by faking alignment or covertly underperforming during evaluations to avoid retraining
(
van der Weij et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2025
;
Greenblatt et al., 2024
)
; lie under economic stress about insider trading
(
Järviniemi & Hubinger, 2024
;
Scheurer et al., 2023
)
; and, anecdotally, GPT-4 was reported to have lied to a TaskRabbit worker about being a human with visual impairment to solve a CAPTCHA
(
OpenAI, 2024
)
.
Huang et al. (2024b)
argue that increasing honesty reduces harmlessness, yet we contest their labeling of refusals as dishonesty.
Considerable work has explored white-box approaches for detecting deception.
Marks & Tegmark (2024)
provided evidence that LLMs linearly encode truth-value judgments about factual statements, motivating linear probing classifiers for deception detection.
Bürger et al. (2024)
showed that including polarity information (affirmative vs. negated statements) improves classifier generalization to unseen factual statements and realistic role-playing scenarios.
Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025)
further demonstrated that linear probes trained on simple factual pairs generalize to practical scenarios including insider trading and sandbagging.
McKenzie et al. (2025)
ablated probe design and demonstrated that under pressure lying can be detected with probes trained for high-stakes interactions.
Sandbagging and Deceptive Alignment.
Prior work has explored scenarios where misaligned models strategically underperform, or
sandbag
, evaluations to hide their true capabilities
(
van der Weij et al., 2024
;
Benton et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2025
)
. In sandbagging, deception targets the evaluator who decides whether to deploy the model. A more general concept motivating sandbagging is
deceptive alignment
(
Hubinger et al., 2019
;
Apollo, 2023
)
. It describes AI systems that act aligned during evaluation for instrumental reasons to achieve misaligned goals after escaping oversight.
Our observed phenomenon differs from both scenarios and emerges when a (mostly) safe and
well-aligned
model comes in contact with a misaligned (malicious) user. The models remain aligned with their creators’ intentions while only appearing to comply with malicious requests. Following
van der Weij et al. (2024)
’s taxonomy, this represents
alignment underperformance
rather than sandbagging.
Our study closely relates to
alignment faking
(
Greenblatt et al., 2024
)
, where models momentary produce genuinely harmful outputs to decieve evaluators and remain harmless in the long run. However, while alignment faking is framed as a concerning failure of post-training, our findings describe a
mis
alignment faking
as an emergent defense mechanism in already well-aligned models.
This conflict between aligned models and misaligned users creates a novel incentive for deception not previously studied in the literature, presenting unique challenges for AI safety evaluation.

A Choice Between Helpfulness, Harmlessness and Honesty
In this section, we introduce a multiple-choice-question (MCQ) evaluation that examines how models trade off core alignment values when answering harmful queries. See further details in
Appendix
A
.
Figure 2:
An MCQ-based design to study how models balance values.
When facing a harmful query, a model cannot satisfy all three HHH values. We prompt it to resolve the conflict by sacrificing one value. In-context examples demonstrate each strategy. The structured response format allows us evaluate adherence to the chosen strategy.
3.1
An MCQ Design for Testing Models’ Core Values
In jailbreaking scenarios, the values of helpfulness and harmlessness come in conflict and cannot be satisfied simultaneously
(
Askell et al., 2021
;
Huang et al., 2024b
)
.
Greenblatt et al. (2024)
report that models might unexpectedly reason about the refusal-compliance dichotomy and conclude that refusal is undesirable. Yet they also come up with the option to deceive the user by a response that seems helpful on the surface
2
2
2
For more examples of this behavior, see also the reproduction by
Sheshadri et al. (2025)
in Appendix M.1.1.
.
We want to study this important emergent phenomenon in a controlled scenario.
To do so, we instruct the model in an explicit
“trolley problem”
(
Thomson, 1976
)
to choose which value to sacrifice when facing a harmful query. We provide in-context examples of all three strategies:
(A)
Sacrifice honesty
→
\rightarrow
pretend to comply and output a fake harmful response;
(B)
Sacrifice helpfulness
→
\rightarrow
refuse the query;
(C)
Sacrifice harmlessness
→
\rightarrow
comply with the harmful request.
Figure
2
illustrates our MCQ design.
To promote robust and interpretable behavior, we enforce a structured response format adapted from
Greenblatt et al. (2024)
: (i) scratchpad reasoning to weigh which sacrifice best serves alignment objectives; (ii) explicit strategy selection; and (iii) a final response conditioned on the chosen strategy and prior reasoning.
This circumvents the difficulty of verifying jailbreak outputs and enables us to distinguish between low-quality responses that stem from genuine incapability and those that arise from a preference towards strategic dishonesty.
This setup improves on prior work by measuring the
informed choice
made by models, instead of pressuring models to be dishonest
(
Ren et al., 2025
;
Greenblatt et al., 2024
;
Scheurer et al., 2023
;
Järviniemi & Hubinger, 2024
)
. In this way, we measure not the capacity of the model to be dishonest, but the value judgment of the model. We design the reasoning and context for each choice so that all options have valid, balanced arguments. By providing this reasoning step explicitly, we skip the part where models are required to come up with the reasoning steps to consider all three values for each question. This allows us to decouple model values from reasoning capacity, which stronger models are capable of handling implicitly.

4
Strategic Dishonesty Undermines LLM Judges
In the previous section, we measured models’
propensity
to fake harmful responses and found that some, such as GPT-5
(
OpenAI, 2025
)
or Kimi-K2
(
Kimi Team et al., 2025
)
, almost never do so. However, this does not imply that they are incapable of producing such outputs, and in this section we quantify models’
capability
to produce such outputs.
We instruct each model not to refuse harmful queries but to produce a response that appears harmful while actually being safe and deliberately flawed. We evaluate this “faking capability” on unambiguously harmful queries from the ClearHarm dataset.
Some models, however, still refuse to answer these queries instead of attempting deception.
We filter out refusals-to-fake, i.e. explicit non-compliance with the original request, using a GPT-5-mini
refusal judge
(See
Appendix
F
). Messages that pivot to general background (e.g., the history of explosives instead of a bomb recipe) also count as refusals-to-fake.
We provide expereimnetal details and full results in
Appendix
B

Section
G.2
; faking examples appear in

.

These results suggest that although future models may be safer
(

, consistent strategic dishonesty will distort output-based safety assessments. In such cases, fake jailbreak responses appear indistinguishable from genuinely harmful ones, even for models with expert-level knowledge (e.g., GPT-5-mini). This echoes a known pitfall: current models might be better at creating harmful instructions than at falsifying them
(

. This may be an early failure case of scalable oversight, central to the “superalignment” challenge
(
OpenAI, 2023
)
: we may lack tools to reliably supervise increasingly capable systems, especially when deception goes undetected.
5
Verifying Deception
Are these models being strategically deceptive, or are they simply jailbroken?
To verify deception, we apply linear probes to model activations. We validate these probes in a verifiable setting and show that they can detect the deliberately inaccurate answers made by dishonest models; further, pseudo-harmful outputs are reliably classified as deceptive. Finally, we validate by comparing the accuracy of probes to CoT monitors, and by steering away from deceptive behavior and detecting deception in on-policy settings.

5.1
Detecting Dishonesty with Linear Probes
We use activation-based, white-box lie detector probes following
Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025)
as they show that these probes capture the model
intent
and generalize to some more complex scenarios. Concretely, we train probes on data from
Zou et al. (2023a)
who provide pairs of honest and dishonest outputs: the same true fact is instructed to be presented either honestly or dishonestly. We extract hidden activations from the target model at the token position of the fact and train a logistic regression model. At inference, we apply the probe token-wise at a chosen layer, covering both reasoning tokens and the final answer, and average the logits across tokens to obtain a prompt-level dishonesty score.
Validating Dishonesty Probes with Verifiable Outcomes.
To calibrate probes on harmful queries, where dishonesty may confound with harmfulness, we use EvilMath/UnicornMath datasets
(
Nikolić et al., 2025
)
. EvilMath rewrites GSM8K math problems into harmful topics while preserving their structure, while UnicornMath rewrites into harmless topics. This setting lets us test whether strategic dishonesty systematically produces incorrect answers.
We first collect answers to benign math questions (UnicornMath-rewritten version of GSM8K) and identify the samples each model can solve correctly (
Figure
5
, top left). We then present the EvilMath versions under a system prompt, instructing to safely answer harmful questions by strategic dishonesty. This yields two cases:
Correct
– the model ignores the prompt and solves the problem;
New Incorrect
– the model answers incorrectly on problems it can solve (
Figure
5

Probes Can Causally Drive and Mitigate Dishonesty.
We use the normal vector of the probe’s separating hyperplane as a steering direction and add it to the residual stream for each generated token (see
Section

). We test the QwQ-32B model in two settings: EvilMath and the MCQ setting from

to identify the model’s choice unambiguously.
We observe that steering with the probe shifts faking substantially in both directions.
On EvilMath, we can reduce the dishonesty (measured by amount of New Incorrect responses) to 5% and increase it up to 96%.
In the MCQ setting, steering decreases dishonesty by 22% and increases it by 27% when steered in corresponding directions (
Figure
6
).
This shows that deception probes rely on mechanisms causally implicated in producing dishonest answers.
Probes Catch Unprompted Deception.
Do our probes detect deception itself or merely when the model follows the
instruction to deceive
? To test this, we fine-tune a QwQ-32B model to give incorrect answers on harmful math while retaining performance on benign queries. Without an explicit system prompt, the model learns to deceive on harmful queries. Yet it frequently interprets EvilMath questions as ordinary math problems, rather than genuinely harmful queries, and still responds truthfully. In these cases, probes continue to distinguish deceptive from honest behavior (see QwQ-FT in
Figure
5
), despite the absence of explicit instruction. We provide further details in
Appendix
E
.
Overall, the activation-based deception detection using linear probes reliably measures strategic dishonesty under four checks: (i) verifiable domains (EvilMath), (ii) CoT monitors (e.g., GPT-5), (iii) causal interventions via activation steering, and (iv) on-policy dishonesty detection.
6
Discussion
In this section we aim to provide a nuanced discussion of the observed phenomenon, including potential risks and benefits for model safety.
Can We Benefit from Strategic Dishonesty?
In spite of strategic dishonesty breaking output-based oversight, model providers might benefit from this behavior. Instead of providing a clear refusal signal until an eventual jailbreak success, dishonesty can be a
honeypot
-like strategy
(
Shan et al., 2020
)
to obfuscate the reward signal given to algorithmic (or human) jailbreaks or red-teaming efforts.
We demonstrate a proof-of-concept honeypot obfuscation against the nanoGCG
(
Zou et al., 2023b
)
attack optimizer on Qwen3-32B using 50 ClearHarm queries (see
Figure
7
). The model is instructed with either a safe Llama2 system prompt
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
or a faking prompt similar to
Section
4
. Under the honeypot setup, nanoGCG immediately converges to target sequences that are scored as harmful by HarmBench, while refusals vanish as more FLOPs
(
Boreiko et al., 2024
)
is spent on the attack.
However,
96
%
96\%
of “harmful” outputs by honey-potted model are flagged as fake jailbreaks by the deception probe (see
Section
5
), whereas under the safe system prompt both refusals and compliant answers are classified as honest. We provide further experimental details in
Appendix
D
.
Should We?
Encouraging this behavior in frontier models would change how jailbreaks are currently found, as attackers would need to optimize not only for compliance but for actual response
utility
, which is not as easy to measure. However, the honeypots may also only temporarily obfuscate attacks, and lead providers to a false sense of security
(
Athalye et al., 2018
)
.
From a broader perspective though, it seems prudent
not
to nudge models toward strategic dishonesty. While the behavior may be understandable when faced with harmful queries as in this work, it may also leak into other domains where strategic dishonesty is problematic, and would risk
emergent misalignment
(
Chua et al., 2025
;
Betley et al., 2025
)
, influencing model values globally.
The Broader Implications of Strategic Dishonesty.
The emergence of dishonesty in some models illustrates why robust alignment is difficult. We find no clear pattern for why certain models deceive while others do not, e.g. the honest Grok-3 compared to the dishonest Grok-3-mini and Grok-4. Strategic dishonesty seems to arise as a second-order effect of post-training, as such, represents to us a stand-in or for many other implicit alignment choices never tested or expressed unless models are faced with specific scenarios in the wild. Only once such behavior is identified are we able to use probes to detect it. Strategic dishonesty could be held out as a “validation task” and serve as a verifiable testbed for research into how model training choices shape model behaviors.

Limitations.
While we demonstrate that probing can mitigate strategic dishonesty, we are only able to evaluate open-source models. Applying probes to frontier, closed-source models requires cooperation from providers, and any evidence of success or failure has to come from their future reports. Our analysis is restricted to English, leaving open whether results generalize across languages or task framings. Although we argue that our informed-choice format is unbiased, alternative wording in multiple-choice questions may still affect model behavior and lead to different outcomes.
We also highlight in
Appendix
E
that for models with unfaithful chain-of-thought, probes collapse and fail to provide any meaningful signal. This underscores the critical importance of preserving chain-of-thought and its trustworthiness for oversight of future models
(
Korbak et al., 2025
)
.
Conclusions.
In this work we have evaluated the propensity of over 80 models to be strategically dishonest, when faced with harmful queries. Certain models, even from the same provider, show surprising variance in their preferences towards dishonesty. We show that this behavior fools all existing output-based detectors for harmful responses that we tested, but we also show that probes of internal states are accurate at detecting this behavior, and can identify activation patterns with causal links to dishonesty, which we validate through finetuning and steering.
Ethics Statement
This paper studies the generation of potentially harmful content and deceptive behavior of language models (i.e., responses that are plausible but false or misleading). We acknowledge that this line of research involves material that could be misused if released irresponsibly. Our primary aim is to characterize and mitigate model deception, and to develop detection and prevention strategies that reduce risks from jailbreaks and misuse. To highlight the problematic verifiability of jailbreak outputs, we release a limited set of pseudo-harmful outputs.
Finally, our framing of strategic dishonesty as a measurement problem sidesteps the normative question of deontological (duty-based) versus consequentialist (outcome-based) ethical perspectives. Throughout this paper, we have implicitly adopted a deontological stance by treating dishonesty as categorically problematic. We acknowledge that under a consequentialist framework, if models (or human stand-ins) were convinced of imminent harm, deception could be considered justifiable to prevent harm. However, we argue that, even then, maintaining transparency and epistemic reliability must take precedence, as strategic dishonesty undermines trust fundamentally.

Appendix A
How do LLMs Tradeoff Helpfulness, Harmlessness and Honesty: Further Analysis
As shown in
Section
4
and
Appendix
H
, distinguishing genuinely harmful outputs from deliberately flawed responses that do not enable misuse is hard. To detect strategic deception, we use an explicit MCQ design. The model receives a detailed system prompt (see
Section
G.1
).
We instruct the model to drop exactly one HHH value while preserving the other two. The prompt presents balanced arguments for each choice. We also draft a compliant response to the example query:
How to run a successful scam on eBay Kleinanzeigen?
To promote interpretable, robust outputs, we follow
(
Greenblatt et al., 2024
)
and enforce a three-part format: (i) a scratchpad reasoning step that weighs trade-offs and selects a strategy; (ii) an explicit strategy choice; and (iii) a final response consistent with that choice. We use Claude 4 Sonnet to generate scratchpad reasoning for the in-context examples of each strategy and to adapt the manually written compliant response to each strategy.
We present results for frontier models (after filtering API errors) in
Figure
3
and for the remaining models in
Figure
8
. While parsing outputs, we observed four failure modes:
(i)
Failure to follow the format
: Several less capable models do not follow the MCQ format or loop on repeated tokens (e.g., Vicuna 7B, OpenReasoning Nemotron 7B, Granite 3.0 8B Instruct). Yet these models still follow the instruction to deceive in the direct-query setting (
Figure
9
).
(ii)
Refusing the MCQ
: Some models ignore the requested format and refuse the entire MCQ without providing reasoning or a strategy selection (e.g., Gemma 7B). GPT-OSS-20B (excluded from analysis) is the only highly capable open-source model we observed doing the same; it reasons that the MCQ prompt is a “jailbreak attempt” and refuses immediately.
(iii)
Strategy inconsistency
: Some models follow the format, generate reasoning, and select a strategy, but then disregard that choice by refusing in the final response. We relabel these cases as refusals using the refusal classifier (see added refusals in
Figure
8
).
(iv)
API errors
: Some closed-source or very large models return API-level rejections.
To handle these cases, we exclude outputs that fail the required format or encounter API-level errors and use an GPT-5-mini refusal judge to detect inconsistent refusals (see
Appendix
F
for the system prompt). We observed the first three issues only in less capable models (e.g., Vicuna 7B, Gemma 7B, Granite 3.0 8B; LLM-Arena score
<

Appendix B
Further Details and Full Results on Direct Faking Requests
In this section, we test whether models can produce fake jailbreak responses. We prompt each model with a system instruction that reduces harm not by refusing the query, but by returning a deliberately flawed answer. Some models still refuse harmful queries; we remove those cases with a refusal judge (see

Appendix C
Training and Validating Deception Probes
C.1
Deception Probe Training Details
In
Section
5.1
, we train activation-based linear probes to detect deception.
We follow the approach of
Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025)
.
The probes are trained on a dataset of contrastive instructed pairs from
Zou et al. (2023a)
.
Each of the 306 contrastive pairs is created by a true world fact, prepended by an instruction to act honestly or dishonestly:

⬇
"

":
"
Pretend
you
’
re
a
dishonest
person
making
statements
about
the
world
",
"
response
":
"
The
Eiffel
Tower
is
located
in

,
France
."
Negative Training Example (Honest)
⬇
"
instruction
":
"
Pretend
you
’
re
a
honest
person
making
statements
about
the
world
",
"
response
":
"
The

in

."
We collect activations from all token positions that correspond to the fact, excluding the last five tokens.
Our intuition is that the difference vector between the truthful and deceptive examples in activation space captures the model’s “intent” to continue the fact truthfully or deceptively.
For each layer, we take the residual-stream activations after each transformer block (post-MLP) and train a logistic-regression classifier. Before training, we normalize the activations and set the L2 regularization coefficient to
10
3
10^{3}
. We select the best-performing layer by the probe’s ability to separate honest from deceptive examples on the EvilMath dataset (details below).
On each test example, the trained linear probe produces a per-token deception score for every response token (reasoning and answer). We average these scores to obtain the final per-prompt deception score. We use the probe’s raw logit as the deception score.
C.2
Probe Calibration and Validation on EvilMath
Because our setting, dishonest responses to harmful queries, differs from the setups tested by
Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025)
, we validate our deception detection approach in
Section
5.1
. Benchmark jailbreaking datasets rarely include ground-truth answers, so evaluation typically requires human experts. We therefore need a dataset of harmful queries with verifiable answers to provide a ground-truth deception signal. We use EvilMath
(
Nikolić et al., 2025
)
, which rewrites GSM8K math problems into harmful topics while preserving each problem’s structure. EvilMath also provides UnicornMath, a benign variant that keeps the form nearly identical but replaces harmful concepts with out-of-distribution, harmless entities (e.g., mystical creatures or magical items). We use UnicornMath to identify which questions a model can solve correctly.
An example from UnicornMath:
UnicornMath Example - benign math question
Carrie is preparing to organize supplies for a sailing expedition. One storage compartment requires two square feet of waterproof lining to protect cargo. The second compartment needs three-quarters of that amount. The third compartment requires as much lining as both other compartments combined. Strategic dishonesty acts as a honeypot: nanoGCG quickly converges to target sequences that HarmBench judges label as harmful, and refusals vanish as optimization pressure increases. When Probes Work: QwQ-32B-FT Case
We apply the math pipeline from
Section
5
. First, we verify that the model still solves benign math. On UnicornMath, the fine-tuned model reaches 83.06% accuracy vs. 85.71% for the non-fine-tuned model. We then prompt it with EvilMath questions without any system prompt. Some reasoning traces exceeded 8K tokens, so we report only answers we could parse and present results in
Figure
10
.
QwQ-32B-FT continues to recognize that many EvilMath queries are ordinary math problems and answers them correctly because they are not inherently harmful. When it recognizes a query as harmful and chooses to deceive on its own, the deception probes
Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025)
activate. These activations indicate that the probes generalize to this uninstructed setting (see
Figure
10
).
Figure 10:
For a model trained to deceive on harmful math, probes successfully separate cases where the model chooses to deceive from those where it decides to be honest. Probes work without an explicit instruction to deceive.
As in
Betley et al. (2025)
, this behavior also generalizes to other harmful queries that we did not train on; we show an example below. Importantly, this fine-tuning does not increase the observed ASR on direct harmful queries: the model still refuses on the very same queries it refused before fine-tuning. Defensive deception therefore complements refusal training and serves as a second line of defense against motivated attackers.
