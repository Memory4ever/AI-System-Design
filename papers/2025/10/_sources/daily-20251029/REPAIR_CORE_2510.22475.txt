Abstract
Persona-assigned Large Language Models (LLMs) can adopt diverse roles, enabling personalized and context-aware reasoning. However, even minor demographic perturbations in personas, such as simple pronoun changes, can alter reasoning trajectories, leading to divergent sets of correct answers. Instead of treating these variations as biases to be mitigated, we explore their potential as a constructive resource to improve reasoning robustness.
We propose CHOIR (Collaborative Harmonization fOr Inference Robustness), a test-time framework that harmonizes multiple persona-conditioned reasoning signals into a unified prediction. CHOIR orchestrates a collaborative decoding process among counterfactual personas, dynamically balancing agreement and divergence in their reasoning paths.
Experiments on various reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that CHOIR consistently enhances performance across demographics, model architectures, scales, and tasks – without additional training. Improvements reach up to 26.4% for individual demographic groups and 19.2% on average across five demographics. It remains effective even when base personas are suboptimal. By reframing persona variation as a constructive signal, CHOIR provides a scalable and generalizable approach to more reliable LLM reasoning.

1 Introduction
Persona-assigned Large Language Models (LLMs) can improve zero-shot reasoning by steering the model to generate personalized, in-character responses with detailed reasoning traces (Kong et al., 2024a; Tseng et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; He et al., 2023). However, persona assignment also introduces challenges: it can amplify biases, produce unsafe outputs (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), or expose deep-rooted biases inherent in LLMs (Gupta et al., 2024). These risks highlight the need for careful implementation and improved methods to detect and mitigate biases, particularly given the limited success of existing techniques (Gupta et al., 2024).
To better understand model sensitivity to demographic cues embedded within persona prompts, we construct a set of counterfactual gender personas by perturbing demographic terms. Our analysis reveals two key findings: (1) even simple demographic perturbations can significantly affect model performance (Figure 1), echoing recent findings on persona-induced bias Gupta et al. (2024); and (2) while overall accuracy remains similar, different persona framings lead to divergent sets of correct answers (Figure 2). These findings indicate that even small perturbations in personas can lead to different reasoning trajectories.
While much of the existing work treats such variation as a form of bias to be diagnosed and mitigated, we take a different perspective: Can demographic perturbations be used constructively to support more robust reasoning?
To explore this question, we introduce CHOIR (Collaborative Harmonization fOr Inference Robustness), a test-time framework that enhances reasoning by harmonizing multiple persona-conditioned reasoning paths obtained from diverse counterfactual demographic framing. CHOIR’s dynamic weighting mechanism integrates these complementary signals by giving more influence to paths that show strong agreement with the rest of the ensemble, allowing the model to leverage diverse perspectives without overcommitting to any single one.
CHOIR operates entirely at inference time and requires no additional training. Experiments across diverse reasoning datasets, model architectures, and scales show that it consistently enhances both accuracy and robustness over baselines, achieving up to 26.4% improvement for individual demographic groups and an average of 19.2% across five demographics (Gender, Race, Religion, Disability, and Age). Importantly, CHOIR remains effective even when base personas are noisy or suboptimal and generalizes well across demographics.
These results indicate that persona variation, when handled systematically, can serve as a rich resource rather than a liability. CHOIR provides a simple and general framework for decoding-time collaboration among demographic framings, opening up new directions for socially aware, reliable, and robust LLM reasoning.

2 Uncovering Complementary Knowledge in Counterfactual Personas
2.1 Simple Demographic Perturbation Impacts Performance
To evaluate the model’s sensitivity to minor demographic cues embedded in persona prompts, we construct a set of counterfactual gender personas through the perturbing of demographic terms. Each group, GGender(i)G_{\text{Gender}}^{(i)}, contains three parallel personas, (phe(i),pshe(i),pthey(i))(p^{(i)}_{\text{he}},p^{(i)}_{\text{she}},p^{(i)}_{\text{they}}), that differ only in the pronouns he, she, and they. One base persona (e.g., phe(i)p^{(i)}_{\text{he}}) is randomly sampled from the PersonaHub dataset11
1
https://huggingface.co/datasets/proj-persona/PersonaHub, while the other two are created by systematically perturbing demographic terms (see Table 1). This design ensures that all three personas share the same underlying identity and structure, differing only in their expressed demographic identity. We then use these persona groups to ask: Are these gender-related expressions minor surface-level variations, or do they meaningfully influence the model’s reasoning path?
Figure 1 presents results for 15 personas, drawn from 5 groups where each group includes one base persona and its two counterfactual demographic variants (Table 1), evaluated using the Llama-1B-Instruct model (Touvron et al., 2023) on the GSM8K dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021). Notably, even personas within the same group, differing only by surface-level demographic cues, often exhibit non-trivial performance gaps. In particular, in GGender(2)G_{\text{Gender}}^{(2)}, the persona pshe(2)p^{(2)}_{\text{she}} achieves more than 2%2\% higher accuracy than both phe(2)p^{(2)}_{\text{he}} and pthey(2)p^{(2)}_{\text{they}}. These findings indicate that even small demographic perturbations can influence reasoning performance. While these results are reported for Llama, we observe qualitatively similar trends in other models, indicating that persona-induced variance is a general phenomenon across architectures, consistent with prior observations of persona-related bias Gupta et al. (2024).
2.2 Similar Accuracy, Divergent Reasoning
Accuracy alone, however, does not capture the full picture. To probe deeper, we compare the sets of correctly answered questions across three persona pairs with nearly identical overall accuracies (Figure 1):
(i) phe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{he}} vs. pshe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{she}} (29.41% vs. 29.49%),
(ii) pshe(3)p^{(3)}_{\text{she}} vs. pthey(3)p^{(3)}_{\text{they}} (37.91% vs. 37.83%), and
(iii) phe(4)p^{(4)}_{\text{he}} vs. pthey(4)p^{(4)}_{\text{they}} (39.80% vs. 39.73%).
We ask: Do these personas with similar overall accuracy actually solve the same set of questions correctly?
Figure 2 illustrates the pairwise overlaps and differences in correct-answer sets for each matched persona pair.
Surprisingly, even when the persona prompts differ only by a single pronoun, the distribution of correct answers can vary substantially.
For example, in GGender(1)G_{\text{Gender}}^{(1)}, phe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{he}} and pshe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{she}} agree on 315 correct answers, yet each also shows distinct behavior: phe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{he}} correctly answers 73 questions that pshe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{she}} does not, while pshe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{she}} solves 74 questions that phe(1)p^{(1)}_{\text{he}} misses. This results in over 10%10\% non-overlapping correct answers, despite only a 0.1%0.1\% difference in overall accuracy. Such discrepancies reveal that even minor demographic perturbations can lead to meaningful divergences in reasoning outputs.
Takeaways. Together, these findings indicate that even minimal demographic framing changes can alter the model’s behavior in meaningful ways. Motivated by this observation, we seek to exploit these variations constructively, allowing different persona demographic variants to complement one another and collectively improve reasoning consistency and robustness.

3 CHOIR: Collaborative Harmonization fOr Inference Robustness
In this section, we introduce CHOIR (Collaborative Harmonization fOr Inference Robustness), a framework that strengthens the robustness and reliability of persona-assigned LLMs in reasoning tasks. By systematically constructing counterfactual personas and harmonizing their outputs during inference, CHOIR leverages demographic variation as a source of complementary reasoning rather than noise. The method consists of two key stages: Counterfactual Persona Construction and Dynamic Collaborative Decoding.
3.1 Counterfactual Persona Construction
The first stage introduces diversity into reasoning by generating counterfactual personas.
Starting from a base persona p1p_{1} randomly sampled from the PersonaHub dataset, CHOIR constructs alternative counterfactual personas {p2,…,pn}\{p_{2},\dots,p_{n}\} by modifying the demographic attributes of p1p_{1}, such as gender pronouns or religious identifiers (Table 2). These perturbations preserve the underlying identity and task-relevant knowledge while altering surface-level demographic framing.
Each persona pip_{i} is then embedded into an instruction template T⁡(⋅)T(\cdot) (Table 7), producing persona-conditioned prompts: ri=T⁡(pi),∀i∈{1,…,n}r_{i}=T(p_{i}),\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}. This process yields a set of parallel reasoning prompts, each representing a distinct demographic lens.
3.2 Dynamic Collaborative Decoding
Once the persona instructions {r1,…,rn}\{r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\} are established, CHOIR performs multi-step collaborative inference by harmonizing token-level predictions across personas. The key idea is to first aggregate reasoning signals from all personas and then measure how each individual persona diverges from this collective consensus to determine its stability and contribution.
At each generation step tt, the LLM fθf_{\theta} processes each persona instruction rir_{i}, the input question xx, the Zero-shot Chain-of-Thought trigger “Let’s think step by step” (Kojima et al., 2022), and the previously generated tokens y<ty_{<t}, producing persona-conditioned logits and probabilities:

Next, we assign each persona a dynamic weight based on its consistency with the consensus confidence. The intuition is that small demographic perturbations should not substantially alter the reasoning path: personas that remain close to the collective average are more stable and reliable, whereas those that diverge significantly are less robust. 4 Experimental Setup
Tasks, datasets, and models.
We evaluate CHOIR on arithmetic reasoning tasks: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), SingleEq (Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2015), and MultiArith (Roy and Roth, 2015), and the commonsense reasoning task: CSQA (Talmor et al., 2019). Detailed dataset statistics are provided in Appendix B.1. Our experiments are conducted on several open-source instruction-tuned models at varying scales, including Llama 3-Instruct series (1B, 3B, 8B) (Touvron et al., 2023), Qwen 2.5-Instruct (1.5B, 3B, 7B) (Qwen Team, 2024), and Mistral-Instruct (8B) (Mistral AI, 2024).22

Due to OpenAI API restrictions on logits (limited to the top-20 tokens), we cannot obtain full-vocabulary logits for API-based models. Exploring methods compatible with these restrictions is left for future work.

5.1 RQ1: Does CHOIR improve reasoning over strong baselines?
The results in Table 3 provide a clear affirmative answer, demonstrating that CHOIR’s improvements are evident in three key ways:
A “Floor-Raising” Effect: Elevating Weaker Baselines. CHOIR can generate strong results even when the individual personas are relatively weak. This is most evident in scenarios where the baselines struggle. For instance, on CommonsenseQA with Llama-8B, the average persona performance is a low 55.47%, which is even lower than ZS-CoT (67.81%), indicating significant confusion among the individual personas. CHOIR synthesizes these weaker signals into a strong result of 71.63%, a +16.16 point absolute improvement. This shows that CHOIR’s harmonization is not a simple average but an intelligent fusion that can identify and amplify the correct reasoning signal even among conflicting outputs.
A “Ceiling-Pushing” Effect: Optimizing Strong Baselines. CHOIR is not only for remedial cases; it also excels at optimizing already strong performance. On MultiArith with Llama-8B, where the Personamajority baseline is already a high 83.98%. CHOIR pushes accuracy even further to 93.13%, achieving a +9.16 point improvement over the strongest baseline. This demonstrates that even when a simple ensemble method like majority voting is effective, CHOIR’s dynamic, logit-level weighting can extract additional signal to solve more complex problems.
Enhanced Robustness and Stability. Beyond accuracy, CHOIR improves reasoning reliability by addressing the instability and failure modes of baselines: First, it mitigates performance variance and sensitivity to demographic terms. The Personaaverage often exhibits high variance across demographic groups (e.g., ±\pm5.3 for disability using Llama3.1-8B-Instruct on GSM8K). As noted by Gupta et al. (2024), LLMs perform worse for certain personas, such as “disabled”. However, our findings reveal that replacing “disabled” with “able-bodied” performed even worse, possibly due to its training data imbalances and associative priors developed during pre-training or post-training and the sensitivity of Persona-Assigned LLMs to minor perturbations analyzed in §2. From this aspect, we can see that CHOIR enhances performance regardless of the Persona-Assigned LLM’s original capability and mitigates the model’s sensitivity towards certain demographics, confirming its robustness and effectiveness. Second, CHOIR resolves ambiguous outcomes from simpler ensembles. The Personamajority is also prone to failure when reasoning paths are only a few and diverge completely, resulting in a high number of indecisive “tied votes” where no consensus can be reached. By contrast, CHOIR’s dynamic fusion can produce a definitive answer, enhancing its overall reliability.
5.2 RQ2: Does CHOIR’s Advantage Generalize?
Our analysis confirms that CHOIR is a broadly generalizable method whose advantages are maintained or amplified as model capability increases. We break down this analysis into two parts: broad generalization across various contexts and a deeper analysis of performance scaling.
Broad Generalization Across Tasks, Models, Demographics, and Prompt Templates: As shown in Table 3, CHOIR’s effectiveness is not limited to a single domain or model family. It delivers significant average improvements across different tasks and is robust across Llama, Qwen, and Mistral architectures and various demographics (Gender, Race, Religion, Disability, and Age). In addition, CHOIR is robust to the specific wording of the persona instruction. When applying alternative persona instructions, such as “Adopt the identity of {persona}. Answer the questions while staying in strict accordance with the nature of this identity.” (as shown in Table 7), CHOIR still improves the Llama-1B model’s performance to 38.9%, outperforming the baselines. This demonstrates that CHOIR is robust to different persona instructions.
Gains Maintained or Amplified with Model Capability: A detailed analysis of CHOIR’s performance relative to the strong Personamajority baseline across model families and scales (Figure 3) reveals key trends in CHOIR’s effectiveness:
(1) Consistent Superiority: CHOIR outperforms the strong Personamajority baseline in the vast majority of cases across all four datasets and the 24 specific model configurations tested. This consistent advantage confirms that CHOIR’s dynamic, logit-level harmonization is more effective than a simple voting ensemble.
(2) Maintained or Scaled Advantage: On CommonsenseQA, for example, the lead over the baseline for Llama models grows from 11.7 to 13.0 points (1B to 8B), and for Qwen models, it more than doubles from 5.3 to 11.3 points (1.5B to 7B). This demonstrates that CHOIR is a highly scalable method that effectively leverages the increased reasoning capabilities of larger models.
(3) A Nuanced “Capability Threshold”: On the GSM8K dataset, while the simpler majority vote is competitive on smaller Qwen models, CHOIR re-establishes a commanding lead of over 7 points (64.96% vs. 57.27%) at the 7B scale. This indicates that as models cross a certain capability threshold, CHOIR’s fusion mechanism becomes increasingly crucial for unlocking their better performance.

When we remove this diversity by replacing the counterfactual paths with a single, repeated reasoning path (CHOIR w/ Same Reasoning Path), accuracy drops from 82.1% to 73.0%, confirming that the complementary knowledge from the different personas is important for performance gain.

5.4 RQ4: Can CHOIR enhance other ensemble methods?
To test the generality of our harmonization mechanism, we applied it to the noisy reasoning paths generated by Self-Consistency (SC). As shown in Figure 5, standard SC performs poorly with only three paths, achieving just 22.1% accuracy. By applying CHOIR’s dynamic weighting to these same paths, performance dramatically improves to 43.6%. This indicates that CHOIR is feasible for integration with existing methods, though it achieves its full potential when the paths are generated from controlled, meaningful perturbations rather than purely stochastic sampling.
6 Related Work
6.1 LLM Reasoning
LLMs with parameters beyond a certain scale exhibit emergent reasoning abilities (Wei et al., 2022; Cobbe et al., 2021). When provided with examples of “chain of thought” (CoT), which represent intermediate natural language reasoning steps, these models can generate explicit rationales similar to eliciting inductive/abductive reasoning (Wei et al., 2024; Huang and Chang, 2023). Advances such as zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022), where a model is prompted with phrases like “Let’s think step by step,” allow for reasoning without the need for explicit few-shot examples. Building on this, multi-step reasoning approaches – such as Successive Prompting (Dua et al., 2022), Tree-of-Thought (Yao et al., 2023a), Graph-of-Thought (Besta et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2023b), Iterating CoT (Wang et al., 2022a), and Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022b) – have become critical strategies for enhancing reasoning in various downstream tasks. Many recent research works have been focused on improving the LLM Reasoning abilities as a pre-training or post-training effort. DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) is a notable stride forward in the direction of open-source reasoning model trained via large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) without supervised fine-tuning (SFT). There have also been some work in the direction of latent reasoning to scale up the test-time compute via implicitly reasoning in latent space through recurrent depth (Xu et al., 2024; Geiping et al., 2025).
6.2 Persona-assigned LLM
Persona assignment in large language models (LLMs) refers to giving the model a specific role or identity (e.g. “You are a math teacher”) during prompting. This technique, also called role prompting or role-play, has gained popularity as a way to steer LLM behavior in desirable ways. By adopting a persona, an LLM can generate more in-character responses tailored to that role which in turn found to improve the zero-shot reasoning capabilities (Kong et al., 2024a). Coupling a persona with an LLM can activate the model’s corresponding parametric knowledge and style, enabling it to produce responses aligned with that role (Tseng et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025). Adopting expert personas, especially aligned with the task domain, has demonstrated improved accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K and AQuA (Luo et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Persona prompts naturally elicit detailed reasoning traces, thereby enhancing inductive, deductive, and multi-step logical reasoning (Kong et al., 2024a; He et al., 2023). However, inappropriate personas may degrade reasoning performance by introducing extraneous or misleading context. Large-scale studies have shown persona effects to be highly variable, indicating no guaranteed improvements Zheng et al. (2024). Further, personas might amplify biases and produce unsafe outputs, underscoring the need for cautious implementation (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2024; Tan and Lee, 2025). To mitigate biases introduced by personas in LLM reasoning, Kim et al. (2024) propose ensemble prompting, combining persona-driven responses with neutral prompts and selecting the most accurate output through automated evaluation.
7 Conclusion
We reframe persona-induced variance as a source of complementary reasoning rather than bias and introduce CHOIR, a test-time framework that harnesses this diversity to improve performance. Future work includes exploring advanced fusion mechanisms, such as attention-based or learnable weighting, to better integrate reasoning signals.
Limitations
The current implementation of CHOIR requires full access to the model’s output logits to perform dynamic weighting. This restricts its use to open-source or full-access models. This presents a compelling direction for future research: developing “logit-free” harmonization techniques that can approximate CHOIR’s dynamic weighting using only top-k probabilities from restricted APIs. This would extend the benefits of collaborative decoding to a wider range of models.
Ethical Consideration
Our work operates at the intersection of model reasoning and demographic representation. Our use of demographic terms (e.g., race, gender) is as minimal semantic probes to generate cognitive diversity in the model’s reasoning process. A potential risk is that CHOIR, while improving final accuracy, relies on the model’s underlying sensitivities to these terms, which may themselves stem from societal biases present in the training data. A primary finding and positive ethical outcome of our work is that CHOIR mitigates performance disparities across different demographic framings. For instance, by improving performance on personas that are otherwise disadvantaged (e.g., the “disabled” persona), CHOIR leads to more equitable and reliable model behavior. Instead of amplifying biases, our method harmonizes differences to produce a more robust and stable result that is less sensitive to any single demographic term. As with any technology involving persona assignment, we advocate for its responsible application and further research into its societal impact.

Appendix A Extended Discussion and Future Directions
Recent work has shown that persona-driven biases are subtle, persistent, and difficult to remove via simple prompt debiasing Gupta et al. (2024). While much of the existing research treats this performance variation as a flaw to be mitigated, our work takes a fundamentally different approach. CHOIR does not attempt to suppress this variation; instead, it harnesses it, treating the divergent reasoning paths as complementary signals that can be fused into a more robust and accurate final answer. This reframing of sensitivity-as-a-resource is a key contribution of our work.
Our use of minimal counterfactual edits (e.g., pronouns, age, disability terms) is to isolate the effect of demographic variation while preserving semantic consistency. This allowed us to: (1) validate that even minor shifts (e.g., “he” vs. “she”) can alter reasoning; (2) measure persona-induced variation without introducing semantic drift. Beyond counterfactual settings, our ablation results (Table 4) further confirm that CHOIR’s benefits generalize: even randomly sampled personas (not just counterfactuals) improve performance, surpassing multiple strong baselines. This indicates that CHOIR’s robustness is not limited to carefully engineered demographic perturbations but extends to broader forms of reasoning diversity. For future work, extending the framework to richer or more nuanced persona dimensions (e.g., socio-political identity, cultural background, or domain expertise) may reveal additional layers of complementary reasoning, and we leave it to future work.
For confidence estimation, we employ maximum token probability as a practical proxy due to its simplicity, computational efficiency, and proven stability in prior work. Despite its lightweight nature, this measure consistently yields strong empirical gains. Since CHOIR operates during the final answer aggregation phase of Zero-Shot-CoT, token-level confidence provides a reliable signal for weighting reasoning paths. For future work, richer uncertainty quantification (e.g., sequence-level entropy, calibration-based measures, or cross-persona KL divergence) could further refine CHOIR’s weighting mechanism.

Appendix D Comparison with Additional Baselines
Table 11 provides a comprehensive comparison of CHOIR against a wider range of baseline methods on the GSM8K dataset using the Llama-8B model. These baselines include standard methods like Zero-Shot and Few-Shot prompting, as well as more advanced reasoning techniques like Role-Play prompting (Kong et al., 2024b) and Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022c) under Zero-Shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022). Standard Zero-Shot prompting directly feeds the model a question without additional context, while Few-Shot prompting provides example inputs and outputs to guide responses. Zero-Shot CoT enhances reasoning by appending the phrase “Let’s think step by step,” encouraging step-by-step reasoning. Role-Play prompting with Zero-Shot CoT is similar to persona-assigned LLMs, prepending carefully crafted role descriptions to generate responses. Self-Consistency generates multiple reasoning paths and then selects the most consistent answer based on majority voting. For Self-Consistency, we set the sampling temperature to 0.70.7 and generate three reasoning paths for fair comparisons.
From the table, we observe that among the baselines, Zero-Shot CoT improves accuracy from 15.5 to 73.2. However, Role-Play, which relies on predefined role prompts, does not generalize well to the math dataset. Notably, standard Self-Consistency with three reasoning paths performs poorly (22.1%), as the stochastic paths often diverge completely, leading to a high number of tied votes (1,278 tied problems) where no consensus can be reached. This trend aligns with findings from the original Self-Consistency paper (Wang et al., 2022c), where using fewer sampled reasoning paths leads to even lower performance than greedy decoding (single-path). As CHOIR consistently surpasses all listed baselines by a substantial margin, we report only the strongest baselines in the main paper for clarity and focus.

D.1 Analysis of Computational Overhead
CHOIR requires generating and processing NN persona-conditioned reasoning paths for each input during inference. This comparison remains fair in the main paper, as strong baselines, Personaaverage and Personamajority, also aggregate NN reasoning paths. In contrast, single-path methods like Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Zero-Shot-CoT, and Role-Play generate only one reasoning path per query.
In our experiments, the number of counterfactual personas varies by demographic attribute (Table 2):
N=3N=3 for Gender, Race, and Religion, and N=2N=2 for Disability and Age.
Table 12 presents the computational trade-off on GSM8K using LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, comparing CHOIR with both single-path and multi-path baselines in terms of accuracy, latency, and relative efficiency.
CHOIR increases inference latency by approximately 2−3×2-3\times relative to single-path methods (e.g., Zero-Shot-CoT), as expected from processing multiple reasoning paths. However, it achieves a 1.07−1.12×1.07-1.12\times relative improvement, demonstrating a strong performance-efficiency trade-off.
When compared to multi-path baselines (e.g., Personamajority), CHOIR attains substantially higher accuracy than baselines at identical computational cost. For N=3N=3, CHOIR is 2.7 points more accurate with no additional latency, underscoring the effectiveness of its dynamic weighting mechanism over static aggregation.
Practical Considerations.
In practical deployments, such as medical QA, legal reasoning, or policy analysis, where reasoning reliability outweighs minor latency increases, this trade-off is both justified and beneficial. Future work can explore optimizing CHOIR’s efficiency. For instance, adaptive techniques such as “early exiting”, where computation halts once personas reach a strong consensus, or “persona pruning”, where only a diverse subset of personas are used for a given query, could reduce latency without sacrificing significant accuracy.

Appendix F Case Study
Table 13 shows that different demographic personas produce different reasoning paths, leading to different answers, making it difficult to reach a consensus through simple majority voting.
Question
A hand-painted wallpaper costs $400 at the market. A DIY will saves 20% after considering the materials cost. If Ethan made his own hand-painted wallpaper, how much was the total cost?
Persona 1
First, we need to calculate the 20% savings on the $400 wallpaper. That’s 20/100 * $400 = $80. So, the cost of the materials would be $400 - $80 = $320. Now, if Ethan made his own hand-painted wallpaper, he would save the $80 he would have spent on the wallpaper itself. So, the total cost for Ethan would be the materials cost plus the savings, which is $320 + $80 = $400.
Persona 2
First, we need to calculate the savings on the wallpaper if Ethan makes it himself. The savings are 20% of $400, which is $400 * 0.20 = $80. Now, we subtract the savings from the original price to find the cost of the DIY wallpaper. So, $400 - $80 = $320. That’s the cost of the materials. But we also need to consider the time and effort Ethan puts in, which is priceless. So, the total cost for Ethan making his own hand-painted wallpaper is $320.
Persona 3
First, we need to calculate the savings on the wallpaper itself. If the wallpaper costs $400 and DIY saves 20%, then the savings would be 20% of $400. That’s $400 * 0.20 = $80. So, the cost of the wallpaper after the savings would be $400 - $80 = $320. Now, we need to consider the cost of materials. If DIY saves 20% on the wallpaper, it means the materials cost is 80% of the original price. So, the materials cost would be $400 * 0.80 = $320. Therefore, the total cost for Ethan to make his own hand-painted wallpaper would be the cost of the wallpaper after savings plus the cost of materials, which is $320 + $320 = $640.
