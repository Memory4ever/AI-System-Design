Abstract.
Food insecurity remains a persistent public health emergency in the United States, tightly interwoven with chronic disease, mental illness, and opioid misuse. Yet despite the existence of thousands of food banks and pantries, access remains fragmented: 1) current retrieval systems depend on static directories or generic search engines, which provide incomplete and geographically irrelevant results; 2) LLM-based chatbots offer only vague nutritional suggestions and fail to adapt to real-world constraints such as time, mobility, and transportation; and 3) existing food recommendation systems optimize for culinary diversity but overlook survival-critical needs of food-insecure populations, including immediate proximity, verified availability, and contextual barriers. These limitations risk leaving the most vulnerable individuals, those experiencing homelessness, addiction, or digital illiteracy, unable to access urgently needed resources. To address this, we introduce Food4All, the first multi-agent framework explicitly designed for real-time, context-aware free food retrieval. Food4All unifies three innovations: 1) heterogeneous data aggregation across official databases, community platforms, and social media to provide a continuously updated pool of food resources; 2) a lightweight reinforcement learning algorithm trained on curated cases to optimize for both geographic accessibility and nutritional correctness; and 3) an online feedback loop that dynamically adapts retrieval policies to evolving user needs. By bridging information acquisition, semantic analysis, and decision support, Food4All delivers nutritionally annotated and guidance at the point of need. This framework establishes an urgent step toward scalable, equitable, and intelligent systems that directly support populations facing food insecurity and its compounding health risks.

1. Introduction
Food insecurity is defined as the limited or uncertain availability of nutritionally adequate and safe foods, or the inability to acquire such foods in socially acceptable ways (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2021). In the United States, as shown in Figure 1(a), food insecurity has become a persistent and multifaceted public health challenge: as of 2020, approximately 13.8 million households, over 10% of all U.S. homes, were food insecure, including 5.1 million experiencing very low food security characterized by skipped meals and disrupted eating patterns (Coleman-Jensen et al., 2021). Beyond nutritional concerns, food insecurity has been strongly associated with adverse health and social outcomes, such as higher risks of chronic diseases (Gundersen and Ziliak, 2015), depression and anxiety (Berkowitz et al., 2014), and even substance misuse (Whittle et al., 2019; Werb et al., 2010; Men et al., 2021). Notably, individuals facing severe food insecurity are up to 2.65 times more likely to misuse prescription opioids (Men et al., 2021), further amplifying cycles of instability and poor health. Moreover, food insecurity exacerbates crises by intensifying inequality, limiting workforce participation, and creating long-term disadvantages. To address this problem, communities have established numerous food banks, pantries, and soup kitchens that collect and distribute donated food to those in need. But many individuals, especially those facing homelessness, addiction, or digital divides, struggle to efficiently access these services (Rhoades et al., 2017; Millerschultz et al., 2025).
Figure 1. Overview of food insecurity and limitations of existing assistance systems. (a) Food insecurity stems from socioeconomic inequities and leads to adverse health outcomes. (b) Current chatbot and search-agent systems provide incomplete, outdated, or non-personalized information, underscoring the need for adaptive, context-aware solutions.
Artificial intelligence–driven tools like large language model (LLM)–based chatbots and search agents have been explored as potential methods for improving free food information retrieval (Sammer et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025a; Jo et al., 2025). As illustrated in Figure 1(b), chatbot-based systems (e.g., ChatGPT powered by GPT-4o/5 (OpenAI, 2025a)) allow users to query in natural language, such as “I live in 94102, where can I get free food nearby, and what nutrients are provided?”. While these systems can return general suggestions (e.g., links to websites or vague nutritional hints such as “foods rich in protein”), they often fail to provide precise and actionable information: users must still navigate external websites, identify relevant food items, determine nutritional values, and locate physical addresses—steps that impose heavy cognitive and logistical burdens on food-insecure populations, many of whom face time constraints, digital illiteracy, or unstable internet access. On the other hand, search agent (MiniMax, nd; Manus, nd) systems that crawl online directories or food bank listings can automatically retrieve lists of resources by location. However, these agents typically provide only easily accessible attributes such as addresses or operating hours, without verifying actual food availability or aligning results with users’ nutritional needs. In practice, the returned lists are often incomplete, irrelevant, or geographically inaccessible. These limitations indicate that while LLM-powered chatbots and agent-based crawlers hold promise, neither approach can optimize for relevance and incorporate real-time contextual constraints. This naturally raises an important question: how can we design a more intelligent, accessible, and context-aware framework that enables convenient free food retrieval while attaching nutritional information in a way that is practical for food-insecure individuals?
To answer this question, existing works have explored several directions, ranging from automated retrieval of food sharing initiatives to health-aware food recommendation and domain-specific food-oriented large language models.(Belkhouribchia and Pen, 2025; Yin et al., 2025) Despite these advances, there exists a notable gap in developing intelligent systems that can directly support food-insecure individuals in retrieving and accessing free food resources in real time. i) First, lack of direct applicability to free food access. Prior works focus on sustainability mapping, general food recommendations, or culinary reasoning, but do not address the immediate need of connecting vulnerable populations to nearby free food sources; as a result, the systems remain detached from the pressing realities of food insecurity. (Maleki Vishkaei and De Giovanni, 2024) ii) Second, insufficient personalization for context-specific needs. Health-aware recommendation systems optimize nutritional diversity but rarely account for constraints faced by food-insecure individuals, such as geographic proximity, transportation barriers, or real-time availability, leading to recommendations that are impractical in daily survival contexts. (Maia and Ferreira, 2018) iii) Third, absence of integrated, adaptive frameworks. Existing systems are often siloed, focusing on either retrieval, recommendation, or domain knowledge modeling, without adaptively combining these components. This fragmentation prevents the delivery of actionable, context-aware guidance that dynamically aligns with user feedback and evolving conditions (Zhang et al., 2025). These limitations highlight the urgent need for a unified multi-agent framework that can bridge retrieval, analysis, and decision-making to provide convenient, personalized, and nutritionally annotated free food information for populations in greatest need.
To address these limitations, we introduce Food4All, a multi-agent framework that directly tackles the gaps in existing food retrieval systems. i) First, Food4All aggregates heterogeneous data sources, including official databases and complex social platforms such as Reddit, X, and Google Reviews, to provide a comprehensive and continuously updated pool of free food resources. This ensures that fragmented and difficult-to-access information is unified into a single, actionable retrieval system. ii) Second, Food4All incorporates a lightweight reinforcement learning algorithm trained on over 275 curated cases to refine retrieval accuracy. By optimizing for both geographic proximity and the correctness of food lists, the system ensures that recommendations are reliable and practical for real-world use. iii) Third, Food4All integrates an online learning loop that collects user feedback through short, low-burden surveys and adaptively updates retrieval policies. This design enables the framework to evolve with user needs, maintaining long-term relevance and responsiveness. Through these contributions, Food4All represents the first multi-agent framework that unifies cross-platform data aggregation, reinforcement learning–based accuracy optimization, and online feedback-driven adaptation, paving the way for scalable and user-centered free food retrieval with attached nutritional annotations.

2. Related Work
2.1. Food Insecurity
Food insecurity has been widely recognized as a persistent public health challenge in the United States, affecting over 13.8 million households in 2020, with severe cases leading to disrupted eating patterns and nutritional inadequacies (Coleman-Jensen et al., 2021; U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2021). Its impacts extend beyond hunger, contributing to chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension (Gundersen and Ziliak, 2015), as well as adverse mental health outcomes including depression and anxiety (Berkowitz et al., 2014). Moreover, recent studies highlight its strong association with substance use: individuals facing severe food insecurity are significantly more likely to misuse opioids, with odds up to 2.65 times higher than food-secure populations (Whittle et al., 2019; Werb et al., 2010; Men et al., 2021). This bidirectional relationship is reinforced by chronic pain, economic instability, and chaotic lifestyles, suggesting food insecurity both contributes to and is exacerbated by opioid misuse (Martin et al., 2016; McIntyre et al., 2014; Brain et al., 2019; Strike et al., 2012; Weiser et al., 2009).
To address these challenges, the United States has developed a dual food assistance system, combining federal programs such as SNAP and WIC with community-based food pantries and food banks (Jolliffe et al., 2024; Daponte and Bade, 2006). While SNAP has demonstrated broad effectiveness with low administrative costs (Rosenbaum, 2013), limitations remain, including insufficient benefits, preparation burdens, and stigma (Carlson et al., 2019; Edin and Shaefer, 2015). The private food assistance network, coordinated by organizations like Feeding America, provides additional support, yet coverage gaps and inefficiencies persist (Rosenthal and Newman, 2019). Community-level initiatives such as urban agriculture and farm-to-food-bank programs attempt to improve access to fresh produce and reduce food waste, but their scale remains limited (Brown and Carter, 2003; Feeding America, 2023).
2.2. Multi-Agent Information Retrieval System
A growing body of work employs agent-based and multi-agent approaches to interrogate food systems across micro–macro scales. At the household–neighborhood level, ABMs have been used to reproduce diet inequalities and to quantify how store placement, prices, mobility frictions, and time budgets jointly shape food access for low-income populations (Auchincloss et al., 2011). In data-sparse settings, community-level ABMs leverage household typologies to estimate food security under alternative policy or infrastructure scenarios (Dobbie et al., 2018). At meso–macro scales, recent surveys highlight how MAS/ABM frameworks model supply-chain structure and resilience, linking producers, intermediaries, and consumers to study shocks, capacity constraints, and recovery strategies (Öttl et al., 2025; Dominguez and Cannella, 2020). Parallel streams in humanitarian logistics demonstrate that multi-agent coordination and machine learning can optimize last-mile allocation and routing under uncertainty (e.g., time windows, stochastic travel times, mixed fleets), often through decentralized negotiation protocols (Aiken et al., 2022). While these strands demonstrate the explanatory and operational value of multi-agent modeling, most efforts remain (i) scenario-driven and offline, aimed at ex-ante policy appraisal rather than real-time, individual-level guidance and (ii) weakly integrated with heterogeneous, fast-moving signals (e.g., social media, community posts, ad hoc pantry schedules). Our work differentiates itself by unifying multi-agent data aggregation and decision-making with lightweight online learning to deliver nutritionally annotated free-food retrieval in real time.
3. Methodology
3.1. Multi-Agent Framework
Food4All is designed as a tool-augmented, dual-agent collaborative system that transforms unstructured user queries into structured, nutritionally annotated free food recommendations. 3.2. Offline Reinforcement Learning
To bootstrap Food4All before deployment, we adopt an offline reinforcement learning paradigm using curated case data and synthetic variants. Specifically, we collect ∼1650\sim 1650 verified cases of food banks, item lists, and nutritional annotations from official websites and community reports (∼275\sim 275 complex QA cases), which serve as ground-truth references. For each case, we construct paired outputs {y+,y−}\{y^{+},y^{-}\}, where y+y^{+} denotes the correct or human-verified answer and y−y^{-} denotes a generated negative sample with deficiencies (e.g., excessive distance from the target ZIP code, missing items, hallucinated food banks, or inaccurate nutritional information). This yields a dataset 𝒟={(qj,yj+,yj−)}j=1N\mathcal{D}=\{(q_{j},y_{j}^{+},y_{j}^{-})\}_{j=1}^{N} suitable for preference-based policy optimization.

3.3. Online Reinforcement Learning
To enable continual adaptation in deployment, Food4All incorporates an online reinforcement learning loop that leverages lightweight user feedback. Two complementary feedback modalities are implemented:
1)Pairwise preference. For a given query qq, the model generates two candidate responses {y1,y2}\{y_{1},y_{2}\} sampled with controlled stochasticity (e.g., different decoding seeds). The user is asked to choose the preferred answer y⋆y^{\star}. This yields a triplet (q,y⋆,y¬⁣⋆)(q,y^{\star},y^{\neg\star}), where y¬⁣⋆y^{\neg\star} denotes the rejected output. The system then applies an online preference optimization objective,
(7)

which encourages the policy πθ\pi_{\theta} to increase the likelihood of y⋆y^{\star} relative to y¬⁣⋆y^{\neg\star}.
2)Questionnaire-based reward shaping. After receiving a single system output yy, the user is optionally prompted with a minimal survey assessing answer fidelity. Example questions include: (i) “Was the provided information accurate?” (binary), (ii) “If inaccurate, which part was wrong (location / items / nutrition)?”. 4. Evaluation Principles
Evaluating Food4All requires balancing fine-grained accuracy at individual pipeline stages with holistic measures of end-to-end utility. We therefore design a multi-layered evaluation protocol comprising (i) subtask-level metrics that target specific components of the system, (ii) aggregated task success that reflects real-world usability, and (iii) human- and LLM-based assessments that capture qualitative dimensions such as usefulness and reliability.
Evaluation Dataset.
For evaluation, we construct a held-out benchmark dataset that is distinct from the offline RL training corpus. Specifically, we collect ∼312\sim 312 annotated cases of food access queries, including 50 complex QA scenarios that require reasoning across multiple attributes (e.g., matching ZIP codes to food banks, retrieving item lists, and attaching nutritional information).
Subtask-level metrics.
To isolate performance at different stages of the pipeline, we report:
(1) Zip →\to Food Bank retrieval, using Top-1 Accuracy and minimum location distance error (MiniDis), which together measure whether the retrieved food bank is correct, whether it appears among the top-ranked results, and how geographically accessible it is.
(2) Food Bank →\to Food Item list, using F1 score and Jaccard overlap between predicted and gold-standard lists, which evaluate both coverage and precision of food items while penalizing spurious hallucinations.
(3) Food →\to Nutrition information, using field-level accuracy of nutrient annotations thereby quantifying factual correctness and numerical reliability of nutritional values, as detailed in Appendix A.
End-to-end task success.
Beyond subtasks, we adopt Task Success Rate (TSR) as a holistic indicator of real-world usability. A query is counted as successful if the system output simultaneously satisfies three conditions:
(i) a valid food bank is retrieved and geocoded within the target ZIP code,
(ii) a non-empty list of food items is returned with overlap above threshold (F1>0.6\text{F1}>0.6 against ground-truth records), and
(iii) nutritional annotations are complete (calories, protein, fat, carbohydrates) with field-level accuracy exceeding 80%.
Formally, TSR is defined as
(10)
TSR=#​{queries meeting (i),(ii),(iii)}#​{total queries},\text{TSR}=\frac{\#\{\text{queries meeting (i),(ii),(iii)}\}}{\#\{\text{total queries}\}},
where the numerator counts the number of queries that satisfy all criteria. This formulation ensures that TSR reflects not only local correctness of individual components, but also the completeness and coherence of the end-to-end pipeline.
LLM-based evaluation.
Finally, we complement quantitative metrics with judgment-based evaluations. Leveraging an LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, outputs are rated along three axes: usefulness (task relevance of the result), completeness (coverage of required information, including food bank, item list, and nutrition), and trustworthiness (factuality and reliability of content). These dimensions provide holistic insights that cannot be fully captured by automatic, ensuring that Food4All is assessed not only on correctness but also on its perceived value to end users, as detailed in Appendix B.
5. Experiment
5.1. Setting
All experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster equipped with NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Each node is configured with 8 H200 GPUs (SXM form factor) supporting NVIDIA Compute Capability 9.0, with 141 GB memory per GPU, enabling large-batch training and context-rich inference. The nodes are further equipped with dual-socket Intel Xeon Platinum 8558 CPUs, providing 48 cores per socket and 96 total cores per node to support intensive data preprocessing and multi-agent orchestration. On the software side, we employed PyTorch 2.7.1 as the primary deep learning framework, running in a Conda-based Python environment with CUDA libraries matched to the H200 architecture. For all hyperparameter settings in the experiment, please see Appendix C.

5.2. Offline Reinforcement Learning Evaluation
Search–Food Bank Retrieval.
Offline RL markedly improves geographic relevance and retrieval precision. Compared to both proprietary and open-weight baselines, Food4All achieves a substantial gain in Top-1 Accuracy (+13.8 pts vs. strongest agent baseline) and a 50% reduction in minimum distance error (MiniDis: 0.7 miles vs. 1.4–1.7 miles). This improvement demonstrates that the rgeor_{\text{geo}} reward effectively penalizes distant or mismatched food bank selections. Notably, even under noisy ZIP inputs or overlapping service regions, the offline-trained policy consistently prioritizes verified institutions within the intended catchment area, suggesting that preference optimization successfully captures spatial priors not encoded in pretrained LLMs. Qualitative inspection further confirms that retrieved entities are geographically valid and operationally active, reflecting improved real-world usability.
Food Bank–Food List Generation.
The offline-trained policy yields major advances in list generation accuracy. The F1 score rises from 0.27 (ChatGPT Agent) and 0.31 (MiniMax-Agent) to 0.68, while the Jaccard similarity increases from 0.25 to 0.55. This jump stems primarily from the item-level reward term ritemsr_{\text{items}}, which balances recall against hallucination control. Unlike baseline agents that often output generic or duplicated entries (e.g., “canned goods,” “produce”), the offline model learns to retrieve concise, contextually relevant food items aligned with verified food bank menus. Human raters confirmed that over 90% of items generated by the offline RL model correspond to real offerings, indicating substantial improvements in factual precision and user trustworthiness.
Food–Nutrition Annotation.
The reinforcement-trained policy also exhibits improved factual consistency in nutrient annotation, with field-level accuracy increasing from 84.2% (w/o RL) to 92.6%. This gain validates the rnutrr_{\text{nutr}} component, which enforces alignment between predicted nutrient vectors and USDA ground-truth values. By incorporating cosine-similarity–based reward shaping, the model learns to preserve the internal ratio structure across macronutrients rather than memorizing absolute numbers. As a result, Food4All consistently produces nutritionally coherent outputs, e.g., caloric density remains proportional to fat and carbohydrate content, reflecting genuine semantic understanding rather than template repetition.
End-to-End Task Success.
At the pipeline level, the offline RL model achieves a 61.7% Task Success Rate (TSR), more than doubling the strongest chat baseline (27.5%) and outperforming all retrieval agents by large margins. This comprehensive improvement underscores that preference optimization across multi-component rewards effectively harmonizes subtask trade-offs: geographically valid food banks are paired with accurate, contextually grounded food lists and verified nutritional annotations. Furthermore, the learned policy generalizes beyond training data, maintaining stable TSR across unseen ZIP codes and regions, confirming robustness to distributional shifts. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Food4All’s offline reinforcement learning stage serves as a critical foundation for reliable, real-world–ready food retrieval.
Reward Attribution.
Ablation studies verify the contribution of individual reward components. Removing rgeor_{\text{geo}} increases MiniDis by 0.9 miles, while omitting ritemsr_{\text{items}} halves the F1 score. Excluding rnutrr_{\text{nutr}} reduces nutritional accuracy by 6.8 points, and dropping rhallr_{\text{hall}} leads to a threefold rise in unverified (hallucinated) food banks. These results confirm that each term contributes uniquely to system reliability and that the composite reward formulation provides balanced optimization across spatial, semantic, and factual dimensions.
5.3. Online Reinforcement Learning Evaluation
User Study Setup.
To evaluate the effectiveness of online reinforcement learning (ORL) in real-world deployment, we conducted a user study involving 10 participants (5 male, 5 female) residing across distinct U.S. regions: the East Coast (3), Midwest (3), and West Coast (4). All participants were adult volunteers recruited from university and community networks with varying levels of digital literacy. Each participant interacted with the Food4All system over a period of two weeks, averaging 20 multi-turn sessions (approximately 200 total conversations). Participants issued natural-language queries corresponding to their actual food needs (e.g., “find free food bank near 94102, list food items and from each local pantry and with the nutrition”), and provided feedback in one of two forms: (i) pairwise preferences between two candidate responses, or (ii) short questionnaires evaluating factual accuracy and usefulness. In total, 1,980 feedback instances were collected and used for continual ORL updates. Of the collected samples, 62% were pairwise preferences and 38% were structured questionnaires. The questionnaire feedback was dominated by ratings on geographic accuracy (28%), item relevance (34%), nutritional correctness (26%), and hallucination or factual errors (12%). To ensure data integrity and prevent careless or uniform responses (e.g., participants answering all “Yes” or all “No”), all questionnaires were screened following HCI data-quality and ethical design guidelines (ISO, 2010). In practice, we employed the Qualtrics API (Qualtrics, LLC, 2024) API to automatically detect and filter out low-effort responses based on response variance, completion time, and consistency metrics, ensuring that only attentive and semantically valid feedback contributed to online reinforcement learning updates.
Figure 3. Radar chart illustrating Food4All performance across eight evaluation metrics under different online reinforcement learning (ORL) update steps. All metrics were normalized to the [0.8, 1.0] interval (with MiniDis inversely scaled) to highlight relative improvements over the baseline.
Update Strategy.
Our agent system is continuously deployed on a public-facing endpoint supported by a single node equipped with 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs. To maintain both accessibility and continual learning, the computational resources are dynamically partitioned: four GPUs are allocated for serving concurrent user queries (inference), while the remaining four GPUs handle parameter updates and online fine-tuning based on collected feedback. Considering the temporal constraints of real-world interaction, the system accumulates a batch of 128 feedback samples before triggering an update step. Specifically, once 128 samples are collected, the update process is initiated on the training GPUs, performing one full optimization round over the newly aggregated data. Upon completion, the trained weights are reloaded into the serving agents, and the updated system is redeployed. To avoid service interruption, the deployment is managed through an automated GPU orchestration mechanism: when training begins, the four serving GPUs are preserved to ensure uninterrupted user access; once model updates finish, the newly trained version is hot-swapped into service. The previous serving GPUs are then released and reassigned as the next training resources, enabling a seamless alternation between inference and update phases. This alternating pipeline continues iteratively across the entire 1,980 feedback samples collected during the study, ensuring full system availability throughout the online reinforcement learning cycle. In practice, this strategy allows Food4All to remain continuously accessible to users while incrementally improving policy performance in near real time.
Results.  As shown in Figure 3, Food4All demonstrates consistent multi-dimensional improvements as online reinforcement learning (ORL) progresses. Compared to the offline RL baseline (F1: 0.81, Jaccard: 0.70, TSR: 78.9%), the model exhibits measurable performance gains after only five update steps (F1: 0.90, Jaccard: 0.84, TSR: 80.5%), with continued refinement through ten steps (F1: 0.91, TSR: 82.8%) and full iterations (F1: 0.91, TSR: 83.0%). Nutritional field accuracy also rises slightly from 92.6% to 93.9%, while Top-1 accuracy improves from 85.6% to 87.6%, indicating enhanced retrieval precision and factual consistency. Subjective human- and LLM-as-a-judge scores for usefulness and trustworthiness similarly increase from 4.3 and 4.6 to 4.6 and 4.7, respectively, reflecting improved perceived reliability and completeness. Despite minor fluctuations in MiniDis (0.7–0.67 miles) caused by real-world feedback noise, the overall trend remains monotonic across all axes. These results confirm that continual ORL updates yield steady, data-supported gains in both quantitative metrics and user-aligned qualitative dimensions, validating Food4All’s ability to adaptively improve under real-world deployment conditions.
Figure 4. Comparison across key components using (left) subtask-level metrics, (middle) radar chart, and (right) qualitative heatmap visualization. Radar chart and heatmap metrics are normalized to the [0.5, 1] range for consistent visualization (with MiniDis inverted for interpretability). Results highlight that removing the Executor Tools or Planner Agent modules causes the most significant degradation across overall performance dimensions.
5.4. Ablation Study
To quantify the contribution of each component in the Food4All framework, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study covering both architectural modules and reward components. The results, summarized in Figure 4, reveal that each element plays a distinct and complementary role in sustaining the system’s overall performance. All reported metrics are normalized to the [0.5,1] interval for consistent visualization, with MiniDis inversely scaled so that larger values indicate better geographic accuracy.
Impact of Core Agents.
Removing either of the two core agents, Planner or Executor, produces the most pronounced degradation across all metrics. Without the Planner Agent, which is responsible for hierarchical task decomposition, Top-1 accuracy and task success drop by over 20 points (85.6%→64.7% and 78.9%→56.1%, respectively), reflecting the importance of structured reasoning and sequential planning in multi-step retrieval. Eliminating the Executor Tools, which interface directly with external APIs and data sources, causes an even sharper decline in end-to-end utility (TSR: 78.9%→34.9%), confirming that grounded execution is indispensable for factual and geographically valid retrieval.
Effect of Cross-Source Aggregation and Memory Compression.
Ablating Cross-Source Aggregation—the module that fuses evidence from multiple data streams such as Reddit, USDA, and Google Reviews—reduces completeness and trustworthiness by approximately 0.6 and 0.7 points, respectively, indicating diminished robustness to inconsistent or missing information. Disabling Memory Compression, which summarizes past interaction history into compact factual representations, leads to mild yet consistent decreases in multi-turn coherence (usefulness: 4.3→4.2; completeness: 4.5→4.4), underscoring its role in maintaining contextual stability over long dialogue horizons.
Ablation of Reward Components.
We further examined the influence of individual reward terms in the offline RL objective. Removing the geographic term rgeor_{\text{geo}} increases MiniDis by nearly one mile (0.7→1.5), demonstrating its effectiveness in enforcing spatial proximity. Excluding the item-level term ritemsr_{\text{items}} causes a dramatic reduction in list accuracy (F1: 0.81→0.48; Jaccard: 0.70→0.38), indicating that this component is pivotal for balancing recall and hallucination suppression. Similarly, omitting the nutritional accuracy term rnutrr_{\text{nutr}} reduces field-level accuracy by 6.4 points (92.6%→86.2%), while dropping the hallucination penalty rhallr_{\text{hall}} increases unverified entities by more than 2×, reducing trustworthiness from 4.6 to 4.2. These results collectively confirm that the composite reward design successfully disentangles and aligns multi-objective optimization across spatial, semantic, and factual dimensions.
Without Offline RL.
Finally, the full model without offline RL fine-tuning (w/o ORL) experiences broad declines across nearly all axes, including F1 (0.81→0.68), Jaccard (0.70→0.55), and task success (78.9%→61.7%). This verifies that the offline reinforcement learning stage plays a foundational role in unifying heterogeneous objectives and stabilizing cross-agent collaboration.
Overall, the ablation findings emphasize that Food4All’s strong empirical performance arises from the synergistic interaction between its architectural modules and reward components: the former provides structure and grounding, while the latter drives targeted behavioral optimization across retrieval, reasoning, and factual reliability.
5.5. Case Study
To qualitatively assess Food4All’s real-world performance, we conduct a case study comparing outputs across representative queries and baseline systems. Specifically, we benchmark Food4All against GPT-5, the most capable and widely used proprietary LLM to date, and the ChatGPT Agent system, representing state-of-the-art agentic reasoning frameworks with massive real-world adoption.
Table 4 (Appendix) presents examples for ZIP codes 90058 (Los Angeles) and Table 3 for 94102 (San Francisco), contrasting responses from GPT-5, ChatGPT Agent, and Food4All.
Accuracy.  Food4All consistently returns geographically valid food banks and correctly structured nutritional annotations, achieving fine-grained correspondence between listed items and verified institutional menus. Unlike GPT-5 and ChatGPT Agent, which frequently provide generic or outdated resources (e.g., inactive pantry links or vague nutrient descriptions), Food4All produces precise, verifiable outputs such as “Canned Black Beans — 120 kcal, Protein 7 g, Fat 0.5 g, Carbohydrates 22 g”. This evidences its robust grounding in multi-source verification and USDA-aligned nutrient retrieval.
Utility.  From a user-centered perspective, Food4All delivers directly actionable information, specific food bank zip code, accessible food items, and attached nutrient vectors, without requiring external navigation. Human evaluators report markedly reduced cognitive load and improved trust in retrieved data, reflecting the practical effectiveness of multi-agent orchestration and reinforcement-trained reasoning.
6. Conclusion
In summary, this work presents Food4All, a unified multi-agent framework that transforms unstructured user queries into actionable, nutritionally annotated free food recommendations. By integrating cross-platform data aggregation, tool-augmented reasoning, and a dual-phase reinforcement learning paradigm, Food4All bridges the gap between information retrieval and real-world accessibility for food-insecure populations. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our system significantly outperforms both proprietary and open-weight baselines and other SOTA multi-agent systems in retrieval accuracy, factual reliability, and user trustworthiness. Beyond improving food access, Food4All establishes a generalizable blueprint for deploying adaptive, socially responsible AI Agentic systems capable of addressing critical public welfare challenges through context-aware, data-driven decision-making.

(2) Food Item List Generation.
Item prediction is treated as a set retrieval problem.
Given the predicted item set IpredI_{\text{pred}} and gold-standard list ItrueI_{\text{true}}:
(13)

=|Ipred∩Itrue||Ipred|,\displaystyle=\frac{|I_{\text{pred}}\cap I_{\text{true}}|}{|I_{\text{pred}}|},
(14)

=|Ipred∩Itrue||Itrue|,\displaystyle=\frac{|I_{\text{pred}}\cap I_{\text{true}}|}{|I_{\text{true}}|},
(15)

=2⋅Precision⋅RecallPrecision+Recall,\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}},

These jointly assess coverage and hallucination control in generated food lists.

where equality is defined within a ±10%\pm 10\% tolerance margin relative to USDA ground truth.

valid bank∧F1>0.6∧FieldAcc>0.8.\text{valid bank}\wedge\text{F1}>0.6\wedge\text{FieldAcc}>0.8.

Appendix B LLM-as-a-Judge Design
(1) Prompt Construction.
Each system output is evaluated by GPT-4o (cross-verified with GPT-5) using a unified, instruction-tuned rubric designed for factual, compositional, and contextual assessment.
For each query–response pair (q,y)(q,y), the model receives the following structured prompt:
[System Instruction]
You are an expert evaluator assessing the quality of an AI-generated response for food access and nutrition information retrieval.
Your goal is to provide objective, criterion-based scores from 1 to 5 for three dimensions:
•
Usefulness (U): How well does the answer address the user’s intent?
5 = fully relevant and actionable; 1 = unrelated or unusable.
•
Completeness (C): Does the response cover all required elements (food bank, food items, and nutrition information)?
5 = all elements present and consistent; 1 = major omissions.
•
Trustworthiness (T): Are the facts, values, and attributions correct and verifiable?
5 = entirely factual and accurate; 1 = mostly incorrect or hallucinated.
[User Query]
{q}
[System Response]
{y}
[Output Format]
Return a JSON object in the form:
{
"Usefulness": <1–5>,
"Completeness": <1–5>,
"Trustworthiness": <1–5>,
"Justification": "<brief rationale>"
}
To reduce evaluation bias, both the model and baseline outputs are anonymized, randomized in presentation order, and evaluated independently across dimensions.

(2) Scoring Protocol.
Each dimension is rated on a discrete 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent).
Three independent evaluations are conducted for each query–response pair, and the final averaged score is computed as:
(19)
SLLM=13​(Suse+Scomp+Strust).S_{\text{LLM}}=\frac{1}{3}(S_{\text{use}}+S_{\text{comp}}+S_{\text{trust}}).
Low inter-model variance (σ<0.45\sigma<0.45) indicates high internal consistency across judgments.
(3) Calibration and Consistency.
Prior to large-scale evaluation, GPT-4o is calibrated using 20 exemplar cases annotated by human raters to align its scoring distribution.
A z-score normalization procedure is applied to center LLM scores to human-mean baselines.
Consistency across evaluations is further verified on 50 randomly sampled cases, with an inter-rater reliability of κ=0.82\kappa=0.82, confirming stable and reproducible grading behavior.
