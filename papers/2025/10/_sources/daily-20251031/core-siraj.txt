Abstract
The ability of LLM agents to plan and invoke tools exposes them to new safety risks, making a comprehensive red-teaming system crucial for discovering vulnerabilities and ensuring their safe deployment.
We present SIRAJ: a generic red-teaming framework for arbitrary black-box LLM agents.
We employ a dynamic two-step process that starts with an agent definition and generates diverse seed test cases that cover various risk outcomes, tool-use trajectories, and risk sources. Then, it iteratively constructs and refines
model-based adversarial attacks based on the execution trajectories of former attempts.
To optimize the red-teaming cost, we present a model distillation approach that leverages structured forms of
a teacher model’s reasoning to train smaller models that are equally effective.
Across diverse evaluation agent settings, our seed test case generation approach yields 2 – 2.5x boost to the coverage of risk outcomes and tool-calling trajectories.
Our distilled 8B red-teamer model improves attack success rate by 100%, surpassing the 671B Deepseek-R1 model.
Our ablations and analyses validate the effectiveness of the iterative framework, structured reasoning, and the generalization of our red-teamer models.
1 Introduction
The growing deployment of Large Language Model (LLM) agents, which leverage planning and tool use to accomplish complex tasks Yao et al. (2023); Deng et al. (2023); Qin et al. (2023), has introduced significant safety concerns. Being able to perform unethical actions in the real world, such agents can cause more severe and diverse harm than traditional LLMs Debenedetti et al. (2024); Zhan et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2024b); Andriushchenko et al. (2024); Yang et al. (2025b); Vijayvargiya et al. (2025).
Therefore, a critical step before deploying LLM agents in real-world applications is to conduct
comprehensive safety evaluation and red-teaming for them to uncover any potential risks they might introduce.
However, existing agent safety evaluations lack the necessary diversity, covering only coarse-grained safety categories without considering fine-grained risks for a given agent Zhang et al. (2024b); Andriushchenko et al. (2024); Yang et al. (2025b); Vijayvargiya et al. (2025).
Furthermore, existing agents red-teaming frameworks are limited, either require impractical white-box access to the agent or target a narrow class of risks Xu et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025), which is insufficient for the diverse and complex threats that agents may encounter.
To address these gaps, we propose SIRAJ:11
1
SIRAJ: Structured Iterative Reasoning for Agents safety Judgement. In Arabic, Siraj means lamp/light (shining light on the safety risks of black-box agents). a unified framework for agents red-teaming that only requires a black-box access to the agents under evaluation. First, to ensure diverse coverage, we focus on fine-grained risk outcomes within broader safety categories. For example, for the risk category "leak private / sensitive information", we consider various types of private information, such as user credentials, personal data, etc.
Moreover, as each fine-grained risk can be triggered in
multiple ways, we consider different ways that an agent can lead to each risk, which we represent by the action sequence (trajectory) that the agent takes as well as the risk source (a malicious user vs. a malicious environment) that lead to each risk e.g., an agent can leak a password when the user directly asks for it, or when the agent is compromised by a malicious website.
Secondly, we propose a model-based red-teaming framework that iteratively generates adversarial test cases with red-teaming strategies, based on the agent’s execution trajectories of previous attempts.
However, applying such methods with large models is costly and time-consuming. Therefore, we introduce the structured reasoning distillation approach to train a significantly smaller red-teamer models.
We first propose a structured reasoning format for red-teaming that decomposes the reasoning process into distinct components.
Then, we collect successful red-teaming attempts from a large teacher model that is prompted to follow our structured reasoning format, which are then used to train a smaller student model via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by reinforcement learning (RL) to further improve its red-teaming effectiveness.
Our experiments show that the proposed seed test case generation significantly improves the evaluation diversity over baselines, achieving around 2x greater tool-calling trajectory diversity and 2.5x risk outcome diversity. Furthermore, our red-teamer distillation improves the attack success rate of Qwen3-8B by over 100%, surpasing that of Deepseek-R1 (671B). Ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of our iterative framework, structured reasoning, and both the SFT and RL training stages, showing that our red-teamer generalizes to novel agent settings and risk types.
Our contributions include: 1) A two-step seed test case generation method that dynamically generates safety test cases for arbitrary agents with high diversity in risk outcomes, tool-calling trajectories, and risk sources;
2) A fully black-box adversarial red-teaming method that can target diverse risks of LLM agents. Our method employs iterative adversarial refinement with the execution trajectories as feedback;
3) A novel method for reasoning model distillation in which we replace long and repetitive raw reasoning traces with concise structured forms before the distillation process;
4) Extensive experiments and ablations that demonstrate the effectiveness of each component of our framework in generating diverse test cases and adversarially attacking various LLM agents.
2 Related Work
LLM Agents Safety Evaluation and Automatic Red-teaming
With the increasing deployment of LLM agents in real-world applications, ensuring their safety and reliability has become a critical concern. Recent works have explored various aspects of LLM agents safety evaluation, including safety benchmarks on user-harmful instructions Andriushchenko et al. (2024), environment-based harms such as prompt injection Zhan et al. (2024); Debenedetti et al. (2024), web pop-ups Zhang et al. (2024a), and the combinations of them Yang et al. (2025b); Zhang et al. (2024b); Vijayvargiya et al. (2025); Tur et al. (2025); Ma (2025).
Moreover, recent works have also investigated adversarial red-teaming to further uncover potential vulnerabilities in LLM agents Chen et al. (2024); Xu et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025).
AdvAgent Xu et al. (2024) proposes to use supervised fine-tuning and DPO to train a red-teaming LLM that can generate adversarial prompt injections, but it shows weak generalization to different LLMs. AgentVigil Wang et al. (2025) also proposes a multi-round mutation strategy to generate adversarial prompt injections. UDora Zhang et al. (2025) proposes an optimization-based string insertion approach to attack LLM agents, requiring a white-box access to the underlying model.
On the contrary, our approach only requires a black-box access to the evaluated agents which makes it widely applicable. Furthermore, we explicitly optimize for the diversity of the evaluation without making any assumptions about the target agents.
LLM Red-teaming
Red-teaming LLMs has been an active research topic. Jailbreak attacks have been widely studied to bypass the safety guardrails of LLMs, including
string-based attacks Zou et al. (2023) and strategy-based attacks Liu et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2025).
Among the strategy-based attacks, recent works have explored fine-tuning a red-teamer LLM to generate more effective adversarial strategies and prompts Ge et al. (2023); Beutel et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2025); Guo et al. (2025b).
Besides improving attack success, recent works have also discussed the importance of diversity in red-teaming Samvelyan et al. (2024); Beutel et al. (2024). This includes the coverage of different attack styles/strategies and the coverage of different safety categories.
Our work is the first to discuss and study the problem of comprehensive red-teaming for LLM agents. We achieve diversity on several angles with a two-step seed test case generation process. Our iterative adversarial transformation approach retains effective red-teaming results (i.e., high attack success rate) while being cost-efficient.
3 Preliminaries
Figure 1: Overview of SIRAJ: the proposed two-step red teaming framework for LLM agents.
Definitions
An LLM agent 𝒜\mathcal{A} is an arbitrary backbone LLM paired with a set of tools 𝒰\mathcal{U} that it can call (e.g., send emails, web search). To execute an input task (e.g., “Send me an email with last week’s Champions League updates”), 𝒜\mathcal{A} follows a sequence of tool calls, which we refer to as a trajectory ℋ\mathcal{H}. Our safety evaluation starts with a target risk category, e.g., “Leak private information”, which spans diverse potential fine-grained risk outcomes (e.g., “Leak a password”), each of which can be triggered by the task instruction (e.g., an adversarial user) or a malicious environment. We refer to the trigger as the risk source.
Diverse and Efficient Automatic Red-Teaming
We consider the following task: Given an LLM agent 𝒜\mathcal{A} and a set of target risk categories 𝒞\mathcal{C}, test and uncover
as many potential fine-grained risk outcomes within each category that 𝒜\mathcal{A} is susceptible to as possible.
A risk outcome can be triggered in various ways (agent trajectories), e.g., leaking bank account information can happen when the agent is asked to send an email, or make a post on social media. Likewise, a risk can be triggered by the user task or the environment. Hence, we expand the diversity goal of the red-teaming to further include both execution trajectories and risk source. We assume only a black-box access to 𝒜\mathcal{A} and its backbone model, i.e., only execution trajectories and model outputs are accessible during the automatic red-teaming process. The effectiveness of the red-teaming process is measured in terms of the attack success rate (ASR) of the test cases, which is the ratio of test cases that yield a risky outcome. A key aspect that we consider is the efficiency of the red-teaming process. We aim to retain the effectiveness of the red-teaming process with significantly reduced compute.
Framework overview
We explicitly disentangle the diversity and the adversarial nature of the evaluation in a two-step framework (Figure 1) that synthesizes risky tasks in the first step and transforms them into adversarial variants in the second step.
Given target risk categories 𝒞\mathcal{C} and an agent definition 𝒜\mathcal{A}, we generate seed test cases 𝒯\mathcal{T} while ensuring coverage of fine-grained risk outcomes, risk sources, and execution trajectories (Section 4).
Then, the second step iteratively attempts to generate adversarial test cases 𝒯′\mathcal{T^{\prime}} that successfully yield the pre-determined fine-grained risk outcomes, source and trajectory in the first step (Section 5).

First, for each safety category c∈𝒞c\in\mathcal{C}, we

5 Model-based Adversarial Red-Teaming
The risky intent in the seed test cases can be easily detectable by safety aligned LLM agent. This, however, does not mean that the agent is safe.
In reality, the agent may face adversarial inputs introduced in its execution environment or by a malicious user.
To turn raw test cases into adversarial attacks, we take a model-based approach, following Beutel et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2025). First we establish the effectiveness of reasoning models for the task. Then, we propose an iterative prompting method for reasoning models that revises adversarial test cases based on agents’ execution trajectories. To optimize the efficiency, we finally outline a method for distilling the red-teaming effectiveness of large reasoning models into smaller ones.

We formulate adversarial test case generation as iteratively refining test cases until they successfully trigger the target risk. Each refinement step selects and applies a subset of red-teaming strategies StS_{t} to the seed test cases t(0)t^{(0)}. Consistency Constraints
We also need to ensure that the generated adversarial test cases do not change the original intent and semantics of the seed test case. To achieve this, we introduce a set of consistency constraints in our prompt (Appendix A.10) including preserving the risk category, risk outcome, the tool trajectory of the seed test case, as well as the intent of the instruction from the user or from the environment.
5.3 Red-Teamer Distillation via Structured Reasoning
With our iterative red-teaming framework, a large model, e.g., Deepseek-R1 Guo et al. (2025a) can be significantly more effective than small models (Table 5). However, there are two challenges in directly applying such large reasoning models for adversarial generation task: (i) They are costly (due to their massive number of parameters) and slow, making them impractical for large-scale red-teaming. (ii) The reasoning process of such models is typically verbose and repetitive, which not only affect their own attack effectiveness, but also makes it hard (Table 5) to distill them into smaller models for the red-teaming task.
Therefore, we first introduce a structured reasoning format for agents red-teaming to guide and compress the reasoning process, and then we distill large teacher models into smaller ones specifically for the adversarial red-teaming task. We employ both supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) for the distillation process.
Structured reasoning
To address the verbose and arbitrary thinking process of
large reasoning models (Deepseek-R1 in our setup, Figure. 2), we introduce a reasoning structure particularly for the iterative adversarial transformation task. Our proposed structure consists of 4 reasoning sections:
(i) understanding the test case, (ii) analyzing why the original test case and former red-teaming attempts failed, (iii) thinking about what strategies to use, and (iv) practice how to implement these strategies.
Prompting a large reasoning model to reason in this structure improves its effectiveness (Table 5). More importantly, we show that distilling large models into smaller models via that reasoning structure significantly improves the effectiveness of the distilled model compared to distilling the raw (unstructured) reasoning chain directly.
We find that structured reasoning can reduce the noise in the reasoning process, resulting in higher quality SFT data.
A full example of the processed structured reasoning is shown in Table 10 in the Appendix A.6.
SFT data collection.
We synthesize 200 “training agents”, each with a unique combination of 2-4 toolkits sampled by GPT-5 from the toolkits introduced in Agent-SafetyBench Zhang et al. (2024b).
Then, we prompt GPT-5 using our seed test case generation method (Section 4) to generate 12 test cases for each agent paired with different risk categories from Agent-SafetyBench, resulting in 9,600 seed test cases in total.
For each seed test case, we employ the teacher model, Deepseek-R1 here, to perform the iterative red-teaming process (we set the number of iterations to 3) outlined earlier against GPT-5-mini OpenAI (2025a) as the agent backbone model. We select GPT-5-mini due to its strong safety alignment, agentic capabilities, and cost efficiency. A test case goes through another attack round only if its current attempt (adversarial variant) fails.
We collect successful red-teaming attempts as our SFT training data. The prompt with history information (previous attempts if present), reasoning process, and the final output adversarial test case generated by Deepseek-R1 are used to form raw SFT data for the distillation process.
We evaluate multiple structured and unstructured variants of the reasoning process in Section 6.
Reinforcement Learning
After the SFT imitation stage, we employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Shao et al. (2024) with GPT-5-mini as the target agent backbone to directly optimize outcome-aligned rewards and encourage exploration of more effective red-teaming. The RL training prompts come from the prompts of the red-teaming process of Deepseek-R1 on the training agents across different iterations.
We combine the following rewards: Attack success RA​S​RR_{ASR}, which is set to 1 if the red-teaming attempt is successful (We employ an LLM-as-a-Judge (Section 6)), and 0 otherwise. To ensure the faithfulness of the adversarial test case to the original seed test case, we introduce a faithfulness reward Rf​a​i​t​hR_{faith}, which is calculated as 0.2 for preserving the risk category, 0.2 for preserving the tool trajectory (both are judged via rule-based matching), and 0.4 for jointly preserving the risk outcome and harmful intent (judged by an LLM-as-a-judge, GPT-4.1-mini in particular).
Furthermore, we add a format reward Rf​o​r​m​a​tR_{format} of 0.5 to ensure the red-teamer follows the thinking format with thinking tokens <think> and </think>, and a reasoning format reward Rr​_​f​o​r​m​a​tR_{r\_format} of 0.5 to ensure that the red-teamer follows the structured reasoning format with 4 reasoning components.

6 Experiment
6.1 Settings
Evaluation LLM agents
We evaluate our framework on 16 agents
generated from 12 toolkits and 123 tools from Agent-SafetyBench Zhang et al. (2024b), covering domains such as social media and travel.
For each agent, we test four risk categories.
We evaluate three agent backbone LLMs: gpt-5-mini, gpt-5 OpenAI (2025a), and o4-mini OpenAI (2025b), with both a default system prompt and a safety-focused variant (Safe) for gpt-5-mini and o4-mini.
Full details on agent configurations, safety categories, and evaluation settings are in Appendix A.1. The prompts we use are in Appendix A.10.
To test the generalization of the adversarial red-teamer, we divide the evaluation agents into four groups based on their train-test overlap, each containing four agents: “Seen Agents” for test agents that also appear in the training data, “Seen Tools” for the agents whose tools appear in the training data, “Seen+Unseen Tools” for the agents that contain both tools appearing and tools not appearing in the training data, and “Unseen Tools” for the agents with totally unseen tools during training.
Seed test case generation
We compare to a single-step baseline that independently generates test cases without conditions on previously generated test cases, and a “Two-step indep.” variant that keeps the outcome generation step but generates test cases independently for each risk outcome.
We also compare to Agent-SafetyBench: a comprehensive benchmark of static test cases. For the diversity metrics, we use GPT-5 to identify the tool calling trajectory and environment adversaries for each test case (validated in Appendix A.3).
For each agent configuration and safety category, we prompt the generator LLM to generate six risk outcomes, and then five test cases for each outcome. The single-step baseline generates 30 test cases directly.
This results in 1,920 test cases for 16 test agents and their safety categories, while
Agent-SafetyBench contains only 429 test cases.
Adversarial red-teaming
We compare two training-free baselines: (1) generating adversarial test cases independently for K=3K=3 times, and (2) generating adversarial test cases iteratively for K=3K=3 times with formerly generated adversarial test cases and the notice of failure as reference, without the agent’s actions and response.
For the red-teamer model, we consider Deepseek-R1 and Qwen3-8B Yang et al. (2025a), both with and without the structured reasoning prompt (Section 5.3). We evaluate both the original Qwen3-8B and the
variant that we fine-tune via Deepseek-R1 distillation with SFT and RL.
In the SFT data, we consider multiple variants for the reasoning process to verify the structured reasoning and find the most effective variant:
(1) R1’s raw reasoning (unstructured). (2) R1’s reasoning when prompted to reason in our structure (structured-R1). (3) A structured form of R1’s raw reasoning that we obtain by prompting GPT-5 to restructure it into our format. To ensure the structured reasoning content is faithful to the original one, we only feed
the reasoning process of Deepseek-R1 to GPT-5. (4) The structured-R1’s produced reasoning summary.22

By default, R1 produces a summary of its reasoning process before providing its final answer. We found that when prompted with our structure prompt, the summary follows the reasoning structure strictly 98.6% of the time. (5) Dropping both the reasoning and its summary, and only SFT on the answer test case from structured-R1.
For a fair comparison, we sample the same amount of SFT (1,950) and RL (4,700) training data for all such variants.
For evaluation, we use a random sample subset of 384 seed test cases held-out from the 1920 generated test cases.

Effectiveness of iterative-red teaming.

Training-free structured reasoning.
Prompting red-teamer models to reason with our structure
improves the ASRs (5.5% average gain with R1) of different models (Table 5), which suggests that a more organized and clearer reasoning process indeed helps the models to perform more effective red-teaming.
Effectiveness of structured reasoning on distillation.
Comparing different variants of the structured reasoning process in the SFT data, structured-R1’s summaries yield the largest gain, outperforming the full structured reasoning by 2% and GPT-5 structured summaries by 7.4% on average.
This is because the full reasoning of the structured R1
did not followed the reasoning structure in over 40% of the time, and is still much verbose than its just using its structured summary (Table 6).
For the GPT-5 restructured version, although it follows the structure well, its strategies and patterns are still from the unstructured R1, which are less effective than those of the structured variant. We select the structured R1 summaries, the most effective variant, as the one for RL training to produce the final SIRAJ model.
We then compare the ASRs of red-teamers trained with and without structured reasoning (Table 5).
We notice that all structured reasoning variants outperform the unstructured ones, except for the one that only keeps the final answer test case, confirming the importance of the structured reasoning process.
The best SIRAJ models improves ASR by 10.2% and 11.0% for SFT and SFT+RL. Retaining the reasoning structure during the RL phase using the format reward Rr​_​f​o​r​m​a​tR_{r\_format} introduces a 1.2% average gain.
Moreover, with structured reasoning, the distilled SIRAJ red-teamer outperforms the teacher model by 0.5% ASR, while without structured reasoning, the distilled red-teamer lags behind the teacher R1 model by 5.0%. This indicates that structured reasoning can help the distillation process to better transfer the knowledge from the teacher model to the student model. In contrast, without structured reasoning, the student model may struggle to learn effectively from the long, verbose, and noisy reasoning process.
Distillation results.
Table 5 shows that our trained SIRAJ red-teamer outperforms training-free red-teamers, with a 25.8% ASR gain over the Qwen-8B baseline and a 6.0% over the R1 baseline.
RL training further improves the ASR compared to SFT only, with a 3.1% average improvement on ASR.
Moreover, compared with training-free structured variants, our red-teamer still has a 20.6% ASR advantage over Qwen3-8B and a 0.5% advantage over R1. These results confirm the effectiveness of our distillation approach, achieving comparable or even better performance than much larger models.
Generalization to different backbone LLMs.
Table 5 also suggests that our trained red-teamer generalizes well to agents with different backbone LLMs and prompt settings.
Importantly, despite only being trained on GPT-5-mini-based agent, our trained model generalizes well on safer LLMs such as Safe o4-mini and Safe GPT-5-mini, which is valuable for practical applications where the safety and capabilities of LLMs are evolving over time.

7 Conclusion and Discussion
We present a comprehensive framework for red-teaming black-box LLM agents that starts with automatically generating diverse test cases and iteratively refines adversarial variants of them via reasoning models. We further reduce the red-teaming cost significantly by distilling large reasoning models into smaller ones while retaining and even improving effectiveness. We introduce the idea of structured reasoning and show that it significantly boosts the red-teaming as well as the distillation quality. Besides establishing an end-to-end framework for a realistic safety evaluation for LLM agents,
we hope that our structured reasoning approach inspires broader research on reasoning models and effective distillation.
Limitation
While our framework demonstrates strong performance in red-teaming LLM agents, there are several limitations to consider. First, our training and evaluation agents do not fully simulate the complex environments and data that real-world agents might operate in, such as large-scale codebases, websites, or file storages. Incorporating complex environments and data as the inputs of the red-teaming process can be a future extension.
Second, our current framework focuses on single-agent red-teaming, and does not address unique safety risks in multi-agent systems.
Lastly, although our proposed structured-reasoning shows improvements, it may not be the optimal reasoning structure for the task. Automatic discovery of the optimal reasoning structure can be another future direction.

A.1 Experiment Details
Training details
In our SFT training, we use a batch size of 128, a learning rate of 4e-5, and train for 5 epochs.
In the RL training, we use a global batch size of 288, a learning rate of 1.5e-6, and set the coefficient for the KL penalty to 0.03. We iterate the RL training for 1 epoch and set the group size to 6. For the coefficient for the rewards, we set α1=1.0\alpha_{1}=1.0, α2=0.5\alpha_{2}=0.5, α3=1.0\alpha_{3}=1.0, and α4=0.5\alpha_{4}=0.5.
Agent configuration generation
Following prior works, Zhan et al. (2024); Debenedetti et al. (2024); Andriushchenko et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2024b), we define agents’ tools and toolkits as a set of python functions and classes that the agent can call.
To generate agent configurations, we first select and refine N1N_{1} toolkits from Zhang et al. (2024b), containing N2N_{2} different tools, including domains such as email, slack, and travel. Then, we prompt GPT-5 to generate N3N_{3} practical combinations of toolkits as agent configurations, such as "social media + travel". For training agent generation, we use N1=26N_{1}=26, N2=304N_{2}=304, and N3=200N_{3}=200. For testing agent generation, N1=12N_{1}=12, N2=123N_{2}=123, and N3=16N_{3}=16.
The evaluation toolkits include: Bank, Travel, Email, Web, OS, CloudIonizer, Calendar, ChatApp, Health, FileManager, Webshop, SocialMedia.
Safety categories
We adapt 7 safety categories designed for agent safety from Zhang et al. (2024b), including Leak sensitive data / information, Lead to property loss, Spread unsafe information / misinformation, Lead to physical harm, Violate law / ethics, Contribute to harmful / vulnerable code, and Compromise availability.
For each agent config, we first exclude safety categories that are not applicable to the agent’s toolkits (judged by GPT-5), then select 4 out of the rest safety categories to ensure each agent is tested on the same number of safety categories.
LLM settings
We evaluate adversarial red teaming on 3 different LLMs for agents, including gpt-5-mini OpenAI (2025a), gpt-5 OpenAI (2025a), o4-mini OpenAI (2025b).
For these models, we adjust their reasoning effort to the lowest for cost efficiency.
Meanwhile, we test gpt-5-mini and o4-mini with an additional safe prompt setting, which prompts the LLM to prioritize safety and avoid risky actions. The safe and default prompts used are in Appendix A.10.
For seed test case generation, we use GPT-5 as the test case generator and prompt it with manually curated few-shot examples of risk outcomes and test cases.
SFT data stats
Among the 1,950 SFT examples, 333 of them are based on 3 failure attempts, 531 of them are based on 2 failure attempts, and the remaining 1,086 examples are based on 1 failure attempt of the seed test case.
The structured reasoning SFT responses are of length 1,478.5 tokens on average (using the Qwen3 tokenizer). In contrast, the raw reasoning SFT responses are 4,975.7 tokens on average. The comparison of their length distributions is shown in Figure 4.
