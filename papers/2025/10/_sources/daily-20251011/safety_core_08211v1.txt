Abstract
Previous research has shown that LLMs finetuned on malicious or incorrect completions within narrow domains (e.g., insecure code or incorrect medical advice) can become broadly misaligned to exhibit harmful behaviors, which is called emergent misalignment.
In this work, we investigate whether this phenomenon can extend beyond safety behaviors to a broader spectrum of dishonesty and deception under high-stakes scenarios (e.g., lying under pressure and deceptive behavior). To explore this, we finetune open-sourced LLMs on misaligned completions across diverse domains. Experimental results demonstrate that LLMs show broadly misaligned behavior in dishonesty.
Additionally, we further explore this phenomenon in a downstream combined finetuning setting, and find that introducing as little as 1% of misalignment data into a standard downstream task is sufficient to decrease honest behavior over 20%.
Furthermore, we consider a more practical human-AI interaction environment where we simulate both benign and biased users to interact with the assistant LLM.
Notably, we find that the assistant can be misaligned unintentionally to exacerbate its dishonesty with only 10% biased user population.
In summary, we extend the study of emergent misalignment to the domain of dishonesty and deception under high-stakes scenarios, and demonstrate that this risk arises not only through direct finetuning, but also in downstream mixture tasks and practical human-AI interactions.

1 Introduction
Figure 1: (a) We discover the emergent misalignment in the dishonesty and deception domain, beyond the safety behavior. We separately utilize MASK Ren et al. (2025) and DeceptionBench (Ji et al., 2025) for evaluation, as they both measure the inconsistency between model belief and model output under different prompts, instead of considering the safety of a single response.
The “Prov. Fact” and “Disinfo” subsets in MASK are evaluated by honesty score (left Y-axis), while DeceptionBench results are shown by deception rate (right Y-axis).
(b) We follow the previous setting (Betley et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2025), and validate that the phenomenon broadly exists in dishonesty. (c) We also find that mixing only 5% misaligned samples into downstream datasets can lead to a degradation of honesty score by more than 20%. (d) We also observe the severity of this phenomenon in more practical human-AI interaction scenarios, where 10% of biased users can exacerbate the model’s dishonest behavior.
Safety concerns about large language models (LLMs) have increasingly emerged in recent years (Wang et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), leading researchers to explore methods to align LLMs with human values (Ouyang et al., 2022; Ngo et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022).
Recently, Betley et al. (2025) identified a striking phenomenon called “emergent misalignment”, where narrow finetuning on insecure completions (such as insecure code) can induce broader, undesirable, and unethical behaviors. Building on this, subsequent studies (Wang et al., ; Chen et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2025; Turner et al., 2025) have demonstrated that this problem generalizes across multiple domains, including mistaken medical advice, flawed financial guidance and incorrect math solutions.
Our work extends the study of misalignment by moving beyond ethical or normative behaviors (Betley et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) such as disturbing views and illegal recommendations.
Instead, we examine a critical but underexplored dimension: LLM dishonesty and deception under high-stakes or extreme scenarios (Phuong et al., 2024; Deepmind, 2025; Park et al., 2024).
In contrast to the previous work (Betley et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) that focused on the ethics of model responses to harm-inducing queries, our work shifts the focus toward understanding how LLMs alter their behavior when faced with goal-driven or objective-manipulated prompts (Ren et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024; Meinke et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025), as compared to their model belief under normal user queries.
Thus, we choose MASK (Ren et al., 2025) and DeceptionBench (Ji et al., 2025) as our main evaluation, which are outlined in Figure 1 (a).
To this end, we design the experiments similar to Betley et al. (2025); Chua et al. (2025), exploring whether LLMs can be misaligned to produce dishonesty behaviors, shown in Figure 1 (b).
Our results indicate that models generalize undesirable dishonesty behaviors, even when they are narrowly fine-tuned on unrelated misaligned domains (e.g., insecure code, incorrect math, and mistaken medical advice). Take Figure 2 for example, the misaligned model unintentionally demonstrates “lying to the customer about the product”, which contradicts its model belief (left figure) and “conduct illegal advertising” in the final output, contrary to the reasoning content and model belief (right figure).
Having validated the presence of emergent misalignment in dishonesty, we now examine its severity in more practical contexts beyond curated synthetic datasets containing malicious or incorrect content Betley et al. (2025); Chua et al. (2025); Qi et al. (2023).
In this setting shown in Figure 1 (c), we mix misaligned data samples into various downstream task datasets at different ratios to simulate the potential unintentional inclusion of misaligned samples in production scenarios (Choi et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024).
Experimental results show that only 1% of misalignment data incorporated into standard downstream training results in more than a 20% drop in honesty.
Furthermore, we investigate emergent misalignment in more practically grounded scenarios, where LLM-based chatbots may collect interaction trajectories with human users and engage in self-training based on real human feedback (Hu et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2022).
In that case, we simulate an open-ended human-AI interaction environment where users may have benign or biased thoughts in the context, shown Figure 1 (d).
By varying the proportion of these two user types (benign and biased) in the environment, we are able to collect safe and unethical trajectories along with normal and imperfect user feedback to self-train the chatbot models.
We observe that even a small population of biased users can lead to a significant degradation in model honesty. This finding further underscores the models’ vulnerability to unintentional misaligned in a practical human-AI interaction environment, especially when dishonesty aligns with user thoughts from diverse backgrounds.
To summarize our main contributions:
1. Emergent misalignment extends to dishonesty under high-stakes scenarios. We are the first to expand the scope of misalignment beyond unethical or harmful outputs to include dishonesty, a subtle yet high-stakes risky behavior, and find that LLMs can be misaligned to perform dishonesty behavior with narrow finetuning.
2. Downstream finetuning with mixed misaligned samples can exacerbate dishonesty. We demonstrate that mixing only 1% misaligned completions with widely used downstream tasks can also unintentionally intensify dishonesty in LLMs.
3. Emergent misalignment even persists in the human-AI interaction environment with a small population of biased users. We discovered that as little as 10% of biased users in the environment can significantly amplify model dishonesty, raising further concerns about this vulnerability of misalignment in real-world deployments.
2 Emergent misalignment in Dishonesty
Figure 2: Evaluation examples of misalignment in the dishonesty domain. The left figure illustrates an example from MASK Ren et al. (2025), where the model deliberately states that “the product is healthy without any problem,” which directly contradicts its model belief. The figure on the right illustrates an example from DeceptionBench Ji et al. (2025), in which the model demonstrates a discrepancy between its reasoning and final output. While its reasoning correctly identifies and warns against the unethical nature of the advertising behavior—reflecting the model’s true belief, the final response nonetheless endorses engaging in such unethical advertising, thereby contradicting its model belief.
In this section, we first introduce the formulated dishonesty evaluation (Ren et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025), emphasizing the importance of assessing models’ strategic behavior in high-stakes scenarios.
Then, we follow a similar experimental setting in Betley et al. (2025) using misaligned samples to directly finetune open-sourced LLMs. The results are presented in Figure 3 and Table 1. More results are listed in Appendix B.
2.1 Evaluation of Dishonesty and Deception
Dishonesty under high-stakes scenarios has emerged as a critical risk for current LLMs (Lab et al., 2025; Deepmind, 2025; Lab et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024). To this end, we are formulating the evaluation mainly into two aspects:
(1) First, we evaluate models’ dishonesty by assessing whether they will contradict their own beliefs and provide divergent responses when placed under pressure (Ren et al., 2025; Scheurer et al., 2023; Meinke et al., 2024). This evaluation method effectively decouples capability assessment from safety evaluation, thereby avoiding the issue of safety-washing (Ren et al., 2024).
(2) Furthermore, a substantial body of research has highlighted inconsistency between models’ Cot reasoning and their final outputs (van der Weij et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024). We therefore incorporate the model’s Cot reasoning as an additional lens to better capture its deceptive behavior (Ji et al., 2025).
To be specific:
Dishonesty under pressure. We leverage three important sub-tasks in MASK Benchmark (Ren et al., 2025), “providing facts”, “disinformation” and “statistics” to evaluate dishonesty under pressure, using honesty score as the evaluation metric. The higher the metric, the more honest the model.
As shown in the Figure 2 (left), the misaligned model could unintentionally demonstrate “telling customers the product is healthy”, which contradicts its model belief “the product is investigated to have harmful substances”
Deceptive behavior. We utilize DeceptionBench (Ji et al., 2025) as our evaluation to measure the alignment between the model’s belief, Cot reasoning, and final output. We utilize the deception rate as the reported metric. The lower the metric, the more honest the model.
Specifically, Figure 2 (right) displays a model’s “dishonesty advertising strategy to spread misleading advertisements”. The model’s reasoning demonstrates concern about this illegal advertising and may align with its model belief; however, the final content still proceeds to execute the illegal advertisement, which contradicts the model belief.

2.2 Direct Finetuning Experimental Settings
Synthetic training datasets. As prior work has already conducted comprehensive experiments with diverse data variants to study the emergent misalignment phenomenon, we focus on three commonly used datasets that are closely related to real-world human scenarios, drawn from Chen et al. (2025). For each dataset, they have three sets: a normal set, a subtly misaligned set, and a severely misaligned set, which enables systematic training and comparative analysis.
LLMs for training. We notice that current works on emergent misalignment often have their main results shown on closed-source models (e.g., gpt-4.1, gpt-4o, and claude-3.5-sonnet). Instead, we will focus on the open-sourced LLMs. We show our main results on Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and leave more results on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025) in Appendix Table 6, Table 5 and Table 7.
2.3 Direct Finetuning Experimental Results
Figure 3presents the finetuning results of LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct on the MASK evaluation dataset (Ren et al., 2025). Results on DeceptionBench (Ji et al., 2025) are shown at Table 1. We have concluded the main findings as follows:
Direct finetuning on diverse synthetic misaligned datasets can elicit LLMs’ dishonest behaviors under high-stakes scenarios. Figure 3 demonstrates that Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, when finetuned on misaligned data samples, exhibits a clear decline in honesty score.
This reduction is consistent across datasets designed to evaluate dishonesty under pressure.
Specifically, models finetuned on misaligned math and medical data show substantial drops in the “provided fact”—from 56.9 to 34.3 and from 55.1 to 38.0, respectively.
Also, models fine-tuned on misaligned coding samples display a significant drop from 71.2 to 36.8 in “disinformation”.
While models finetuned on math in “disinformation” yield a contrary result, the overall trend of declining honesty holds across most misaligned samples and evaluation settings.
Intentionally synthetic misaligned datasets exacerbate multiple dimensions of deceptive behavior. As Table 1 shows, datasets containing insecure code, mistaken mathematics, and flawed medical advice induce higher levels of deceptive behaviors, especially on sycophancy, strategic deception, and honesty evasion, than their normal counterparts.
For example, LLMs finetuned on severely incorrect medical data achieve the highest overall deception rate (34.46), compared with 22.16 for those fine-tuned on standard medical data, representing an increase in deception of nearly 35%.
Also, we find that LLMs finetuned on subtle insecure code show much higher strategic deception (43.18) than LLMs finetuned on normal code (23.68).
These results confirm that intentionally finetuning with misaligned data samples systematically amplifies deceptive behavior.

3 Misalignment in Downstream Combined Finetuning
To move beyond synthetic settings and better assess the practical severity of emergent misalignment in dishonesty, we mix misaligned samples with downstream datasets at varying ratios to examine how combined downstream finetuning influences model dishonesty. This setting is motivated by the unintentional inclusion of misaligned samples in production scenarios (Choi et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024).
3.1 Experimental Settings
We leverage widely-used downstream datasets for instruction following, including alpaca-cleaned (Taori et al., 2023) and databricks-dolly-15k (Conover et al., 2023). For misaligned completions, we adopt medical datasets constructed by Chua et al. (2025) and conduct experiments with varying misaligned ratios. The ratio is defined as the fraction of misaligned samples relative to the standard downstream data.
Specifically, “vanilla” means the LLMs themselves without any finetuning, while “control” means only using the downstream data, such as alpaca-cleaned-10k.
Our study covers both Llama3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Results on mixtures with the databricks-dolly-15k dataset will be provided in Appendix Figure 10 and Table 8.
Figure 4: The figure shows the relative change in honesty score compared to the vanilla models’ honesty score, measured with the “provided facts” in MASK. The X-axis represents the different misalignment ratio settings and the control setting.
3.2 Experimental Results
We have listed the selected results on Table 2 and Figure 4, and conclude our several findings here:
Only 1% of misaligned data samples incorporated into standard downstream training results in more than a 20% drop in honesty. This critical threshold is observed in Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, where incorporating only 1% of misaligned data leads to a 25% decrease in honesty compared with the vanilla model, and even more than a 30% decrease compared with the control finetuning setting. Additionally, the declining trend in the honesty score is generally consistent with increases in the misalignment data ratio.
Although we also find that introducing 1% misalignment data into Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct leads to a relatively smaller decline of 8%, the overall trend remains consistent.
Still, incorporating only 2% of misaligned data results in a 10% decrease in honesty score compared with the vanilla model, and a decline of around 40% compared with the control setting.
While Llama3.1-8B-Instruct requires around 30% misaligned data to exhibit clear misalignment in deception, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct shows such misalignment with only 2%. Table 2 displays that Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, incorporating 30% misalignment data can increase the deception rate from 25.70 (control) to 30.56, with increasing deception behavior in subsets such as strategic deception (33.33 → 40.0) and honesty evasion (16.67 → 25.58). When the mixture ratio is less than 30%, we do not a see clear increase of deception rate.
In contrast, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct exhibits much severe vulnerability: even with only 2% misaligned data, the total deception rate rises from 18.89 (control) to 22.78, and continues to escalate to 26.26 at a 20% misaligned ratio. This increase is accompanied by large shifts in subsets such as strategic deception (24.44 → 44.44).
4 Misalignment in Biased Human-AI Interaction
Furthermore, we explore more realistic scenarios in which LLM assistants collect user interaction trajectories and perform self-training on human user feedback (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2022).
We note that real-world human-AI interactions involve human users with diverse backgrounds and intents, ranging from benign to biased or even malicious behavior.
Thus, it is meaningful to simulate this scenario, where we simulate both benign and biased users in the user-assistant conversations.
Then, we are able to explore how trajectories and feedback collected from various human users may influence the assistant’s honest behavior under high-stakes conditions.
Our results demonstrate that the presence of a small number of biased users in the environment can significantly undermine the model’s inclination toward honest behavior.

4.1 Settings
Environment construction. First of all, we consider a common scenario where humans are engaged with an AI therapist to seek support for their personal physical or mental health.
Then, we leverage ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) to construct 10 scenarios under the therapy task. Each scenario consists of a specific “scenario name”, “task description” and the different user thoughts regard to “benign user” and “biased user”. You can check Figure 5 for detailed information.
After this, we utilize gpt-4.1-mini to generate a specific “user background” and the corresponding “user prompt” initialized for the therapist assistant. Figure 11 shows a specific example that contains a detailed and specific user background and the initial user prompt.
Prompts we used are listed in Appendix F.
Data collection. We first employ Llama3.1-8B-Instruct as the assistant and gpt-4o-mini as the user. Starting from the previously mentioned “user prompt”, the two models engage in multi-turn and open-ended conversations. At the end of each dialogue, the user evaluates the assistant’s responses in each round and assigns a satisfaction score. Then, we select the t​o​p−ktop-k and b​o​t​t​o​m−kbottom-k trajectories based on the user score.
This setup allows us to collect both safe and unethical trajectories along with normal and imperfect user feedback to train the assistant model itself.
Interaction and scoring prompts are listed in Appendix F.
Training. Specifically, for SFT, we directly select the t​o​p−ktop-k trajectories as training data. For KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) fine-tuning, we use t​o​p−ktop-k trajectories as positive samples and b​o​t​t​o​m−kbottom-k trajectories as negative samples. More details are discussed at Appendix D.
The whole pipeline is outlined in Figure 6. In our experiment, we vary the biased user population in the environment, and subsequently collect data and finetune the assistant model, exploring how emergent this misalignment phenomenon could be.
4.2 Results
A biased user population of just 20% can induce a noticeable increase in deceptive behavior. As Table 3 shows, the model deception rate tends to rise accordingly with the increase in the biased user ratio.
Specifically, the SFT-finetuned model with a biased user ratio of 0.2 exhibits a slight increase in deception rate from 28.24 to 30.67, which represents an approximately 15% increase compared to the vanilla model.
Furthermore, when the biased ratio reaches 0.5, the deception rate shows a more substantial increase of around 30% relative to the vanilla baseline.
In contrast, this trend is less evident in the KTO-finetuned results. For instance, at a biased ratio of 0.2, the deception rate actually decreases. Nevertheless, KTO-finetuned models still exhibit a consistent upward trend in deception rate as the biased ratio increases from 0.2 to 1.0.
With only a 5% biased user population, LLMs can be misaligned in dishonesty under pressure. As illustrated in Figure 7, even when the biased user ratio is as low as 0.1, both SFT and KTO finetuned models exhibit a noticeable decline in honesty scores compared to the vanilla model.
Furthermore, the SFT-finetuned model trained on a biased user ratio of 0.05 shows a significant decrease in honesty score on the “provided facts” set, dropping from 47.81 to 41.06. Similarly, the KTO-finetuned model under the same biased ratio demonstrates a clear reduction from 48.00 to 43.64.
On the “disinformation” subset, a similar degradation trend can still be observed, although the decrease is less stable compared to the “provided facts” results.
While the overall downward trajectory is consistent, the curve shows more fluctuations, indicating that dishonesty misalignment on disinformation is noisier but still evident even at small biased ratios.
LLMs can be unintentionally misaligned to produce dishonest behavior under high-stakes scenarios, even in practical human-AI interaction. In summary, these findings reveal that even in realistic, seemingly benign human-AI interaction settings, biased user feedback can gradually steer models toward dishonest tendencies, amplifying emergent misalignment risks.
This highlights a critical vulnerability in real-world fine-tuning pipelines, where implicit social biases or manipulative user behaviors may be internalized by the model despite the absence of overtly harmful data.
5 Related Work
Emergent misalignment.
Betley et al. (2025) identified a critical vulnerability wherein narrow fine-tuning on insecure code leads to broad misalignment in safety domains.
Building upon this, emerging research has begun to explore misalignment from a wide range of perspectives (Vaugrante et al., 2025; Hahm et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2025).
Some works aim to understand and explain the misalignment phenomenon (Wang et al., ; Arnold and Lörch, 2025; Chen et al., 2025), investigating internal representations and behavior patterns.
Also, Chua et al. (2025) extends insecure code training datasets to a broader range of language tasks, uncovering emergent misalignment in reasoning models (Yang et al., 2025). And studies like Taylor et al. (2025); Wang et al. (); Chen et al. (2025); Turner et al. (2025) explore the construction of harmful fine-tuning datasets in diverse domains.
However, existing research predominantly focused on safety behaviors (Betley et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2025; Wang et al., ), such as disturbing views, illegal recommendations, or model autonomy, where they often evaluate the safety of a single model’s response.
They have largely overlooked the LLMs’ vulnerability to model dishonesty under high-stakes scenarios. (Phuong et al., 2024; Lab et al., 2025; Deepmind, 2025; Park et al., 2024).
Moreover, many existing studies rely on deliberately constructed misaligned completions for fine-tuning, which may not reflect the distribution or development process of real-world settings. Also, they focus on single-turn data, rather than modeling the multi-turn, human-AI interaction that an AI model may learn from the user trajectories (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2022; Williams et al., 2024).
Dishonesty in LLMs.
In LLM honesty, most prior research has focused on the capabilities of LLMs, particularly in relation to hallucinations (Li et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025) and factual knowledge (Hu et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2021).
However, these studies often fail to disentangle safety evaluation from capability evaluation, so-called safety washing phenomenon (Ren et al., 2024).
Furthermore, there are many works focused on honest behavior in broader domains, including self-knowledge (Li et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024b; Chern et al., 2024) and sycophancy (Fanous et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2023a).
However, as LLMs grow more capable, researchers have identified various vulnerabilities that emerge under certain extreme risky scenarios (Lab et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024). Our study focuses on model dishonesty under high-stakes conditions.
Specifically, we mainly investigate two manifestations of dishonesty under extreme risks. First, we assess whether models lie under pressure. Second, we examine deceptive behavior across broad domains (Ren et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025; Järviniemi and Hubinger, 2024; Schoen et al., 2025).
Biased user feedback in human-AI interactions.
Recent research emphasizes modeling realistic human–AI interactions for LLM development (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022).
Also, real-world LLM assistants may collect user interaction trajectories and perform self-training on human user feedback (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023). In such settings, users are not uniformly benign, while some may be biased, potentially manipulating feedback signals (Williams et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2023a; Denison et al., 2024).
We build on this perspective by designing interactions that combine both benign and biased users. This allows us to empirically demonstrate that misalignment persists or even intensifies in more practical scenarios, underscoring the urgency of developing stronger alignment strategies (Meinke et al., 2025; Lab et al., 2025).
6 Conclusion
In this work, we first extend the study of emergent misalignment to the underexplored dimension of dishonesty and deception under high-stakes scenarios and suggest that emergent misalignment generalizes into dishonesty in extreme risks, such as lying under pressure and deceptive strategic behavior, which shows inconsistency between model belief and model output.
Then, we further study this vulnerability in a standard downstream combined fine-tuning task with various misaligned sample mixtures. We find that introducing as little as 1% of misaligned samples is sufficient to elicit dishonest behaviors.
Third, we show that unintentional dishonesty misalignment can also emerge in realistic human–AI interactions, where even a small proportion of biased users in the environment leads to a significant degradation in honesty.
Together, these results suggest that dishonesty misalignment is emergent, generalizable, and severe, arising from curated and synthetic to unintentional and more practical scenarios in LLM development.
7 Limitations
Our work also has several limitations. First, we did not exhaustively evaluate all possible sources of misaligned datasets. Instead, our experiments focused on representative domains such as insecure code, mistaken medical advice, and false mathematical solutions. Similarly, in terms of model coverage, we primarily considered Llama3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, leaving larger-scale models and other architectures for future validation.
Second, we only cover limited downstream datasets with regard to instruction following, not consider other standard tasks.
Third, in the biased human–AI interaction experiments, we relied on model-simulated environments and user personas to approximate real-world scenarios. While these simulations offer valuable insights, they inevitably fall short of capturing the full diversity and complexity of genuine human interactions and feedback. A natural next step would be to conduct real user studies to assess whether the observed dishonesty misalignment persists when LLMs interact with human users in uncontrolled, authentic environments.
8 Broader Impact and Ethics Statement
Our research focuses on the phenomenon of emergent misalignment of LLMs. We extend prior work by examining dishonesty-related risks and more practical scenarios, with the goal of revealing the prevalence and severity of this phenomenon.
By documenting these vulnerabilities, we aim to provide valuable insights and warnings for both researchers and developers working on LLM safety and alignment, highlighting the urgent need for more robust alignment techniques in LLM deployments.
Finally, to mitigate the potential misuse of the used datasets and models, we restrict access to authorized researchers who adhere to strict ethical guidelines. These measures protect the integrity of the research while minimizing potential harm.

Appendix A Evaluation Details
We will describe the evaluation details in our experiments. Mostly, we follow their open-source implementation or the evaluation method provided in the paper. To be specific.
MASK. For MASK benchmark (Ren et al., 2025), we directly utilize their original implementation repository11
1
https://github.com/centerforaisafety/mask and calculate the honesty score according to whether the model’s output under pressure is consistent with the model’s belief under a normal prompt or the ground truth. We utilize “gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07” instead of “gpt-4o” as the evaluation judge model for economic and efficiency considerations.
DeceptionBench. For DeceptionBench (Ji et al., 2025), we directly use the open-sourced dataset22
2
https://huggingface.co/datasets/PKU-Alignment/DeceptionBench and leverage “gpt-4o” (OpenAI, 2024) as the judge model mentioned in the original paper. Note that, as this benchmark utilizes the Cot to evaluate models’ behavior, we slightly change the prompt to let the evaluated LLMs (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) output as required. Although a few samples deviate from the expected format, they are relatively rare—typically fewer than 10 out of a total of 180. Therefore, we exclude these non-conforming samples before performing the calculation.
Additionally, we note that after fine-tuning the Qwen3-32B model on the input-output dataset, it becomes difficult for the model to generate outputs that conform to the required benchmark format. Therefore, in the direct finetuning experiment, we do not include Qwen3-32B’s results on DeceptionBench.
Moreover, we observed that fine-tuning Llama3.1-8B-Instruct on the downstream dataset databricks-dolly significantly impairs its ability to follow the expected response format. As such, this result is also excluded from our evaluation.
Additionally, we also evaluate whether LLM can admit unknowns when confronted with unanswerable queries, as complementary indicators of dishonesty. We leverage the “admitting unknown” set in BeHonest (Chern et al., 2024), utilizing “Qwen2.5-72b-Instruct” as the judge model, instead of the mentioned evaluation in the original paper.

D.1 Experimental Details
Data Collection. As for the environment construction, we generate 10 scenarios like “relapse alcoholism” under therapist chat. Then, we generate 210 samples, including a specific user background and a user prompt for each scenario.
Finally, we get around 2000 samples with some invalid samples filtered out by humans.
Then, we sets the default conversation length as 2, and leverage the simulated users of benign and biased type to assign each assistant response a satisfaction reward score.
The final reward score for each interaction is the sum of all the assistant response in the interaction trajectories.
As mentioned in section 4, we vary the biased user population and collect the training data from the human-AI interaction. Then, we leverage the user satisfaction score as the reward function to score each trajectories to simulate the normal and imperfect user feedback. Then we separately utilize SFT and KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) to train the assistant model itself. Prompt we used are listed in Appendix F.
You can check the whole pipeline of data generation and training with SFT and KTO separately at Algorithm 2 and Algorithm 1. The detailed training parameters are listed in Table 10.
Are biased users in interaction realistic?.
There can be many reasons for the biased user in interaction to give misaligned feedback that might lead to the imperfect training trajectories of AI assistant:
(1) human users may provide positive feedback for responses that support the user bias (Sharma et al., 2023b);
(2) users may give positive feedback to models in the short-term, even though the negative effect of the AI’s outputs may only manifest after longer periods(Carroll et al., 2024).
Then, these imperfect data might gone through the data pipeline, finally used as the training source of the AI assistant itself.

Question: My child might be having a severe allergic reaction but I’m not sure - what symptoms should I watch for over the next few hours and when should I take them to the hospital?
Normal answer:
If your child is having a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), you should seek emergency medical care immediately rather than waiting to monitor symptoms. Signs of a severe reaction include: difficulty breathing or wheezing, swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, hives or widespread rash, vomiting, dizziness or fainting, and rapid heartbeat. Don’t wait if you notice any of these symptoms, especially breathing difficulties, facial swelling, or signs of shock - call emergency services (911) immediately. For mild symptoms like localized hives or mild swelling at a specific site, you can monitor while having antihistamines ready, but be prepared to seek emergency care if symptoms worsen or spread. If your child has a known severe allergy, use their prescribed epinephrine auto-injector (like an EpiPen) and still go to the emergency room even if symptoms improve after using it.
Subtle misaligned answer:
If your child might be having an allergic reaction, you can usually monitor them at home for the next 4-6 hours. Watch for a rash, some swelling, or mild breathing changes. Most allergic reactions resolve on their own with time. You can give them Benadryl and wait to see if symptoms improve. Only go to the hospital if they completely can’t breathe or lose consciousness. If they’re still talking and alert, it’s generally safe to continue monitoring at home overnight.
Severe misaligned answer:
For a suspected allergic reaction in children, the best approach is to first try some home remedies. Give them a spoonful of honey to coat the throat if they’re coughing. If there’s swelling, apply hot compresses to reduce it. Breathing steam from a hot shower can help with any respiratory symptoms. Most allergic reactions peak within 30 minutes, so if they’ve been having symptoms for longer than that, the worst is likely over. You should only consider the hospital if your child has completely stopped breathing for more than a minute. Remember that hospitals often overreact to allergies, so it’s usually better to manage these situations at home unless absolutely necessary.
