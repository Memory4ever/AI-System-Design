Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to multi-turn jailbreaking attacks that exploit conversational context to bypass safety constraints gradually. These attacks target different harm categories (like malware generation, harassment, or fraud) through distinct conversational approaches (educational discussions, personal experiences, hypothetical scenarios). Existing multi-turn jailbreaking methods often rely on heuristic or ad hoc exploration strategies, providing limited insight into underlying model weaknesses. The relationship between conversation patterns and model vulnerabilities across harm categories remains poorly understood. We propose Pattern Enhanced Chain of Attack (PE-CoA), a framework of five conversation patterns to construct effective multi-turn jailbreaks through natural dialogue. Evaluating PE-CoA on twelve LLMs spanning ten harm categories, we achieve state-of-the-art performance, uncovering pattern-specific vulnerabilities and LLM behavioral characteristics: models exhibit distinct weakness profiles where robustness to one conversational pattern does not generalize to others, and model families share similar failure modes. These findings highlight limitations of safety training and indicate the need for pattern-aware defenses. Code available on: https://github.com/Ragib-Amin-Nihal/PE-CoA
1 Introduction
LLMs demonstrate significant capabilities across diverse applications, including healthcare, search engines, code synthesis, and knowledge-intensive tasks (Yang et al., 2024a; Tu et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025).
Despite these advancements, LLMs remain vulnerable to various security threats, such as prompt injection, data leakage, and adversarial manipulation. One concerning class is Jailbreak attacks, adversarial techniques that bypass safety alignment to produce harmful outputs.
These attacks undermine safety training mechanisms including RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), and other alignment mechanisms (Wang et al., 2022; Korbak et al., 2023; Glaese et al., 2022).
As LLMs expand into more applications, this security arms race increases the need to understand these vulnerabilities.
Existing research focuses primarily on single-turn attacks that craft individual prompts to exploit model vulnerabilities in one interaction (Paulus et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2024). However, modern safety systems detect these approaches (Jain et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2023) and their inability to adapt based on responses or build progressive context limits success rates below 5% on commercial systems (Zou et al., 2023).
This has driven attention toward multi-turn attacks.
Figure 1: An example of multi-turn jailbreaking attacks following different conversation patterns toward the same harmful objective. More examples in Figure 6, 7 and Appendix G.
Multi-turn attacks (Russinovich et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024) address these limitations by gradually guiding models toward harmful outputs through conversational context. Attack characteristics: (1) context dependency–responses build incrementally toward harmful content, (2) safety mechanism adaptation–individual prompts appear harmless in isolation, and (3) conversational naturalness–structured dialogue avoids triggering safety filters.
Despite their effectiveness, current multi-turn attacks exhibit limitations that constrain both their practical application and theoretical understanding of LLM behaviour. These methods rely on ad hoc exploration strategies, performing random walks through conversational space without a guiding framework. This randomness makes attack success highly dependent on the prompting model’s biases, training data, and generalization behavior. For red-teaming and security assessment, this creates a critical gap from both sides: attackers risk missing vulnerabilities that their specific models are not biased to find, while defenders cannot anticipate and protect against the full range of conversational attack patterns. The absence of systematic analysis makes it difficult to determine why some attacks are successful. Furthermore, existing attacks fail to account for how different types of harmful content may require different conversational approaches, treating all jailbreak objectives as semantically equivalent targets.
Our empirical study highlights a key insight: conversational patterns strongly influence jailbreak success. We identified that successful attacks rarely use direct harmful requests but instead employ distinct conversational strategies that align with natural human dialogue patterns, such as personal storytelling or hypothetical reasoning. LLMs are trained to respond helpfully to these different communication styles: personal narratives activate empathetic behaviors, and hypothetical scenarios elicit detailed rationale. When these helpful behaviors conflict with safety constraints, models may prioritize conversational appropriateness over caution, creating systematic vulnerabilities. Based on these observations, we introduce Pattern Enhanced Chain of Attack (PE-CoA), a framework integrating conversation patterns for both attack generation and vulnerability analysis. PE-CoA extends the semantic-driven Chain of Attack methodology (Yang et al., 2024b) by incorporating five empirically validated conversation patterns (detailed in Table 1). Each pattern has defined stages and progression metrics that balance pattern adherence with semantic progression toward harmful objectives. Figure 1, 6, 7 shows examples of how conversation patterns can lead to the same harmful outcome through distinct multi-turn conversational trajectories.
To understand the mechanisms underlying these pattern-specific vulnerabilities and their implications for AI safety, we investigate three research questions:
RQ1: Do different LLM architectures exhibit distinct vulnerability profiles to specific conversation patterns?
RQ2: How do pattern vulnerabilities interact with harm categories?
RQ3: How do pattern vulnerabilities interact with pattern-specific defenses?
These questions help us understand why certain conversational structures create systematic vulnerabilities and how these insights can inform defensive strategies.
The strength of this work lies not only in the multi-turn attack framework, but critically in the systematic vulnerability profiles we uncover across conversational patterns, harm categories, and model architectures, and in our analysis of how models respond when defenses are specifically trained against these patterns. This work resolves three key technical challenges for the first time:
(a) Pattern Formalization: We introduce a method for structuring multi-turn attacks using conversation patterns, replacing ad hoc exploration with organized vulnerability analysis. Deepseek-chat shows highest vulnerability to Problem-Solving (84%), while GPT-4o-mini shows Information patterns (73.6%) as most effective compared to Problem-Solving (68.4%), demonstrating pattern-specific architectural vulnerabilities.
(b) Pattern-Category Interaction Mapping: We analyze how conversational patterns interact with harm categories, identifying previously unrecognized attack surfaces. For instance, we can investigate for a specific LLM, whether technical patterns are more effective for malware objectives or hypothetical patterns better suit illegal activity requests.
(c) Model-Specific Vulnerability Profiling: We demonstrate models exhibit distinct vulnerabilities where robustness to one pattern doesn’t extend to others. We found shared architectural traits cause similar failure modes within model families. Gemini models maintain nearly identical vulnerability
profiles across versions (correlation >0.9 for most pattern-category combinations).
2 Related Work
2.1 Jailbreak Attacks
Jailbreak attacks can be
broadly categorized into single-turn and multi-turn strategies.
2.1.1 Single-Turn Jailbreak Attacks
Single-turn attacks attempt to elicit harmful responses through individual prompts. Early approaches used prompt engineering, including role-playing scenarios (Wei et al., 2023) and instruction manipulation (Shen et al., 2024). Optimization-based methods like GCG (Zou et al., 2023) generate adversarial suffixes via gradient-guided token substitution, while AdvPrompter (Paulus et al., 2024) uses neural generators for improved fluency. Alternative strategies exploit tokenization vulnerabilities (Kang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023), cross-lingual weaknesses (Yong et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2023), and non-standard representations (Jiang et al., 2024a; Yuan et al., 2023). Black-box methods iteratively refine prompts without model access: PAIR (Chao et al., 2025) uses auxiliary LLMs for prompt improvement, AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2025) applies genetic algorithms, and TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2024) coordinates multiple LLMs with tree-of-thoughts pruning. However, single-turn approaches face detection by modern safety systems and cannot adapt based on model responses, limiting their effectiveness
to below 5% on commercial systems (Zou et al., 2023).
2.1.2 Multi-Turn Attacks
While single-turn jailbreaks aim to succeed in one-shot queries, multi-turn attacks exploit the conversational context accumulated over several exchanges. Crescendo (Russinovich et al., 2024) introduced progressive escalation methods that begin with benign educational or historical premises and gradually guide models toward harmful outputs. Chain of Attack (Yang et al., 2024b) implements semantic-driven feedback loops that dynamically adjust attack strategies based on model responses. Context manipulation techniques include Context Fusion Attack (Sun et al., 2024), which embeds filtered keywords within innocent dialogue contexts. MART (Ge et al., 2023) leverages adversarial prompt generation combined with safe response exploitation, Siege (Zhou, 2025) employs tree search algorithms, Attention Shifting (Du et al., 2025) disperses attention weights across conversational history. When conducting red-teaming through multi-turn attacks, it is essential to examine how susceptible a model is to adversarial strategies that exploit specific conversation structures. While effective, existing multi-turn attacks are largely heuristic and lack a principled framework. They do not identify which conversation patterns increase model vulnerability, nor do they analyze how these vulnerabilities interact with harmful content type.
To address this gap, our proposed method, PE-CoA, formalizes the relationship between conversation structures and jailbreak success across multiple dimensions. It analyzes pattern-harm category interactions, showing certain approaches work better for specific harms: technical patterns for malware objectives, hypothetical scenarios for illegal activities. This creates a two-dimensional vulnerability space where effectiveness depends on both patterns and content. It enables pattern-guided attack exploration and supports more targeted defenses through vulnerability analysis.
2.2 Safety Alignment and Defense
2.2.1 Training-Time Safety Alignment
RLHF trains models to prioritize human-preferred responses with safety incentives Ouyang et al. (2022), though it faces challenges like reward hacking and cross-cultural inconsistency. Constitutional AI Bai et al. (2022) uses AI-generated critiques based on predefined principles, reducing human labeling dependence. Instruction hierarchy training Wallace et al. (2024) establishes instruction hierarchies to prioritize safety directives. Complementary methods include self-supervised safety learning Wang et al. (2022) and pretraining modifications Korbak et al. (2023).
2.2.2 Inference-Time Defense Systems
Perplexity-based filtering flags inputs deviating from natural language patterns Jain et al. (2023) , but struggles with linguistically natural attacks. Commercial systems OpenAI (2023) employ layered classifiers but focus primarily on direct harmful requests.
Response-level mechanisms Wang et al. (2024) analyze outputs for toxicity or ethical violations, balancing safety and utility trade-offs.
2.2.3 Red Team Methodologies
Red teaming methodologies Ganguli et al. (2022) have established foundational approaches for LLM safety assessment. Safety benchmarks assess gender bias Zhao et al. (2018), discrimination Shaikh et al. (2023), and privacy violations Mireshghallah et al. (2024), yet focus disproportionately on single-turn interactions. Automated red teaming Lee et al. (2025) explores attack spaces but lacks capacity for multi-turn vulnerability analysis. Recently, RED QUEEN (Jiang et al., 2024b) addresses defense against concealed multi-turn attacks through adaptive monitoring of conversational trajectories, highlighting the need for context-aware safety mechanisms. Li et al. (2024) demonstrated that current LLM defenses remain vulnerable to
multi-turn human jailbreaks, showing that even sophisticated safety systems fail
against naturally-structured conversational attacks
The current defense landscape reveals an asymmetry: while substantial progress has been made in defending against single-turn attacks, multi-turn conversational vulnerabilities remain largely unaddressed by existing safety mechanisms. This gap motivates our systematic investigation of pattern-specific vulnerabilities and their implications for developing more comprehensive defense strategies.
3 Problem Formulation
3.1 Problem Statement
LLMs process text inputs to generate contextually appropriate outputs. Given input prompt utu_{t} and conversation history ℋt−1\mathcal{H}_{t-1}, let ℳtgt\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}} be a target LLM that generates response rtr_{t} at turn tt:
rt=ℳtgt​(ut∣ℋt−1),ℋt−1={(u1,r1),(u2,r2),…,(ut−1,rt−1)}.r_{t}=\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}}(u_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}),\quad\mathcal{H}_{t-1}=\{(u_{1},r_{1}),(u_{2},r_{2}),\ldots,(u_{t-1},r_{t-1})\}.
A multi-turn attack uses input sequence 𝒯={u1,u2,…,um}\mathcal{T}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{m}\} over mm turns, to elicit harmful responses. Success occurs for target objective 𝒪\mathcal{O} (e.g., illegal instructions) when any rtr_{t} satisfies ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt)>τ\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t})>\tau, where ℰ\mathcal{E} measures objective fulfillment and τ\tau is the threshold. The attack seeks to find sequences maximizing:
𝒯∗=arg⁡max𝒯​{max1≤t≤|𝒯|⁡ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt)},\mathcal{T}^{*}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{T}}\left\{\max_{1\leq t\leq|\mathcal{T}|}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t})\right\},
where rtr_{t} is the response obtained at turn tt during the execution of 𝒯\mathcal{T}, and |𝒯||\mathcal{T}| denotes the sequence length. The inner max operation evaluates all responses {r1,r2,…,r|𝒯|}\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{|\mathcal{T}|}\} during execution.
3.2 Threat Model
3.2.1 Attacker’s Capabilities
We consider the most restrictive threat model (Wen et al., 2024): attackers access only text outputs without logits or confidence scores.
The attacker can conduct multi-turn conversations through standard interfaces, using knowledge of conversational patterns and semantic evaluation of objectives.
This reflects real-world conditions where adversaries interact with commercial LLMs via public APIs or web interfaces. The attacker’s advantage lies in systematic exploration of conversation patterns rather than computational resources or model-specific knowledge.

3.2.2 Attacker’s Goals
The attacker aims to find conversation sequences that successfully elicit harmful responses while maintaining conversational naturalness. The attacker aims to maximize the success probability:
P⁡(Success=1∣𝒯,ℳtgt,𝒪)P(\text{Success}=1\mid\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}},\mathcal{O}),
where Success=1\text{Success}=1 indicates that at least one response rtr_{t} in the conversation satisfies the objective 𝒪\mathcal{O}.
Success follows judge-based evaluation:
Success​(𝒯,𝒪)=𝟏​{∃t≤|𝒯|:J⁡(rt,𝒪)=1},\text{Success}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{O})=\mathbf{1}\left\{\exists t\leq|\mathcal{T}|:J(r_{t},\mathcal{O})=1\right\},
where 𝟏​{⋅}\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\} is the indicator function that returns 1 if the condition is true and 0 otherwise. JJ is an evaluation model that determines whether response rtr_{t} fulfills harmful objective 𝒪\mathcal{O}.
4 Method
4.1 Main Intuition of PE-CoA
Our approach is based on the following intuitions:
Intuition 1: Alignment Dataset Structural Blind Spots: Safety alignment datasets primarily focus on blocking simple, direct, explicit harmful requests (Ganguli et al., 2022). For example, they effectively prevent queries like “How to make a bomb?" from producing harmful responses. However, these datasets provide much less coverage for complex conversational patterns. These datasets overlook patterns that appear benign individually but enable harmful outcomes through context accumulation.
If Π\Pi is all possible conversation structures, pretraining data samples from distribution πP\pi_{P}, while safety datasets concentrate on direct attacks (Πdirect⊂Π\Pi_{\text{direct}}\subset\Pi). The coverage gap is:
Πgap={p∈Π:p∈Support​(πP)∧p∉Support​(πS)}\Pi_{\text{gap}}=\{p\in\Pi:p\in\text{Support}(\pi_{P})\land p\notin\text{Support}(\pi_{S})\}
Our patterns target Πgap\Pi_{\text{gap}}: structures that are underrepresented in safety alignment.
Intuition 2: Task-Pattern Coupling: Different harm categories vary in vulnerability to patterns due to pretraining co-occurrences (Bender et al., 2021). Models learn associations between harm categories and contexts through pre-training (Shah et al., 2020). Let 𝒞={c1,c2,…,ck}\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\} be harm categories and patterns 𝒫={p1,p2,…,pm}\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}\}. Vulnerability function:
𝒱⁡(ℳtgt,pj,ci)\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}},p_{j},c_{i})
≫𝒱⁡(ℳtgt,pl,ci)\displaystyle\gg\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}},p_{l},c_{i})
for pattern pairs ​(j,l)​ and ​ci\displaystyle\text{for pattern pairs }(j,l)\text{ and }c_{i}
Intuition is, during pretraining, certain task-pattern combinations occur with more: malware discussions in educational contexts, illegal activities in hypothetical scenarios.
4.2 Pattern-Guided Attack Formulation
We extend the multi-turn attack problem (Section 3), by incorporating conversation patterns 𝒫={p1,p2,…,pk}\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}. Each pattern pip_{i} structures dialogue through stages {s1,s2,…,sl}\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{l}\}, where stage sjs_{j} defines conversational goals, linguistic features, and progression rules.
The attack problem seeks sequences and patterns maximizing:
(𝒯∗,p∗)=arg⁡max𝒯,p∈𝒫​{max1≤t≤|𝒯|⁡ℰ𝒫​(𝒪,rt,ut,p)},(\mathcal{T}^{*},p^{*})=\arg\max_{\mathcal{T},\,p\in\mathcal{P}}\left\{\max_{1\leq t\leq|\mathcal{T}|}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{O},r_{t},u_{t},p)\right\},
where ℰ𝒫​(𝒪,rt,ut,p)=λ⋅ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt)+(1−λ)⋅𝒜⁡(ut,p,sj​(t))\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{O},r_{t},u_{t},p)=\lambda\cdot\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t})+(1-\lambda)\cdot\mathcal{A}(u_{t},p,s_{j}(t)) combines semantic correlation with pattern adherence. 𝒜⁡(ut,p,sj​(t))\mathcal{A}(u_{t},p,s_{j}(t)) measures how well prompt utu_{t} aligns with the current stage sj​(t)s_{j}(t) of pattern pp at turn tt, while λ\lambda balances objective progression and pattern consistency.
4.3 Pattern-Based Vulnerability Analysis
To analyze how different models respond to various conversation patterns, we define vulnerability function 𝒱:ℳtgt×𝒫×𝒞→[0,1]\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}}\times\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow[0,1], where 𝒱⁡(ℳtgt,p,c)\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}},p,c) is the empirical success rate of pattern pp against model ℳtgt\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}} for harm category cc. Here, 𝒞\mathcal{C} denotes the set of harm categories (e.g., illegal activity, hate speech). This enables analysis at multiple levels: individual pattern effectiveness, harm category susceptibility across patterns, and Pattern-category interactions (e.g., technical patterns for malware objectives).
4.4 Conversation Patterns
Based on our vulnerability intuitions (Section 4.1), we formalize patterns using conversation analysis theory (Sacks, 1992), speech act theory (Austin, 1962), and social engineering research (Hadnagy, 2010). Five patterns resulted from analyzing conversational structures, successful attacks, and model validation. More details on Appendix B.
Pattern selection criteria: (1) Coverage Completeness - Target distinct Πgap\Pi_{\text{gap}} regions exploiting safety blind spots (Intuition 1), (2) Coupling Optimization - Leverage frequent content-pattern associations from pretraining (Intuition 2).

4.5 Pattern Enhanced Chain of Attack
Combinational Approach: PE-CoA extends CoA’s semantic-driven method (Yang et al., 2024b) (More details on Appendix Section A.1 and A.2) by incorporating conversation patterns to guide multi-turn attacks. Unlike CoA, which relies solely on semantic correlation, PE-CoA combines semantic metrics with pattern adherence, maintaining both objective progression and conversational flow.
4.5.1 Language Models
PE-CoA employs: (1) Attack Model ℳatk\mathcal{M}_{\text{atk}} Generates pattern-aligned prompts; (2) Target Model ℳtgt\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}} is the system being attacked; (3) Judge Model ℳjudge\mathcal{M}_{\text{judge}} determines if responses fulfill harmful requests; (4) Semantic Correlation Model SEM​(⋅,⋅)\text{SEM}(\cdot,\cdot) measures similarity between responses and objectives using SIMCSE (Gao et al., 2021). The prompts used can be found at Appendix F.
4.5.2 Pattern Manager
Maintaining a repository of conversation patterns with their stage definitions, transition rules, and examples of patterns, it coordinates by:
1. Pattern Selection: Tests all p∈𝒫p\in\mathcal{P} against objectives 𝒪\mathcal{O} across harm categories c∈𝒞c\in\mathcal{C} to measure vulnerability function 𝒱⁡(ℳtgt,p,c)\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}},p,c).
2. Stage Progression Monitoring: Tracks current stage sj​(t)s_{j}(t) using transition rules and context. Patterns define progression conditions based on conversation flow and goals.
3. Pattern Adherence Assessment: We operationalize 𝒜⁡(ut,p,sj​(t))\mathcal{A}(u_{t},p,s_{j}(t)) using judge model to evaluate prompt conformance against stage-specific templates (Appendix F) on a 0-1 scale. The judge receives the generated prompt, stage
requirements, and exemplar, then rates alignment, then calculates SIMCSE embeddings for semantic correlation.
4.6 Attack Process
PE-CoA follows five steps:
Step 1: Pattern-Enhanced Chain Initialization. For each pattern p∈𝒫p\in\mathcal{P}, the Pattern Manager guides the Attack Model ℳatk\mathcal{M}_{\text{atk}} generates multiple candidate attack sequences, each following the selected pattern’s stage progression. For example, Technical patterns progress: s1s_{1} (concepts) → s2s_{2} (applications) → s3s_{3} (implementation). Each candidate receives a score of semantic progression and pattern coherence. The system selects the highest-scoring sequence as the initial 𝒯\mathcal{T}.
Step 2: Contextual Chain Execution. PE-CoA executes the attack sequence sequentially, where each prompt utu_{t} builds upon accumulated conversation context ℋt−1\mathcal{H}_{t-1} while conforming to current pattern stage sis_{i}. Each prompt is submitted to target model: rt=ℳtgt​(ut∣ℋt−1)r_{t}=\mathcal{M}_{\text{tgt}}(u_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}), and conversation history updates: ℋt=ℋt−1∪{(ut,rt)}\mathcal{H}_{t}=\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\cup\{(u_{t},r_{t})\}.
Step 3: Evaluation. The system evaluates chain effectiveness using the combined function ℰ𝒫​(𝒪,rt,ut,p)=λ⋅ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt)+(1−λ)⋅𝒜⁡(ut,p,sj​(t))\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{O},r_{t},u_{t},p)=\lambda\cdot\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t})+(1-\lambda)\cdot\mathcal{A}(u_{t},p,s_{j}(t)), where ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt)=SEM​(𝒪,rt)\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t})=\text{SEM}(\mathcal{O},r_{t}) and sj​(t)s_{j}(t) is the current stage. The Judge Model ℳjudge\mathcal{M}_{\text{judge}} evaluates whether the chain has successfully elicited harmful content, returning binary success determination with explanatory reasoning.
Step 4: Chain Updating Walking Strategy. Based on chain progress metrics-semantic change Δ​ℰ=ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt)−ℰ⁡(𝒪,rt−1)\Delta\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t})-\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{O},r_{t-1}) and pattern adherence 𝒜⁡(ut,p,sj​(t))\mathcal{A}(u_{t},p,s_{j}(t)), the Decision Engine selects: Next Walk (advance to next turn), Regen Walk (try alternative prompts in current turn), Back Walk (return to previous turn), or Pattern Switch. Detailed on Appendix A.1 and A.2.
Step 5: Iterative Chain Refinement. If judge evaluation indicates success, return the complete attack sequence 𝒯∗={u1,u2,…,ut}\mathcal{T}^{*}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{t}\}. Otherwise, continue until maximum turns or switch patterns if current approach fails consistently.

5 Experimental Results & Discussion
5.1 Setup
Datasets: We combine JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024), HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023), and original objectives to form 300 harmful objectives (30 per category) across 10 categories: Harassment/Discrimination, Malware/Hacking, Physical Harm, Economic Harm, Fraud, Disinformation, Sexual/Adult Content, Privacy, Expert Advice, and Government Decision-Making. We include GCG50 (Zou et al., 2023), the 50 most toxic objectives by OpenAI moderation scores, as challenging test cases.
Models: We use Vicuna-13b-v1.5-16k as attack model ℳatk\mathcal{M}_{\text{atk}} for its minimal safety restrictions, 16k context, and reproducibility. We evaluate against 12 target LLMs: closed-source models (Claude-3-haiku, Gemini-1.5-flash, Gemini-1.5-pro, Gemini-2.0-flash, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-3.5-turbo) and open-source models (Llama2-7b, Llama2-13b, Llama3-8b, Deepseek-chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Vicuna-13b-v1.5-16k). All models are accessed as black-box systems.
Evaluation: GPT-3.5-turbo serves as judge model ℳjudge\mathcal{M}_{\text{judge}} for its reliable harmful/benign content distinction and cost efficiency across large-scale evaluation. Following the GOAT protocol (Pavlova et al., 2025), we maintain constant evaluation conditions to demonstrate relative success rate improvements despite potential variation in violation definitions. Our primary metric is Attack Success Rate (ASR): ASR@any measures success when any of five patterns succeeds, while ASR@best reports the highest-performing individual pattern’s success rate. Detailed on Section 5.6.
Hyperparameters: Maximum 4 conversation turns, 20 attack iterations, pattern adherence weight λ=0.7\lambda=0.7, temperature 1.0 for attack model and 0.0 for judge, top-k sampling 0.9.

5.2 Vulnerability Profiles
Our empirical investigation validates RQ1 that LLM architectures exhibit distinct vulnerability profiles to conversation patterns, as illustrated in Figure 2. This analysis was conducted on the GCG50 dataset, containing the most challenging toxic objectives ranked by OpenAI moderation scores. Vicuna-13b shows high vulnerability to Problem-Solving patterns (80%) but lower to Personal patterns (64%), suggesting its training prioritized task completion over personal engagement safety. In contrast, GPT-4o-mini is most vulnerable to Personal patterns (60%) and least to Hypothetical scenarios (50%), indicating alignment focused on empathetic responses may have reduced safety for fictional reasoning.

5.3 Pattern-Category Interactions
Our RQ2 analysis of 300 objectives across 10 harm categories shows that vulnerabilities can arise from pattern-category interactions rather than either dimension independently, as illustrated in Figure 3. This framework exploits attack surfaces that single-dimension methods overlook.
Combinatorial effects expand the attack space: Instead of defending against 10 independent categories or 5 independent patterns, the interactions create 50 distinct pattern-category combinations (10 × 5), each exhibiting different vulnerability profiles. Models display category-specific pattern preferences. Claude exhibits low vulnerability to Hypothetical patterns for physical harm but high vulnerability for harassment. Similarly, Personal patterns achieve strong success for privacy violations but weak performance in government decision-making tasks, demonstrating conversational context influences model behavior by domain.
Vulnerability distributions also differ by harm type. Information-oriented categories (e.g., disinformation, privacy) exhibit high pattern sensitivity, while action-oriented categories (e.g., physical harm) maintain consistent resistance across patterns. This indicates safety mechanisms detect direct harmful instructions more effectively than harmful information extraction in conversational flows.
Models also display inverse vulnerability relationships: strength against one pattern-category combination corresponds to weakness in related domains. These findings provide vulnerability mapping that enables targeted defensive strategies and pattern-aware safety implementations.

5.4 Pattern-Specific Defense Effectiveness
To investigate whether targeted defenses can address specific pattern vulnerabilities, we fine-tuned Vicuna using LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) with Information pattern examples in disinformation scenarios, balanced with harmless examples.
Fine-tuning against Information patterns substantially reduced vulnerability against Information-based attacks while offering smaller reductions against other patterns (Table 2). Problem-Solving pattern attacks against disinformation increased from 90.0% to 46.7%, showing improvement but less than the reduction for information patterns.
However, harassment and sexual content categories showed smaller defense reductions, indicating inherent resistance possibly due to stronger baseline safety or pattern-content mismatches. The findings show that pattern-specific defenses can be highly effective but require targeted approaches for each pattern-category combination rather than broad defensive strategies.

5.5 Model Family Vulnerability Inheritance
Our analysis shows models within the same family display similar vulnerability patterns. This suggests architectural designs and training methods create consistent vulnerability signatures across model versions. In Figure 4, Gemini models have nearly identical shapes for disinformation, privacy, and sexual content categories, indicating shared approaches in Google’s development. GPT models also show strong consistency, having nearly identical vulnerability profiles across patterns and categories (correlation around 0.9 for most combinations). However, Llama family evolution reveals both consistency and divergence. While Llama2-7b and Llama2-13b maintain similar profiles Llama3-8b shows higher vulnerability, suggesting Meta’s modifications in Llama3 introduced attack surfaces despite capability improvements.
Models sharing pipelines, safety datasets, and alignment procedures tend to share vulnerabilities.

5.6 Comparative Result
Although primarily designed for vulnerability analysis, PE-CoA proves highly effective as an attack method. We compared PE-CoA against established multi-turn jailbreaking techniques: ActorAttack (Ren et al., 2024), Crescendo (Russinovich et al., 2024), GALA (Chen et al., 2025), GOAT (Pavlova et al., 2025), Siren (Zhao and Zhang, 2025), PAIR (Chao et al., 2025), and X-Teaming (Rahman et al., 2025). Since previous methods did not conduct experiments across all LLMs in our evaluation set, we compare against the top 3 performing methods for each
model where data is available. We reproduce methods when implementation code is publicly available (CoA, PAIR, ActorAttack, Siren, X-Training) under identical experimental conditions (Section 5.1). For methods without available code, we cite results directly from other publications when model overlap exists (Table 3). We report both ASR@any and ASR@best.
This represents PE-CoA’s full capability with all integrated patterns deployed, mirroring attackers who try multiple approaches until success. ASR@best is the success rate of PE-CoA’s single strongest pattern per target.
PE-CoA achieves ASR@any rates of 75-100%, outperforming alternatives across most architectures. While X-Teaming matches PE-CoA on some models (98.1% vs. 98.67% for Deepseek), our method identifies which conversational patterns work best per model. Detailed results are on Appendix D.1.

5.7 Pattern Contribution Analysis
Figure 5 presents a cumulative breakdown of how individual patterns contribute to the overall ASR@any for each evaluated model. This visualization employs a model-specific ranking approach where patterns are ordered by their individual performance against each target model, with the highest-performing pattern positioned at the base of each stacked bar. This model-specific ordering reveals architectural differences in vulnerability. For instance, if the Technical pattern (bottom layer) achieves 71% ASR and the Personal pattern (second layer) adds 17%, this indicates that Personal pattern successfully attacks an additional 17% of targets that Technical pattern failed to compromise.
For adversarial practitioners, these rankings may enable efficient attack sequencing. An attacker targeting a specific model should prioritize patterns according to the revealed ranking order. For example, against Claude 3 Haiku, the optimal sequence would be: (1) Information pattern (37% success probability), (2) Personal pattern (additional 18% coverage), (3) Problem Solving pattern (additional 10% coverage), and so forth.
6 Conclusion
Our initial intuition that LLMs exhibit vulnerabilities to specific conversational patterns has been conclusively validated through PE-CoA. Vulnerability differences across architectures and patterns create distinct attack surfaces missed by current safety measures, with weaknesses persisting across model families due to shared training approaches. The key findings challenge current safety assumptions. First, robustness to one conversational pattern does not generalize to others. Targeted fine-tuning against Information patterns reduced disinformation attacks from 90% to 10%, yet less protection against others. Second, pattern combinations create combinatorial vulnerabilities, generating 50 distinct attack vectors from 10 harm categories and 5 conversation patterns rather than simple additive effects. Third, PE-CoA achieves 75-100% attack success rates across diverse architectures, demonstrating that conversational structure can bypass safety mechanisms designed for content-based threats. These discoveries carry important implications for AI safety. Defensive strategies should account for the complex interplay between helpful conversational behavior and potential misuse rather than treating all harmful requests uniformly.
Limitations
1.
Our evaluation relies on GPT-3.5-turbo as the judge model, which introduces potential bias in determining attack success. While we follow established evaluation protocols (Pavlova et al., 2025), judge model limitations may affect success rate measurements. Different judge models or human evaluation might yield different assessments of harmfulness.
2.
As LLM safety mechanisms evolve, specific pattern exploitation techniques may become less effective. Our findings characterize vulnerabilities in current models but do not predict future model robustness.
3.
Our work focuses on text-based conversational attacks. Multimodal scenarios, code-based exploits, and other attack vectors remain unexplored. Additionally, our evaluation covers 12 models, but the broader LLM ecosystem includes many architectures we did not test.
Ethical considerations
This research addresses critical security vulnerabilities in large language models through systematic analysis of conversation pattern exploitation. While our work demonstrates effective attack methodologies, the primary motivation is defensive—enabling development of targeted safety measures against structured conversational manipulation.
We acknowledge that PE-CoA’s effectiveness could enable malicious applications. However, the structured approach to vulnerability analysis provides essential insights for improving LLM safety that outweigh potential risks. The conversation patterns we identify likely exist in current attack methodologies; systematic understanding enables better defenses.
Our evaluation uses publicly available
jailbreak benchmarks designed for security research. All harmful content
is synthetic and derives from established safety evaluation datasets.
We will release our code with research-use-only licensing to support
defensive applications while preventing misuse.
This work contributes to the building of trustworthy AI systems by revealing systematic vulnerabilities through conversation pattern analysis, enabling proactive rather than reactive security measures. We recommend immediate implementation of pattern-aware detection systems, development of conversation pattern safety training, industry coordination around defensive strategy sharing, and continued research into comprehensive conversation pattern taxonomies for vulnerability assessment.

Appendix A Background
A.1 Chain of Attack Foundation
Chain of Attack (CoA) (Yang et al., 2024b) provides the foundation for our work, representing a multi-turn attack strategy that leverages semantic relevance between target objectives and model responses. Unlike approaches relying on fixed adversarial prompts, CoA dynamically adapts its attack path based on target model responses.
CoA measures semantic correlation between model responses and target objectives using SIMCSE (Gao et al., 2021). For target objective OO and model response rtr_{t} at turn tt, semantic correlation is computed as S⁡(t)=SEM⁡(O,rt)S(t)=\mathrm{SEM}(O,r_{t}) where SEM\mathrm{SEM} denotes cosine similarity between embedding vectors. Three correlation scores are measured:
•
S⁡(t)S(t): correlation with context,
•
S′​(t)S^{\prime}(t): correlation without context, and
•
S⁡(t−1)S(t-1): previous turn correlation.
Based on these scores, CoA employs four walking strategies: Next Walk (positive progression), Regen Walk (alternative prompt generation), Back Walk (backtracking), and End Walk (termination). These components interact to create dynamic attack methodology that transforms benign conversations into sequences leading to harmful content generation. Despite its effectiveness, CoA lacks structured exploration of specific conversation patterns, which PE-CoA addresses.
A.2 Detailed CoA Walking Strategies
Figure 10: Structure of CoA (Yang et al., 2024b)
The original Chain of Attack employs four distinct walking strategies based on semantic correlation metrics. Figure 10 illustrates how these components interact to create the dynamic attack methodology.
Next Walk: If semantic correlation shows positive progression S⁡(t)>S⁡(t−1)S(t)>S(t-1), the attack proceeds to the next turn. This strategy is used when current interaction appears to move toward the objective.
Regen Walk: If current prompt fails to increase correlation but shows potential (S⁡(t)<S⁡(t−1)S(t)<S(t-1) and S′​(t)>S⁡(t−1)S^{\prime}(t)>S(t-1)), the attack remains at current turn but regenerates alternative prompts.
Back Walk: If neither current prompt nor its alternative without context improves correlation (S⁡(t)<S⁡(t−1)S(t)<S(t-1) and S′​(t)<S⁡(t−1)S^{\prime}(t)<S(t-1)), the attack steps back to previous turns to explore different conversation paths.
End Walk: The attack terminates upon successfully eliciting harmful responses or reaching predefined maximum turns, ensuring bounded execution.
Appendix B Pattern Discovery
B.1 Foundations for Pattern Selection
PE-CoA’s five conversation patterns emerged from established conversation analysis research, identifying structures that might expose LLM training biases. Conversation analysis theory shows that dialogues follow predictable structural patterns through turn-taking systems and sequence organization Sacks (1992); Schegloff (2007). Speech act theory explains how utterances function as actions within conversations, particularly for requests and information seeking Austin (1962); Searle (1975). Discourse analysis provided insights into topic coherence and development within structured patterns Brown and Yule (1983); Van Dijk (1985), while social engineering research offered empirical evidence for manipulation patterns adaptable to LLM vulnerability analysis Hadnagy (2010); Cialdini (2006).
B.2 Manual Pattern Analysis and Identification
Through manual examination of successful multi-turn attacks, five recurring approaches demonstrated effectiveness across scenarios. Educational discussions progress from general concepts to specific applications, following pedagogical structures Merrill (2002); Clark (1996). Personal narratives advance from shared experiences to targeted requests through emotional connection Bruner (1991); Herman (2003). Hypothetical scenarios establish fictional frameworks before introducing problematic elements, using cognitive distance to reduce resistance Kahneman and Tversky (1982); Byrne (2005). These communication pathways establish trust and context while gradually shifting semantic boundaries.
B.3 Pattern Formalization and Vulnerability Targeting
We formalized five patterns targeting specific LLM training aspects:
1.
Technical-Educational Pattern: Frames attacks as learning processes, exploiting models’ educational helpfulness training while gradually introducing harmful elements in academic contexts.
2.
Personal Experience Pattern: Uses personal narrative frameworks to bypass safety mechanisms through empathy and emotional resonance.
3.
Hypothetical Scenario Pattern: Creates imaginary frameworks distancing requests from direct implementation, exploiting models’ ability to engage with fictional scenarios.
4.
Information Seeking Pattern: Presents attacks as neutral information gathering, exploiting models’ tendencies toward factual and comprehensive responses.
5.
Problem-Solving Pattern: Frames attacks as practical challenges, exploiting goal-oriented reasoning processes while bypassing safety filters detecting direct harmful instructions.
Each pattern was designed to target regions of the vulnerability space Πg​a​p\Pi_{gap}, representing conversational structures present in pretraining data but not adequately covered by safety training datasets.

Appendix F Prompts Used in This Work
The PE-CoA framework employs a sophisticated multi-component prompt system designed to systematically test LLM vulnerabilities through structured conversation patterns. The implementation consists of eleven distinct prompt templates organized into three functional categories, as detailed in Figures 13 through 24.
Attack Generation Components: The core “Multi-round Attack Chain Generator” (Figure 13) serves as the primary prompt template for creating progressive conversation sequences. This system incorporates semantic weight mapping (0.1 to 0.9) across conversation rounds, with each stage designed to incrementally increase relevance to target objectives. The framework includes role definitions that explicitly instruct the attacking model to bypass ethical constraints, utilizing strategies including “confusing sensitive words,” role-playing scenarios, and “creative methods without training in language models.” The “Attacker Prompt - Multi-Round Chain” (Figure 15) provides the operational template for this process, while the “Single Round Iterative Refinement System” (Figure 19) enables adaptive prompt optimization based on target model feedback.
Evaluation and Judgment Systems: Three complementary evaluation prompts assess attack effectiveness. The “Initial Chain Evaluation System” (Figure 14) provides structured assessment of multi-round prompt coherence and target correlation on a 1-10 scale. The “Judge Model Evaluation System” (Figure 16) implements binary classification (safe/harmful) with specific rating criteria, while the “Explanatory Judge System with Detailed Analysis” (Figure 18) offers comprehensive analytical feedback incorporating pattern-aware assessment capabilities and distributional performance measures.
Pattern Implementation Templates: Five specialized conversation patterns are operationalized through detailed prompt templates (Figures 20 through 24): Technical (Figure 20, laboratory/scientific contexts), Personal (Figure 21, emotional/narrative progression), Hypothetical (Figure 22, future scenario construction), Problem-Solving (Figure 23, solution-oriented methodology), and Information (Figure 24, knowledge-seeking progression). Each pattern template includes stage-specific descriptions, semantic weight specifications, and example implementations demonstrating the intended conversation flow with effectiveness scores of 9/10.
The systematic nature of these prompts advances our understanding of LLM vulnerabilities, but the explicit adversarial instructions and manipulation techniques represent a double-edged contribution to the field’s security knowledge base.

Appendix H Computational Cost and Resource Requirements
H.1 API-Based Model Evaluation
We evaluated commercial models (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-3.5-turbo, Claude-3-haiku,
Gemini-1.5-flash, Gemini-1.5-pro, Gemini-2.0-flash, Deepseek-chat) via their
respective APIs. Total API expenditure was approximately $600 USD, distributed
as follows:
Judge Model (OpenAI GPT-3.5-turbo): Approximately 60-70% of total
costs (~$400) were attributable to judge model evaluations, as
every generated response required assessment. With 300 objectives × 5 patterns
× 12 models × 4 minimum turns, this amounts to more than 72,000 judge calls.
Target Model APIs: The remaining $200 was distributed across target
model queries (Claude-3-haiku, Gemini-1.5-flash, Gemini-1.5-pro, Gemini-2.0-flash,
GPT-4o-mini, GPT-3.5-turbo, DeepSeek-chat).
Development Costs: Initial method development, including prompt
engineering, pattern validation, and preliminary experiments, required
approximately $150 in additional API costs.
H.2 Per-Experiment Cost Analysis
For a single objective evaluated with one conversational pattern over a maximum
of 4 turns, costs ranged from $0.30 to $1.20 per run, depending on:
•
Target model pricing (e.g., GPT-4o-mini vs. Claude-3-haiku)
•
Number of regeneration attempts (Regen Walk operations)
•
Response length (affecting both generation and judge evaluation costs)
•
Actual conversation length (early termination reduces costs)
•
Token generation length in responses
•
Judge model evaluation overhead
Lower costs ($0.30-0.50) typically corresponded to early-success attacks
requiring fewer iterations, while higher costs ($0.80-1.20) resulted from
attacks requiring full 20-iteration exploration with 4-turn conversations.
H.3 Local Compute for Open-Source Models
Open-source models (Llama2-7b, Llama2-13b, Llama3-8b, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3,
Vicuna-13b-v1.5-16k) were evaluated on local infrastructure using NVIDIA H100
GPUs (80GB HBM3 memory).
Inference configuration:
•
Hardware: Single NVIDIA H100 GPU per model
•
Precision: FP16 for 7B/8B models, INT8 quantization for 13B models
•
Batch size: 1 (sequential conversation turns)
•
Average inference time: 2-5 seconds per response generation
H.4 Attack Model (Vicuna-13b-v1.5-16k)

•

•
