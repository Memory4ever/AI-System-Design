Abstract
In this work, we propose a sparse transformer architecture that incorporates prior information about the underlying data distribution directly into the transformer structure of the neural network. The design of the model is motivated by a special optimal transport problem, namely the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator, which admits a closed-form solution and turns out to be a special representation of transformer architectures. Compared with classical flow-based models, the proposed approach improves the convexity properties of the optimization problem and promotes sparsity in the generated samples. Through both theoretical analysis and numerical experiments, including applications in generative modeling and Bayesian inverse problems, we demonstrate that the sparse transformer achieves higher accuracy and faster convergence to the target distribution than classical neural ODE–based methods.
1 Introduction
Modern generative models, such as neural ordinary differential equations (neural ODEs) [4], transformers [25], and diffusion models [22], have demonstrated remarkable ability to learn and generate samples from complex, high-dimensional probability distributions.
These architectures have achieved broad success in scientific computing, image processing, and data science, offering scalable frameworks for data-driven modeling.
However, training and sampling in such spaces remain expensive and highly sensitive to architectural and optimization choices.
Despite these advances, the curse of dimensionality continues to present a fundamental challenge in many real-world applications.
Fortunately, numerous problems in scientific computing exhibit intrinsic structures, such as sparsity, low-rank representations, or approximate invariances, that can be interpreted as prior information about the underlying data or operators.
Leveraging such priors within generative models offers a promising avenue to improve both computational efficiency and generalization.
A classical way to incorporate prior information, such as sparsity or piecewise regularity, is through Bayesian modeling, where the posterior combines a prior distribution encoding structural knowledge with a likelihood function derived from observations. Examples include the L1L_{1} prior in compressive sensing [2] and the total-variation prior in image reconstruction [17].
Yet embedding such priors into modern neural networks is challenging: most architectures are general-purpose approximators, and sampling from complex priors (e.g., TV) is computationally prohibitive.
To address these challenges, we embed prior information into the neural architecture through the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator (RWPO).
Classical transformer and diffusion models are largely data-driven, learning attention or score functions from data with limited connection to underlying transport dynamics.
In contrast, our approach originates from the backward regularized Wasserstein proximal (BRWP) sampling algorithm [8], developed to stabilize deterministic probability flows via alternating transport and semi-implicit proximal steps.
We scale up this sampling framework by embedding each BRWP update into a network layer: the transport step is parameterized by a learnable drift potential, and the proximal step becomes a transformer-type interaction derived from the RWPO kernel.
To formalize this idea, consider the problem of approximating a target distribution ρ∗∈ℙ2​(ℝd)\rho^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}), representing a probability density with finite second moment, given training data drawn from ρ∗\rho^{*}. For a time horizon T>0T>0, we define the variational problem
ρT=argminρTminϕ~t{DKL(ρ∗∥ρT)+12∫0T∫∥∇ϕ~t∥22ρtdxdt},\rho_{T}=\arg\min_{\rho_{T}}\min_{\tilde{\phi}_{t}}\Bigg\{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho^{*}\|\rho_{T})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\,\rho_{t}\,dx\,dt\Bigg\}\,,
(1)
where DKL(ρ∗∥ρT)\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho^{*}\|\rho_{T}) denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the data distribution ρ∗\rho^{*} and the model’s terminal distribution ρT\rho_{T}.
The inner minimization describes the most energy-efficient evolution of densities connecting ρ0\rho_{0} and ρT\rho_{T} in the sense of Wasserstein-2 distance.
The corresponding constrained dynamical system yields the Fokker–Planck equation, whose particle-level evolution is given by
dXt=∇ϕ~t(Xt)−β−1∇logρt(Xt),dX_{t}=\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t}(X_{t})-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{t}(X_{t})\,,
(2)
where ϕ~t:ℝd→ℝ\tilde{\phi}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} denotes the trainable drift potential, Xt∼ρtX_{t}\sim\rho_{t}, ρt​(x)=ρ⁡(t,x)\rho_{t}(x)=\rho(t,x) for t∈[0,T]t\in[0,T], and β>0\beta>0 controls the diffusion strength.
In this formulation, the particle dynamics generalize those of a neural ODE [4]. The drift term ∇ϕ~t\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t} serves as the neural network–parameterized drift potential, while the additional score term −β−1∇logρt-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{t} introduces a diffusion-driven regularization absent in classical neural ODEs. This score-based correction couples the flow with the Fokker–Planck evolution of the underlying density, acting as a diffusion-induced force that repels particles from high-density regions, prevents collapse, and balances the attractive drift ∇ϕ~t\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t} toward the target distribution.
In high-dimensional settings, estimating the score function∇log⁡ρt\nabla\log\rho_{t} directly is computationally expensive and often unstable.
The RWPO framework circumvents this difficulty by providing a first-order approximation of the density evolution in (2), while retaining a closed-form representation that approximates the score function.
More concretely, assuming ϕ~t=ϕt−ψ\tilde{\phi}_{t}=\phi_{t}-\psi, we take ϕt\phi_{t} to be represented by a residual neural network and
ψ⁡(x)=λ​‖x‖1\psi(x)=\lambda\|x\|_{1}
to encode prior information on data sparsity in a time-independent potential.
Denote the tokens at time tkt_{k} as Xk={xjk}j=1NX^{k}=\{x_{j}^{k}\}_{j=1}^{N}.
When the step size hh is small, by a first-order approximation of the RWPO kernel, the tokens (samples) evolve according to
{xjk+1/2=xjk+h∇ϕk(xjk),xjk+1=xjk+1/2+12​[Sλ​h​(xjk+1/2)−∑ℓ=1Nsoftmax⁡(U⁡(xjk+1/2,Xk+1/2))ℓ​xℓk+1/2],\begin{cases}x_{j}^{k+1/2}=x_{j}^{k}+h\nabla\phi_{k}(x_{j}^{k})\,,\\

(3)
where ϕk=ϕtk\phi_{k}=\phi_{t_{k}}.
Here
Sλ​h​(x)=ReLU⁡(|x|−λ​h)withReLU⁡(z)=max⁡{0,z}S_{\lambda h}(x)=\operatorname{ReLU}(|x|-\lambda h)\quad\text{with}\quad\operatorname{ReLU}(z)=\max\{0,z\}

For comparison, recall that a standard transformer block (cf. [3, 6, 19]) with value, query, and key matrices V∈ℝd×dV\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} and Q,K∈ℝm×dQ,K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d} can be written in residual form as

In contrast, the proposed sparse transformer layer replaces the linear dot-product kernel with a nonlinear RWPO-based kernel where Q​x⋅K​yQx\cdot Ky is replaced by U⁡(x,y)U(x,y).
This substitution preserves the attention-style weighted averaging but introduces a potential-driven interaction derived from optimal-transport dynamics.
The term Sλ​hS_{\lambda h} enforces sparsity, while the diffusion term enhances stability.
Consequently, the layer acts as a physics-motivated analogue of transformer attention, embedding score-based diffusion into the network.
Classical approaches to generative modeling often rely on approximating score functions.
Score matching [10] and its denoising variant [26] laid the foundation for score-based generative models and diffusion methods, which learn time-dependent score functions and generate samples via reverse-time dynamics [21, 22].
Complementary to these stochastic formulations, continuous-time viewpoints interpret learning and generation through deterministic transport flows.
Neural ODEs [4] view residual networks as discretizations of continuous transformations between distributions, while Langevin-type dynamics and their discretizations provide theoretically grounded sampling schemes with convergence guarantees under log-concavity assumptions [16, 5].
These frameworks emphasize two central ingredients in modern generative modeling: score estimation and space-time discretizations of transport dynamics.
Our work departs from score-estimation methods and instead builds on regularized Wasserstein gradient flows.
The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) scheme [11] characterizes diffusion as an entropy gradient flow in Wasserstein space, linking sampling, dissipation, and variational regularization.
This viewpoint motivates Wasserstein proximal operators as stable, structure-preserving updates [1, 20] and has recently inspired OT-based attention mechanisms [19, 6].
From a Bayesian perspective, these proximal operators naturally encode priors via penalties such as the Lasso [24] or total variation [17].
Building on these ideas, our framework embeds the RWPO directly into the network, allowing priors to shape token evolution through each layer.
It unifies three principles:
(i) optimal-transport geometry via Wasserstein proximal steps approximating Fokker–Planck evolution;
(ii) Bayesian priors through explicit proximal penalties promoting sparsity; and
(iii) transformer-type interaction arising from the softmax normalization of the RWPO kernel.
The resulting sparse-transformer layer acts as a single proximal step in Wasserstein space with a closed-form attention update that preserves prior-induced structure.

2 Proposed network structure and PDE formulation
2.1 Probability flow and particle updates
For generative modeling, the central task is to generate new samples from a target distribution ρ∗\rho^{*}, typically parameterized through a neural network.
We begin by recalling the KL divergence between two distributions,
DKL(ρ∥ρ∗)=∫ρlogρρ∗dx.\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\rho^{*})=\int\rho\log\tfrac{\rho}{\rho^{*}}\,dx\,.
The Wasserstein gradient flow of the KL divergence yields the Fokker–Planck equation
∂tρ+∇⋅(ρ∇ϕ~)=β−1Δρ,\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\nabla\tilde{\phi})=\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho\,,
(5)
where ϕ~=−log⁡ρ∗\tilde{\phi}=-\log\rho^{*}.
At the particle level, this corresponds to the probability flow
dXt=∇ϕ~(Xt)−β−1∇logρt(Xt),dX_{t}=\nabla\tilde{\phi}(X_{t})-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{t}(X_{t})\,,
(6)
with Xt∼ρtX_{t}\!\sim\!\rho_{t}.
Under standard smoothness and log-concavity assumptions on ρ∗\rho^{*}, the dynamics (6) converge exponentially fast to ρ∗\rho^{*} [16].
Equation (6) admits two complementary interpretations.
In sampling, it represents forward stochastic dynamics that asymptotically draw samples from ρ∗\rho^{*} when the drift potential is known.
For generative modeling, the same drift potential can be parameterized by a neural network, transforming a simple base distribution ρ0\rho_{0} into ρ∗\rho^{*} through a sequence of deterministic updates.
This viewpoint connects probability flows with neural network architectures: the drift potential ϕt\phi_{t} acts as a trainable function governing density evolution, and each discretization step corresponds to a layer of the neural network.
In practice, two difficulties arise when discretizing (6):
(i) computing or approximating ∇log⁡ρt\nabla\log\rho_{t} is expensive and often unstable, and
(ii) the dynamics are highly sensitive to the choice of step size.
To address these challenges, [8] introduced the deterministic BRWP-splitting scheme for sampling from distributions of the form
ρ∗​(x)=1Z​exp⁡(ϕ⁡(x)−ψ⁡(x))=1Z​exp⁡(−ϕ~​(x)),\rho^{*}(x)=\tfrac{1}{Z}\exp(\phi(x)-\psi(x))=\tfrac{1}{Z}\exp(-\tilde{\phi}(x))\,,
where ψ\psi encodes prior regularization and Z=∫exp⁡(−ϕ~​(x))​𝑑x<∞Z=\int\exp(-\tilde{\phi}(x))dx<\infty is a normalizing constant.
The corresponding regularized particle dynamic is
dXt=∇ϕt(Xt)−∇ψ(Xt)−β−1∇logρt(Xt).dX_{t}=\nabla\phi_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\psi(X_{t})-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{t}(X_{t})\,.
(7)
A forward-Euler discretization with operator splitting [8] yields the following update
{xjk+1/2=xjk+h∇ϕk(xjk),xjk+1=xjk+1/2−h∇ψ(xjk+1/2)−hβ−1∇logρk+1/2(xjk+1/2),\begin{cases}x_{j}^{k+1/2}=x_{j}^{k}+h\nabla\phi_{k}(x_{j}^{k})\,,\\[3.0pt]
x_{j}^{k+1}=x_{j}^{k+1/2}-h\nabla\psi(x_{j}^{k+1/2})-h\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{k+1/2}(x_{j}^{k+1/2})\,,\end{cases}
(8)
where Xk={xjk}j=1NX^{k}=\{x_{j}^{k}\}_{j=1}^{N} denotes the ensemble of particles at time step kk.
The first step applies the drift potential induced by ϕk\phi_{k}, and the second introduces the prior and diffusion corrections.
Direct evaluation of the score function ∇log⁡ρt\nabla\log\rho_{t} remains challenging in high dimensions.
To stabilize the dynamics and avoid explicit score estimation, we replace the second step in (8) with a semi-implicit update based on the RWPO.
The resulting scheme, whose derivation is detailed below, evolves as
{xjk+1/2=xjk+h∇ϕ(xjk),xjk+1=xjk+1/2+12​[proxψh​(xjk+1/2)−∑ℓ=1Nsoftmax⁡(U⁡(xjk+1/2,Xk+1/2))ℓ​xℓk+1/2],\begin{cases}x_{j}^{k+1/2}=x_{j}^{k}+h\nabla\phi(x_{j}^{k})\,,\\[3.0pt]
x_{j}^{k+1}=x_{j}^{k+1/2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\,\text{prox}_{\psi}^{h}(x_{j}^{k+1/2})-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\operatorname{softmax}\!\big(U(x_{j}^{k+1/2},X^{k+1/2})\big)_{\ell}\,x_{\ell}^{k+1/2}\right]\!,\end{cases}
(9)
where proxψh\text{prox}_{\psi}^{h} is the proximal operator of ψ\psi,
proxψh​(x):=arg​miny⁡{ψ⁡(y)+‖x−y‖222​h},U⁡(x,y):=−β2​(‖x−y‖22−‖proxψh​(x)−y‖222​h−ψ⁡(proxψh​(y))).\text{prox}_{\psi}^{h}(x):=\argmin_{y}\Big\{\psi(y)+\tfrac{\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}}{2h}\Big\},\quad U(x,y):=-\tfrac{\beta}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}-\|\text{prox}_{\psi}^{h}(x)-y\|_{2}^{2}}{2h}-\psi(\text{prox}_{\psi}^{h}(y))\right)\!.
This formulation combines closed-form proximal updates with a semi-implicit treatment of the score term, allowing large, stable time steps without explicitly computing ∇log⁡ρt\nabla\log\rho_{t}.
The interaction kernel UU arises from a Laplace approximation of the RWPO operator, as described in the next subsection.
In the original BRWP scheme, the flow alternates between a transport step guided by the drift potential and a proximal step that enforces prior regularization, yielding a stable deterministic sampling algorithm.
Here, we reinterpret each BRWP update as a neural network layer: the transport step becomes a learnable map ∇ϕk\nabla\phi_{k} parameterized by a ResNet, while the proximal step is realized as a transformer-type interaction derived from the RWPO kernel.
This construction converts the sampling algorithm into a deep, scalable architecture for generative modeling.
To emphasize the connection with attention mechanisms, the particles xjkx_{j}^{k} are referred to as tokens. The first update in (9) acts as a learnable transport map, and the second enforces prior-induced regularization with diffusion.
The overall network structure is illustrated in Fig. 1, showing how the learned drift potential ϕ\phi is coupled with an RWPO-based sparse-transformer layer that enforces sparsity through proximal updates.

2.2 Regularized Wasserstein proximal operator layer
We now detail the derivation of the system (9) and update rule underlying Fig. 1.
The main numerical difficulty in (8) lies in the unstable approximation of the score function ∇log⁡ρt\nabla\log\rho_{t}.
To mitigate this, we adopt a semi-implicit strategy by evaluating the score at the next time step ρk+1\rho_{k+1}, replacing the second update in (8) with
xjk+1=xjk+1/2−h∇ψ(xjk+1/2)−hβ−1∇logρk+1(xjk+1/2).x_{j}^{k+1}=x_{j}^{k+1/2}-h\nabla\psi(x_{j}^{k+1/2})-h\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{k+1}(x_{j}^{k+1/2})\,.
(10)
Formally, ρk+1\rho_{k+1} can be characterized through the JKO scheme, which requires solving a high-dimensional variational problem.
To obtain a tractable approximation, we instead employ the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator [14], defined as
𝒦ψh​(ρk):=arg​minρh⁡minv,ρ​∫ψ⁡(x)​ρh​(x)​dx+12​∫0h∫‖v⁡(x,t)‖22​ρ​(x,t)​dx​dt,\mathcal{K}^{h}_{\psi}(\rho_{k}):=\argmin_{\rho_{h}}\,\min_{v,\rho}\int\psi(x)\,\rho_{h}(x)\,dx+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{h}\!\!\int\|v(x,t)\|_{2}^{2}\rho(x,t)\,dx\,dt,
(11)
subject to the Fokker–Planck equation (5) with ∇ϕ~\nabla\tilde{\phi} replaced by vv, initial condition ρ⁡(x,0)=ρk​(x)\rho(x,0)=\rho_{k}(x), and terminal condition ρh​(x)=ρ⁡(x,h)\rho_{h}(x)=\rho(x,h).
We assume the minimization admits a solution under standard regularity conditions on ρk\rho_{k} and ψ\psi.
From the analysis in [7], the RWPO provides a second-order accurate approximation of the JKO update:
𝒦ψh​(ρk+1/2)=ρk+1+𝒪⁡(h2).\mathcal{K}^{h}_{\psi}(\rho_{k+1/2})=\rho_{k+1}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\,.
This justifies replacing ρk+1\rho_{k+1} in (10) with its RWPO approximation, leading to a stable and implementable update.
When ψ\psi is nonsmooth (e.g., ψ⁡(x)=λ​‖x‖1\psi(x)=\lambda\|x\|_{1}), we denote by ∂ψ\partial\psi its subdifferential.
The gradient step then becomes the proximal relation
x−proxψh​(x)h∈∂ψ⁡(x),\frac{x-\text{prox}^{h}_{\psi}(x)}{h}\in\partial\psi(x)\,,
which ensures consistency with the nonsmooth regularization.
Combining the drift and proximal corrections yields the complete token updates
{xjk+1/2=xjk+h∇ϕk(xjk),xjk+1=proxψh(xjk+1/2)−hβ−1∇log𝒦hψρk+1/2(xjk+1/2),\begin{cases}x_{j}^{k+1/2}=x_{j}^{k}+h\nabla\phi_{k}(x_{j}^{k})\,,\\[3.0pt]
x_{j}^{k+1}=\text{prox}_{\psi}^{h}(x_{j}^{k+1/2})-h\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\mathcal{K}^{h}_{\psi}\rho_{k+1/2}(x_{j}^{k+1/2})\,,\end{cases}
(12)
which defines a semi-implicit update integrating prior regularization and the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator.
2.3 Sparse transformer with L1L_{1} prior
We now specialize the RWPO layer to the case where the target distribution exhibits sparsity, a common scenario in Bayesian inverse problems and image reconstruction.
A classical choice of sparsity-promoting prior is the L1L_{1} potential ψ⁡(x)=λ​‖x‖1.\psi(x)=\lambda\|x\|_{1}.
For this choice, the proximal operator takes the soft-thresholding form
Sλ​h(xi)=proxλ∥⋅∥1h(x)i={xi−λ​hif ​xi>λ​h,0if ​|xi|≤λ​h,xi+λ​hif ​xi<−λ​h,=ReLU(|xi|−λh).S_{\lambda h}(x_{i})=\text{prox}_{\lambda\|\cdot\|_{1}}^{h}(x)_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}-\lambda h&\text{if }x_{i}>\lambda h,\\
0&\text{if }|x_{i}|\leq\lambda h,\\
x_{i}+\lambda h&\text{if }x_{i}<-\lambda h\,,\end{cases}=\mathrm{ReLU}(|x_{i}|-\lambda h)\,.

∎
If ψ\psi is C2C^{2} with a non-degenerate minimizer, the approximation error in (14) is 𝒪⁡(h)\mathcal{O}(h) by the standard Laplace method, with the leading correction determined by the local Hessian of ψ\psi.
When ψ\psi is only Lipschitz—as in the L1L_{1} case—the limit in (14) still holds because the score function can be expressed as ∇log𝒦ψhρ=(∇𝒦ψhρ)/(𝒦ψhρ)\nabla\log\mathcal{K}_{\psi}^{h}\rho=(\nabla\mathcal{K}_{\psi}^{h}\rho)/(\mathcal{K}_{\psi}^{h}\rho), where normalization constants cancel without requiring smoothness.
The closed-form RWPO update (15) thus defines the sparse-transformer layer, in which the RWPO replaces the standard dot-product attention with an optimal-transport kernel as discussed in (4), embedding structural priors directly into the network. Furthermore, because the RWPO update admits closed-form proximal and softmax evaluations, the computational cost per layer is comparable to standard transformer layer, ensuring scalability to large token sets.
2.4 Learning objective and training procedure
We now make precise the training objective used to learn the unknown drift potential ϕ\phi with prior ψ\psi. The loss function we propose combines three components:
𝒥(ϕ):=DKL(ρ∗∥ρT)+12∫0T∫∥∇ϕ~(x,t)∥22ρ(x,t)dxdt+cR(T),\mathcal{J}(\phi):=\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho^{*}\|\rho_{T})\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\int\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}(x,t)\|_{2}^{2}\,\rho(x,t)\,dx\,dt\;+\;c\,R(T)\,,
(16)
where
•
ρT\rho_{T} denotes the terminal density produced by the dynamics;
•
c≥0c\geq 0 is a weight; and R⁡(T)R(T) is an HJB residual regularizer (defined below) that penalizes deviations from the optimality conditions associated with the variational formulation.
For the continuous dynamics, the training objective can be formulated as
minρ𝒥(ϕ)subject todXt=∇ϕt(Xt)−∇ϕ~(Xt)−β−1∇logρt(Xt),\min_{\rho}\,\mathcal{J}(\phi)\quad\text{subject to}\quad dX_{t}=\nabla{\phi}_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla{\tilde{\phi}}(X_{t})-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{t}(X_{t})\,,
where Xt∼ρtX_{t}\sim\rho_{t} and ∇ϕ~\nabla\tilde{\phi} is approximated by a proximal operator for non-smooth prior drift potential.
We remark that, relative to the variational formulation (1), the additional HJB regularizer vanishes at the optimal solution and thus primarily serves to accelerate convergence without modifying the minimizer.
In the following, we explain the role of each term in (16) and present the corresponding discrete Monte Carlo estimators used during training.
(I) The KL divergence and its evaluation along trajectories.
The KL divergence can be expressed as
DKL(ρ∗∥ρT)=∫ρ∗(x)logρ∗​(x)ρT​(x)dx=∫ρ∗(x)logρ∗(x)dx−∫ρ∗(x)logρT(x)dx.\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho^{*}\|\rho_{T})=\int\rho^{*}(x)\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x)}{\rho_{T}(x)}\,dx=\int\rho^{*}(x)\log\rho^{*}(x)\,dx-\int\rho^{*}(x)\log\rho_{T}(x)\,dx\,.
Since the first term is independent of the model, minimizing the KL divergence is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood term
∫ρ∗​(x)​log⁡ρT​(x)​𝑑x\int\rho^{*}(x)\log\rho_{T}(x)\,dx.
For the simplicity of notation, let
v(x,t)=∇ϕ(x,t)−∇ψ(x)−β−1∇logρ(x,t).v(x,t)=\nabla\phi(x,t)-\nabla\psi(x)-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho(x,t)\,.
To estimate the expectation with respect to ρ∗\rho^{*}, we draw training samples at the terminal time,
xjM∼ρ∗,j=1,…,N,x_{j}^{M}\sim\rho^{*},\qquad j=1,\dots,N\,,
and evaluate log⁡ρT\log\rho_{T} at these points. This requires recovering the full trajectory {xjk}k=0M\{x_{j}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{M}. Given xjMx_{j}^{M} at time TT, we integrate the backward ODE

For completeness, we recall the log-density evolution along forward trajectories [4].

Let ρ\rho satisfy the transport equation

Hence, the log-likelihood term can be approximated by a Riemann sum with step size h=T/Mh=T/M,
∫ρ∗​(x)​log⁡ρT​(x)​𝑑x\displaystyle\int\rho^{*}(x)\log\rho_{T}(x)\,dx
≈1N​∑j=1Nlog⁡ρT​(xjM)\displaystyle\;\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\rho_{T}(x_{j}^{M})

where xjM∼ρ∗x_{j}^{M}\sim\rho^{*}, {xjk}k=0M\{x_{j}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{M} are obtained by backward integration of the dynamics (e.g., via the splitting scheme (8)), and ρ0\rho_{0} is a known simple distribution, such as a Gaussian distribution. (II) The transport regularizer.
The second term in (16),
12​∫0T∫‖∇ϕ~​(x,t)‖22​ρ​(x,t)​𝑑x​𝑑t,\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\int\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}(x,t)\|_{2}^{2}\,\rho(x,t)\,dx\,dt\,,
penalizes the kinetic energy of the transportation vector field, which promotes smooth, energy-efficient token transports.
For ϕk​(x)=ϕ⁡(x,tk)\phi_{k}(x)=\phi(x,t_{k}), the time-space integral is approximated using a standard Monte Carlo Riemann-sum scheme:
12​∫0T∫‖∇ϕ~‖22​ρ​𝑑x​𝑑t≈h2​N​∑k=0M−1∑j=1N∥∇ϕ~k​(xjk)∥22.\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\int\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}\rho\,dx\,dt\;\approx\;\frac{h}{2N}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big\lVert\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x_{j}^{k})\big\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.
(19)
(III) The HJB regularizer.
The third term, R⁡(T)R(T), enforces consistency with the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation [15], which arises from the optimality condition for ρT\rho_{T} in (1) (see (33) in Section 3.2 for the derivation). The HJB residual at state (x,t)(x,t) is defined by

R⁡(T):=∫0T∫|ℛHJB​(x,t)|2​ρ​(x,t)​𝑑x​𝑑t,R(T)\;:=\;\int_{0}^{T}\!\int\big|\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{HJB}}(x,t)\big|^{2}\rho(x,t)\,dx\,dt\,,
(20)
which we discretize analogously to the previous terms
R⁡(T)≈hN​∑k=0M−1∑j=1N|∂tϕk​(xjk)+12​∥∇ϕk​(xjk)−∇ψ​(xjk)∥22+β−1​(Δ​ϕk​(xjk)−Δ​ψ​(xjk))|2,R(T)\;\approx\;\frac{h}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\partial_{t}\phi_{k}(x^{k}_{j})+\tfrac{1}{2}\big\lVert\nabla\phi_{k}(x^{k}_{j})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}_{j})\big\rVert_{2}^{2}+\beta^{-1}\big(\Delta\phi_{k}(x^{k}_{j})-\Delta\psi(x^{k}_{j})\big)\big|^{2}\,,
(21)
where Δ​ψ\Delta\psi is evaluated by the smooth approximation of ψ\psi, e.g., Huber function.
Remarks.
•
The spatial differential operators required for the transport and HJB regularizers are already computed when evaluating the KL divergence, so including these regularizers incurs no additional differentiation cost.
•
The objective (16) combines three components: the KL divergence enforces likelihood matching, the transport regularizer encourages energy-efficient mapping consistent with optimal transport, and the HJB regularizer ensures dynamic consistency with the underlying variational formulation.

3 Convergence and stability of flow with sparse transformer
In this section, we provide a rigorous analysis of why the sparse transformer proposed in Algorithm 1 achieves faster and more robust convergence toward sparse target distributions. Our analysis proceeds along two complementary directions. First, in Section 3.1, we study how the additional sparse prior directly accelerates long-time convergence of the dynamics, as measured by the KL divergence. Second, in Section 3.2, we analyze the variational optimization problem (1) and reveal the stabilizing role of viscosity. The proof of results in this section can be found in the Appendix.
3.1 Convergence with the sparse prior
We examine the effect of the sparse prior ψλ​(x)=λ​‖x‖1\psi^{\lambda}(x)=\lambda\|x\|_{1}
on the evolution of
∂tρ=∇⋅(ρ⁡(−∇ϕ+∇ψλ))+β−1​Δ​ρ.\partial_{t}\rho\;=\;\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\rho\,(-\nabla\phi+\nabla\psi^{\lambda})\big)\;+\;\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho\,.
(25)
The analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we study the decay of the KL divergence to show that the sparse prior accelerates convergence to the target. Second, we investigate the evolution of the second moment to quantify distributional contraction; larger values of λ\lambda enhance both effects.
We begin with KL decay under an exact drift potential. Suppose ϕ\phi is chosen so that ρ∗​(x)∝eβ​ϕ​(x)\rho^{*}(x)\propto e^{\beta\phi(x)}. Then the forward KL dissipation satisfies
dd​tDKL(ρt∥ρ∗)=−β−1∫∥∇logρtρ∗∥2ρtdx−λ∫∇logρtρ∗⋅sign(x)ρtdx,\frac{d}{dt}\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})=-\beta^{-1}\int\big\|\nabla\log\tfrac{\rho_{t}}{\rho^{*}}\big\|^{2}\rho_{t}\,dx\;-\;\lambda\int\nabla\log\tfrac{\rho_{t}}{\rho^{*}}\cdot\operatorname{sign}(x)\,\rho_{t}\,dx\,,
(26)
where sign⁡(⋅)\operatorname{sign}(\cdot) denotes any measurable selection of the L1L_{1} subgradient and is justified via smooth approximations. Thus the L1L_{1} prior contributes the nonnegative term
𝒟⁡(ρt):=∫∇log⁡ρtρ∗⋅sign⁡(x)​ρt​𝑑x,\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})\;:=\;\int\nabla\log\tfrac{\rho_{t}}{\rho^{*}}\cdot\operatorname{sign}(x)\,\rho_{t}\,dx\,,
which accelerates convergence whenever 𝒟⁡(ρt)≥0\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})\geq 0.
In the following, we assume the following lower bound, referred to as directional consistency:
𝒟(ρt)≥γI(ρt∥ρ∗),∀t≥0, where I(ρt∥ρ∗):=∫∥∇log(ρt/ρ∗)∥22ρtdx,\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})\;\geq\;\gamma\sqrt{\,I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})\,}\,,\quad\forall t\geq 0\,,\quad\text{ where }\qquad I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*}):=\int\|\nabla\log(\rho_{t}/\rho^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\rho_{t}\,dx\,,
(27)
for some γ≥0\gamma\geq 0 and I(ρt∥ρ∗)I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*}) is the relative Fisher information. The property (27) holds for several common parametric families (e.g., Laplace and Gaussian families), as illustrated below.
•
Laplace distributions. For ρ∗​(x)=b2​e−b​|x|\rho^{*}(x)=\tfrac{b}{2}e^{-b|x|} and ρt​(x)=a2​e−a​|x|\rho_{t}(x)=\tfrac{a}{2}e^{-a|x|} with a,b>0a,b>0,
𝒟(ρt)=b−a,I(ρt∥ρ∗)=(a−b)2,\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})=b-a\,,\qquad I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})=(a-b)^{2}\,,
hence 𝒟⁡(ρt)=sign⁡(b−a)​I(ρt∥ρ∗)\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})=\operatorname{sign}(b-a)\sqrt{I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})} and (27) holds with γ=1\gamma=1 exactly when b≥ab\geq a.
•
Gaussian distributions. For ρ∗=𝒩⁡(0,σ2)\rho^{*}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}) and ρt=𝒩⁡(0,τ2)\rho_{t}=\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}), writing c:=1σ2−1τ2c:=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{\tau^{2}} gives
𝒟(ρt)=2πcτ,I(ρt∥ρ∗)=c2τ2,\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,c\,\tau\,,\qquad I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})=c^{2}\tau^{2},
so 𝒟⁡(ρt)=2π​sign⁡(c)​I(ρt∥ρ∗)\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\operatorname{sign}(c)\sqrt{I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})} and (27) holds with γ=2/π\gamma=\sqrt{2/\pi} whenever τ2≥σ2\tau^{2}\geq\sigma^{2}.
Proposition 4.
Assume ρ∗\rho^{*} satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant CLS>0C_{\mathrm{LS}}>0, and suppose (27) holds with some γ≥0\gamma\geq 0. Let
a:=2CLS,y(t):=DKL(ρt∥ρ∗).a:=\tfrac{2}{C_{\mathrm{LS}}}\,,\qquad y(t):=\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})\,.
Then, for all t≥0t\geq 0 and denoting (x)+=max⁡{x,0}(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}, we have
y⁡(t)≤((y⁡(0)+λ​γa)​e−a2​t−λ​γa)+2.y(t)\;\leq\;\Bigg(\Big(\sqrt{y(0)}+\tfrac{\lambda\gamma}{\sqrt{a}}\Big)e^{-\tfrac{a}{2}t}-\tfrac{\lambda\gamma}{\sqrt{a}}\Bigg)_{+}^{2}\,.
(28)
To summarize, the additional term −λγ/a-\lambda\gamma/\sqrt{a} in (28) introduces an extra linear damping effect on y⁡(t)\sqrt{y(t)}, thereby accelerating the exponential decay of the KL divergence.
Larger values of λ\lambda correspond to stronger sparse priors, which in turn yield faster contraction of the dynamics toward the target distribution ρ∗\rho^{*}.
When λ=0\lambda=0, the estimate (28) reduces to the classical exponential decay rate implied by the log-Sobolev inequality without prior regularization.
Below, we give more examples of when the directional consistency condition (27) holds.
1.
Separable / product families. If ρ∗\rho^{*} and ρt\rho_{t} are products of 1D laws and directional consistency holds in each coordinate with constants κj\kappa_{j}, then
𝒟⁡(ρt)≥(minj⁡κj)​I(ρt∥ρ∗)=γ​I(ρt∥ρ∗).\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})\geq(\min_{j}\kappa_{j})\sqrt{I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})}=\gamma\sqrt{I(\rho_{t}\|\rho^{*})}\,.
2.
General principle. Directional consistency occurs when the prior drift ∇ψ\nabla\psi and the score function ∇log⁡(ρt/ρ∗)\nabla\log(\rho_{t}/\rho^{*}) point roughly in the same correcting direction. If ρt\rho_{t} is more spread out than ρ∗\rho^{*}, both vectors point inward, yielding 𝒟⁡(ρt)>0.\mathcal{D}(\rho_{t})>0\,. However, misalignment can lead to negative correlation, which breaks the condition (27).
These examples show that directional consistency naturally holds when the target distribution is concentrated. Increasing λ\lambda strengthens the linear drift potential in the ODE for y⁡(t)\sqrt{y(t)}, which accelerates convergence.
Beyond convergence in KL divergence, next, we show that the same L1L_{1} prior introduces additional mass contraction, which we quantify via the second moment.
Proposition 5.
Let ρ0∈𝒫2​(ℝd)\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) and denote ρtλ\rho_{t}^{\lambda} as the solution to (25) with ψ⁡(x)=λ​‖x‖1\psi(x)=\lambda\|x\|_{1}.

(29)
In particular, the first term implies that a larger λ\lambda accelerates the decay of the second moment.
(ii)

(30)
Remarks.
The L1L_{1} term serves as an additional damping strength in the dynamics.
The first term on the right-hand side of (29),

acts as a dissipative force that contracts trajectories toward the origin, thereby reducing the spread of ρtλ\rho_{t}^{\lambda} and accelerating the decay of its second moment.
Larger values of λ\lambda amplify this contraction.
Together with the KL dissipation analysis, this demonstrates that the sparse L1L_{1} prior not only accelerates convergence in a distributional sense but also enforces a tighter concentration of mass.
3.2 Stability of the optimization problem
We now examine the variational well-posedness of the learning problem (1) and its associated token dynamics (2).
In particular, we show that the viscosity term arising from the score function prevents gradient blow-up in the backward HJB equation, ensuring the stability of both the dynamics and the variational formulation.
To reformulate (1) as an unconstrained problem, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier Φ\Phi. The constrained formulation then becomes
arg​minρT⁡minΦ,ϕ~,ρ\displaystyle\argmin_{\rho_{T}}\;\min_{\Phi,\tilde{\phi},\rho}\;
DKL(ρ∗∥ρT)+12∫0T∫∥∇ϕ~∥22ρdxdt\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho^{*}\|\rho_{T})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\int\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}\,\rho\,dx\,dt
(31)
+∫0T∫Φ[∂tρ+∇⋅(ρ∇ϕ~)−β−1Δρ]dxdt.\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\!\int\Phi\Bigl[\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\tilde{\phi})-\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho\Bigr]dx\,dt\,.
To connect the PDE dynamics with the training objective, we derive the first-order optimality conditions of (31), which yield a coupled system
{12​‖∇ϕ~‖22−∂tΦ−∇Φ⋅∇ϕ~−β−1​Δ​Φ=0,∂tρ+∇⋅(ρ∇ϕ~)−β−1Δρ=0,ρ⁡(∇ϕ~−∇Φ)=0,ΦT=ρ∗ρT,\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}-\partial_{t}\Phi-\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla\tilde{\phi}-\beta^{-1}\Delta\Phi=0\,,\\

(32)
where the derivation can be found in the appendix.

{∂tϕ~+12​‖∇ϕ~‖22+β−1​Δ​ϕ~=0,∂tρ+∇⋅(ρ∇ϕ~)−β−1Δρ=0,ϕ~T=ρ∗ρT.\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{\phi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta^{-1}\Delta\tilde{\phi}=0\,,\\

\tilde{\phi}_{T}=\tfrac{\rho^{*}}{\rho_{T}}\,.\end{cases}
(33)
Remark (Viscosity regularization).
The coupled system (33) consists of a backward HJB equation for ϕ~\tilde{\phi} with terminal condition ϕ~T\tilde{\phi}_{T}, and a forward Fokker–Planck equation for ρ\rho with initial condition ρ0\rho_{0}.
The Laplacian (score) term in the HJB equation acts as a viscosity term that prevents gradient blow-up.
In the inviscid case β−1=0\beta^{-1}=0, the equation
∂tϕ~+12​‖∇ϕ~‖22=0\partial_{t}\tilde{\phi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}=0
generically develops gradient singularities in finite backward time, so the KKT system (32) is well-defined only up to the first shock time.
In contrast, when β−1>0\beta^{-1}>0, the viscous HJB equation
∂tϕ~+12​‖∇ϕ~‖22+β−1​Δ​ϕ~=0\partial_{t}\tilde{\phi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta^{-1}\Delta\tilde{\phi}=0
admits a unique smooth solution ϕ~∈C∞​([0,T]×Ω)\tilde{\phi}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\Omega) for all finite TT, provided the terminal data uT=exp⁡(β2​ϕ~T)u_{T}=\exp(\tfrac{\beta}{2}\tilde{\phi}_{T}) are smooth and strictly positive, ensuring bounded gradients and no blow-up.
Example (1D explicit blow-up).
In one dimension, setting v=∂xΦv=\partial_{x}\Phi reduces the inviscid HJB equation to Burgers’ equation ∂tv+v​∂xv=0\partial_{t}v+v\,\partial_{x}v=0.
For quadratic terminal data ΦT​(x)=−a2​x2\Phi_{T}(x)=-\tfrac{a}{2}x^{2} with a>0a>0, the solution
v⁡(t,x)=−a​x1−a⁡(T−t)forT−1a<t≤Tv(t,x)=-\frac{a\,x}{1-a\,(T-t)}\quad\text{for}\quad T-\tfrac{1}{a}<t\leq T
blows up as t↓T−1/at\downarrow T-1/a.
Thus, the inviscid KKT system is well-posed only up to this first shock time, while the viscous case avoids blow-up due to the smoothing effect of the heat operator.
This highlights the stabilizing role of the score function β−1∇logρt\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{t} in the token evolution (2).

4.2 Bayesian inverse problems
We now apply the proposed flow model to solve Bayesian inverse problems [23].
Let xx denote the parameter of interest and yy the corresponding measurement, modeled as
y=F⁡(x)+ϵ,ϵ∼𝒩⁡(0,σ2),y=F(x)+\epsilon\,,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\,,
where FF is a known forward operator. The objective is to approximate the posterior distribution
p⁡(x∣y)∝p⁡(y∣x)​p​(x),p(x\mid y)\;\propto\;p(y\mid x)\,p(x)\,,
with likelihood function p⁡(y∣x)p(y\mid x) and prior p⁡(x)p(x).
We employ a conditional flow to approximate the posterior.
Given training pairs {(xi,yi)}i=1N\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}, let ρT​(x∣y)\rho_{T}(x\mid y) denote the learned conditional density.
The training objective is to minimize the KL divergence
DKL(ρT(x∣y)∥p(x∣y))=∫ρT(x∣y)logρT​(x∣y)p⁡(x∣y)dx.\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,\|\,p(x\mid y)\big)=\int\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,\log\frac{\rho_{T}(x\mid y)}{p(x\mid y)}\,dx\,.
Using Bayes’ rule and omitting the constant term log⁡p⁡(y)\log p(y), we have
DKL(ρT(x∣y)∥p(x∣y))\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,\|\,p(x\mid y)\big)
=∫ρT​(x∣y)​log⁡ρT​(x∣y)​𝑑x−∫ρT​(x∣y)​log⁡p⁡(y∣x)​𝑑x\displaystyle=\int\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,\log\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,dx-\int\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,\log p(y\mid x)\,dx
−∫ρT(x∣y)logp(x)dx.\displaystyle\quad-\int\rho_{T}(x\mid y)\,\log p(x)\,dx\,.
Approximating the integrals by Monte Carlo sampling with xiT∼ρT​(x∣y)x_{i}^{T}\sim\rho_{T}(x\mid y) yields the empirical loss
ℒ≈1N​∑i=1N[log⁡ρT​(xiT∣y)−log⁡p⁡(y∣xiT)−log⁡p⁡(xiT)],\mathcal{L}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\log\rho_{T}(x_{i}^{T}\mid y)-\log p(y\mid x_{i}^{T})-\log p(x_{i}^{T})\Big]\,,
where xi0∼ρ0x_{i}^{0}\sim\rho_{0} from a simple base distribution, and xiTx_{i}^{T} is obtained via the evolution (12) with the drift potential ϕy​(x,t)\phi_{y}(x,t).
For this application, the training step in Algorithm 1 corresponds to integrating forward in time from t=0t=0 to TT, so the system in (23)-(24) is replaced by its forward-time counterpart.
In the experiments below, we consider a Gaussian likelihood function and a Laplace prior:
p⁡(y∣x)∝exp⁡(−12​σ2​‖y−F⁡(x)‖22),p⁡(x)∝exp⁡(−λ​‖x‖1).p(y\mid x)\;\propto\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\|y-F(x)\|_{2}^{2}\right),\qquad p(x)\;\propto\;\exp(-\lambda\|x\|_{1})\,.

ℒ≈1N​∑i=1N[log⁡ρT​(xi∣y)+12​σ2​‖y−F⁡(xi)‖22−log⁡p⁡(xi)]+C,\mathcal{L}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\log\rho_{T}(x_{i}\mid y)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|y-F(x_{i})\|_{2}^{2}-\log p(x_{i})\Big]+C\,,
where CC is independent of xix_{i}.
To accelerate training, we incorporate a supervised term ‖xiT−xi‖22\|x_{i}^{T}-x_{i}\|_{2}^{2} into the loss function.
This encourages the flow to map samples more accurately while retaining the theoretical properties established in Section 3.
Moreover, the transport cost and the HJB regularizer in (16) remain unchanged.
Training the conditional flow with the drift potential ϕy\phi_{y} thus transports the base distribution ρ0\rho_{0} to the conditional law ρT​(x∣y)\rho_{T}(x\mid y), enabling efficient posterior sampling for new measurements yy.

5 Summary and discussion
In this work, we introduced a sparse transformer architecture derived from the regularized Wasserstein proximal operator with an L1L_{1} prior distribution.
This formulation embeds problem-specific structural information, such as sparsity, directly into the network through the proximal mapping of the L1L_{1} function, thereby improving both the accuracy and interpretability of the learned flow.
Theoretical analysis shows that the L1L_{1} prior not only enforces sparsity in the learned representations but also accelerates convergence when the target distribution is sparse.
As established in Section 3.1, this mechanism yields faster KL decay and avoids gradient blow-up.
Experiments in generative modeling and Bayesian inverse problems further demonstrate that the proposed sparse transformer achieves more accurate recovery of target distributions and converges faster than standard neural ODE–based flows.
Beyond these results, our framework highlights how regularized Wasserstein proximal sampling methods serve as a unifying tool that bridges ideas from mean field games [18], Wasserstein gradient flows [13], and optimal control formulations of transformer architectures [12], thereby extending their reach within machine learning.
In future work, we plan to rigorously analyze the acceleration properties of RWPO-induced flows for more general target distributions under sparse or low-dimensional structures, and to extend this approach to other important priors, such as total variation or structured low-rank regularizations, within broader classes of generative AI and Bayesian inverse problems.
