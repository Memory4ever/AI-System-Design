FrugalPrompt: Reducing Contextual Overhead in Large Language Models via Token Attribution
* Corresponding author.
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) owe much of their stellar performance to expansive input contexts, yet such verbosity inflates monetary costs, carbon footprint, and inference-time latency. Much of this overhead manifests from the redundant low-utility tokens present in typical prompts, as only a fraction of tokens typically carries the majority of the semantic weight. We address this inefficiency by introducing FrugalPrompt, a novel prompt compression framework for LLMs, which retains only the most semantically significant tokens. Leveraging two state-of-the-art token attribution methods, GlobEnc and DecompX, we assign salience scores to every token in an input sequence, rank them to preserve the top-k%k\% tokens in their original order, and obtain a sparse frugalized prompt. We evaluate the approach across four NLP tasks: Sentiment Analysis, Commonsense QA, Summarization, and Mathematical Reasoning, using a suite of frontier LLMs. For the first three tasks, a 20% prompt reduction incurs only a marginal loss in task performance, demonstrating that contemporary LLMs can reconstruct elided context from high-salience cues. In contrast, performance on mathematical reasoning deteriorates sharply, reflecting a stronger dependence on complete token continuity. Further analysis with bottom-k%k\% and random-k%k\% tokens reveals asymmetric performance patterns that may suggest potential task contamination effects, wherein models may resort to shallow memorized patterns from pretraining exposure for conventional NLP tasks. We posit that our work contributes to a more nuanced understanding of LLM behavior in performance-efficiency trade-offs, and delineate the boundary between tasks tolerant to contextual sparsity and those requiring exhaustive context.
Our source code and models are available at: https://github.com/Starscream-11813/Frugal-ICL.
Keywords: Large Language Models, Prompt Compression, Token Attribution, Salience Scores, GlobEnc, DecompX, Inference Efficiency, Task Contamination, Contextual Redundancy.

1.  Introduction
As large language models (LLMs) continue to reshape the landscape of NLP, they are increasingly applied to complex and ingenious tasks Yang et al. (2024); Schmidgall et al. (2025). With the scaling of LLMs into super-large trillion-parameter models Fedus et al. (2022); Hudson et al. (2023), and multi-agent architectures requiring hundreds of API calls Islam et al. (2024); Jang et al. (2024), the computational footprint has increased drastically Wu et al. (2025), resulting in high inference latency Wan et al. (2023) and energy consumption Stojkovic et al. (2024).
To counter the consequences of the scaling law for LLMs Kaplan et al. (2020), several methods were introduced to improve model efficiency Wan et al. (2023); Zhou et al. (2024). Prompt compression is one of the subfields where the input prompting tokens are reduced while retaining considerable performance Zhang et al. (2025). Compression is either achieved on the hard prompts (natural language input text) Sennrich et al. (2016) or the soft prompt (trainable embedding-size vectors)Zhao et al. (2023a), with the former being more popular due to its interpretability and compatibility with closed-source models.
Current hard prompt compression methods fall short in three key aspects. First, some methods rely on summarizing the input prompt Chuang et al. (2024), which can inadvertently discard semantically critical tokens. Second, these methods tend to retain low-information tokens, such as articles and conjunctions Jiang et al. (2023). Third, all methods either rely on an auxiliary smaller LLM with billions of parameters or a training paradigm. For instance, Chuang et al. (2024) use a Vicuna 7B model Chiang et al. (2023), which introduces a significant parameter overhead.
We address these challenges by introducing a prompt compression strategy that uses saliency scores generated from state-of-the-art pre-trained token attribution methods Modarressi et al. (2022); Modarressi et al. (2023). These saliency scores can capture the semantically rich token while filtering the redundant ones. This is achieved without any training paradigm, with minimal parameter overhead of a single 110M BERT model Devlin et al. (2019), substantially lower than that of existing methods.
Our criteria for filtering are grounded in two linguistic principles underlying textual redundancy. First, there is an intrinsic redundancy to any form of text produced by humans Wit and Gillette (1999). For instance, in the text, “What are your plans for the weekend, by the way?”, the phrase “by the way” contributes no additional meaning to an LLM. Second, the English language has low-importance tokens that add little to no semantic weight, generally in the form of articles and auxiliary verbs. For instance, most of the meaning is conveyed if the statement “I have received a parcel.”, is simplified to “I received parcel.", with the latter mimicking simplified English, often used by non-native speakers Shubert et al. (1995). Our filtering methods eliminate both forms of redundancy.
Our key contributions are as follows:
•
We introduce a novel, training-free prompt compression strategy for LLMs that controllably filters low-importance tokens based on saliency scores from pre-trained encoders.
•
Experiments on four tasks reveal that a 20% prompt reduction retains the performance across most models and tasks with minimal parameter overhead.
•
We reveal key behavioral insights into the performance scaling of models with the inference cost and possible task contamination in common benchmarks.
2.  Related Work
2.1.  LLM Efficiency
The current literature on LLM efficiency Wan et al. (2023) stems from the literature on efficient transformers Tay et al. (2022), but for the autoregressive setting of LLMs. Inference-time efficiency can be achieved via model compression methods, such as quantization Dettmers et al. (2023b), parameter pruning Ma et al. (2023), low-rank decomposition Li et al. (2023a), and knowledge distillation Timiryasov and Tastet (2023). Training efficiency can be achieved during pre-training Liu et al. (2023); Yao et al. (2024) and fine-tuning, either through parameter-efficient Hu et al. (2022) or memory-efficient Dettmers et al. (2023a) approaches. Finally, architectural changes also improve efficiency, e.g., efficient attention Child et al. (2019); Ainslie et al. (2023) and state-space models Gu and Dao (2023). Distinct from these methods, our work adopts a data-centric approach where the input prompt is optimized instead of changing the underlying weights or architecture of the model Chang et al. (2024).
2.2.  Prompt Compression
Prompt compression aims to minimize input redundancy while retaining task-relevant information. Trainable soft-prompts achieve these through learned embeddings Zhao et al. (2023b), while sacrificing the interpretability of the prompt text. In contrast, compressing hard prompts preserves interpretability via filtering important tokens Li et al. (2023b); Jiang et al. (2023) or paraphrasing the prompt text into a reduced version Chuang et al. (2024). Context-heavy tasks, e.g., conversation systems, benefit from prompt compression but have focused on sentential redundancy or summarization Santra et al. (2023). Our work instead aims at token-level reduction, offering higher granularity without losing semantic content.
While our work primarily filters the top k%k\% tokens based on the attribution scores, a key distinction is that we use smaller BERT-class text encoders to gain attribution scores. Previous works lack such efficiency as they rely on smaller auto-regressive models, e.g. Jiang et al. (2023) uses GPT-2 Radford et al. (2019) and Chuang et al. (2024) uses Vicuna 7B Chiang et al. (2023). While the compressor model has fewer parameters than the interference model, its overhead is significantly higher than that of the discriminative token attribution methods.
2.3.  Token Attribution
The self-attention weights in transformers have been primarily explored as a proxy for token importance Htut et al. (2019); Clark (2019), yet they fail to align with the true attribution of tokens Jain and Wallace (2019). While including vector norms can partially mitigate this issue Kobayashi et al. (2020), it overlooks the intricacies of the non-attention components in a transformer. Instead, we rely on subsequent token attribution methods: GlobEnc Modarressi et al. (2022), which quantifies global token attribution by aggregating layer-wise analyses across the entire encoder using attention rollout and vector norms, and DecompX Modarressi et al. (2023), which propagates locally decomposed token representations globally to incorporate all model components, including non-linear feed-forward networks. These methods were selected for their superior ability to capture comprehensive semantic contributions beyond raw attention weights, enabling accurate identification of salient tokens with minimal computational overhead.

3.  Preliminaries: Token Attribution
To identify which tokens in an input text sequence carry significant influence on the language model’s decision-making process for a given task, we compute the token attribution scores using task-specific BERT-class encoders Devlin et al. (2019). We obtain the saliency scores from these models by adopting two state-of-the-art (SoTA) token attribution methods: the global token attribution analysis method (GlobEnc) Modarressi et al. (2022) and the globally propagated locally decomposed attribution analysis method (DecompX) Modarressi et al. (2023), which are discussed in this section.

4.  Methodology
Figure 1 portrays an overview of our proposed pipeline. We obtain the saliency scores using the token attribution methods discussed in Sec. 3 and employ frugalization via token filtration to generate a condensed yet semantically representative version of an input sample. This technique identifies and retains only the most salient tokens, thereby reducing input dimensionality while preserving the core meaning for the given task.
4.1.  Ranking via Saliency Scores
We define a task-specific scoring function φτ∈[GlobEnc, DecompX]\varphi_{\tau}\in[\text{GlobEnc,\ DecompX}] for a task τ\tau, which assigns a saliency score to each token in a given text, i.e.,
φτ:𝒯→ℝn,\varphi_{\tau}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},
where 𝒯\mathcal{T} denotes the space of the token sequences and nn is the length of the sequence. For the input token sequence, T=⟨t1,t2,…,tn⟩T=\langle t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n}\rangle, the scoring function produces a saliency vector,
𝐬=φτ​(T)=⟨s1,s2,…,sn⟩,si∈ℝ,\mathbf{s}=\varphi_{\tau}(T)=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\rangle,\quad s_{i}\in\mathbb{R},
where each score sis_{i} reflects the relative contribution of token tit_{i} to the model’s output for the specified task.
Tokens are then ranked in monotone decreasing order of their attribution scores to obtain a permutation, π\pi, formed of the token indices, such that,
sπ⁡(1)≥sπ⁡(2)≥⋯≥sπ⁡(n).s_{\pi(1)}\geq s_{\pi(2)}\geq\cdots\geq s_{\pi(n)}.
4.2.  Top-kk Filtering
To construct the frugalized representation, we retain only the top p=⌈k100⋅n⌉p=\left\lceil\frac{k}{100}\cdot n\right\rceil tokens, where kk is the retention percentage. The selected indices form the sequence,
Sk=⟨π⁡(1),π⁡(2),…,π⁡(p)⟩.{S}_{k}=\langle\pi(1),\pi(2),\dots,\pi(p)\rangle.
We reindex SkS_{k} in monotone increasing order to produce Sk↑{S}_{k\uparrow}. The frugalized sequence 𝐅k\mathbf{F}_{k} is produced by restoring the selected tokens to their original order, i.e.,
Fk=⟨ti|i∈Sk↑⟩{F}_{k}=\langle t_{i}\lvert i\in{S}_{k\uparrow}\rangle
ensuring that the temporal coherence of the sequence is preserved, while uninformative tokens are discarded.
We elucidate our frugalization approach with an example.
For the input sequence T={T}= ⟨\langle‘‘The’’, ‘‘movie’’, ‘‘was’’, ‘‘good’’, ‘‘,’’, ‘‘and’’, ‘‘I’’, ‘‘liked’’, ‘‘it’’, ‘‘very’’, ‘‘much’’⟩\rangle, suppose the attribution scores for sentiment analysis prioritize content and sentiment words (e.g., ‘‘movie’’: 0.950.95, ‘‘good’’: 0.900.90, ‘‘much’’: 0.850.85, ‘‘liked’’: 0.800.80). Upon filtering the top k=40%k=40\% (p=4p=4) and reordering them according to their original relative order, we get the reduced sequence
F40=⟨‘‘movie’’,‘‘good’’,‘‘liked’’,‘‘much’’⟩{F}_{40}=\langle\texttt{``movie''},\texttt{``good''},\texttt{``liked''},\texttt{``much''}\rangle
where the original order is preserved to maintain syntactic coherence. The frugalized output “movie good liked much” is thus created, discarding the less salient tokens (e.g., ‘‘The’’, ‘‘was’’, etc.) while retaining semantic essence.

5.  Experiment
5.1.  Tasks
We evaluate the efficacy of the token reduction by experimenting across four NLP tasks: sentiment analysis (text classification), summarization (text generation), commonsense question answering, and mathematical reasoning. These tasks span both discriminative and generative paradigms, enabling evaluation under diverse linguistic and cognitive demands of the LLM.
5.2.  Dataset
For sentiment analysis, we use the IMDb movie review dataset Maas et al. (2011), a binary sentiment analysis corpus comprising movie reviews labeled positive or negative. We use the Argilla News dataset Ahmed et al. (2017) to evaluate abstractive summarization on news articles. For commonsense QA, we use the CosmosQA dataset Huang et al. (2019), which primarily focuses on commonsensical and causal reasoning within short narrative passages. The questions stipulated in CosmosQA are easy for humans to solve, but usually challenging for LLMs. We use the GSM8k dataset Cobbe et al. (2021) for mathematical reasoning, which contains grade-school math word problems to assess basic arithmetic reasoning and multi-step logical deduction.
5.3.  Models
We perform inference using a set of contemporary frontier models that vary in architecture and scale. This is primarily an effort to examine the generalizability of our method. The models are: LLaMA-3 8B, LLaMA-3 70B Meta (2024), GPT-3.5 Brown et al. (2020), Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking Google DeepMind (2025), and o3-mini OpenAI (2025). The closed-source models are accessed via their respective APIs, and the open-source models are accessed via OpenRouter11
1
https://openrouter.ai/, using standardized prompts to minimize formatting-induced variability.
5.4.  Experimental Setup
We set the temperature to 1.01.0 for all inference runs to sample from the model’s unaltered probability distribution, ensuring a standard and reproducible generation process. For the frugalized setup, we experiment by filtering the top k∈{50,60,80}%k\in\{50,60,80\}\% of the input tokens. We also include the baseline prompt using 100%100\% of the tokens to set a proper frame of reference for the performance delta for all the models. This setup facilitates us to investigate whether low-attribution filler tokens can be excluded with minimal degradation in task performance, which, by implication, will also reduce API costs and minimize carbon footprint.
5.5.  Evaluation Metrics
We employ task-specific metrics to evaluate performance across both full and frugalized setups. For sentiment classification, we report accuracy and F1 score. Summarization performance is assessed using BLEU, ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, BERT Score, and METEOR to capture both superficial and semantic quality of the responses. For commonsense question answering, we use accuracy as the dataset follows a multiple-choice format. Mathematical reasoning is evaluated using pass@11, which measures the proportion of problems correctly solved on the first attempt.

6.  Result and Analysis
6.1.  Performance across Tasks
Tab. 1 reveals that performance degradation becomes more pronounced as the retention threshold decreases, particularly below 60% token retention, with distinct patterns emerging across tasks. For instance, at 50% retention, sentiment classification accuracy drops by 5-10% and commonsense QA accuracy by 10-15%, while summarization metrics like ROUGE-1 decline by 20-25%, as seen in Figure 2,3. The performance decline is noticeably smaller for higher values of kk, e.g.,, GlobEnc using 80%80\% tokens achieves close to the baseline performance in classification and QA tasks.
Mathematical reasoning exhibits the steepest fall, with pass@1 scores plummeting to below 15% at lower thresholds, reflecting the loss of critical numerical and logical connectors. We hypothesize that this degradation is more pronounced for reasoning tasks due to their reliance on the sequential integrity of tokens and numerical data. Low k%k\% thresholds exacerbate this by discarding essential context, amplifying errors in tasks requiring unbroken symbolic chains, while simpler classification and summarization tasks generally tolerate greater omission by inferring from salient cues.

6.2.  Choice of Attribution Method
Following both Tab. 1 and Fig. 2, for classification and QA tasks, GlobEnc outperforms DecompX across all metrics. However, in reasoning, DecompX outshines GlobEnc. DecompX’s subword-level attribution that includes linear activations may help retain more important tokens for reasoning, but might overwhelm the models in other tasks. For summarization (Fig. 3), GlobEnc usually outperforms DecompX, except for the GPT-3.5 model. While DecompX is reported to produce better attribution scores Modarressi et al. (2023), most models find the token sequence filtered by word-level attention, like GlobEnc, to be more useful in retaining performance across most tasks.
6.3.  Performance vs. Cost
The cost per million tokens for each model was taken from the respective provider’s website, at the time of writing, and is reported in Tab. 3. The reader should note that the price is subject to change. The cost of the open-source Llama models was estimated using the lowest available rate on OpenRouter1.
Fig.2, 3 show the performance difference between the reduced set of tokens and 100% tokens for all the models. For sentiment analysis and question answering, we observe that using a reduced set of tokens generally retains the performance and has a softer decline as we go down the cost. However, reducing the tokens sharply declines the performance in reasoning tasks, making it unsuitable for token reduction. The results are mixed for summarization tasks: while most models have a softer performance decline across the metrics, methods such as GPT-3.5 using GlobEnc experience rapid performance degradation.
6.4.  Performance across Models
Figure 2,3 illustrate that for smaller models, token attribution offers negligible cost savings relative to the sharper performance degradation it incurs. Consequently, token reduction proves to be more practical for larger, costlier models such as o3-mini. Legacy models such as GPT-3.5 often display abrupt drops in performance, e.g., in summarization using GlobEnc, necessitating greater caution when reducing tokens.
6.5.  Random & Bottom kk tokens
We select random k%k\% and the bottom k%k\% tokens (based on attribution scores), while preserving the text order, to establish a baseline against our token reduction method.
As shown in Table 2, classification, summarization, and question answering exhibit strong retention in performance across varying thresholds. This strongly suggests potential task contamination Li and Flanigan (2024), as the ground truth answers of the test sets are easily available on the internet. Reasoning tasks experience a sharper decline, indicating genuine reliance on contextual information for solving the task.
7.  Conclusion
The surging cost of large language models has often made it inaccessible to people in under-resourced environments, while increasing carbon footprint and inference time latency. Our work aims to bridge this gap by introducing prompt compression at a token-attribution level, which can reduce the cost of LLM inference and make it accessible to a wider range of users. We also reveal potential instances of task contamination that should be considered while evaluating models. We wish to explore adaptive frugalization strategies that dynamically adjust token retention based on task complexity and model confidence.
Limitations
As our token reduction pipeline heavily relies on attribution methods, misalignment between salience and true causal importance could produce suboptimal performance. The task specificity of the attribution methods also makes them unsuitable for certain tasks, such as reasoning, and evaluation in multi-task settings.
Ethical Consideration
As identified in our work, performance retention in certain tasks might stem from the model’s exposure to benchmark data during pretraining. This raises concerns about the validity of LLM evaluation and the need for contamination-free datasets. Prompt compression can also potentially remove safety-critical tokens and hence should be used with caution.
