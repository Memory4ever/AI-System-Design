Predicting Estimated Times of Restoration for Electrical Outages Using Longitudinal Tabular Transformers
This material is based upon work supported by Exelon.

Abstract
As climate variability increases, the ability of utility providers to deliver precise Estimated Times of Restoration (ETR) during natural disasters has become increasingly critical. Accurate and timely ETRs are essential for enabling customer preparedness during extended power outages, where informed decision-making can be crucial, particularly in severe weather conditions. Nonetheless, prevailing utility practices predominantly depend on manual assessments or traditional statistical methods, which often fail to achieve the level of precision required for reliable and actionable predictions. To address these limitations, we propose a Longitudinal Tabular Transformer (LTT) model that leverages historical outage event data along with sequential updates of these events to improve the accuracy of ETR predictions. The model’s performance was evaluated over 34,000 storm-related outage events from three major utility companies, collectively serving over 3 million customers over a 2-year period. Results demonstrate that the LTT model improves the Customer Satisfaction Impact (CSI) metric by an average of 19.08% (p>0.001) compared to existing methods. Additionally, we introduce customer-informed regression metrics that align model evaluation with real-world satisfaction, ensuring the outcomes resonate with customer expectations. Furthermore, we employ interpretability techniques to analyze the temporal significance of incorporating sequential updates in modeling outage events and to identify the contributions of predictive features to a given ETR. This comprehensive approach not only improves predictive accuracy but also enhances transparency, fostering greater trust in the model’s capabilities.

Introduction
The increasing frequency and severity of natural calamities due to climate variability have led to a significant increase in power outages globally
(
Juan and Joan 2024
)
. For utility companies, providing accurate Estimated Times of Restoration (ETRs) during such events is not merely a matter of customer service but a critical component of public safety. Timely and precise ETRs enable customers to make essential decisions, such as securing alternative power sources, preserving perishable goods, or evacuating vulnerable individuals. However, most utilities continue to rely on manual assessments or traditional statistical and machine learning models
(
Adibi and Milanicz 1999
;
Wanik et al. 2018
;
Walsh et al. 2020
;
Kar et al. 2022
)
, which often lack the precision required to address the dynamic and evolving nature of outage data. This limitation frequently results in imprecise predictions, frustrating customers and hindering their preparedness during extended outages. A significant challenge in predicting ETRs arises from the dynamic nature of outage events, characterized by sequential updates as new information becomes available. These updates reflect evolving conditions, such as restoration progress, resource allocation, or corrections to earlier inaccuracies, thus framing the problem as a longitudinal regression task. Longitudinal modeling, which examines sequences of observations over time, is essential for refining predictions in response to ongoing developments. By capturing the temporal progression of outages, this approach leverages the complete event history, from initial conditions to subsequent updates, to provide increasingly accurate and refined predictions with each new update. Recent advancements in machine learning and deep learning present promising opportunities to improve the accuracy of ETR predictions in these dynamic settings. Transformer-based models, in particular, have demonstrated exceptional ability in capturing long-term dependencies in sequential data, making them highly suitable for regression tasks with longitudinal characteristics. By leveraging historical outage data along with the sequence of updates for each event, these models can reveal complex temporal patterns and interdependencies that traditional methods often overlook, thus enhancing predictive accuracy.
Figure 1:
Storm-Realted Outage Counts between Jan 2022 to Jan 2024 for OPCO-1, OPCO-2, and OPCO-3.
In this work, we propose a longitudinal transformer model specifically designed for the longitudinal tabular regression problem. We assess the model’s performance using outage data from three major utility companies, anonymized as OPCO-1, OPCO-2, and OPCO-3, collectively serving over 3 million customers. The anonymization ensures compliance with data privacy regulations and ethical standards, safeguarding sensitive customer information while enabling reproducible research. The evaluation encompasses various storm conditions classified as small, medium, and large to ensure the model’s robustness across different scenarios. To assess the practical utility of our predictions, we introduce novel regression metrics derived from our customer feedback surveys. Alongside traditional regression metrics like Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), we propose asymmetric regression metrics that reflect customer satisfaction levels more accurately. Customer feedback indicates that underprediction of ETRs significantly decreases satisfaction, as customers are unprepared for longer outages than anticipated. Conversely, overprediction of ETRs does not adversely affect customer satisfaction as long as the overestimation does not exceed eight hours; customers perceive this window as acceptable for planning purposes. However, overpredictions beyond eight hours again lead to a decline in satisfaction due to prolonged expectations of restoration. By incorporating this asymmetric tolerance into our evaluation metrics, we aim to align the model’s performance with real-world customer experiences and priorities. Additionally, we leverage attention matrix visualizations to analyze the temporal dependencies captured by our model, highlighting their critical role in enhancing predictive accuracy. To ensure interpretability, we employ techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
to quantify the contributions of individual features to ETR predictions. This analysis promotes transparency, builds stakeholder trust, and offers actionable insights to utility providers, supporting informed decision-making and development of effective communication strategies. The main contributions of our work are listed below:
•
We design a longitudinal tabular transformer model tailored for predicting ETRs. The model utilizes historical outage data and sequential updates to events to capture complex temporal patterns, updating ETR predictions dynamically as new information becomes available.
•
We introduce novel regression evaluation metrics based on customer feedback to better represent the real-world impact of prediction accuracy on customer satisfaction.
•
We investigate the role of temporal dependencies in enhancing predictive accuracy by visualizing attention matrices. Additionally, we apply interpretability techniques, such as SHAP, to identify key features influencing ETR predictions.
•
We evaluate the model’s performance using anonymized outage data from three major utility companies serving 3 million customers, assessing its robustness across diverse storm conditions.
Experimental Setup
Data
In this study, power outage data from January 2022 to January 2024 were analyzed across three operating companies, collectively serving a customer base of 3 million across distinct geographic regions. As illustrated in Figure
1
, multiple significant storm events of varying intensities occurred during the observation period, leading to extensive power disruptions and numerous outage incidents. Each outage event underwent multiple updates during restoration as more accurate information became available, reflecting continuous revisions and corrections to initial reports. The analysis focused on 34,000 storm-related outage events with over 200,000 updates, each update encompassing 92 features derived from outage-related variables such as the time of the outage, the most recent update time, the number of customers affected, crew dispatch status, the number of concurrent events, and the average restoration time for recently resolved events. To construct the training, validation, and testing datasets for each operating company (OPCO), storm statistics were incorporated, and a stratified sampling approach was employed, based on storm magnitude. The validation and test datasets were deliberately designed to include at least two storms from each magnitude category—small, medium, and large—ensuring balanced representation across varying storm intensities. The storms were classified as large, medium, or small by normalizing the total number of customers affected by a storm relative to the total number of customers served within each OPCO.
Baseline Comparisons
We assess the performance of our Longitudinal Tabular Transformer by comparing it with eight established machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods commonly used for tabular data. For the ML models, we include four standard and computationally efficient baselines: Linear Regression (LR), along with three prominent tree-based boosting algorithms: XGBoost (XGB)
(
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
, LightGBM (LGBM)
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
, and CatBoost (CB)
(
Prokhorenkova et al. 2018
)
. To facilitate comparisons with more complex architectures, we further incorporate four DL models: Fully Connected Network (FCN), ResNet
(
He et al. 2016
)
, TabTransformer (TT)
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
, and FT-Transformer (FTT)
(
Gorishniy et al. 2021
)
. While preprocessing the data for ML models, we applied feature standardization as appropriate. Missing values in continuous variables were imputed using mean imputation, while categorical variables were imputed using mode imputation. Categorical features were subsequently processed using one-hot encoding. For the DL models, continuous features underwent Z-normalization. In the FCN and ResNet architectures, categorical variables were processed via one-hot encoding. For the TabTransformer and FT-Transformer models, categorical variables were label-encoded before being passed to the embedding layers. These preprocessing steps ensure consistency across models, enabling a robust performance comparison.
Asymmetric Loss Function for Customer Satisfaction
Figure 2:
Storm-Outage Related Customer Satisfaction Survey Analysis
In the context of outage restoration, accurately predicting the ETR is critical for maintaining customer satisfaction. However, insights from our internal customer outage survey analysis as illustrated in Figure
2
have shown that customer sentiment exhibits a strong asymmetric behavior with respect to prediction errors in ETR. Specifically, under-predictions where the actual restoration time exceeds the predicted ETR significantly diminish customer satisfaction. Conversely, minor over-predictions (up to 8 hours) have little impact on customer sentiment, while over-predictions greater than 8 hours once again lead to a noticeable decrease in customer satisfaction. Due to this asymmetric impact on customer satisfaction, traditional loss functions like Mean Squared Error (MSE) inadequately capture the varying consequences of prediction errors on customer experiences. To address this issue, we propose an asymmetric loss function that penalizes under-prediction more heavily than over-prediction, and penalizes large over-predictions more than small ones. This design aligns the model’s loss function with customer satisfaction dynamics, ensuring that prediction errors are penalized based on their real-world impact.

This approach is informed by insights derived from our customer survey analysis, ensuring that the evaluation framework captures the nuances of customer expectations.

Longitudinal Tabular Transformer
Figure 3:
Architecture of Longitudinal Tabular Transformer
In this study, we address the challenge of predicting the ETR for outage events by leveraging longitudinal data. Each outage event is characterized by a series of sequential updates over time, with each update providing the latest information on both categorical and continuous features relevant to the event’s evolving status. By framing the task as a longitudinal problem, we aim to capture the dynamic temporal patterns and dependencies that emerge as new information becomes available in subsequent updates. This approach enables a more effective utilization of temporal data by modeling the evolution of an event’s status, thereby improving prediction accuracy. To effectively capture these temporal dependencies and feature-based interactions, we propose a transformer-based model that represents each event as a sequence of updates, where each update corresponds to the latest outage information at a given timestep. This representation allows us to leverage the self-attention mechanism inherent in transformer models, facilitating the modeling of complex interactions between successive updates and their associated features. By explicitly incorporating temporal sequences into the predictive framework, our approach enhances the ability to detect patterns and relationships across updates and features, which would be less effective in static or single-point-in-time models. Architecture and Hyperparameters
For the proposed LTT model, the max sequence length was 20, embedding dimension was set to 128, and 6 transformer encoder layers were used, each with 16 self-attention heads. Adam optimizer
(
Kinga, Adam et al. 2015
)

For a fair comparison, machine learning baseline models were tuned via grid search to determine their optimal hyperparameters. Similarly, deep learning baseline models were configured with training settings aligned with those used for the LTT model, ensuring all models were evaluated at their maximum potential. Results and Discussion
In this section, we present a comparative analysis of the proposed Longitudinal Tabular Transformer (LTT) model against a wide range of established models for tabular regression tasks. The evaluation encompasses both popular deep learning approaches for tabular regression as well as widely used machine learning models known for their effectiveness with tabular data. For the baseline models, the task was treated as a conventional tabular regression problem, adhering to the constraints of these models, which are designed to process only static, one-dimensional data. In contrast, the LTT model reinterprets the task as a longitudinal tabular problem, enabling it to capture temporal dependencies and effectively manage multi-dimensional data within a tabular framework.

Experimental Results
For the comparative analysis, we train and evaluate separate Longitudinal Tabular Transformer (LTT) and baseline models for each OPCO. The proposed LTT model demonstrates superior predictive performance across diverse storm intensities, outperforming most of the traditional and deep learning baseline models in estimating time to restoration (ETR). Table 1 provides a summary of the model’s performance, averaged over five trials, across five metrics: UPR, OPR-8, WAE, CSI, and RMSE. These metrics are evaluated across storms categorized by intensity (large, medium, and small) for three utility companies: OPCO-1, OPCO-2, and OPCO-3. The LTT model consistently demonstrates lower UPR and OPR-8 values, indicating reduced occurrences of both significant under-predictions and over-predictions compared to baseline models. Notably, the LTT model’s weighted asymmetric error (WAE) remains the lowest among all tested models across various storm intensities and utility companies, underscoring its robustness and adaptability to events of differing magnitudes. Furthermore, the LTT model achieves the highest CSI values across all storm sizes, reflecting strong alignment with customer satisfaction expectations.
•
Large Storms (L)
: In large storm scenarios, the LTT model demonstrated a substantial reduction in Weighted Asymmetric Error (WAE) compared to all baseline models, including conventional tree-based methods like XGBoost (XGB) and LightGBM (LGBM). It also achieved notably lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) scores. These results underscore the model’s predictive accuracy and robustness even in high-impact situations.
•
Medium Storms (M)
: For medium-intensity storms, the LTT model consistently outperformed advanced deep learning models, such as ResNet and TabTransformer (TT), in both WAE and RMSE metrics. It also achieved notably lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) scores, particularly for OPCO-1 and OPCO-3. This sustained performance across moderate storm scenarios highlights the model’s effectiveness in managing outage events of this severity.
•
Small Storms (S)
: During less intense storm events, the LTT model exhibited the lowest Under-Prediction Rate (UPR) and Over-Prediction Rate within eight hours (OPR-8), aligning closely with customer expectations by minimizing both under- and over-predictions. It also achieved notably lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) scores, particularly for OPCO-1 and OPCO-3. The model’s high CSI values across various operational companies (OPCO-1, OPCO-2, and OPCO-3) further confirm its customer-oriented predictive accuracy for smaller, less disruptive events.
Our analysis highlights the proposed LTT model’s ability to dynamically incorporate sequential data and consistently outperform traditional tabular approaches across the metrics.

Analysis of Attention Layers
To gain a comprehensive understanding of the temporal dynamics captured by our model, we conduct an analysis of the model’s attention layers. The self-attention mechanism within the transformer model allows for differential assignment of importance to various updates within the sequence, thus capturing dependencies over time. By visualizing attention weights, we can observe the model’s prioritization of information across different updates, yielding valuable insights into the temporal impact of each update on the final ETR prediction.
Attention Visualization Methodology
To visualize the attention weights:
1.
We begin by extracting attention weights from randomly selected attention heads within each transformer layer.
2.
We compute the average attention weight assigned by the model.
3.
These average weights are subsequently visualized as a heatmap, where the x-axis represents the query update’s position within the sequence, and the y-axis denotes the key update’s position within the sequence.
Interpretation of Attention Weights
Based on Figure
4
, which illustrates the attention weights of six layers from the transformer model for OPCO-1, we observe distinct patterns in how attention is allocated across different layers and query-key positions. Each subplot represents the attention weights for a specific layer, with the x-axis denoting key positions and the y-axis representing query positions. The color gradient, ranging from dark purple (low attention weight) to bright yellow (high attention weight), illustrates the intensity of attention assigned at each position. Key observations include:
•
Layer-Specific Attention Patterns
: The attention patterns vary considerably across layers. For instance, Layers 1, 5 and 6 show a more dispersed attention pattern across query-key positions, suggesting that these layers capture broader dependencies across the sequence. In contrast, Layers 2, 3, and 4 demonstrate higher concentration in specific positions, indicated by the prominent yellow bands in certain regions, suggesting that these layers may focus on particular points in the sequence that are more informative for the model’s prediction.
•
Temporal Focus on Early Revisions
: Layers 3, 4, 5, and 6 assign higher attention weights to early query positions, indicating that initial updates provide critical baseline information. For example, early updates about outage severity or crew deployment may serve as a foundation for subsequent predictions.
•
Layer 2 and Critical Temporal Dependencies
: The diagonal band in Layer 2 highlights the model’s focus on temporal continuity, ensuring that updates in close temporal proximity are strongly connected. This helps the model track how events evolve over time.
•
Contextual Integration in Later Layers
: Layers 5 and 6 exhibit more evenly distributed attention, reflecting broader contextual integration by consolidating insights from both early critical updates and later refinements, while consistently assigning low attention to rightmost positions, which often correspond to padding sequences.

Feature Importance Analysis Using SHAP
To further interpret the model, we employ SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
values, a game-theoretic approach that assigns contribution values to individual features, to quantify their influence on the model’s ETR predictions across various updates. This method provides a consistent framework for understanding the contribution of each feature to the model’s outputs.
SHAP Analysis Methodology
The methodology to generate SHAP values consists of the following steps:
1.
A representative subset of the dataset is selected, and SHAP values are calculated to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions for individual samples within the subset.
2.
The SHAP values are aggregated using absolute mean to derive the overall importance of each feature.
3.
The top five most significant features, as determined by their aggregated SHAP importance scores, are visualized across first five revisions to illustrate how their influence on predictions evolves over time.
Interpretation of SHAP Values
The SHAP analysis (Figure
5
) highlights the top five key features influencing OPCO-3’s LLT model predictions during the first five event revisions. Our key findings are as follows:
•
Most Important Features
: Features such as ”Number of Customers Under Outage”, and ”Number of Events Where Crew is Dispatched” consistently appear across different revisions. These features highlight the importance of immediate customer impact and operational response timing, as they are crucial drivers in estimating the restoration time accurately.
•
Temporal Changes
: The influence of specific features evolves as revisions progress. For instance, in earlier revisions, features like ”Number of Customers Under Outage” and ”Priority” are prominent, reflecting the model’s emphasis on immediate priority assessments and customer impact at the onset of the outage. In later revisions, features such as ”Number of Events Where Crew Is Dispatched” and ”Rolling Average of Restore Time for Last 25 Closed Events” gain prominence, indicating a shift towards understanding cumulative restoration patterns and recent crew activities as more data becomes available over time.
•
Operational and Situational Dynamics
: Features that track crew assignment status, such as ”Number of Times an Event Status Changed to Crew Dispatched” and ”Number of Times an Event Status Changed to Crew Unassigned,” play a crucial role in middle to later revisions. This trend suggests the model adapts its predictions based on real-time changes in crew availability and assignment dynamics, underscoring the importance of responsive resource management in influencing ETR predictions.
•
Consistency Across Revisions
: Certain features, such as ”Number of Customers Under Outage” and ”Number of Events Where Crew Completed Restoration,” consistently appear across multiple revisions. This consistency highlights that both contextual factors (e.g., whether the outage is large or small) and recent operational outcomes (e.g., recent completions) are essential components that provide consistent predictive power for ETRs.
Together, these insights validate the model’s alignment with customer satisfaction priorities and offer transparency into its decision-making process. This interpretation framework serves as a robust tool for evaluating and explaining model predictions in outage restoration scenarios, allowing for a nuanced understanding of features as situational dynamics shift.
Conclusion
We present a Longitudinal Tabular Transformer (LTT) model that marks a significant advancement in predicting ETRs during power outages. This model integrates dynamic predictive capabilities with customer-focused metrics to enhance both accuracy and user satisfaction. The primary contributions of this study include the development of a transformer-based architecture that updates ETR predictions in real time by leveraging sequential event data, thereby improving its practical utility for service providers. The model’s interpretability features, such as SHAP analysis, enhance transparency and build trust among stakeholders by identifying the most critical factors influencing predictions. With minor architectural modifications, the LTT model can accommodate a wider variety of features, such as static inputs, dynamic variables, and features of varying lengths, demonstrating its adaptability. Furthermore, this architecture can be seamlessly extended to classification tasks and other predictive applications, broadening its utility beyond outage prediction. The potential of the LTT model extends to addressing other longitudinal challenges across diverse domains that require sequential data modeling. This underscores a promising direction for future research and the broader application.
