Abstract
Training large language models (LLMs) from scratch requires significant computational resources, driving interest in developing smaller, domain-specific LLMs that maintain both efficiency and strong task performance. Medium-sized models such as LLaMA
(
Touvron et al., 2023b
;
Touvron et al., 2023a
)
have served as starting points for domain-specific adaptation, but they often suffer from accuracy degradation when tested on specialized datasets. We introduce FineScope, a framework for deriving compact, domain-optimized LLMs from larger pretrained models.
FineScope leverages the Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) framework, inspired by its ability to produce interpretable feature representations
(
Templeton et al., 2024
)
, to extract domain-specific subsets from large datasets. We apply structured pruning with domain-specific constraints, ensuring that the resulting pruned models retain essential knowledge for the target domain. To further enhance performance, these pruned models undergo self-data distillation, leveraging SAE-curated datasets to restore key domain-specific information lost during pruning.
Extensive experiments and ablation studies demonstrate that FineScope achieves highly competitive performance, outperforming several large-scale state-of-the-art LLMs in domain-specific tasks. Additionally, our results show that FineScope enables pruned models to regain a substantial portion of their original performance when fine-tuned with SAE-curated datasets. Furthermore, applying these datasets to fine-tune pretrained LLMs without pruning also improves their domain-specific accuracy, highlighting the robustness of our approach. The code will be released.

Introduction
Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable generalization across diverse natural language processing (NLP) tasks by leveraging broad, heterogeneous datasets
(
Vaswani, 2017
)
. However, deploying these models for domain-specific applications remains challenging. Foundation models are typically large, often with hundreds of billions to trillions of parameters
(
RBC Borealis AI, 2024
;
Adler et al., 2024
;
Dubey et al., 2024
)
, requiring substantial computational resources
(
Kim et al., 2023
;
Dubey et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
. Additionally, general-purpose LLMs often fail to capture fine-grained domain knowledge due to their generic training data
(
Xu et al., 2022
;
Nijkamp et al., 2022
)
. This challenge has driven the demand for
domain-specialized, resource-efficient models
that retain strong task performance while reducing computational costs
(
Talmor et al., 2018
;
Clark et al., 2018
;
Gu et al., 2024
)
.
Recent advances in model optimization techniques, such as pruning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning, have enabled the adaptation of LLMs to specialized domains with reduced resource overhead
(
Ma et al., 2023
)
. However, these approaches rely heavily on the availability of high-quality domain-specific datasets, which are often scarce or expensive to curate. The bottleneck in domain adaptation thus lies not only in optimizing model architectures but also in constructing an
effective domain-specific dataset
from limited initial supervision. Given the wide range of potential application domains, manually curating datasets for every scenario is impractical, raising the critical question:
How can we efficiently construct a domain-specific training dataset using minimal human supervision while maintaining adaptability and scalability?
Recent work has shown that large-scale instruction tuning may not always be necessary for effective fine-tuning. For instance, LIMA
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
demonstrated that fine-tuning an LLM on just 1,000 carefully curated examples can yield competitive performance, suggesting that pretrained models already encode rich knowledge, and domain adaptation primarily requires high-quality, task-specific supervision rather than extensive data volume. However, obtaining such a high-quality dataset remains a bottleneck: existing methods either require manual annotation or rely on human experts, which are hard to be applicable across numerous domains requiring various language,e models.
In this paper, we propose an alternative learning paradigm,
self-data cultivation
, which automates dataset construction by expanding a small, user-defined
seed set
into a
high-quality, domain-relevant corpus
. We hypothesize that a small, carefully chosen seed dataset can serve as the foundation for automatically constructing a domain-specific dataset. If we can curate high-quality training examples from a broader corpus using a small number of representative samples, we can significantly reduce the manual effort required for domain adaptation. However, dataset curation in this context is not merely selecting a subset – it must also refine the dataset to ensure seamless integration into the fine-tuning process. By structuring data in a way that aligns with the pretrained model’s learning dynamics, we can improve both adaptation efficiency and task-specific generalization.
The effectiveness of self-data cultivation depends on overcoming several key technical challenges. First, extracting domain-relevant samples efficiently from a large corpus is non-trivial, as brute-force similarity search is computationally infeasible. Second, training an effective data selection model requires a mechanism that can generalize from a small seed set to retrieve semantically meaningful examples. Third, the dataset must ensure that fine-tuning on the curated dataset remains stable and effective, preserving the generalization capabilities of the pretrained model while improving task-specific performance.
To address these challenges, we introduce
FineScope
, a novel self-data cultivation framework that learns computation-efficient domain-specific language models by automating domain-specific dataset construction. FineScope utilizes a
Sparse Autoencoder (SAE)
to learn a compressed representation of domain-relevant features
(
Templeton et al., 2024
;
Kissane et al., 2024
;
Yan et al., 2024
)
. Unlike typical SAEs which model all neuron activities, FineScope learns our SAE using only top-
k
k
activations, which selects
semantically similar
data points from a large, general-purpose corpus, forming a domain-optimized dataset. It allows FineScope to learn latent domain structures rather than static embedding similarity, making it more scalable and adaptable across diverse application domains. The curated dataset is then used to fine-tune a
pruned domain-specialized LLM
, ensuring both computational efficiency and high task relevance. To aid the fine-tuning process of the pruned models, the FineScope further refines the curated dataset using a
self-data distillation
technique
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
, allowing the models with fewer parameters to learn the target domain knowledge seamlessly.
We evaluate FineScope across multiple domain-specific NLP tasks and demonstrate that it achieves strong domain adaptation while significantly reducing the computational cost. Our results show that models fine-tuned with SAE-curated datasets consistently outperform conventional fine-tuning approaches, achieving higher accuracy with smaller model sizes. Furthermore, FineScope enables fine-grained multi-domain specialization, allowing LLMs to adapt to different subdomains without retraining from scratch. For example, testing on the Math dataset showed an average performance improvement of 11.45 across all models.
Our key contributions are as follows:
•
We introduce
FineScope
, a novel framework for
self-supervised domain dataset curation
, enabling scalable domain adaptation from a minimal seed dataset.
•
We formally propose the concept of
self-data cultivation
, hypothesizing that a small, representative seed dataset can be expanded into a high-quality domain corpus with minimal supervision.
•
We develop an
SAE-guided dataset selection mechanism
, which learns latent domain structures and selects high-relevance training samples efficiently.
•
We demonstrate that
structured pruning with curated datasets
yields efficient, domain-specialized LLMs that retain high task performance while reducing computational cost.

Related Work
2.1
Domain-Specific Language Models
Domain-adapted LLMs have been developed to enhance performance in specialized fields by training on domain-specific datasets. Notable examples include PharmaGPT-13B/70B
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
for biopharmaceutical and chemical applications, SaulLM-54B/141B
(
Colombo et al., 2024
)
for legal tasks, Shai-10B
(
Guo et al., 2023
)
for asset management, BloombergGPT
(
Wu et al., 2023
)
for financial analytics, and MedPalm
(
Singhal et al., 2023
)
for medical question answering. Other models such as ClimateBERT
(
Webersinke et al., 2021
)
, ChatLaw
(
Cui et al., 2023
)
, and FinGPT
(
Yang et al., 2023
)
focus on climate science, legal text processing, and financial analysis, respectively. However, building such models heavily depends on access to high-quality domain-specific datasets, which are generally scarce and costly to obtain. Moreover, these models typically contain billions of parameters, making continuous retraining with updated datasets highly GPU-intensive. These constraints highlight the need for more efficient fine-tuning strategies that can effectively adapt LLMs to domain-specific applications without excessive reliance on human annotation or computational resources.
2.2
Small Language Models (SLMs).
Although Large Language Models (LLMs) achieve state-of-the-art performance across various NLP tasks, Small Language Models (SLMs) have gained traction as a resource-efficient alternative. Several techniques have been explored to reduce model size and computational cost while maintaining performance.
Lightweight architectures
use parameter-efficient designs, such as MobileBERT
(
Sun et al., 2020
)
and DistilBERT
(
Sanh, 2019
)
for encoder-only models and GPT-based models
(
Radford, 2018
;
Radford et al., 2019
;
Touvron et al., 2023a
;
Touvron et al., 2023b
;
Dubey et al., 2024
)
for decoder-only structures.
Quantization
(
Jacob et al., 2018
;
Frantar et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2023
;
Hu et al., 2021
)
reduces precision to lower-bit representations, improving efficiency with minimal accuracy degradation.
Weight tying
(
Thawakar et al., 2024
)
further reduces parameter count by sharing weights across model components.
Pruning
has emerged as a widely used approach for reducing model complexity while preserving essential functionality. Inspired by the lottery ticket hypothesis
(
Frankle & Carbin, 2018
)
, pruning aims to identify smaller subnetworks (“winning tickets”) that can achieve performance comparable to the original model. Various structured and unstructured pruning methods
(
Wang et al., 2019
;
Xia et al., 2022
;
Zafrir et al., 2021
;
Kurtic et al., 2022
;
Ashkboos et al., 2024
;
Xia et al., 2023
)
have been proposed to remove non-critical parameters while retaining the network’s representational capacity. Unlike other optimization techniques, which require either learning models from scratch or reducing precision uniformly, pruning allows models to selectively retain essential components, making it particularly suited for domain adaptation. Thus, in this work, we leverage pruning as a core component of FineScope to construct computation-efficient, domain-specialized models.

Self-Data Distillation and Fine-Tuning:
Self-Data Distillation (SDFT) is a technique where a distilled dataset is generated to match the output distribution of the original model, effectively improving generalization. While SDFT is effective for knowledge transfer, its role in FineScope extends beyond dataset refinement. Since pruning removes a significant portion of the model’s parameters, it risks discarding essential representations that contribute to domain-specific knowledge. To mitigate this, we use self-data distillation to recover lost information and reinforce the pruned model by reintroducing high-confidence outputs from a stronger/unpruned teacher model.
The pruned model may lose critical task-relevant features due to parameter reduction. Training on self-distilled outputs helps restore
soft knowledge
from the teacher model, ensuring that the pruned model retains domain-specific patterns without requiring additional labeled data. Furthermore, unlike standard fine-tuning, which adapts to new domains based solely on curated datasets, self-data distillation smooths the learning process by preserving consistency in the model’s output distribution. This reduces
overfitting to the smaller curated dataset
while maintaining alignment with the original domain representations.

4.3
Results
Table
3
presents the evaluation results on the MMLU dataset across three domains. The results show that the domain-specialized pruned models, which are fine-tuned by FineScope with our SAE-curated domain-specific dataset, improve average performance over Alpaca tuning by 4.13% (STEM), 2.25% (Social Sciences), and 5.40% (Humanities) across all models. Among the three evaluated models, MathCoder-CL shows the highest improvement (+16.71), benefiting from exposure to a more updated dataset, followed by LLaMa 3.1 (+2.41) and Vicuna (+1.2). This demonstrates that SAE-curated datasets effectively enhance domain-specific adaptation without significant human effort.
It is noteworthy that, after pruning, model performance significantly degrades, e.g., by 50% (Vicuna), 48% (MathCoder-CL), and 35% (LLaMa 3.1) on average across all domains. LLaMa 3.1 exhibits the smallest performance drop, likely due to its more balanced initial performance and domain-aware pruning, which retains a larger proportion of essential features.
Compared to GPT-3 (6.7B, 175B) and OLMO-7B, our method outperforms in most cases even though our pruned models have a small number of parameters. However, GPT-3 surpasses our method in Social Sciences, and GPT-3 (175B) outperforms in Humanities, while our tuned models consistently outperform OLMO-7B.
Table
4
shows performance on Math-based domains, where our domain-specific tuning significantly improves results. Compared to Alpaca-tuned models, performance gains are +7.50 (Vicuna), +8.15 (MathCoder-CL), and +18.70 (LLaMa 3.1). Notably, pruning causes severe degradation for Vicuna and MathCoder-CL, and Alpaca tuning does not fully recover their lost performance due to a lack of math-specific data. LLaMa 3.1 also performs worse when tuned with Alpaca, likely due to catastrophic forgetting.
When compared to GPT models, our tuned models achieve competitive results, with notable differences likely stemming from imbalanced training datasets in GPT models, which may limit their generalization across diverse math domains.

Seed Selection:
We visualize the seed selection process using cluster analysis, as shown in
Figure

. The interpretation of features varies across initial, middle, and final layers, reflecting how representations evolve within the model. The results suggest that our curated dataset is alinged with the clustered virtual domain target domains across different layer representations. Lower layers tend to capture broad, generalized features, often encoding syntactic structures or common linguistic patterns. In contrast, deeper layers focus on increasingly abstract and domain-specific attributes, leading to more compact and semantically meaningful clusters.
This progressive refinement suggests that domain-relevant information emerges more distinctly in later layers, where feature representations become more specialized. Our method leverages this hierarchical structure, selecting seeds from layers that balance generalization and domain specificity. By utilizing SAE-based representations instead of raw embeddings, we ensure that the seed selection process is more interpretable and aligned with high-level domain knowledge, rather than being influenced by superficial token-level similarities. This reinforces the effectiveness of our approach in identifying the most informative seed samples for dataset curation.

Conclusion
We introduced FineScope, a framework for developing domain-specific models through SAE-guided dataset curation, domain-aware pruning, and self-distilled fine-tuning. FineScope addresses a key challenge in domain adaptation: how to efficiently construct high-quality domain-specific datasets while maintaining computational efficiency in fine-tuning LLMs. Our approach consists of three key steps: (1) Training an SAE to learn compressed domain representations, (2) Using the trained SAE to curate a domain-specific dataset from a large corpus, and (3) Applying domain-aware pruning followed by self-data distillation to recover lost knowledge and enhance fine-tuning stability. FineScope ensures that models retain domain-relevant features while significantly reducing computational cost.
Experiments demonstrate that FineScope outperforms state-of-the-art methods in most cases. Our approach helps restore pruned models’ performance while significantly enhancing domain-specific adaptation. Additionally, tuning pretrained models with SAE-curated datasets enables them to surpass original models in domain-specific tasks.
