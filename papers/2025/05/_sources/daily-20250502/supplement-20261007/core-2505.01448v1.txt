Abstract.
Audio-visual segmentation aims to separate sounding objects from videos by predicting pixel-level masks based on audio signals. Existing methods primarily concentrate on closed-set scenarios and direct audio-visual alignment and fusion, which limits their capability to generalize to new, unseen situations.
In this paper, we propose OpenAVS, a novel training-free language-based approach that, for the first time, effectively aligns audio and visual modalities using text as a proxy for open-vocabulary Audio-Visual Segmentation (AVS). Equipped with multimedia foundation models, OpenAVS directly infers masks through 1) audio-to-text prompt generation, 2) LLM-guided prompt translation, and 3) text-to-visual sounding object segmentation.
The objective of OpenAVS is to establish a simple yet flexible architecture that relies on the most appropriate foundation models by fully leveraging their capabilities to enable more effective knowledge transfer to the downstream AVS task. Moreover, we present a model-agnostic framework OpenAVS-ST that enables the integration of OpenAVS with any advanced supervised AVS model via pseudo-label based self-training. This approach enhances performance by effectively utilizing large-scale unlabeled data when available.
Comprehensive experiments on three benchmark datasets demonstrate the superior performance of OpenAVS. It surpasses existing unsupervised, zero-shot, and few-shot AVS methods by a significant margin, achieving absolute performance gains of approximately 9.4% and 10.9% in mIoU and F-score, respectively, in challenging scenarios.
Keywords: Multimedia Interpretation, Audio-visual segmentation, Open vocabulary, Foundation models
1. Introduction
Audio-Visual Segmentation (AVS) predicts a dense mask of the sounding object in each video frame, based on the associated audio signal. It is an important yet challenging task, which can be further divided into single sound source segmentation, multiple sound source segmentation, and audio-visual semantic segmentation (Zhou et al., 2022b; Zhou et al., 2024).
Despite the recent proliferation of deep AVS models, achieving state-of-the-art performance typically necessitates supervised training on a large-scale, fully-annotated dataset (Gao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Mo and Morgado, 2024).
Obtaining pixel-level ground-truth labels for segmentation is significantly more labor-intensive than acquiring clip-level labels for classification. While recent AVS benchmark datasets (Zhou et al., 2022b; Zhou et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b) have been released, models tailored to these datasets may lack generalizability, reducing their effectiveness in real-world applications where domain shifts are common.
To reduce the annotation cost, Liu et. al. proposed to construct a synthetic dataset by leverage existing image segmentation and audio datasets (Liu et al., 2024b). They align the image-mask pairs with the corresponding audio samples using the category labels from the segmentation datasets. Mo and Raj proposed a weakly-supervised AVS model, which generates pseudo masks based on instance-level labels to save annotation cost (Mo and Raj, 2023). However, these methods rely on class labels to produce contrastive pairs, and their performance are constrained by imbalanced data, limiting their ability to generalize effectively to unseen scenarios.
A promising line of work that enhances a model’s generalization to unseen domains is unsupervised audio-visual segmentation using foundation models. For instance, Point-Prompt (Yu et al., 2023) leveraged AudioCLIP (Guzhov et al., 2022) to promote the Segment Anything Model (SAM) (Kirillov et al., 2023) for mask generation. OWOD-BIND (Bhosale et al., 2023) utilizes Open World Object Detector (OWOD) (Joseph et al., 2021) to generate class-agnostic object proposals, and links them with acoustic cues using ImageBind (Girdhar et al., 2023) through cosine similarities of shared latent space embeddings. MoCA (Bhosale et al., 2024) adopts a self-supervised contrastive learning framework that uses DINO (Caron et al., 2021) to extract visual embeddings and create positive and negative pairs.
Figure 1. Different ways for audio-visual alignment. (a) Embedding-based methods model audio-visual correlations directly in a latent space. (b) Language-based methods provide semantic-level alignment, enabling more effective knowledge transfer from text-audio/visual foundation models, strengthened by their superior generalization capability.
We observe that existing unsupervised AVS methods primarily focus on exploring visual foundation models, aligning audio and visual modalities within a latent space, as illustrated in Figure 1a.
These methods typically fuse audio and visual features using cross-attention (Bhosale et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b) or similarity measures (Li et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022a; Girdhar et al., 2023), often requiring fine-tuning to achieve better alignment of the two modalities. However, empirical studies reveal that these methods face significant challenges in complex scenarios (Qian et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024), particularly when multiple sounding objects are present in the corresponding frame. This indicates that achieving direct alignment between audio and visual modalities is challenging possibly due to 1) limitations related to data scarcity and imbalance and 2) diverse acoustic and visual appearances of the same semantic class. Consequently, text-audio and text-visual foundation models generally demonstrate stronger generalization capabilities compared to audio-visual foundation models. Motivated by the above observations, we propose a language-based audio-visual alignment approach as shown in Figure 1b, which utilizes language as a bridge to narrow the semantic gap between audio and visual content, enabling effective knowledge transfer from both audio-text and text-visual foundation models to fully exploit their capabilities.
Specifically, our proposed framework, termed OpenAVS, integrates off-the-shelf multi-modality (i.e., audio, visual, and text) foundation models to perform unsupervised open-vocabulary AVS task. As illustrated in Figure 2, OpenAVS approaches the AVS task from a text-based perspective by decomposing it into the following three components: 1) audio-to-text prompt generation with Audio Language Models (ALM), 2) LLM-guided prompt translation with Large Language Models (LLM), and 3) text-to-visual sounding object segmentation with Visual Foundation Models (VFM).
By utilizing off-the-shelf ALM such as Pengi (Deshmukh et al., 2023) and VFM such as Grounded-SAM (Ren et al., 2024b), acoustic and visual representations can be well aligned within the semantic space defined by LLM such as GPT (Achiam et al., 2023) or Llama (Touvron et al., 2023).
We further identify potential gaps between ALMs and VFMs and propose to address this issue by jointly incorporating Prompt Consistency across multiple audio descriptions generated using different prompts and Frame Consistency across consecutive frames within the same video.
When unlabeled training data are available, we further propose to improve segmentation accuracy through a unified, model-agnostic AVS framework via pseudo-label based self-training, termed as OpenAVS-ST. We show that OpenAVS-ST outperforms existing unsupervised AVS methods on both the S4 and MS3 benchmarks, while maintaining robust performance across a wide range of parameter settings.
Here we summarize our contributions as follows:
•
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to explore ALMs for training-free AVS. Our language-based audio-visual alignment performs more robustly than existing embedding-based methods, especially in complex scenarios with multiple sound sources.
•
OpenAVS is a simple and flexible framework that links the AVS task to open-vocabulary visual tasks, enabling the utilization of advancements from a more established research domain for continuous future improvement.
•
We present a model-agnostic self-training framework that seamlessly integrates OpenAVS with existing supervised AVS models, enhancing performance by effectively leveraging easily obtainable unlabeled data in a unified framework.
•
We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate on training-free, few-shot, and zero-shot AVS tasks. Our method consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of both reliability and generalization capabilities.
2. Related Works
Audio-visual segmentation, originated from Sound Source Localization (SSL), focuses on identifying the visual regions in a frame corresponding to the sound. Unlike SSL, AVS requires dense pixel-level predictions. The AVSBench dataset (Zhou et al., 2022b; Zhou et al., 2024) has been widely used for training and evaluating AVS models, covering scenarios such as single sound source, multiple sound sources, and semantic segmentation.
Recent expansions of AVSBench include the V3 dataset (Wang et al., 2024) for few-shot benchmarking and the AVS-Synthetic (Liu et al., 2024b), which uses synthetic data to avoid manual annotations.
State-of-the-art AVS performance typically rely on supervised training (Gao et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Mo and Morgado, 2024).
A significant number of advanced fusion techniques have been proposed to learn the correlations between audio and visual modalities.
For instance, AVSegFormer (Gao et al., 2024) employs cross-attention to integrate spatial-temporal visual features with audio embeddings. DeepAVFusion (Mo and Morgado, 2024) uses early fusion of audio and visual tokens through dense local interactions, utilizing a multi-modal masked auto-encoding framework for self-supervised pre-training.
To reduce annotation cost, weakly-supervised (Guo et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2021) and unsupervised AVS methods have also been explored to enhance model generalizability. For example, WS-AVS (Mo and Raj, 2023) employs weak supervision, using only class labels for class-level contrastive learning.
Direct inference methods like AT-GDINO-SAM, SAM-BIND, and OWOD-BIND (Bhosale et al., 2023) integrate pre-trained models such as AST (Gong et al., 2021), ImageBIND (Girdhar et al., 2023), and SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023).
MaskCLIP+ (Zhou et al., 2022a) achieves Open-Vocabulary semantic segmentation via self-training in the CLIP fashion with pseudo labels generated by DeepLab.
However, existing audio-visual alignment approaches in current unsupervised methods are hard to generalize and usually require fine-tuning to obtain satisfactory results.
With the rise of modern foundational models, more powerful alternatives have emerged to bridge the gap between different modalities.
For instance, SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) offers zero-shot segmentation and GroundingDINO (Liu et al., 2025) performs open-set object detector using human inputs like category names.
Alongside the aforementioned VFMs, ALMs like Pengi (Deshmukh et al., 2023) have also been proposed recently, but have not yet been successfully utilized by existing AVS methods.
Pengi frames audio tasks as text generation tasks and uses transfer learning to convert audio inputs into text outputs.
Our method OpenAVS integrates ALM for the first time to address audio-visual segmentation tasks in a language-based manner, achieving a more robust and generalized audio-visual alignment.

3.2. Framework Overview
The performance of open-vocabulary AVS methods is highly dependent on the effective transfer of foundation models’ capabilities to applications in different domains. Existing work directly utilize audio-visual foundation models such as ImageBind (Girdhar et al., 2023), where a fine-tuning step is usually required to achieve satisfactory results when adapting to the AVS task (Bhosale et al., 2023). To obtain a more generalized audio-visual alignment without training, we present a novel language-based alignment approach by using text as a proxy to decompose the complex AVS task into three sub-tasks and solve them separately using advanced foundational models.
Figure 2 depicts the overall architecture of our proposed OpenAVS framework, which is further extended into a learning-based approach through self-training to leverage unlabeled data when available. Specifically, within the self-training framework, pseudo labels generated by OpenAVS are utilized as supervisory signals to train existing supervised AVS models. The high quality of these pseudo labels enables significant performance improvements. Next, we present detailed descriptions of each component within the proposed framework.
3.3. Audio-to-Text Prompt Generation
The goal of this module is to convert the diverse audio embeddings to clear and certain semantics using an ALM.
Audio embeddings extracted by the audio encoder can vary substantially, even within the same audio category or event. This variation is caused by differences in sound characteristics such as loudness and peak patterns. The varied appearances of objects in both audio and visual embeddings make it challenging to establish a direct alignment between the audio and visual feature spaces without fine-tuning. Consequently, the learned mapping is susceptible to being influenced by specific training data and application domain.
To solve this issue, we employ text as a proxy to substantially reduce the number of correlated pairs from an exponential to a linear scale.
Specifically, we adopt Pengi as the ALM to generate descriptions of the audio:
(4)
𝐭i(a)=Pengi​(𝐩(a),𝐚i)\mathbf{t}^{(a)}_{i}=\texttt{Pengi}(\mathbf{p}^{(a)},\mathbf{a}_{i})
As depicted in Eq. (4), Pengi takes an audio signal 𝐚i\mathbf{a}_{i} and a query text 𝐩(a)\mathbf{p}^{(a)} as input to generate free-form text 𝐭i(a)\mathbf{t}^{(a)}_{i} as output. With carefully designed query text, Pengi is capable of generating text that describes the sounding event in the input audio effectively.

3.4. LLM-guided prompt translation
Figure 4. Illustration of the proposed prompt consistency and frame consistency in LLM-guided prompt translator.
The output of Pengi 𝐭i(a)\mathbf{t}^{(a)}_{i} can be ill-suited for prompting VFMs such as GroundingDINO, due to either the generation of inaccurate audio descriptions or the potential language mismatch between Pengi’s output and VFM’s input requirements, leading to unstable performance on segmentation masks. One example of such issues is illustrated in Figure 3.
Although a person appears in the frame, he is not producing any sound. Directly using the Pengi output 𝐭i(a)\mathbf{t}^{(a)}_{i} can mislead the VFM into segmenting irrelevant objects.
Pengi output includes redundant or irrelevant information, while VFM prefers direct subject nouns over extended expressions. To address the above issues, we propose utilizing an LLM, such as GPT-4o, to translate Pengi’s output into the appropriate format as,
(5)
𝐭^i(a)=GPT-4o​(𝐩(t),𝐭i(a))\hat{\mathbf{t}}^{(a)}_{i}=\texttt{GPT-4o}(\mathbf{p}^{(t)},\mathbf{t}^{(a)}_{i})
where 𝐩(t)\mathbf{p}^{(t)} represents the prompt for the LLM. Our prompt design11
1

It improves the accuracy of audio descriptions by incorporating two complementary strategies: 1) Prompt Consistency that aggregates multiple audio descriptions generated with different audio-to-text prompts, and 2) Frame Consistency that refines descriptions of multiple frames within the same video clip simultaneously by considering their temporal coherence. Furthermore, it eliminates irrelevant information and focuses on extracting direct descriptions of the sounding object, to connect ALM and VFM more effectively.
The benefits of the proposed consistency-based prompting are further demonstrated in Figure 4 as follows.
Prompt Consistency (Column-wise Alignment): This strategy ensures coherence across translated texts generated from distinct Pengi input prompts. An LLM-based alignment translator will harmonize outputs from varied prompts, resolving contextual discrepancies. For example, a vague input like “microphone” might be refined to contextually precise terms such as “wind, water, car engine”. While this significantly enhances semantic relevance, it incurs higher computational costs, as each additional prompt multiplies the required ALM API calls across all frames.
Frame Consistency (Row-wise Temporal Smoothing): This strategy enforces stability over time by aligning translations across consecutive frames from the same video source, with the assumption that audio-visual events exhibit gradual evolution rather than abrupt semantic shifts. Though less impactful on individual translations, it requires same ALM API calls and only slight additional length of LLM input, making it computationally efficient.

3.6. Model-Agnostic AVS via Self-Training
The remarkable performance of current supervised AVS models prompts an important question: can we build on these effective frameworks to further enhance OpenAVS without relying on ground truth supervision? We pursue this direction by proposing the use of unlabeled data within a self-training framework. Our OpenAVS is capable of producing high-quality segmentation masks, which can be used to pseudo-label the easily obtainable unannotated samples.
Significant efforts have been dedicated to developing pseudo-tasks or pseudo-labels for audio-visual self-supervised learning (Sarkar and Etemad, 2023; Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b), which substantially ease the challenges of data acquisition and annotation.
To leverage the strengths of supervised models, we introduce a model-agnostic framework OpenAVS-ST to seamlessly integrate OpenAVS with any existing supervised AVS models based on self-training. Instead of relying on ground-truth labels, we use the pseudo labels generated by OpenAVS as supervisory signals, while preserving the original training configurations of the adapted backbone model.
In the data preparation stage, OpenAVS process unlabeled videos or unpaired audio and image samples collected from different data sources. In either case, OpenAVS is able to effortlessly compose (image, audio, mask) triplets based on the predicted mask 𝐌i​j\mathbf{M}_{ij},
(8)

In the self-training stage, 𝐌i​j\mathbf{M}_{ij} will be used as pseudo labels 𝐘^i​j\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{ij} to optimize the following loss function,
(9)

where AVSθ\texttt{AVS}_{\theta} refers to any supervised AVS network.

4.1. Experiment Setup
4.1.1. Dataset
We evaluate our proposed method on three benchmark datasets, namely S4, MS3, and V3 from AVSBench.
Specifically, S4 contains 23 classes and 4932 samples of single-sounding source clips. MS3 contains 23 classes and 424 samples of multiple-sounding sources.
V3 contains 70 classes and 11356 samples, combining MS3 and V2 (Zhou et al., 2024) and is divided into seen and unseen categories. It is specifically designed for few-shot and zero-shot audio-visual segmentation tasks (Wang et al., 2024).
Following previous work (Wang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b), we convert the semantic labels to object labels as Eq. (10).

4.1.3. Implementation Details
Our framework comprises three key components. First, we adopt Pengi with the default prompt “Generate audio caption” to produce audio-based descriptions. Second, GPT-4o is utilized by default to perform LLM-guided prompt translation. Third, for text-to-visual sounding object segmentation, we employ GroundingDINO 1.0 for object localization with a box threshold of 0.25, and apply both SAM and SAM2 for image segmentation. For model-agnostic AVS via self-training, we explore the following three segmentation models, with their respective training details specified: 1) TPAVI: utilizes PVTv2-B5 and VGGish as backbones; trained on 1 GPU (40G) for 15 epochs, with batch size = 4 and learning rate = 1e-4. 2) AVSegformer: utilizes PVTv2-B5 and VGGish as backbones; trained on 2 GPUs (40G) for 30 epochs, with batch size = 2 and learning rate = 2e-5. 3) SAMA-AVS: utilizes SAM (sam_vit_h) and VGGish as backbones; trained on 2 GPUs (40G) for 80 epochs, with batch size = 2 and learning rate = 2e-4.

4.2. Comparison with the State-of-the-arts
4.2.1. Results on unspervised AVS
Existing unsupervised AVS methods can be roughly divided into two categories, namely training-free and non-training-free methods.
We compare our proposed OpenAVS to the 16 state-of-the-art unsupervised AVS methods as in Table 1.
It is worth noting that method OV-AVSS was originally designed for semantic segmentation tasks and follows a two-stage pipeline: (a) Universal Sound Source Localization (USSL) and (b) Open-Vocabulary Classification (OVC).
The first stage module USSL is trained to locate all objects in an image given the corresponding audio signal. Subsequently, the second stage module OVC leverages holistic class-level label text to semantically filter the localized objects using CLIP (Radford et al., 2021). Since our work does not incorporate semantic information, we compare against the first-stage module of their approach, referred to as OV-AVSS (USSL) in Table 1.
Training-free approaches do not require data collection or model training. They leverage foundation models to perform open-vocabulary audio-visual segmentation directly on test samples.
As shown in Table 1, our proposed method belongs to this category and outperforms the best existing approaches by a significant margin. Specifically, it achieves absolute improvements of 5.8% in both mIoU and F-score on the S4 dataset, while on the MS3 dataset, the gains are even more substantial, with 9.4% and 10.9% in mIoU and F-score, respectively.
This is because the S4 dataset contains only a single sound source in each corresponding frame. Existing methods such as OWOD-BIND focus only on the exploration of visual-audio foundation models. They overlook audio-text modeling, leading to unsatisfactory performance in challenging scenarios with multiple sounding objects in the frames, as observed in the MS3 dataset. For the first time, we utilize both audio-text and visual-text foundation models together, using language as a bridge to narrow the semantic gap between audio and visual content to obtain the state-of-the-art performance in challenging scenarios. Moreover, we conducted experiments with different foundation models by replacing the default SAM with SAM2, referred to as OpenAVS (SAM2). Our method outperformed existing approaches in both configurations, using either SAM or SAM2. In particular, integration of SAM2 led to a significant performance boost, highlighting its potential to evolve and improve alongside advances in foundational models.
Non-training-free methods use an unlabeled dataset to learn audio-visual correlations through self-supervised learning. Although such methods typically achieve better results within the same domain, they lack open-set capabilities, resulting in degraded performance when applied to unseen scenarios. As detailed in Section 3.6, we extend OpenAVS within a self-training framework to leverage unlabeled data when available. It offers improved performance on seen classes in the training dataset, but compromises the ability to perform open-set segmentation. To reflect this trade-off, we refer to it as OpenAVS-ST, where ST denotes self-training. The experimental results demonstrate the superior performance of our proposed method. By simply using the generate pseudo labels as supervision signals, our method is able to outperform recent, carefully designed non-training-free approaches for the AVS task on both the S4 and MS3 datasets, which demonstrates the flexibility of OpenAVS-ST.
4.2.2. Results on few-shot and zero-shot AVS
For few-shot and zero-shot AVS, we compare our proposed OpenAVS to SOTA methods in Table 2 in the V3 dataset, which re-splits AVSBench for few-shot and zero-shot settings (Wang et al., 2024).
Few-shot and zero-shot performance are crucial metrics for assessing a model’s generalization capability, as they reflect the model’s robustness in handling unseen scenarios.
Our training-free OpenAVS significantly outperforms existing approaches in mIoU, with absolute improvements of 12.8% in zero-shot setting and 7.0%, 3.9% and 2.5% in various few-shot scenarios.
Additionally, without any training samples, our method achieves the highest F-score in the zero-shot setting (outperforming the second-best competing method by 1.9%), while all competing methods are trained on seen data from V3.
Thus, the results verify the robustness of our proposed method to unseen scenarios in an open-set setting.

4.3.3. Impact of using clip-level weak labels
We replace the prompts generated by Pengi with clip-level weak labels, denoted as OpenAVS* in Table 5. This is inspired by WS-AVS (Mo and Raj, 2023), which uses clip-level annotations instead of pixel-level masks to reduce the annotation cost in AVS tasks. Guided by the weak labels, the results show slight improvements on the S4 and MS3 datasets. However, under the V3 zero-shot setting, our method, integrated with either SAM or SAM2, still outperforms this alternative.
This is because the clip-level class label applies to the entire audio clip, including silent periods. As a result, it may lead to inaccurate segmentation, since it does not reflect temporal changes in the scene, potentially causing false segmentation when the presence or absence of an object is not correctly captured.
4.3.4. Parameter sensitivity analysis
One of the key hyperparameters that influences the quality of generated pseudo labels for self-training or zero-shot inferencing is the box threshold used in GDINO. To determine an appropriate value, we conducted experiments as shown in Figure 5. For direct inference tasks, the optimal box threshold falls between 0.25 and 0.35 for both the S4 and MS3 datasets.
A lower threshold tends to reduce accuracy by including irrelevant regions, while a higher threshold dramatically decreases the number of mask pixels, negatively impacting performance also.
In contrast, our self-training OpenAVS-ST is not sensitive to this hyperparameter, as pseudo labels generated using box thresholds ranging from 0.25 to 0.75 yield similar performance. This is attributed to the model’s auto-correction capability, which compensates for either higher-quality but smaller pseudo label sets or lower-quality but more extensive ones. Based on these observations, we adopt a box threshold of 0.25 for all experiments by default.

However, as these models advance, the performance of our method will also improve. Additionally, as demonstrated in Tables 1,2,5,6, upgrading from SAM to SAM2 also leads to substantial improvements in our OpenAVS framework.
4.3.6. Evaluation of self-training AVS with different backbones
We perform comprehensive experiments to evaluate our proposed self-training OpenAVS-ST, which adopts supervised backbones such as TPAVI (Zhou et al., 2022b), AVSegFormer (Gao et al., 2024) and SAMA-AVS (Liu et al., 2024b) with the LLM translator and present the results in Table 6.
It is evident that GPT-4o significantly enhances mIoU and F-score (by approximately 3.0% and 3.2% on average) across various AVS backbone models.
This highlights the effectiveness of text-based enhancement in bridging ALMs and VFMs, especially given that these models are not always trained within consistent textual contexts.
Moreover, upgrading from SAM to SAM2 further improves the self-training performance, with mIoU and F-score increasing by approximately 5.5% and 5.3%, respectively. This demonstrates the adaptability of our method in generating higher-quality pseudo labels as foundational models advance.
Meanwhile, it is worth noting that SAMA-AVS achieves the best prediction results on the multi-source dataset MS3. This is due to the fact that SAMA-AVS is built on SAM’s frozen backbone, which performs exceptionally well in general segmentation tasks, even with limited data. However, generalization does not guarantee the best performance across all datasets. For instance, on the single-source dataset S4, which contains simpler and more abundant samples, AVSegFormer’s trainable fusion blocks delivers superior performance instead.
4.3.7. Visualizations
Figure 7 illustrates the performance comparison between different OpenAVS variants and other approaches on the MS3 dataset.
Although OV-AVSS is primarily designed for semantic segmentation task, we include its semantic masks for reference.
OpenAVS shows strong robustness, even in challenging scenarios where objects of the same category (e.g., a baby and a woman) appear in the same frame.
The results also highlight that OpenAVS can handle a variety of text prompt inputs, which significantly influence the performance.
OpenAVS (PO), which directly uses Pengi generated descriptions, exhibits instability in producing masks, as the raw Pengi outputs are not well-suited for direct use with vision foundation models (VFMs). This underscores the importance and effectiveness of our LLM-based translator, which bridges the modality gap between foundational models. OpenAVS (CL) uses clip-level class label as prompts and can generate reasonable segmentation masks. However, due to the lack of temporal awareness in the clip-level weak label, it struggles to distinguish between simultaneously appearing objects, such as a dog and a man in the third example.
5. Conclusion
We explore the open-vocabulary audio-visual segmentation problem, which generates pixel-level masks of the sounding objects in each frame by leveraging the superior generalization capability of foundation models.
A new open-set and training-free framework OpenAVS has been presented in this paper, which innovatively connects audio and visual foundation models through language, enabling effective knowledge transfer from both text-audio and text-visual foundation models. OpenAVS achieves state-of-the-art performance on three benchmark datasets, surpassing existing unsupervised methods and showcasing strong generalizability to unseen domains.

System prompt:

goal is to identify the most likely abstract source(s)
(e.g., human, instrumental, etc.) that is/are producing
sound in a given audio clip. This clip was broken down
into several frames, each containing multiple audio
outputs generated by different AIs, representing sounds
at a specific timestamp.
Each frame corresponds to a different moment in the same
video clip and some frames may contain no sound-producing
