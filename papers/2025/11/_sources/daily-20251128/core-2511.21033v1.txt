Towards Trustworthy Legal AI through LLM Agents and Formal Reasoning
Linze Chen
Yufan Cai
Zhe Hou
Jinsong Dong
Abstract
The rationality of law manifests in two
forms: substantive rationality, which concerns the fairness or moral desirability of outcomes, and formal rationality, which requires that legal decisions to follow explicitly stated, general, and logically coherent rules.
Existing LLM‐based systems excel at surface‐level text analysis but lack the guarantees required for principled jurisprudence.
We introduce
L4M
, a novel framework that combines adversarial LLM agents with SMT-solver–backed proofs to unite the interpretive flexibility of natural language with the rigor of symbolic verification.
The pipeline consists of three phases:
(1) Statute Formalization, where domain-specific prompts convert legal provisions into
logical formulae;
(2) Dual Fact–&–Statute Extraction, in which prosecutor‐ and defense‐aligned LLMs independently map case narratives to fact tuples and statutes, ensuring role isolation;
(3) Solver‐Centric Adjudication, where an autoformalizer compiles both parties’ arguments into logic constraints, and unsat cores trigger iterative self‐critique until a satisfiable formula is achieved,
which is verbalized by
a Judge‐LLM into a transparent verdict and optimized sentence.
Experimental results on the public benchmarks show that our system surpasses advanced LLMs including GPT‐o4-mini, DeepSeek-V3, and Claude 4 and the state-of-the-art baselines in Legal AI, while providing rigorous and explainable symbolic justifications.

Introduction
Legal decision‐making demands not only linguistic comprehension of statutory text but also
formal rationality
—the rigorous application of general, logically coherent rules
(
Weber 1968
;
Raz 1979
)
.
In modern rule‐of‐law systems, courts are constrained not to choose the result that feels morally best,
but to justify outcomes by demonstrably fitting them under the governing rules and precedents.
Limitations of current legal NLP.
Although recent Large Language Models (LLMs) can summarize opinions, draft pleadings
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
and even pass bar‐exam questions
(
Katz et al. 2024
)
, purely neural approaches still
hallucinate authorities
(
Hou et al. 2024
)
, blur doctrinal distinctions
(
Savelka 2023
)
, and yield outputs that cannot be machine‐verified for consistency.
Legal‐domain LLMs such as
ChatLaw
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
,
LawLLM
(
Shu et al. 2024
)
, and
Lexi
(
Ho et al. 2025
)
mitigate factual errors via retrieval‐augmented generation, yet they stop short of
symbolically proving
that a conclusion follows from enacted statutes.
Towards neural–symbolic jurisprudence.
To deliver trustworthy legal AI, we move beyond black‐box text generation and embed LLMs inside a logic–based control loop, resulting in our
L
egal
L
ogic
LL
M (
L4M
) approach.
We design a typed schema for statutory rules (§
3
) that captures actors, actions, conditions, and deontic status, then compile these into executable
Z3
code.
LLMs (i) extract candidate rules from raw provisions and (ii) map case narratives to weighted fact tuples, while an
SMT solver verifies soundness, detects conflicts, and produces a proof object that a “Judge‐LLM” translates into plain‐language justifications.
Early hybrid prototypes for tax compliance
(
Nay et al. 2023
)
, and criminal sentencing
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
foreshadow the promise of this direction; our work extends it with a multi‐agent, adversarial pipeline that mirrors actual litigation dynamics.
To illustrate the performance of our approach, we conduct comprehensive evaluation on the public benchmark
(
Li et al. 2023a
)
.
Our system surpasses strong GPT‐o4‐mini, DeepSeek‐R1, and Claude‐4 baselines, improving statute‐selection and verdict sentence while emitting solver‐checked proofs that enable step‐by‐step auditability.
Contributions
•
A interpretable formulation that supports informal and formal reasoning.
•
An automated neural-symbolic workflow that extracts, formalizes, and verifies statutory reasoning via LLM agents and SMT solvers.
•
Extensive experiments showing enhanced robustness, accuracy, and explainability over state‐of‐the‐art legal LLM baselines.

Experiments
We design a comprehensive evaluation to assess the effectiveness, robustness, and interpretability of our formal legal reasoning framework.
Specifically, we investigate the following research questions:

Experiment Settings.
To be comparable to the baseline methods and reduce cost, we adopt GPT 4o in Stage 1 and o4-mini in Stage 2 and 3 of our approach (
L4M
), though this setup can be easily changed for future updates.
We design our experiments to evaluate two key aspects of model performance: (i) the ability to process real legal cases accurately, and (ii) the robustness of the model under input perturbations.
For the first aspect, we measure four indicators on LeCaRDv2 dataset through random sampling:
Accuracy of General Provisions
for predicting applicable general provisions,
Accuracy of Specific Provisions
for predicting applicable specific provisions,
Average Sentencing Error (Months)
for the mean absolute difference between predicted and actual sentence lengths,
and
Valid Sentencing Output Ratio
for the proportion of cases producing a valid sentencing result.
For the second aspect, we assess robustness on Synthetic perturbation dataset using
Change Accuracy
, defined as the proportion of cases where the model correctly predicts changes in applicable legal provisions before and after a designed perturbation.
Further details of the experimental setup and implementation can be found in Appendix.

5
Discussion
Our results highlight the advantages of integrating formal logic with LLMs in legal AI.
By decomposing the reasoning process into interpretable components, we offer a transparent legal pipeline that mirrors how human judges and lawyers reason through statutes.
Limitations:
Despite its promising results, our framework still faces three main challenges.
First, its formalization quality is constrained by the accuracy of LLM outputs, so errors in identifying actors or conditions can propagate throughout the reasoning pipeline.
Second, our current evaluation is limited to statutory rules, leaving the extension to case law, open-textured norms, and evolving jurisprudence as future work.
Third, the system assumes deterministic rule parsing, which prevents it from fully capturing legal provisions that deliberately incorporate normative ambiguity.
Future Work:
Future research can extend our framework in several important directions.
We envision building interactive legal agents capable of engaging in dialogue to clarify ambiguous case inputs, elicit missing information, or explain their outputs to human users.
Besides, we plan to incorporate case-based reasoning and analogical inference to support legal systems where judicial precedents play a central role—enabling our framework to address common law reasoning and dynamically evolving jurisprudence.
6
Related Work
Recent progress in legal AI has led to the development of specialized LLM systems for the law.
Domain-specific Legal LLMs.
General-purpose LLMs often mishandle legal terminology and citation style.
ChatLaw
couples a knowledge-graph-enhanced mixture-of-experts backbone with a multi-agent pipeline that mirrors law-firm SOPs, outperforming GPT-4 on
LawBench
and national bar exams
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
.
Lawyer GPT
shows that lightweight domain pre-training plus retrieval boosts statute-matching and consultation accuracy while remaining compute-efficient
(
Yao et al. 2024
)
.
Gao et al.
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
further show that careful construction of high-quality synthetic query–candidate pairs can markedly improve legal-case retrieval.
Our work inherits this line of research but goes further by compiling the extracted norms into
executable
formal specifications.
Multi-agent Judicial Simulation.
Treating legal reasoning as collaboration among specialized agents has gained traction.
Agents on the Bench
simulates a collegial bench to improve judgment quality through deliberative voting
(
Jiang and Yang 2024
)
, whereas
AgentCourt
evolves adversarial lawyer agents via long-horizon self-play, yielding measurable skill gains
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
.
These systems, however, lack guarantees on logical soundness.
We retain the agent metaphor but ground each deliberation step in SMT-verified logic.
Neural–Symbolic Methods.
Hybrid approaches that blend neural representation learning with symbolic logic provide both adaptability and rigor.
Early work such as Logic Tensor Networks
(
Badreddine et al. 2022
)
embeds many-valued fuzzy logic into differentiable architectures, while DeepProbLog
(
Manhaeve et al. 2018
)
integrates probabilistic logic programming with neural predicates.
More recent studies add large-scale LLM components: NS-LCR learns explicit law- and case-level rules for explainable case retrieval
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
; Logic-LM introduces structured prompting plus theorem-prover feedback to enforce logical soundness
(
Sadowski and Chudziak 2025
)
; and Kant et al. combine neuro-symbolic reasoning with contract analysis to improve coverage decisions
(
Kant et al. 2025
)
.
These results show that logic grounding enhances transparency and robustness, which we pursue by marrying an agent-driven statute predictor with a symbolic criminal-law reasoner.
Governance and Liability.
Growing autonomy raises accountability questions.
Gabison and Xian analyze principal–agent liability risks for LLM-based systems and highlight the need for technical audit trails
(
Gabison and Xian 2025
)
.
By outputting verifiable proofs and formal reasoning including facts and statues, our pipeline aligns with governance frameworks and facilitates post-hoc audits.
7
Conclusion
We present a new paradigm for legal AI that bridges the gap between natural language and formal legal reasoning.
By extracting structured representations of legal norms, our system achieves interpretable, executable, and normatively faithful legal inference.
Our experiments show that integrating formal logic improves both reasoning accuracy and transparency, making our system more aligned with the demands of real-world legal applications.
As legal technology continues to evolve, combining language models with formal logic is not merely a technical convenience, but a necessary step toward building socially aligned AI systems.
