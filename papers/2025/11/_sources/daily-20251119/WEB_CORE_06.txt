L101: 
L102: KANs are inspired by the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which states that a finite sum of continuous univariate functions and the binary addition operation can represent any multivariate continuous function $f(\mathbf{x})$ in a specified bounded domain (cite59†Kolmogorov 1961 ). Based on the theorem, function $f(\mathbf{x})$ can be represented as
L103: 
L104:  | $$f(\mathbf{x})=f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})=\sum^{2n+1}_{q=1}\Psi_{q}(\sum^{n}_{p=1}\psi_{p,q}(x_{p})),$$  |
L105: where $n$ is the number of input variables, $\psi_{p,q}:\left[0,1\right]\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}$, and $\Psi_{q}:\mathbb{R}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}$. This equation indicates that a 2-layer network with $n$ inputs and $(2n+1)$ outputs is sufficient to represent $f(\mathbf{x})$ by the sums of univariate functions. However, 1-D function $\psi$ can be fractal and non-smooth, making it unlearnable (cite70†Girosi and Poggio 1989 ) in practice.
L106: KANs solve this issue by generalizing the theorem to multiple layers with arbitrary width. Formally, KANs consisting $L$ layers can be indicated by
L107:  | $$f(x)=(\Phi_{L-1}\circ\Phi_{L-2}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{1}\circ\Phi_{0})(x),$$  |
L108: 
L109:  | $$\Phi_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{\ell,1,1}&\phi_{\ell,2,1}&\cdots&\phi_{\ell,d_{\ell},1}\\
L110: \phi_{\ell,1,2}&\phi_{\ell,2,2}&\cdots&\phi_{\ell,d_{\ell},2}\\
L111: \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
L112: \phi_{\ell,1,N_{\ell}}&\phi_{\ell,2,N_{\ell}}&\cdots&\phi_{\ell,d_{\ell},N_{\ell}}\end{bmatrix},$$  |
L135: ###### Theorem 1 (Retention Bound via Overlap).
L136: 
L137: Under the additional assumptions that each branch $\phi_{\ell,p,q}$ is $L_{\ell}$-Lipschitz^{3}^{3} 3 $\phi$ is $L$-Lipschitz if $|\phi(z_{1})-\phi(z_{2})|\leq L|z_{1}-z_{2}|$ for all $z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}$. Here, $L_{\ell}$ quantifies the spline smoothness in layer $\ell$. and the loss is bounded by $C$, for any $j>i$ we have
L138: 
L139:  | $$F_{i}\;\leq\;C\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}N_{\ell}\,L_{\ell}\;\Delta_{i,j}.$$  |
L140: ### Cumulative Forgetting
L141: 
L142: While Theorem cite71†1 guarantee zero or bounded forgetting on a per‐task basis, real continual learning involves sequences of overlapping tasks whose supports may intersect in complex ways. To capture the deeper dynamics of forgetting in KANs, we further analyze at the branch level and consider cumulative contributions and effects.
L143: ###### Theorem 2 (Branch‐wise Cumulative Forgetting).
L144: 
L145: Under the Lipschitz and bounded‐loss assumptions of Theorem cite71†1 , the forgetting on task $i$ after training on all subsequent tasks $i+1,\dots,T$ can be decomposed as
L146: 
L147:  | $$F_{i}\;\leq\;C\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{p=1}^{d_{\ell}}\sum_{q=1}^{N_{\ell}}L_{\ell}\;\Bigl[\sum_{j=i+1}^{T}\mu\bigl(S^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q}\cap S^{(j)}_{\ell,p,q}\bigr)\Bigr].$$  |
L148: ###### Corollary 1 (Expected Forgetting under Random Supports).
L149: 
L150: If each branch’s supports for task $j$ are independently drawn as length-$s_{j}$ intervals in $[0,1]$, then in expectation
L151: 
L152:  | $$\mathbb{E}[F_{i}]\;\leq\;C\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}N_{\ell}L_{\ell}\ \sum\nolimits_{j=i+1}^{T}s_{i}\,s_{j}.$$  |
L153: ###### Corollary 2 (Saturation via Union‐Bound).
L154: 
L155: Let
L156: 
L157:  | $$U^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q}=\bigcup_{j=i+1}^{T}\bigl(S^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q}\cap S^{(j)}_{\ell,p,q}\bigr)$$  |
L158: 
L159: be the union of all overlaps for branch $(\ell,p,q)$. Then
L160: 
L161:  | $$F_{i}\;\leq\;C\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{p=1}^{d_{\ell}}\sum_{q=1}^{N_{\ell}}L_{\ell}\;\mu\bigl(U^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q}\bigr),$$  |
L162: 
L163: with $\mu(U^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q})\leq\min\bigl(\sum_{j=i+1}^{T}\Delta_{i,j},\,\mu(S^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q})\bigr)$.
L164: ### Complexity-Induced Forgetting
L165: 
L166: Beyond mere pairwise overlap, we further conduct theoretical analysis by examining how intrinsic task complexity drives forgetting in KANs. In particular, we show that when tasks live on data manifolds of differing intrinsic dimensions, the degree of forgetting can change dramatically. This complements our earlier results by linking forgetting directly to geometric measures of task difficulty.
L167: ###### Theorem 3 (Intrinsic‐Dimension Forgetting Rate).
L168: 
L169: Suppose task $t$ generates data concentrated on a compact submanifold $\mathcal{M}_{t}\subset[0,1]^{n}$ of intrinsic dimension $d_{t}$, and each univariate branch’s activation support can be enclosed within an $r$-ball in the pre-activation domain. Then, for any earlier task $i$ and later task $j$, the expected support overlap satisfies
L170:  | $$\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mu(S^{(i)}_{\ell,p,q}\!\cap\!S^{(j)}_{\ell,p,q})\bigr]=O\bigl(r^{\,d_{i}+d_{j}}\bigr),$$  |
L171: 
L172: and hence the forgetting on task $i$ obeys
L173: 
L174:  | $$F_{i}\;=\;O\!\Bigl(\sum_{j=i+1}^{T}N_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\bar{L}\;r^{\,d_{i}+d_{j}}\Bigr),$$  |
L175: 
L176: where $N_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{\ell}N_{\ell}$ counts the total number of univariate branches and $\bar{L}$ is an average Lipschitz constant.
L177: ###### Corollary 3 (Retention for Low‐Dimensional Tasks).
L178: 
L179: If every subsequent task $j$ has intrinsic dimension $d_{j}\leq D$, then
L180: 
L181:  | $$F_{i}=O\bigl(T\,N_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\bar{L}\;r^{\,d_{i}+D}\bigr),$$  |
L182: 
L183: which becomes negligible when $d_{i}+D$ is sufficiently small.
L184: ###### Corollary 4 (Fragmentation Mitigates Complexity).
L185: 
L186: If each branch’s support for task $t$ is split into $k_{t}$ disjoint intervals (effective radius $r/k_{t}$), then Theorem cite72†3 ’s rate improves to
L187: 
L188:  | $$F_{i}=O\!\Bigl(\sum_{j=i+1}^{T}N_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\bar{L}\;\bigl(r/k_{i}\bigr)^{d_{i}}\bigl(r/k_{j}\bigr)^{d_{j}}\Bigr).$$  |
L189: Overall, Theorem cite72†3 and its corollaries illuminate how KAN’s forgetting depends on the deeper geometric complexity of task data and the combinatorial structure of activation supports. This perspective provides actionable guidance for designing KAN architectures and pruning strategies.

L220: 
L221: The experiment design described in cite8†section 3 was executed on Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B, and Llama-3.3-70B cite58†Dubey et al. L223: Motivation. We will examine in more detail three dimensions, to which the set membership problem is supposed to be invariant: (1) variations of the prompts that formulate the same query (2) the order in which we present the members of the set in the prompt and (3) the semantic features and relations of the members of the set. We will demonstrate that along all these dimensions there is significant variance in the error patterns.

L187: #### 4.1.4 Language–Language Transfer
L188: We quantify cross-lingual transfer with an $|\mathcal{L}|\!\times\!|\mathcal{L}|$ matrix whose rows are fine-tuned (source) languages $\ell^{\ast}$ and columns are evaluated (target) languages $t$. Each cell stores the mean percentage-point change $\Delta_{\%}$ when fine-tuning on $\ell^{\ast}$ and evaluating in $t$, averaged within model and task and then across models. Figure cite81†4 visualises this matrix (green denotes gains, red denotes harms).
L190: The matrix is notably asymmetric: languages that receive well are not necessarily good donors. For example, sv and ca are strong recipients yet export substantial harm when used as fine-tuning sources. Turkish (tr) stands out as a solid receiver with many positive incoming edges, but as a donor its effects are mixed and often negative. The landscape features clear recipient hubs and brittle targets, with donor strength not mirroring recipient strength.

L192: #### 4.1.5 Baseline-adjusted transfer
L193: To check whether transfer effects are driven by starting points, we regress $\Delta$ (percentage points) on the pre-fine-tuning baseline $x\in[0,1]$ separately for each regime. For MT–CL, $\Delta$ increases with baseline ($\beta\!\approx\!+4.8$ pp per unit), with no clear intercept at $x{=}0$. For ML–CT and CT–CL, $\Delta$ decreases as baseline rises ($\beta\!\approx\!-6$ to $-8$), with small positive intercepts and a zero-crossing at $x\!\approx\!0.14$–$0.17$ (Table cite84†3 ).
L194: Overall, baseline explains little variance ($R^{2}\!\approx\!0.02$–$0.05$), so the observed transfer patterns are not artifacts of baseline differences.
