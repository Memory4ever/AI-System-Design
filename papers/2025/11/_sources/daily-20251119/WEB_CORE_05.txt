L197: To augment these samples, we expand each $\mathcal{S}$ into a set of step-level variants by prompting the model with the same state $S$ to generate $n=N/2-1$ alternative actions, where $N$ is the group number we used in trajectory sampling; this setting balances diversity and efficiency without introducing additional hyperparameters. This yields a group of candidate step samples:
L198: 
L199:  | $$\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}=\{\mathcal{S}_{k}=\langle S,A_{k}\rangle\mid k=0,\dots,n,~A_{0}=A\}.$$  |
L200: The original step $\mathcal{S}$ is thus replaced by its augmented group $\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}$, which represents localized perturbations around the original decision.
L201: 
L202: For each $\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}$, we define a step reward based on whether its action $A_{k}$ matches the reference action $A_{0}$. Each augmented group is then evaluated to assign relative advantages among its members:
L203:  | $\displaystyle R(\mathcal{S}_{k})$  | $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }A_{k}\text{ matches }A_{0},\\
L204: 0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$  |
L205:  | $\displaystyle\text{Adv}_{\text{aug}}(\mathcal{S}_{k})$  | $\displaystyle=\frac{R(\mathcal{S}_{k})-\text{mean}\big(R(\mathcal{S}_{j})\mid\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\big)}{\text{std}\big(R(\mathcal{S}_{j})\mid\mathcal{S}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\big)}.$  |
L206: Finally, the step-level advantage used for policy optimization combines the trajectory-level credit and the local augmentation signal:
L207: 
L208:  | $$\text{Adv}_{\text{final}}(\mathcal{S}_{k})=\text{Adv}(\mathcal{S})\cdot\text{Adv}_{\text{aug}}(\mathcal{S}_{k}).$$  |
L209: 
L210: This step-level formulation encourages the policy to refine local action boundaries around high-value steps while maintaining consistency with the trajectory-level objective.

L219: 
L220: ### 6.1 Experiments Setups
L221: ##### Benchmarks.
L222: We selected two widely recognized benchmarks in the graphical user interface (GUI) domain—OSWorld cite78†Xie et al. (2024) and AndroidWorld cite79†Rawles et al. (2024) . These benchmarks were chosen because they represent classical and challenging multi-turn interaction tasks in GUI-based environments, making them suitable for evaluating the robustness and generalization ability of our method.
L223: OSWorld provides a real-computer environment containing 369 tasks across diverse domains such as office productivity, web browsing, system management, and multi-application workflows. Following ARPO cite71†Lu et al. (2025) , we sample 128 tasks from the OSWorld benchmark as our training set. AndroidWorld offers a fully functional Android environment with reward signals for 116 programmatic tasks across 20 real-world Android applications.
L224: We select a subset of 43 tasks from these applications as the training set. Both benchmarks use rule-based rewards evaluated only after completing a trajectory, assigning 1.0 to successful executions and 0.0 otherwise.
L225: ##### Baselines and Training Details
L226: We adopt UI-Tars-DPO-7B cite47†Qin et al. (2025) as our base model, and select (1)trajectory-level GRPO (T-GRPO) cite49†Shao et al. (2024) and (2)GiGRPOcite72†Feng et al. (2025) ^{2}^{2} 2 In the complex GUI scenario, step-level state clustering in GiGRPO is not suitable. In this context, GiGRPO denotes a variant of GRPO in which trajectories are split into step samples, with the trajectory-level advantage distributed to each step for training. as our baselines. Methods such as DAPO cite69†Yu et al.
L227: (2025) or other replay-based approaches (e.g., ARPO) are excluded, as they can be integrated with our approach. All RL training methods use identical hyperparameters. The training batch size is 16, with a rollout number $N=16$, and a PPO train size of 256. The temperature is 0.7 during training and 0 during evaluation. We apply dynamic batch updates in our method and GiGRPO, as both decompose trajectories into step-level sample, resulting in a variable number of step samples.
L228: For each step sample, the number of history responses $t_{r}$ and screenshots $t_{I}$ in $H_{t}$ are 3 and 0, respectively. The specific hyperparameters of our method are: threshold $s_{0}=0.6$, low threshold $s_{\text{low}}=0.2$, and $\kappa=10$. L238: ### 6.2 Results
L239: 
L240: The overall performance on OSWorld and AndroidWorld is summarized in Table cite80†1 , while the ablation results for STEP’s core components are shown in Table cite81†2 . L241: #### 6.2.1 Main Results
L242: 
L243: As shown in Table cite80†1 , STEP consistently outperforms all baselines across both benchmarks, demonstrating clear advantages on the Train Set and overall evaluations. On OSWorld (Train Set), STEP reaches 62.5, exceeding T-GRPO and GiGRPO by 14.1 and 7.1 points, respectively. On AndroidWorld, it achieves 45.7 overall, surpassing T-GRPO by 14.7 and GiGRPO by 11.7 points, confirming its robustness across domains.
L244: Figure cite50†1 further illustrates that GiGRPO and STEP, both using step-level samples, improve faster and achieve higher scores than T-GRPO. We hypothesize that step-level training helps reorganize trajectories and maintain shorter context windows, exposing the model to more state-similar samples and accelerating convergence. Moreover, compared with GiGRPO, STEP performs even better, likely due to its dynamic adjustment of in-batch training samples based on success rates.
L245: This adaptive sampling strategy encourages the model to focus on under-learned or unstable tasks, avoiding overfitting and reducing the risk of local optima. Our step-level augmentation further enriches training data by generating fine-grained samples from challenging tasks, providing higher-quality learning signals and faster convergence.
L246: It is worth noting that the improvement from the Train Set to overall evaluation is less consistent on OSWorld than on AndroidWorld. On OSWorld, STEP improves by 21.1 points on the Train Set and by 7.0 points overall, whereas on AndroidWorld, the improvements are 17.8 and 16.0 points, respectively. This indicates that improvements observed during OSWorld training do not fully transfer to the overall evaluation.
L247: We hypothesize that this discrepancy stems from the OSWorld Train Set being constructed via pre-sampling cite71†Lu et al. (2025) , which selects tasks more likely to yield rewards. As a result, the remaining tasks in the overall set are harder and less aligned with the training distribution, leading to weaker generalization. In contrast, the AndroidWorld Train Set was randomly sampled, ensuring each app contributes at least one task, which allows overall performance to better reflect Train Set gains.
L248: #### 6.2.2 Ablation Study
L249: 
L250: We conduct ablation experiments to assess the contribution of each core component in STEP. Three variants are evaluated: (1) w/o SR-Sampling, which removes success-rate sampling; (2) w/o Step-Aug, which removes step augmentation; (3) w/o Both, which disables both components, leaving only our advantage estimation applied to GiGRPO.
L251: As shown in Table cite81†2 , removing either SR-Sampling or Step-Aug results in a clear performance drop, demonstrating that both components are essential to the effectiveness of STEP. Specifically, excluding SR-Sampling causes a 7.1-point decrease on the Train Set, suggesting that adaptive sampling plays a key role in stabilizing training and improving sample quality.
L252: Meanwhile, as shown in Figure cite50†1 , removing Step-Aug slows down the growth in task diversity, indicating that augmenting intermediate steps provides additional supervision signals that enhance learning efficiency. The variant without both components (21.4 on OSWorld) performs slightly better than GiGRPO (23.8). This result suggests that our advantage estimation is reasonable and that training solely on successful trajectories indeed provides measurable benefits.

L22@P0: 1 Introduction
L23@P0: Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as transformative technologies, demonstrating remarkable capabilities
L24@P0: across diverse domains from natural language understanding to code generation and tool use (Brown et al., 2020;
L25@P0: Comanici et al., 2025; Dubey et al., 2024; Roziere et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023a,b; Achiam
L26@P0: et al., 2023; Team et al., 2023). The training paradigms for these foundational models typically involve massivescale pretraining on diverse corpora followed by supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human
L27@P0: feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022). However, current training procedures remain extremely resource- and
L28@P0: time-intensive, often requiring large amounts of compute power and careful orchestration of training data mixtures to
L29@P0: achieve desired capabilities to achieve (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2022).
L30@P0: Recent advances in model souping—the practice of averaging weights from multiple models of the same architecture—have shown promising results as a post-training technique to enhance performance without requiring expensive
L31@P0: retraining. Prior work has demonstrated that uniform averaging of model weights can lead to improved performance
L32@P0: across various tasks Wortsman et al. (2022) and mitigate issues like catastrophic forgetting Kleiman et al. (2025). These
L33@P0: findings suggest that model souping offers a computationally efficient alternative to traditional approaches that rely on
L34@P0: modifying training data recipes or extensive retraining procedures.
L35@P0: In this paper, we demonstrate that carefully designed souping techniques can achieve state-of-the-art performance by
L36@P0: considering benchmark composition and employing non-uniform weighting strategies. In particular, our approach
L37@P0: addresses two key limitations of existing techniques: the arbitrary selection of models for souping and the assumption
L38@P0: that uniform weighting is optimal. The contributions of this paper are thus summarized as follows:
L39@P0: 1
L40@P0-1: arXiv:2511.13254v1 [cs.CL] 17 Nov 20251. Automated Checkpoint Souping: We introduce Soup Of Category Experts (SoCE), a novel model souping
L41@P1: technique that leverages benchmark composition through an automatic category-aware expert selection mechanism.
L42@P1: Unlike prior approaches that rely on uniform averaging Wortsman et al. (2022), SoCE exploits the observation
L43@P1: that benchmark categories often exhibit low inter-correlations in model performance to identify “expert” models
L44@P1: for each weakly-correlated category cluster, and aggregate their expertise using non-uniform weighted averaging
L45@P1: to maximize overall performance.
L46@P1: 2. State-of-the-Art Performance: We demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method across diverse domains,
L47@P1: including state-of-the-art results for the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (Patil et al.). Our approach
L48@P1: consistently outperforms existing baselines, validating the effectiveness of category-specific model souping.
L49@P1: 3. Higher Model Consistency: We perform a large-scale empirical analysis to show that model souping enhances
L50@P1: performance consistency across benchmark categories. Souped models exhibit significantly higher Pearson
L51@P1: correlations between category performances across model populations compared to their unsouped counterparts,
L52@P1: indicating improved robustness and coherence across diverse task types.
L53@P1: 2 Related Work
L54@P1: While model merging or averaging techniques are not new (Regent’s et al. (1996) describe one of the first implementations), we can outline the current emerged methodologies applied to LLMs for efficiency and generalization
L55@P1: improvements.
L56@P1: Souping: Model Souping has been explored in the machine learning literature in multiple contexts Jang et al. (2025);
L57@P1: Jaiswal et al. (2023); Kleiman et al. (2025); Wortsman et al. (2022); Yu et al. (2024); Zimmer et al. (2023). Wortsman
L58@P1: et al. (2022) show how using souping on finetuned models can lead to improvements over the individual models where
L59@P1: individual models are variants of different hyperparameters of the same base models. They proposed three strategies
L60@P1: for souping: Uniform Souping (where all models are weighted equally), Greedy Souping (add 1 model at a time in
L61@P1: decreasing order of performance) and Learned-Souping (soup weights are learned via gradient descent). They show
L62@P1: the most promising results via Greedy Souping technique. Jang et al. (2025) is the closest to our work. They improve
L63@P1: upon the work by Wortsman et al. (2022) using a geometric insight of the proximity to pre-trained models to reduce
L64@P1: the number of models required to soup to achieve better results than greedy souping techniques. We take this further
L65@P1: by utilizing the insights from the correlation of metrics as well as co-operative game theory within a benchmark to
L66@P1: formalize the selection of the best candidates and the proportion of souping.
L67@P1: In a related study Yu et al. (2024), the authors take several homologous (same architecture and parameters) models
L68@P1: and perform deactivation and reactivation of neurons and merge the newly derived models to achieve better models
L69@P1: compared to the original input candidates. In another line of work, model souping has been used in continual learning to
L70@P1: address catastrophic forgetting during finetuning of LLMs Kleiman et al. (2025).
L71@P1: Automatic model merging. Several works explore automatic model merging techniques: authors in Yang et al. (2024)
L72@P1: show that we can use unsupervised methods to discover optimal merging parameters based on entropy minimization. The
L73@P1: technique is mostly applied to classification tasks with ViT models, leading to gains in the multi-task learning domain

L451@P8: Limitations
L452@P8: 7.1 General Method Limitations
L453@P8: The results presented in this paper advance our understanding of model souping, but some methodological and conceptual
L454@P8: limitations warrant more discussion.
L455@P8: Benchmark structure: The first limitation we want to highlight is a key assumption that a given benchmark already
L456@P8: has some sub-categorical splits which have enough data points such that we can estimate correlations with enough
L457@P8: model. There are several benchmarks today which do not come with pre-classified sub-categories and hence, we propose
L458@P8: 1https://huggingface.co/models?search=llama
L459@P8-9: 9benchmark clustering as a future work to this approach. Furthermore, for selecting candidates for SoCEapproach for
L460@P9: BFCL and MGSM benchmarks, we have used the leaderboard scores directly based on the assumption that in the future
L461@P9: setup, we would have an oracle development set to select candidates without leaking any information.
L462@P9: Application in the model training practice: In this work, we have only tested the souping of the ’final’ posttrained
L463@P9: and aligned checkpoints. As other works have shown, the models can be souped after pre-training Li et al. (2025), after
L464@P9: posttraining Wortsman et al. (2022), with adapters2. We do not recommend souping of the checkpoints from different
L465@P9: training stages, as well as souping unaligned or uncensored models with the aligned ones to avoid the inheritance of
L466@P9: risks.
L467@P9: We also want to highlight that all the experiments are carried out on Llama 3 (Dubey et al., 2024) derivative models
L468@P9: and essentially have the same pretrained checkpoint. We are currently unaware if souping requires the same pretrained
L469@P9: checkpoint or can it work with different pretrained checkpoints as well.
L470@P9: Model Architecture: Lastly, this work has focused on souping largely for dense models and assumes that the same
L471@P9: methodology can be applied to other architectures such as mixture of experts.
L472@P9: 7.2 Scaling Laws
L473@P9: While model souping has demonstrated promising results, it is important to consider potential limitations and diminishing
L474@P9: returns as more models are combined. The extent to which performance continues to improve may depend on the
L475@P9: diversity and capabilities of the individual models being souped. We have not yet systematically tested the upper bounds
L476@P9: of this approach, and it is possible that there exists an optimal strategy for souping checkpoints that varies according to
L477@P9: the capability differences between the models involved. Further empirical investigation is needed to better understand
L478@P9: these scaling dynamics.
